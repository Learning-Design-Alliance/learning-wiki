# Faculty Course Design

> **Design Process** · [All design processes](index.md)

## Description
Most courses in higher education are not designed by instructional designers. They are designed by the person who will teach them, in the weeks before term, alongside a full teaching and research load, with no budget, no team and no client. A teaching and learning centre may consult, run a workshop or read a draft syllabus — but it advises, it does not approve, and the instructor holds the decision. This is the most common design process in the sector and it is a distinct pattern, not a degraded version of a professional one.

Its shape follows from its constraints. Analysis is compressed into what the instructor already knows about the subject and remembers about last year's cohort. The syllabus, not a design document, is the design artifact: sequence, assessment, policy and objectives all land there because that is the document the institution requires. Iteration is real but slow — the improvement cycle is one offering long, and the evidence is grades, evaluations and memory rather than instrumentation. Disciplinary norms substitute for a lot of the analysis a systematic process would do explicitly.

Treating it as an inferior [systematic process](systematic-instructional-design.md) leads to advice nobody can follow. Treating it on its own terms leads to a different question: given one instructor, a few weeks and one revision per year, which design moves are worth their cost? The answer that teaching centres have converged on is small — align assessment to objectives, map the course before writing the weeks, collect feedback early enough to act within the term — and that shortness is the pattern's substance.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Subject-matter authority in the designer** — the instructor is the SME, which removes the elicitation problem a professional process spends most of its analysis on
- **A catalogue description or departmental outcome set** the course must satisfy
- **Enough lead time to map before writing.** Where design begins in week one, the course is being written weekly and no design pattern applies
- **A consultative teaching centre, or a colleague** — the outside reader who is the only structural check on a single designer's blind spots

#### Constraints
- **The designer is also the audience-model**, and knows the material too well to feel where it is hard. This is the expert blind spot, and it is the pattern's characteristic failure
- **No independent evaluation.** The instructor designs, teaches, assesses and judges the result, so a design flaw and a cohort effect are hard to distinguish
- **One revision cycle per year**, and the evidence arrives — end-of-term evaluations — after the cohort that generated it has gone
- **Time is the binding constraint, not skill.** Recommendations that assume a design project's budget are not adopted; they are nodded at
- **Course evaluations measure satisfaction**, and satisfaction tracks fluency — [Fluent Illusions Mislead Self Assessment](../claims/fluent-illusions-mislead-self-assessment.md) [~S]. A course made harder in a way that improves learning can read as a course made worse

#### Grain Size
- Course
- Unit (a single week or module revised in isolation, which is the most common real unit of change)

### Target Goals
- A coherent course that an individual can build and run within an ordinary workload
- Alignment between what is claimed, what is taught and what is assessed — the single highest-value move available at this budget
- Improvements that survive the instructor: a course map and rationale another colleague can pick up

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and graduate cohorts in a disciplinary programme, where the course sits in a sequence and its prerequisites are set by the curriculum rather than by analysis
- Cohorts more diverse than the instructor's own student experience, which is the assumption the pattern most often gets wrong

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — the disciplinary-apprenticeship framing most instructors already hold
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — the course as entry into a discipline's practice

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — the expert blind spot is a load problem: an expert cannot feel the element interactivity a novice faces, so the instructor-as-designer systematically under-scaffolds

### Claims
#### Supporting
- [Goal setting improves performance](../claims/goal-setting-improves-performance.md) [+M] — the case for writing objectives before the schedule
- [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S] — the argument for early, low-stakes checks rather than a midterm-and-final architecture
- [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Contradicting
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [-M] — read in the direction that matters here: the designer's own expertise is what makes their intuitions about difficulty unreliable
- [Fluent Illusions Mislead Self Assessment](../claims/fluent-illusions-mislead-self-assessment.md) [~S] — applies to the instructor's read of their own course as much as to the learner's

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Start from the outcomes the course owes** — the catalogue description, the programme outcome map, the prerequisite it is for someone else's course. Write three to five course-level objectives. [Learning Objectives](../elements/learning-objectives.md)
2. **Decide how each will be evidenced** before planning any week — [Backward Design](../methods/backward-design.md), the move this pattern's whole cost-benefit argument rests on
3. **Map the course** — a one-page grid of weeks against objectives, assessments and workload, built before any week is written. Gaps and pile-ups are visible here and nowhere else
4. **Write the syllabus as the design artifact** — sequence, assessment weights, policies and the rationale for them, in the document the institution already requires
5. **Build one unit properly** and let it set the template, rather than building fourteen weeks at uniform shallow depth
6. **Collect feedback early enough to use it** — a short mid-term check, not only the end-of-term evaluation.
7. **Consult** — the teaching centre, or a colleague running [peer observation](../strategies/peer-observation.md). One outside reader, once, catches the expert blind spot better than any amount of solitary revision
8. **Record what to change while teaching it**, not at the end. The next offering is the revision cycle, and the memory does not survive it. [Reflective Practice](../strategies/reflective-practice.md)

### Personalization
- **Inheriting a course** — run steps 1–3 as an audit of what exists before changing anything; the map usually shows the misalignment the previous instructor felt but could not locate
- **A team-taught or multi-section course** turns the syllabus into a coordination document, and the course map into the thing colleagues actually negotiate over
- **Where a teaching centre offers a course design institute**, that is this pattern with the analysis and mapping steps given protected time — the single most effective intervention available, precisely because time, not skill, is the constraint

- **Teaching-centre course design institutes** — multi-day workshops in which faculty build the objective map and assessment plan with facilitation, then write the course
