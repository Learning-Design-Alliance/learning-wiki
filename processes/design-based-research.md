# Design-Based Research

> **Design Process** · [All design processes](index.md)

## Description
Design-based research (DBR) develops an educational intervention and a theory together. The designer-researcher builds the intervention in a real teaching and learning setting, tests it, revises it, and tests it again. Each revision is a small experiment, and what is learned is meant to improve the design and contribute to theory about learning. Christensen and West's review trace it to Brown (1992) and Collins (1990, 1992), who took Herbert Simon's call in *The Sciences of the Artificial* for a rigorous science of designed things and applied it to classrooms.

The literature has not agreed on a single definition. Christensen and West identify three ways it has been characterised:

- **By purpose.** DBR develops design principles grounded in theory and validated by research, while contributing directly to practice (van den Akker, 1999). It addresses the tension between locally usable knowledge and generalisable knowledge (Sandoval & Bell, 2004).
- **By characteristics.** The seven most often named are that it is **design driven, situated, iterative, collaborative, theory building, practical and productive** (the design itself must meet its intended outcomes).
- **By steps.** Published models differ: Collins's ten design-experiment steps, Brown's example, Bannan-Ritland's (2003) four stages (informed exploration, enactment, evaluation of local impact, evaluation of broad impact), Reeves's (2006) four steps ending in reflection, and Anderson and Shattuck's (2012) eight-step aggregate.

Borrowing from interdisciplinary design research, Christensen and West recommend that each DBR study state which kind of design research it is. **Research into design** studies the design process from outside. **Research for design** serves the development of an effective product. **Research through design** uses the design process itself to test and develop theory.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **An authentic setting with practitioner partners**: teachers and learners who can take part as collaborators in repeated cycles, not only as participants
- **Time and budget for several iterations.** A single build-and-test is not DBR
- **An explicit theory the design embodies**, so that each revision tests something beyond "did this version work better"
- **Coordination of an interdisciplinary team**: researchers, designers, practitioners and developers

#### Constraints
- **Competing roles.** One person is often researcher, project manager, theorist and designer at once. The authors note that researchers can focus on only one role at a time, so over-investing in one puts the others at risk. See [the claim on competing roles](../claims/design-based-research-roles-compete-and-threaten-credible-claims.md) [~W]
- **Credibility of claims.** A researcher who designed, built, implemented and studied an approach is exposed to bias in analysis (Barab & Squire, 2004; Anderson & Shattuck, 2012)
- **Fidelity to theory is suspect.** In an iterating, multi-tasking project, the design as built may not embody the theory it claims to test (Hoadley, 2004)
- **Hard to control variables in real settings**, and the qualitative data many studies produce are heavy to collect and analyse
- **Hard to recognise and replicate**, because the terminology has proliferated and studies are often published in a standard research format that hides the design process

#### Grain Size
- Course
- Unit
- Programme (a multi-year intervention in a district or network of schools)

### Target Goals
- An intervention that works in the setting it was designed for, and design principles that explain why
- Local theory about how learning happened in this setting, and transferable theory beyond it

### Target Learners
- Learners in the real setting the intervention is built for, often a context existing research has not reached

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — learning is studied in the context that shapes it, which is why DBR refuses the laboratory
- [Designerly Stances](../theories/designerly-stances.md) — design as a way of knowing, which DBR treats as a source of research knowledge

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- The difficulty of isolating variables in authentic settings qualifies how far a DBR finding can support a causal claim. The authors describe researchers as torn between isolating critical variables and studying the design experience as a whole

### Claims
#### Supporting
- None yet recorded in the wiki from a DBR study's own evidence

#### Contradicting
- [Design-based research asks one person to be researcher, designer, project manager and theorist, and those roles compete.](../claims/design-based-research-roles-compete-and-threaten-credible-claims.md) [~W]

## Design

### Sequence
The literature offers no single sequence. This composite follows the stages Bannan-Ritland (2003) and Reeves (2006) share, which Christensen and West set side by side.
1. **Informed exploration**: analyse the practical problem with practitioners, review the literature, and name the theory the design will embody ([Needs Analysis](../methods/needs-analysis.md), [Stakeholder Mapping](../methods/stakeholder-mapping.md))
2. **Enactment**: design the intervention and implement it in the real setting
3. **Iterate**: test, revise and re-test, treating each revision as a small experiment and recording what changed and why
4. **Evaluate local impact**: does the design meet its intended outcomes here?
5. **Evaluate broad impact** and **reflect**: produce design principles and theory, local and transferable

### Affordances
- [Formative Assessment](../principles/formative-assessment.md) — the iteration loop is formative evaluation of the design, run as research

### Personalization
- **State the type of design research** (into, for or through design), so a study does not try to be all three
- **Report the design process in the paper.** Christensen and West suggest methods subheadings for design research type, design process and product, design and learning context, design collaborations, and each iteration with its data. Conclusions would then be split into local theory, transferable theory, and applications for practice
