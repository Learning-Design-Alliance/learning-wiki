# Miller's pyramid of clinical competence assessment

## Description
Miller's pyramid is a four-level framework ordering assessment targets from factual knowledge to real-world performance: "Knows" (best assessed with written exams), "Knows How" (written or oral exams), "Shows How" (performance-based assessments with standardized simulations or oral exams with standardized scenarios), and "Does" (clinic-based performance assessments). The review uses it to map which assessment formats and construct types are prevalent in physical therapy education and to identify which levels are underrepresented.

- **Miller's four competency levels as an organising framework for patient safety assessment**: The review uses Miller's four competency levels — 'knows', 'knows how', 'shows how', and 'does' — as its central organising framework for categorising assessment tools. At each level "the trainee should be able to demonstrate the ability to imitate or replicate a protocol, apply principles in a familiar situation, adapt principles to new situations, and associate new knowledge with previously learned principles". The review maps all 34 identified tools onto these levels and argues that ideally a toolbox would assess each competency at the Miller level fitting the stage of training reached. (Okuyama Ayako et al. (2011))
- **Miller's pyramid as a framework for locating what a CBL module assesses**: The authors use Miller's pyramid to characterize what their module evaluates: "the module primarily assessed Knows and Knows How through the pre/postquiz and application of knowledge to case-based questions." They note that individual performance at the Shows How or Does level was not directly observed through simulation, role-play, or workplace-based assessment, while most learners reported anticipated practice change, suggesting transfer to clinical care. The framework is also used to argue the module's alignment with competency frameworks and entrustable professional activities. (Huynh G et al. (2026))
- **Miller's Pyramid of Clinical Competence as organizing framework for MgSO4 competency assessment**: The study used Miller's Pyramid of Clinical Competence, which "categorises clinical performance into four hierarchical levels: 'knows', 'knows how', 'shows how', and 'does'." This framework structured the exploration of three domains: knowledge (what providers know), performance (how they apply knowledge in real or simulated situations), and confidence (how assured they feel managing hypertensive emergencies). It provided a structured approach to examining provider-level factors contributing to suboptimal MgSO4 administration. (Kamanga Martha et al. (2026))
- **Miller's Pyramid as the framework for conceptualizing progression in procedural training**: The article uses Miller's Pyramid as its educational-theory lens for procedural training. It "describes progression from knowledge acquisition (“knows”) to demonstration of competence (“shows how”) and ultimately independent clinical performance (“does”)". Within this framework, the article argues that variability in supervised clinical exposure may influence how trainees translate simulated competence into independent practice, providing a structure for interpreting participants' accounts of the gap between manikin-based preparation and real clinical performance. (Abou Farah Joy et al. (2026))
- **Miller's Pyramid of Clinical Competence as a framework for locating simulation in nursing education**: The article uses Miller's Pyramid of Clinical Competence to frame how learners progress from theoretical knowledge to clinical practice across four levels: "knows, knows how, shows how, and does". It situates traditional classroom instruction at the knows-how level and simulation-based learning experiences at the shows-how level, arguing that SBLE bridges the gap between theory and practice in a safe, supportive environment, which may in turn prepare students for real-world stressors and potentially reduce burnout. (Martín‐Parrilla et al. (2025))
- **Miller's pyramid as the organizing framework for aligning ultrasound assessment formats with competence levels**: The review organizes ultrasound assessment formats using Miller's assessment and clinical competence pyramid, which orders test formats by competence level. At the base, knowledge of ultrasound physics and techniques is measured by self-assessment, MCQs, or oral exams; knowledge application is evaluated through case-vignette MCQs; behavioral skills are assessed via OSCE; and the top level, controlled clinical application, is assessed by workplace-based tools such as DOPS. The article states that "According to Miller's pyramid, different test formats align with various competence levels (see Fig. 1)", and uses this structure to justify aiming assessment "at the highest possible level" of practical skill. (Neubauer R et al. (2025))
- **Miller's pyramid of competence as a framework for assessing clinical ultrasound competence**: Miller's pyramid, first described in 1990, defines conceptual levels of clinical competence that progress from "knows" to "knows how" to "shows how" and finally to "does", each of which may be individually assessed. The review uses it as its organizing framework: written assessments best examine "knows" or "knows how", objective structured clinical examinations assess "shows how", and logbooks may strengthen validity for "does" if used robustly. It underpins the review's argument that multimodal assessment is required to cover every facet of competence. (McCracken DJ et al. (2019))
- **Miller Pyramid learning model structuring the PEAK training program**: The PEAK training program was developed in line with the Miller Pyramid, "a learning model designed for use in health education". The model proposes a foundation of knowledge (knows), then competence (knows how) demonstrated via a mock video consultation with a simulated patient, then performance (shows how) demonstrated via practice consultations with pilot patients, and finally action (does), when the learner integrates knowledge, skills, and attitudes into clinical practice. The PEAK program mapped its asynchronous modules, mock consultation, and practice consultations onto these sequential stages. (Sarah E Jones et al. (2021))
