## Description
The article frames cognitive task analysis (CTA) as a process in which "experts provide complex information to trainees in a logical and simplified manner", making procedural steps easier to visualize and digest. CTA packages technical steps, cognitive decision points, and potential errors and solutions, delivered here through simultaneous written and audiovisual modalities. It has been used to train pilots and in other surgical specialties, and the authors position it as relatively new to orthopedic training.

- **Modified task analysis procedure for operationalizing ACER without a knowledge extraction expert**: This is the article's method for turning the general ACER structure into tool-specific elements. A subject matter expert solves canonical problems while documenting reasoning and mapping solution elements onto ACER components; other SMEs then reach consensus on element classification. Unlike formal task analysis, "Our modified task analysis does not include a KEE", a removal made for practical reasons. The resulting operationalized framework is refined against patterns in student work that the experts' own solutions did not exhibit. (Wilcox et al. (2013))
- **Task analysis of an assessment question as the organizing structure for a difficulties rubric**: To classify student approaches and group the variety of errors, "a task analysis [12] was carried out for the question," enumerating the necessary steps for constructing the differential equation: visualizing the scenario, determining distances, writing force expressions, determining signs, writing the net force, and recognizing the force as mass times acceleration. The difficulties rubric then categorizes student difficulties within these steps. The steps need not be completed in a fixed order, which think-aloud interviews confirmed. (Leanne Doughty et al. (2014))
- **Cognitive task analysis of troubleshooting**: The article grounds its analysis in a cognitive task analysis of troubleshooting that identifies six kinds of knowledge facilitating competent troubleshooting — "Domain, System, Procedural, Strategic, Metacognitive, and Experiential" — and, following Schaafstal et al., subdivides the recursive troubleshooting process into four iterative subtasks: Formulate Problem Description, Generate Causes, Test, and Repair and Evaluate. It also names three common strategies: Exhaustive, Topographic, and Split-Half. The article maps these knowledge types and tasks onto the Modeling Framework, and uses the four tasks as an a priori coding scheme for the think-aloud data. (Dimitri R. Dounas-Frazer et al. (2016))
- **Experimental Physics Cognitive Task Analysis (EPCTA) framework**: The EPCTA is a framework of eight cognitive task elements used by physicists to carry out an experiment from conception to completion, defined by Wieman as the "mental tasks or types of thinking ('cognitive task analysis') associated with a physicist doing tabletop experimental research". The elements span establishing research goals, defining criteria for suitable evidence, determining feasibility, experimental design, construction and testing of apparatus/code, analyzing data, evaluating results, and presenting the work. This article uses the EPCTA as the coding lens for characterizing students' cognitive processes in UREs and coursework, noting the process is highly iterative and cyclic rather than linear. (N.G. Holmes et al. (2016))
- **Cognitive task analysis of troubleshooting: four subtasks and four knowledge types**: The article describes troubleshooting as a cognitive task subdivided into four subtasks: "formulating the problem description, generating causes, performing tests, and making and evaluating repairs." Troubleshooting is facilitated by multiple types of knowledge: "domain, system, strategic, and metacognitive knowledge." The process is nonlinear and recursive, with troubleshooters transitioning between subtasks. The authors use this cognitive task analysis to identify key episodes (transitions between subtasks) where socially mediated metacognition is likely to arise. (Van De Bogart et al. (2017))
