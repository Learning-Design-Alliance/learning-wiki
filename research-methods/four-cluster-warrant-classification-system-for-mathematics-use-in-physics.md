# Four-cluster warrant classification system for mathematics use in physics

## Description
From an iterative knowledge analysis of about 50 argument snippets, the authors classify students' mathematical justifications into four framing clusters. As they report, "Four main clusters emerged from this data set's examples of physics students' framing of their math use": Calculation, Physical Mapping, Invoking Authority, and Math Consistency. Each cluster is identified primarily by the general class of warrant used (e.g., algorithmic steps yield trustable results; goodness-of-fit between math and physical situation) and secondarily by indicators such as math chaining, demonstrative gesturing, quoting rules, or analogy with another math idea (Table 1).

- **Taxonomy of four epistemological resources and framings in students' mathematical use in upper-level physics**: From iterative analysis of video data, the authors identified four common epistemological framings in upper-level physics students' use of mathematics: Calculation, Physical Mapping, Invoking Authority, and Mathematical Consistency. The identification "originally came from th is data set" of approximately 150 hours of video of students solving problems. Most explicit warrants students articulated in conflict or challenge situations fit these categories, though the authors state the list "is not meant to be an exclusive list." Warrants, borrowed from rhetoric and argumentation theory, link claims to supporting data. (Bing et al. (2009))
- **Bing and Redish's four-category framework of epistemological warrants in mathematical problem solving**: The article describes Bing and Redish's framework for analyzing how upper-division physics majors justify their reasoning, dividing warrants into four categories: invoking authority, mathematical consistency, calculation, and physical mapping. These warrant classes reflect four epistemological stances, and physical mapping corresponds to the sense-making aspect the article focuses on. The framework grounds the article's account of students switching among stances. (Gupta et al. (2011))
