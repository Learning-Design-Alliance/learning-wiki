# Item Response Theory (IRT)

## Description
The article frames computerized adaptive testing on plain Item Response Theory, which "uses mathematical models to estimate the underlying ability level ('theta') of the test-taker based on their responses to different items", with each item characterized by difficulty and discrimination parameters. This contrasts with Classical Test Theory, the basis of the conventional fixed linear PT, which assumes the observed total score equals true ability with identical measurement error for all scores, potentially yielding less precise ability estimates.

<!-- How each source describes or uses the method -->
- **Item Response Theory (IRT) as the psychometric basis of computer adaptive testing, contrasted with Classical Test Theory**: The article frames computerized adaptive testing on plain Item Response Theory, which "uses mathematical models to estimate the underlying ability level ('theta') of the test-taker based on their responses to different items", with each item characterized by difficulty and discrimination parameters. This contrasts with Classical Test Theory, the basis of the conventional fixed linear PT, which assumes the observed total score equals true ability with identical measurement error for all scores, potentially yielding less precise ability estimates. (Van Wijk EV et al. (2024))
- **Rasch/IRT-based item banking for reusable admission test scales**: The article adopts item response theory, specifically the 1PL Rasch model, as the framework for building an interchangeable item bank for admission testing. Its stated long-term aim is "to accumulate a database of tested items that fit a common scale and thus are interchangeable with respect to that scale", because fresh items are needed yearly. IRT is chosen for test equating via linear transformation, model-fit testability, extensive item and person diagnostics such as infit and outfit statistics, and the item-person map, which classical test theory does not offer. Rasch scaling additionally guarantees interval-quality trait scores at the cost of severe item selection. (Hissbach Johanna C et al. (2011))
- **Three-parameter item response model for multiple-choice test analysis**: The three-parameter item response model describes the probability of a correct response as a logistic function of the difference between examinee proficiency θ and item difficulty bi, scaled by discrimination ai with lower asymptote ci representing guessing. The article states "ci is the lower asymptote of the item characteristic function as θ → -∞, describing the probability of giving a correct answer by an examinee with extremely low proficiency". It treats item difficulty and examinee proficiency as separate parameters, unlike classical test theory where item parameters are inseparable from the group. (Jing Wang et al. (2008))
- **Item response theory with the graded response model as a framework for scaling misconception difficulty independent of pedagogy**: The article applies item response theory (IRT), specifically Samejima's graded response model, to recoded misconception-retention scores so that item difficulty is established independently of participant abilities and teaching technique. Category response curves and item information curves locate transitions between levels of misconception retention, and CRC intersection coordinates sequence items by difficulty. The authors state IRT is preferable to classical test theory because “IRT is generally more precise than CTT” given large samples of well-correlated items. (Andrej Favia et al. (2013))
- **Item-response-theory model of exam scores with exam-dependent discriminatory power for estimating standardized effect sizes**: The article proposes modeling a student's score on exam j as Yij = σj(θi + τWij + εij) + μj, where θi is latent ability, μj exam difficulty, and σj exam-dependent discriminatory power. As the article states, "By recognizing that exams not only vary in difficulty (i.e., mean) but also in discriminatory power (i.e. variance), we obtain a more nuanced model of exam scores." Under this model the standardized effect size τ is constant across exams and is estimated by averaging per-student differences between treatment and control z-scores; the article derives consistency and asymptotic normality of this estimator via the Central Limit Theorem. The model justifies aggregating data across the two quarters. (Dennis L. Sun et al. (2014))
- **Unidimensional Item Response Theory with three-parameter logistic item characteristic curves**: IRT is a latent trait model in which, as the article puts it, "each student is described by a number of latent traits, also call proficiencies", and each question is modeled by an item characteristic curve Pi(θ) giving the probability of a correct answer. In the three-parameter model, Pi(θ) depends on the question's discrimination ai, difficulty bi and guessing parameter ci. The article applies this unidimensional model to the FCI, using parameter invariance to borrow item functions from Wang and Bao, and converts raw scores into the continuous proficiency θ used to quantify progression. (Jean-François Parmentier et al. (2015))
- **2PL-NRM nested logit item response theory model for ranking incorrect responses**: The model combines the two-parameter logistic IRT model, which estimates each student's latent trait theta and each question's difficulty and discrimination, with Bock's nominal response model applied to the incorrect responses only. The ak parameter of each incorrect response measures its correlation with the latent trait, so "a higher value indicating a response that is more closely correlated with the latent trait and, therefore, better". The authors use the mirt package in R and rank responses by comparing ak distributions generated by repeated random sampling. (Trevor I. Smith et al. (2019))
- **IRT nominal response model (NRM) for ranking all response options to multiple-choice items**: Bock's nominal response model defines the probability of selecting each response option as a function of the person parameter (latent trait) and per-option parameters. The article states that "The ak parameter is a measure of how much each response option aligns with the latent trait θ, and is thus used as a metric for ranking the responses", while the dk parameter relates to choice likelihood independent of θ and is not used for ranking. Unlike the 2PL-NRM nested-logit model, the NRM can be applied to all response choices without a priori specification of the correct response. (Trevor I. Smith et al. (2024))
- **Bayesian nonparametric dynamic IRT framework integrating B-spline engagement-drift functions and an MFM prior**: The framework models binary item responses with a 1PL observation model, tracks within-individual ability across instructional chapters via a first-order Markov state-space process, and specifies expected ability drift through B-spline basis expansions of engagement covariates with cluster-specific scaling coefficients. As the article states, it uses "a dynamic IRT process to track within-individual ability trajectories across instructional chapters, a B-spline basis expansions to capture nonlinear engagement-ability drift, and an MFM prior to infer the number of latent learner clusters jointly with cluster-specific parameters". The MFM prior treats the number of clusters as random, avoiding prespecified K, and the spline specification lets covariate-effect shapes be learned from data. Posterior inference uses tailored MCMC sampling in NIMBLE with ECR relabeling. (Zhihua Ma et al. (2026))
