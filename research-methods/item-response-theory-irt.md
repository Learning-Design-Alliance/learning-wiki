## Description
The article frames computerized adaptive testing on plain Item Response Theory, which "uses mathematical models to estimate the underlying ability level ('theta') of the test-taker based on their responses to different items", with each item characterized by difficulty and discrimination parameters. This contrasts with Classical Test Theory, the basis of the conventional fixed linear PT, which assumes the observed total score equals true ability with identical measurement error for all scores, potentially yielding less precise ability estimates.

- **Item Response Theory (IRT) as the psychometric basis of computer adaptive testing, contrasted with Classical Test Theory**: The article frames computerized adaptive testing on plain Item Response Theory, which "uses mathematical models to estimate the underlying ability level ('theta') of the test-taker based on their responses to different items", with each item characterized by difficulty and discrimination parameters. This contrasts with Classical Test Theory, the basis of the conventional fixed linear PT, which assumes the observed total score equals true ability with identical measurement error for all scores, potentially yielding less precise ability estimates. (Van Wijk EV et al. (2024))
- **Rasch/IRT-based item banking for reusable admission test scales**: The article adopts item response theory, specifically the 1PL Rasch model, as the framework for building an interchangeable item bank for admission testing. Its stated long-term aim is "to accumulate a database of tested items that fit a common scale and thus are interchangeable with respect to that scale", because fresh items are needed yearly. IRT is chosen for test equating via linear transformation, model-fit testability, extensive item and person diagnostics such as infit and outfit statistics, and the item-person map, which classical test theory does not offer. Rasch scaling additionally guarantees interval-quality trait scores at the cost of severe item selection. (Hissbach Johanna C et al. (2011))
