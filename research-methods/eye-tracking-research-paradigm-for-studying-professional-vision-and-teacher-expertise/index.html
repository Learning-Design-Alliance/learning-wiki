Eye-tracking research paradigm for studying professional vision and teacher expertise¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 3 studies (2 associational, 1 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 3 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article characterizes an emerging research paradigm in which eye-tracking methodologies are used to study professional vision and teacher expertise. It argues the paradigm brings together "different and originally separated strands of research": professional vision in teacher education, expertise structure and development in cognitive science, eye-tracking study of cognitive processes in psychology, and educational effectiveness and instructional quality research. The seven special-issue contributions instantiate this paradigm.

Accounts¶

  • An emerging research paradigm joining four originally separated research strands on visual perception in teaching and learning: The article characterizes an emerging research paradigm in which eye-tracking methodologies are used to study professional vision and teacher expertise. It argues the paradigm brings together "different and originally separated strands of research": professional vision in teacher education, expertise structure and development in cognitive science, eye-tracking study of cognitive processes in psychology, and educational effectiveness and instructional quality research. The seven special-issue contributions instantiate this paradigm. (Nückles (2021))
  • Teacher gaze as an additional operationalization of the noticing component of the cognitive model of teacher professional vision: The article works within a cognitive model of teachers' professional vision comprising perceptual noticing and knowledge-based reasoning (Lachner et al. 2016). It argues that gaze, measured with eye-movement methodology, "provides a suitable operationalization for noticing processes in the forefront of further processing and interpreting observed events." The study extends this model to diagnosing individual student characteristics, distinguishing bottom-up (stimulus-driven) and top-down (goal-driven) gaze drivers, and tentatively concludes that "adding teacher gaze to the model can enrich our understanding of the processes involved in knowledge-based reasoning." (Seidel et al. (2021))
  • Gaze proportions as implicit micro-level indicators of explicit macro-level teacher priorities, analysed separately for attentional and communicative gaze: The article proposes that gaze proportions—how much a person looks at one region relative to alternatives—serve as implicit, intensive measures of teachers' enduring decision-making priorities, extending a decision-making analytic approach to educational psychology. It distinguishes attentional gaze (information-seeking, e.g., questioning) from communicative gaze (information-giving, e.g., lecturing), classifying gaze events by the speech co-occurring with them. Higher gaze proportions toward a region are interpreted as greater priority for that region. (McIntyre et al. (2019))

Claims¶

  • Experienced teachers diagnose underestimating, overestimating, and uninterested student profiles more accurately than novices, with partial eye-tracking support [+W]
  • Only a minority of teacher educators interpreted a critical classroom-management incident as theoretically expected, despite marked eye-tracking differences [+W]
  • Teachers and students use gaze to express agency and communion during classroom problem-solving [+W]
  • Expert teachers fixate more on struggling, underestimating, and uninterested students during individual seatwork, while novices show longer fixations on strong and overestimating students [+M]
  • Culture did not predict any attentional gaze proportions, but Hong Kong teachers looked more at teacher materials and UK teachers more at non-instructional regions during communicative gaze [+M]
