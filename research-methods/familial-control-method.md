## Description
The Familial Control Method is defined as "Using a measure of the same trait in the parent as the child as a covariate in models estimating the effect of the rearing environment"; the covariate "serves as a proxy control for the genetic transmission effect". The authors advise assessing the trait in both parents, because the child shares all their genes with both parents but only 50% with one. It is designed for traits transmitted mostly through genes, like reading ability, and is currently the most feasible proxy genetic control for most behavioral researchers, requiring neither twin/adoption samples nor DNA.

(Hart et al. (2021))
- **Children-of-siblings (cousin fixed-effect) design as a quasi-experimental within-family approach**: The children-of-siblings design compares parents who are siblings and their children (cousins), using "the siblings of parents as de facto matched controls". By comparing family members, factors shared between them — including roughly 50% of segregating genes and aspects of the childhood family environment — are held constant, providing an adjusted estimate without measuring confounders. The article uses it to test whether a parent with more symptoms is more likely to have a child with lower academic achievement than their sibling. (Demange Perline A. et al. (2026))
- **Use quasi-experimental family designs to identify modifiable targets in intergenerational research**: The article argues that observational parent-child associations may reflect confounding familial influences rather than direct environmental effects, and that "Within-family designs reduce confounding from genetic and environmental factors shared within family members, without requiring these factors to be directly measured". It recommends such designs for identifying modifiable targets in intergenerational health and education research, since only causal effects allow evaluation of potential interventions. (Demange Perline A. et al. (2026))
