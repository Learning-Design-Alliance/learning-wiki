# ACER framework for analyzing mathematical tool use in upper-division physics

> **Research Method** · [All research methods](index.md)

## Description
ACER is an analytical framework designed to guide and structure investigations of students' difficulties with sophisticated mathematical tools in upper-division physics. It organizes expert-identified solution elements into four components: "Activation of the tool, Construction of the model, Execution of the mathematics, and Reflection on the result". The framework focuses on key nodes in solutions to reduce noise, is grounded in a resource view of knowledge, and is deliberately usable by instructors without deep backgrounds in learning theories. It targets back-of-the-book or exam-type problems rather than open-ended ones.

- **The ACER framework as operationalized for delta function problems in junior-level electrostatics**: ACER (Activation, Construction, Execution, Reflection) is "an analytical framework that characterizes student difficulties with mathematics in upper-division physics by organizing the problem-solving process into four general components." The authors operationalize it for delta function use in expressing volume charge densities by having a content expert document solution steps and refining the outline against student work. The resulting element codes include A1, A2, C1, C2, C3, E1, R1, and R2, which were used to code exam solutions and classify interview moves. (Bethany R. Wilcox et al. (2014))
- **The ACER framework organizes upper-division students' use of mathematics in problem solving into four components**: ACER is an analytical framework developed through a modified task analysis with disciplinary experts to organize upper-division students' use of mathematics on canonical back-of-the-book problems. Expert negotiation "produced the four components of the ACER framework: Activation of the tool - selecting which mathematical tool will be used to facilitate a solution to the problem", followed by Construction of the model, Execution of the mathematics, and Reflection on the results. The authors stress the process is fluid and non-linear and that ACER organizes rather than models student work. (Caballero et al. (2015))
- **The ACER framework for analyzing mathematical tool use in physics problem solving**: ACER (Activation, Construction, Execution, Reflection) organizes the problem-solving process into four general components: "activation of mathematical tools, construction of mathematical models, execution of the mathematics, and reﬂection on the results". It was designed to be operationalized for specific mathematical tools in specific physics contexts via expert task analysis, producing a researcher-guided outline of key elements of a correct solution. In this article the authors operationalize ACER for SoV problems in Cartesian and spherical electrostatics and use it to organize and categorize student difficulties. (Bethany R. Wilcox et al. (2015))
- **ACER framework operationalized for boundary conditions in electrodynamics**: The ACER framework is an analytical tool that characterizes student difficulties with upper-division problem solving by organizing the process into four components: "Activation of the tools, Construction of the models, Execution of the mathematics, and Reﬂection on the results". It is explicitly based on a resources view of learning and is designed to be operationalized for specific physics topics. In this article the authors operationalize it for BCs in electromagnetic wave problems, with elements A1-A2, C1-C3, E1, and R1-R2, and use it to code exam mistakes and design interview protocols targeting aspects of problem solving not accessed by traditional exams. (Qing X. Ryan et al. (2015))
- **ACER framework for analyzing mathematics use in upper-division physics problem solving**: The ACER (Activation-Construction-Execution-Reflection) framework models physics problem solving as four components: "Activation (determining the proper mathematical tool); Construction (making a mathematical model); Execution (performing mathematical steps); and Reflection (checking the final solution)." Students may move freely among components in any order, visiting each several times with different sub-codes. The authors modified it for mechanics homework, extending sub-codes to cover errors. (Nandana Weliweriya et al. (2017))
- **Modified ACER codebook for mechanics with error codes**: The authors extended the existing ACER sub-codes for mechanics: "We extended the extant set of sub-codes to include codes appropriate for mechanics, as well as to cover students’ errors (e.g., mistakes done when taking derivatives, using an incorrect strategy, etc.)." The appendix codebook lists Activation (A1-A3, ANX), Construction (C1-C11, CNX), Execution (E1-E10, ENX) and Reflection (R1-R5, RNX) codes, including unhelpful/incorrect-strategy codes. Reliability was established iteratively until the code book covered more than 95% of student work and two graders achieved over 90% agreement. (Nandana Weliweriya et al. (2017))
- **The ACER framework: Activation, Construction, Execution, and Reflection on mathematical tools in physics problem solving**: The ACER framework is an analytical framework for structuring investigations of student difficulties with sophisticated mathematical tools in physics problem solving. It comprises four components: "activation of the mathematical tool, construction of the mathematical model, execution of the mathematics, and reﬂection on the results." The framework was designed to be applicable across contexts and tools while being operationalizable for specific topics, and is grounded in resources and epistemic framing perspectives. In this article it facilitates cross-context comparison of integration difficulties across gravitation, electrostatics, and magnetostatics. (Wilcox et al. (2019))
