# Selection-adjusted observational comparison of optional instructional components

> **Research Method** · [All research methods](index.md)

## Description
Because labs are almost always paired with lecture and students self-select into them, prior small studies comparing pedagogies within labs confound the lab treatment itself. The authors apply a bounding strategy comparing unconditional and conditional correlations across increasingly restrictive regressions with lecture-section fixed effects and student covariates, arguing that if the correlation shrinks as controls are added, "selection bias is inflating the correlation." This design moves observational studies toward more credible causal interpretation.

- **Studies of lab effects on content outcomes should condition on selection, since self-selection inflates apparent lab benefits**: Because labs are almost always paired with lecture and students self-select into them, prior small studies comparing pedagogies within labs confound the lab treatment itself. The authors apply a bounding strategy comparing unconditional and conditional correlations across increasingly restrictive regressions with lecture-section fixed effects and student covariates, arguing that if the correlation shrinks as controls are added, "selection bias is inflating the correlation." This design moves observational studies toward more credible causal interpretation. (DeFeo et al. (2020))
- **Normalized-ratio methodology for isolating the effect of an optional lab course on exam performance**: A quasi-experimental comparison method for measuring the contribution of an optional stand-alone lab course to content mastery. Exam questions are coded as lab-related or not against the lab's stated goals, and each student's ratio of (average score on lab-related questions)/(average score on non-lab-related questions) is computed; the article explains that non-lab questions "normalize for the differences in the two populations". Group differences in ratios are then compared statistically. Coding agreement reached over 80% initially and 100% after discussion. (Carl Wieman et al. (2015))
