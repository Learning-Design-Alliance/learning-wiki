Infrared time-lapse photography for unobtrusive audience-reaction observation¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 design), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

The report developed a practical means of recording audience reactions in a darkened room using infrared photographs, so that reactions can be related to film content. The technique "made use of high speed infrared film, time lapse photography on 16 mm films at one frame a second, infrared reflector heat lamps with ruby glass, mirror reflection of the picture on the screen for synchronization purposes, and a special projector for analyzing the scenes." Counting persons watching the screen yields a measure presumably related to a sequence's effectiveness, replacing time-consuming test administration.

Accounts¶

  • Infrared time-lapse photography apparatus for recording audience reactions to film: The report developed a practical means of recording audience reactions in a darkened room using infrared photographs, so that reactions can be related to film content. The technique "made use of high speed infrared film, time lapse photography on 16 mm films at one frame a second, infrared reflector heat lamps with ruby glass, mirror reflection of the picture on the screen for synchronization purposes, and a special projector for analyzing the scenes." Counting persons watching the screen yields a measure presumably related to a sequence's effectiveness, replacing time-consuming test administration. (Greenhill (1955))
  • Design audience-reaction recording methods to be continuous, synchronized, economical, portable, and usable at very low illumination: Before selecting equipment, the report analyzed needs for infrared photography of audiences and derived five requirements: the method "should permit relatively continuous records of reactions," records "should be easily synchronized with the film," it "should be economical," "portable," and "permit the making of recordings at very low levels of illumination." These needs then guided equipment selection and method development. The principle generalizes as requirements analysis preceding instrument design for classroom observation. (Greenhill (1955))
