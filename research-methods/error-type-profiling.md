# Error-type profiling

## Description
Error-type profiling is the analytic lens the article introduces to judge fairness interventions by how they redistribute false positives and false negatives across groups, rather than only by whether fairness metrics converge. The article introduces it as "a lens that translates fairness metrics into the terms advisors actually encounter: which students appear on caseloads and which are never seen." With predicted-successful as the positive class, a false negative is a successful student predicted at-risk who occupies a scarce advising slot and risks stigmatization, while a false positive is a struggling student predicted successful who never appears on any advisor's caseload.

- **Iteration branch of the taxonomy: seven categories of loop difficulties**: The iteration branch classifies difficulties with loops into seven categories: the repeating nature of iteration statements, interaction with code outside the loop, order of execution of loop parts, state (including control-variable difficulties), difficulties with the loop condition, nesting, and break and continue. Examples include the "loop is if" belief and thinking nested loops execute simultaneously. The branch draws on both cited literature and the authors' own interview and test data. (Dimitri Eckert et al. (2026))
- **Error Importance Model Accounting for Carelessness in Automated Assessment**: Not all errors students make are of the same importance, and not all are due to lack of knowledge. Some are due to carelessness or inattention. The system distinguishes two types of single-error answers: Type I (only one operation required to achieve matching) and Type II (only one node relabeling operation for two consecutive nodes that have been switched). Special edit operations (Second_Node_Insertion, Second_Node_Relabeling, Goal_Node_Relabeling) carry a cost of 2 versus 1 for basic operations, reflecting that errors in selecting the second node or goal node are considered more serious. (Grivokostopoulou et al. (2016))
- **A three-phase taxonomy of microvascular anastomosis errors (pre-operative, intra-operative, post-operative)**: The review organizes microsurgical errors by operative phase, subdividing the intra-operative phase into dissection, needle passage, and suture handling domains. It states: "Errors were categorized according to the operative phase: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative." Tables 1–3 enumerate specific errors per phase, e.g. empty grasp, backwall suture, tight suture tear, and crushing patency tests. (Esanu V et al. (2026))
- **Error analysis as a diagnostic technique for recognizing, classifying and interpreting learner errors**: The article adopts error analysis, operationalized through its error analysis system (EAS), as the diagnostic framework underlying constructive feedback. Following Crystal's definition quoted in the paper, "error analysis is a technique for recognizing, identifying, classifying and interpreting methodically the deviant forms produced by a foreign language learner". The study uses this framework to delimit error categories (punctuation, articles, verbs, prepositions, sentence structure) for pre/post-test scoring and to guide remedial teaching. (Malik Ajmal Gulzar et al. (2013))
- **Error typology framework for LLM answer explanations in medical multiple-choice questions**: A qualitative coding framework, developed with the Framework Method through iterative review and consensus among three reviewers, for categorizing the causes of incorrect LLM answers. The article states "Three main error domains were categorized: reasoning, factual, and context." Reasoning errors include reaching incorrect conclusions despite factual accuracy, ignoring information in the question, and misweighing given information; factual errors split into medical and nonmedical; context errors stem from misunderstanding cultural or social background such as Swiss care processes. It was applied to explanations from GPT4o and Claude-Sonnet-3.7. (Strasser Livia Maria et al. (2026))
- **A refined multi-dimensional error taxonomy for LLM responses in specialty medical exams, with 7 error types and 2 non-error types in four classes**: The article develops a multi-dimensional taxonomy for characterizing LLM failure modes on specialty medical exams, drawing on prior work on LLMs in clinical contexts and adapted to the cognitive demands of the CONACEM exam. It defines "a total of 7 distinct error types and 2 non-error types, grouped into four major classes": reasoning-based errors, knowledge-based errors, comprehension-related errors, and non-errors. Each category captures a specific failure pattern, such as adhering to an incorrect answer despite correct reasoning or inventing information not present in the question. The classification system was developed and validated by anesthesiology experts for clinical relevance. (Altermatt Fernando R. et al. (2025))
- **Three-category taxonomy of AI answer errors: logical fallacy, informational fallacy, and explicit fallacy**: The article classifies errors made by humans or AI when answering questions into three categories. A logical fallacy "occurs when the response demonstrates a stepwise process but ultimately fails to correctly answer the question"; an informational fallacy misinterprets or omits key information from the stem; an explicit fallacy combines incoherent reasoning with misapplied information. The taxonomy is used as the output indicator for labeling incorrect answers in the study, with two collectors assigning types independently and a third adjudicating disagreements. (Goodings Anthony James DEC et al. (2024))
- **Dominant incorrect answer method for FCI misconception diagnosis**: An analysis method, drawn from Martín-Blas et al. and Bani-Salameh, that concentrates on students' wrong answers rather than total scores. The article states that "in each option (e.g. A, B, C, D, or E), if the percentage of the ratio between the number of selected answers associated with a determined misconception and the number of the total incorrect answers, in each question, is equal or greater to 50%, then this answer, related to the respective option, is a dominant incorrect answer." This yields per-question, per-attempt misconception percentages that can be correlated across groups. (F. Escalante (2021))
- **Taxonomy of logical error sources: conceptual, strategic, misinterpretation, carelessness, and compilable syntactic errors**: The article organizes logical errors by their underlying source, drawing on prior literature. A conceptual error 'involves a misunderstanding of how a programming construct works'; a strategic error occurs when the underlying algorithm is flawed despite sound understanding of constructs; misinterpretation and carelessness errors stem from task misreading or oversight. The authors add a third observed category, compilable syntactic errors (e.g., using & instead of &&), which some compilers accept. They focus on conceptual and strategic errors because the other categories cannot be readily inferred from code. (Muntasir Hoq et al. (2025))
- **Five-category error taxonomy for student math problem-solving processes**: The article defines a taxonomy of student error categories used by expert teachers to annotate MathEDU, inspired by Wijaya et al. (2014). Three base categories are defined first — "Wrong Mathematical Operation/Concept", "Calculation Error" and "Incomplete Answer" — and two additional categories, "Careless Error" and "Lack of Necessary Mathematical Concepts", address inattentive mistakes and inability to begin due to missing foundational knowledge. Appendix C distinguishes conceptual misapplication (category 1) from inability to begin due to missing knowledge (category 5). The taxonomy grounds both dataset annotation and per-category evaluation of generated feedback. (Wei-Ling Hsu et al. (2025))
- **A six-category taxonomy of physics-specific AI reasoning errors**: The article organizes its qualitative analysis into recurring, systematic error categories observed across all four models: diagram interpretation errors, graph interpretation and construction errors, direction-related (vector) errors, qualitative–quantitative reasoning inconsistencies, circuit diagram errors, and right-hand rule errors. It states that "Diagram misinterpretation represented one of the most frequent failure modes" and that errors originated from "incomplete extraction of spatial information embedded in diagrams rather than incorrect use of physics formulas". The taxonomy serves as an explanatory account of why aggregate scores mask capability gaps and as a diagnostic tool for educators and developers. (Bilas Paul et al. (2026))
