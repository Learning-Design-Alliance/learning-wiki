# Latent Trait-State Theory (Revised) longitudinal trait-state measurement framework

## Description
LST-R is a longitudinal latent measurement framework distinguishing stable traits from situationally fluctuating states: "latent traits typically change little over time and situations (although trait change is possible), while latent states reflect the influence of persons in situations or person × situation interactions". The article applies its latent single-trait multiple-states model to parent-child outcomes with up to four repeated measures, estimating the ICC for each outcome and testing treatment effects on state variability at T2 and T3 after removing trait variance. It was adopted because prior pre-post designs conflated trait-like and state-like variability.

<!-- How each source describes or uses the method -->
- **Latent Trait-State Theory (Revised) applied as a latent single-trait multiple-states model for intervention outcomes**: LST-R is a longitudinal latent measurement framework distinguishing stable traits from situationally fluctuating states: "latent traits typically change little over time and situations (although trait change is possible), while latent states reflect the influence of persons in situations or person × situation interactions". The article applies its latent single-trait multiple-states model to parent-child outcomes with up to four repeated measures, estimating the ICC for each outcome and testing treatment effects on state variability at T2 and T3 after removing trait variance. It was adopted because prior pre-post designs conflated trait-like and state-like variability. (Jiang Su et al. (2026))
- **STARTS model of construct stability**: The STARTS (Stable Trait, AutoRegressive Trait, and State) model, postulated by Kenny and Zautra, decomposes individual differences in a construct measured across time into three variance components: "a completely stable trait variance component, a partly stable autoregressive trait variance component, and a completely occasion-specific state variance component." It estimates four parameters (stable trait, autoregressive trait, and state variances plus the autoregressive stability parameter β) and requires at least four measurement waves for identification. This article is the first to apply it to domain-specific academic self-concept, using a Bayesian estimation approach. (Malte Jansen et al. (2020))
