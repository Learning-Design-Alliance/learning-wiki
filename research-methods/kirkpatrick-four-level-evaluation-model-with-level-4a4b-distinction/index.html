Kirkpatrick four-level evaluation model with Level 4a/4b distinction¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 21 claims (20 for, 1 mixed) · 15 studies (10 review, 5 quant-synthesis), q2–q3 · 0 of 15 report an effect size · 21 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The review organizes its evidence synthesis around the Kirkpatrick model, "a widely used four-level framework for evaluating the effectiveness of educational interventions, including SBE". Level 1 assesses reaction, Level 2 learning, Level 3 behavior in clinical practice, and Level 4 results; the review adopts the subdivision of Level 4 into 4a (system-level process metrics such as response times and protocol adherence) and 4b (direct patient outcomes such as mortality and complications). This distinction structures its primary outcome coding and thematic synthesis.

Accounts¶

  • Kirkpatrick four-level model with the Level 4a/4b distinction as a framework for evaluating simulation-based education: The review organizes its evidence synthesis around the Kirkpatrick model, "a widely used four-level framework for evaluating the effectiveness of educational interventions, including SBE". Level 1 assesses reaction, Level 2 learning, Level 3 behavior in clinical practice, and Level 4 results; the review adopts the subdivision of Level 4 into 4a (system-level process metrics such as response times and protocol adherence) and 4b (direct patient outcomes such as mortality and complications). This distinction structures its primary outcome coding and thematic synthesis. (Zanin A et al. (2026))
  • Adapted Kirkpatrick Model as organizing framework for medical education outcomes: The review uses a medical-education adaptation of Kirkpatrick's four-level training-outcome model to categorize the heterogeneous outcomes reported across included studies. The adapted model, per the article, "encompasses the same four levels, with some modification: the learners' satisfaction with program content and delivery (Level 1), change in opinion about the topic (Level 2A), change in knowledge and/or skills (Level 2B), change in behavior (Level 3), and patient outcomes (Level 4)." It served to group studies by outcome type and identify gaps for future research rather than requiring studies to assess all levels. (Clemens Megan et al. (2026))
  • Kirkpatrick model mapping as the organising framework for evaluating technology-enhanced surgical training evidence: The review uses the Kirkpatrick model of training evaluation as its organising axis, mapping each included study's reported outcomes to a level: level 1 reaction, level 2 learning (simulator scores, kinematic metrics, R-OSATS), level 3 transfer to operative behaviour, and level 4 patient outcomes. It reports that "the available evidence is concentrated at level 2 (learning — simulator scores, kinematic metrics, R-OSATS)". This framework lets the review show that no study reached transfer or patient-level outcomes, bounding what the technology evidence can support. (Shakir T et al. (2026))
  • Kirkpatrick and Miller framework mapping of psychiatry education technology evidence: The review maps its included studies onto two established evaluation frameworks to locate the evidence's level. According to Kirkpatrick's model, most studies demonstrated effects at Level 2 (learning), with only a few reporting Level 1 (learner satisfaction) and none reaching Levels 3 or 4. On Miller's pyramid, most interventions addressed the 'knows' and 'knows how' levels, while HFSs and immersive VR achieved the 'shows how' level. The review uses this mapping to highlight "the critical gap in evidence for their long-term impact on professional practice and patient care." (Mokhtari Saba et al. (2026))
  • Kirkpatrick's four levels of evidence for evaluating the attributable effect of teaching programs.: Kirkpatrick's framework outlines four ascending levels of attributable benefit of a teaching program: (1) how well trainees received a program, (2) measured learning, (3) change in behaviour attributable to the program, and (4) outcomes of training such as reduced cost or higher quality of outcomes. The review uses this framework to examine the quality of evidence available for RAS curricula assessment, noting that "The importance of an adequate before-and-after assessment approach with a control group and statistical considerations were highlighted as key to this evaluation." (Kieslich Anna K. et al. (2026))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level model as an organizing framework for evaluating NTS training outcomes in emergency care.: The review organises its synthesis of NTS training outcomes using Kirkpatrick's four-level model, covering reaction (Level 1), learning (Level 2), behavioural change (Level 3), and clinical results (Level 4). The authors state that "the outcomes of non-technical skills training were categorized according to Kirkpatrick's four-level model". Because no included study reported participants' reactions, the summary focused on Levels 2–4, "covering learning outcomes, behavioural performance, and clinical results". This mapping clarifies that the most consistent gains occur at the learning and behaviour levels, while evidence at the clinical outcome level remains sparse. (Zhang YuKun et al. (2026))
  • Kirkpatrick model levels 2–4 as framework for evaluating learning effects of video review in PGME: The review applies the Kirkpatrick model, "which is globally used to evaluate the results of training and learning programs", as its organizing framework for classifying learning effects beyond participant opinions. Level 2 captures changes in residents' knowledge and skills observed during video review, level 3 captures how residents change behaviour by optimising clinical care, and level 4 captures impact on patient and healthcare outcomes. The review explicitly excludes Kirkpatrick level 1 studies because that level "only measures their opinion on the method used". (Robbrecht M et al. (2025))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level framework as an organizing axis for categorizing outcome evaluations in online active learning research.: The review used Kirkpatrick's framework to categorize the levels of outcome evaluation performed in the included studies, sorting findings into "categories representing levels of outcome evaluation suggested in using Kirkpatrick's framework particularly reaction and learning outcome levels." This framework provided the structure for the review's content analysis, distinguishing reaction-level evaluations (perceptions and satisfaction) from learning-level evaluations (measured knowledge and skills). The review found that all included studies evaluated outcomes at only these two levels, with none reaching behavioral or organizational levels. (Apel Z et al. (2025))
  • Kirkpatrick four-level evaluation model for categorizing PBL outcomes in PGME: The review uses the Kirkpatrick Evaluation Model as its organizing framework, in which "reaction" assesses participant satisfaction, "learning" measures effect on knowledge and skill via scored examinations, "behaviour" describes observable shifts in thinking leading to practical changes, and "results" specifies impact on practice or patient care. The 21 included studies were classified against these four levels to synthesize PBL outcomes in PGME. (Mensour Emma A et al. (2025))
  • Modified Kirkpatrick model for classifying simulation training outcomes: The review applies a modified Kirkpatrick model with four levels to classify outcomes of ultrasound‐guided regional anaesthesia simulation training. Level 1 assesses learners' perceptions including satisfaction and confidence; level 2 evaluates "knowledge acquisition, skills and attitudes"; level 3 examines behavioural changes in the professional setting such as transfer of skills to clinical practice; and level 4 measures outcomes of learners' actions such as patient outcomes and block success. The model structures the review's synthesis of which instructional design features accompany which outcome levels. (Sekhavati P et al. (2025))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level evaluation model applied to NTS training outcomes in ICUs: The review organizes outcomes using Kirkpatrick's evaluation model, encompassing reaction, learning, behaviors, and organizational results. Reaction covers "the participant's response to the training"; learning covers "acquisition of knowledge, skills and attitudes after training"; behavior covers "changes in participants' behavior in real or controlled care environments"; and results cover "measurable impacts of the training program at the organizational and health level." This framework structures the summary of findings table and GRADE certainty ratings. (Fernandes Pimenta Isac Davidson Santiago et al (2025))
  • Kirkpatrick Model as the review's framework for stratifying training impact across four levels: The Kirkpatrick Model, first developed by Donald Kirkpatrick in 1959, is a tool for evaluating the effectiveness of a training program. The review uses it as its organizing evaluation axis: "The model stratifies the level of impact of training results according to four levels: performance and patient outcomes (level 4), behavioral change after training (level 3), individual learning (level 2), and participants' reactions to training (level 1)." Each included study's main results were sorted by the Kirkpatrick level investigated. (Nouira Hajer et al. (2024))
  • Kirkpatrick's Expanded Outcomes Typology for categorizing educational effectiveness of IPE: The review applies Kirkpatrick's Expanded Outcomes Typology as its organizing framework for evaluating educational effectiveness, which "delineates four levels of educational effectiveness: reaction, attitude, behavior, and results". Simulation methods were classified by multidimensional aspects including human versus computer simulation and fidelity, as described by Elshama. The review uses these frameworks to categorize the ten included studies' outcome evaluations and to diagnose the field's concentration on lower levels. (Lee Sunmin et al. (2024))
  • Kirkpatrick four-level evaluation model applied to simulation-based education: The review uses the Kirkpatrick model as its evaluation framework, which "includes 4 levels: reaction, learning, behavior, and results," with Level 4 (results) denoting changes in final outcomes such as reduced patient length of stay. Studies were included only if they quantitatively evaluated at Level 4, clearly linked to patient outcomes rather than inferred from context. The review treats each level as requiring individual evaluation, since successful evaluation at Level 4 is not causally related to other levels. (Robinson SJA et al. (2024))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level model used to classify outcome evaluations of active learning: The review used Kirkpatrick's Model (1998) to summarize evaluation data by level. The article states "This model suggests four levels of outcome evaluation, including reaction (satisfaction and perceived outcomes), learning (direct measures of outcomes such knowledge, skills, and attitudes), behavior (behavioral changes resulting from the intervention), and results (organizational changes resulting from the intervention)". Papers reporting more than one outcome level were classified separately to calculate evaluations per level. In this review, all outcome evaluations fell at the reaction and learning levels. (Perez Arnaldo et al. (2023))
  • Kirkpatrick four-level model for evaluating training impact: The review applies the Kirkpatrick model, described as "a four-level model to evaluate training impact: reaction (level 1), learning (level 2), behavior (level 3), and results (level 4)." In the FB SBT context, level 2 means improved skills in a simulation setting, level 3 improved bronchoscopy performance in a patient setting, and level 4 improved patient outcomes such as less discomfort or fewer complications. The review uses these levels to classify outcome measures and judge the relevance of included studies' results. (Gerretsen Eveline C.F. MSc et al. (2023))
  • Modified Kirkpatrick educational outcomes typology as a multi-level framework for mapping research education outcomes: The review used Barr and colleagues' modified Kirkpatrick educational outcomes typology as its organising framework for classifying the outcomes that research education programs report. The typology "gives rise to the identification of outcome measures at multiple levels", reflecting that individuals, programs, organisational and other factors interact to produce various outcomes. The review mapped every included program's reported outcomes onto this typology, which exposed the concentration of evaluation at lower levels such as satisfaction and confidence. (King et al. (2022))
  • The Kirkpatrick Model as a four-level framework for evaluating simulation-based training: The Kirkpatrick Model is an evaluation framework the review uses to classify the level of evaluation assessment completed for the training reported in each reviewed article. "The model consists of 4 levels: (1) participants responses—REACTION—to training (eg, satisfaction); (2) extent of knowledge, attitude, or skills gained—LEARNING; (3) extent to which desired behaviors are being used on the job—BEHAVIOR; and (4) extent to which desired behaviors are producing the desired outcomes in practice—RESULTS." The review assigns each of the 30 articles a level, reported in Table 1, and uses the levels to argue that higher-level evaluation is needed to determine actual impact. (Smith Madison B. RN et al. (2018))
  • Modified Kirkpatrick framework for evaluating educational interventions in ECG training studies: The review applied a modified version of the Kirkpatrick framework to classify the outcomes of the included educational interventions. It appraises outcomes at levels measuring "participants' perceptions of (reactions to) the learning experience (level 1)", modification of attitudes and perceptions (level 2a), modification of knowledge and/or skills (level 2b), transfer to the workplace (level 3), change in organisational practice (level 4a) and benefits to patients (level 4b). All thirteen included studies reported level 2b outcomes; none reported level 3 or 4 outcomes. (Viljoen Charle André et al. (2019))
  • Adapted Kirkpatrick hierarchy for assessing educational impact of training interventions: The review structures its evidence synthesis around an adapted Kirkpatrick hierarchy (Fig. 1) with four levels: learner reaction/opinion (level 1), learner knowledge (level 2), learner behaviour (level 3), and change in patient outcome (level 4), described as "the highest level of impact assessment in educational research". Each included study was assigned to a level by its outcome measure type, enabling the review to show that higher-level evidence is scarcer and of variable quality. (James et al. (2019))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level hierarchy for evaluating educational interventions: The review describes Kirkpatrick's model as the most commonly known tool, used frequently for assessing individual trainee performance and for measuring the effectiveness of teaching-the-teacher courses. It states that "Evaluation of educational interventions must concern at least four dimensions embedded in Kirkpatrick's hierarchy, such as participant's satisfaction, learning (both knowledge and skills), behavioral change (understood as a transfer of knowledge and skills to workplace), and outcomes which are mainly considered as impact on patients". The review uses this hierarchy as a benchmark against which the included articles' assessment practices fall short. (How are "teaching the teachers" courses in evidence based medicine evaluated? A systematic)
  • Kirkpatrick's four levels of educational outcomes as a synthesis framework for WBA feedback evidence: The review uses Kirkpatrick's four levels of educational outcomes to organise and grade the evidence from included WBA feedback studies. The framework ranges from learners' reactions (Level 1) through learning of skills and knowledge (Level 2) and changes in learner behaviour (Level 3) to wider changes in the delivery of care (Level 4). Findings were grouped by type of intervention and described according to levels of outcome, with the review concluding that "The highest Kirkpatrick level reached by any of the studies was level 3 which indicates a change in behaviour". (Saedon H et al. (2012))
  • Kirkpatrick's four-level hierarchy as a classification tool for communicating learning outcome levels in healthcare education evaluation: Kirkpatrick's hierarchy classifies learning outcomes resulting from educational interventions in healthcare into four levels: Level 1 (Reaction) measures how learners perceive the educational intervention; Level 2 (Learning) measures acquisition of skills/knowledge/attitudes in a non-clinical setting; Level 3 (Behaviour) measures "learners' behavioural changes in the professional setting, i.e., transfer of learning to the clinical setting"; and Level 4 (Results) measures the effect of learners' actions, i.e., improved patient outcomes. This review used a modified Kirkpatrick model to set eligibility, deliberately focusing on Levels 3 and 4 and excluding studies measuring only Levels 1 and 2. Multiple levels are possible within a single study. (Boet Sylvain et al. (2014))

Claims¶

  • Simulation-based education studies in pediatric and neonatal critical care more consistently report system-level process improvements (Level 4a) than significant direct patient-level outcome improvements (Level 4b) [+M]
  • Evidence for technology-enhanced basic robotic skills training is concentrated at Kirkpatrick level 2, with no studies reporting transfer to operative behaviour or patient outcomes [+M]
  • Technology-based interventions in psychiatry education mostly show positive short-term effects on knowledge, skills, confidence, and empathy, but evidence of sustained learning is inconsistent [+M]
  • Kirkpatrick's four levels of evidence can be applied to RAS training, with level 2 equating to RAS skills assessed with validated scoring systems during simulation. [+M]
  • NTS training for emergency department professionals is associated with consistent pre- to post-training improvements in knowledge, teamwork attitudes, and self-confidence (low to moderate certainty). [+M]
  • NTS training improves observable team behavioural performance in EDs, including more frequent closed-loop communication, clearer role allocation and prioritisation, improved situational awareness, and higher teamwork ratings (moderate certainty). [+M]
  • Evidence that NTS training improves clinical or process outcomes in EDs is limited but generally favourable in single-centre studies, including shorter resuscitation times and improved protocol adherence (low to very low certainty). [~M]
  • Video review of resident performance in clinical PGME settings shows positive learning effects, with 8 of 11 studies reporting significant results [+M]
  • Video review improves knowledge and skills outcomes (Kirkpatrick level 2), with mixed findings across studies [+M]
  • Video review improves resident behaviours in clinical practice (Kirkpatrick level 3), including leadership, prescribing accuracy and communication [+M]
  • Video review improves patient outcomes (Kirkpatrick level 4) in the single study reporting this level [+M]
  • Simulation training for ultrasound‐guided regional anaesthesia most often shows significant improvement at Kirkpatrick level 2 outcomes [+M]
  • Simulation training reached Kirkpatrick level 3 (behavioral change) in five studies, but no study assessed patient outcomes [+M]
  • Korean simulation-based IPE studies report high satisfaction but concentrate on Kirkpatrick levels 1 and 2, with no study measuring level 4 training efficacy [+M]
  • Most LMIC simulation-based education studies testing significance demonstrate at least partial improvement in patient-level (Kirkpatrick Level 4) outcomes [+M]
  • Evidence that FB SBT improves bronchoscopy performance in a patient setting (Kirkpatrick level 3) is sparse and conflicting [+M]
  • Outcome evaluation of research education programs concentrated at lower levels of the modified Kirkpatrick typology, with few objective or organisational outcomes [+M]
  • Existing generic assessment instruments (RSES, SOC model) lack face validity for EBM TTT courses, and no included article used Kirkpatrick's model [+M]
  • No included WBA feedback study demonstrated improved learning and performance as a direct result of WBA (Kirkpatrick Level 4); the highest level reached was Level 3 [+M]
  • Simulation-based CRM training transfers to the workplace: three of four studies found simulation-enhanced CRM training significantly more effective than no intervention or didactic teaching [+M]
  • Simulation-based CRM training may translate to improved patient outcomes, including decreased mortality, based on a small number of studies [+M]
