## Description
The article uses the learning curve as its central organizing measure for judging surgical training approaches: it "applies the learning curves as a measure to demonstrate their teaching ability" and compares simulation types by analyzing their learning curves. Learning curves track how operative performance (e.g., operating time, conversions, complications) changes with accumulated operative experience, allowing identification of case thresholds at which performance stabilizes. The review surmises the applicability of different surgical simulation types by comparing and analyzing learning curves across procedures such as laparoscopic hemihepatectomy, pancreatoduodenectomy, and robotic pancreatoduodenectomy.

- **Learning curve as a measure of surgical training method effectiveness**: The article uses the learning curve as its central organizing measure for judging surgical training approaches: it "applies the learning curves as a measure to demonstrate their teaching ability" and compares simulation types by analyzing their learning curves. Learning curves track how operative performance (e.g., operating time, conversions, complications) changes with accumulated operative experience, allowing identification of case thresholds at which performance stabilizes. The review surmises the applicability of different surgical simulation types by comparing and analyzing learning curves across procedures such as laparoscopic hemihepatectomy, pancreatoduodenectomy, and robotic pancreatoduodenectomy. (Zhang Jun et al. (2024))
- **Experience-group learning-curve method with an experienced-surgeon quality reference for assessing surgical trainees**: The article devises learning curves by categorizing trainee operations into "experience groups" where all trainees share the same operation count ("all first TVT operations labeled as “experience = 1,”" and so on), plotting outcomes operation by operation against the averaged results of Sweden's most experienced surgeons with 95% confidence intervals, and additionally comparing group averages for operations 1–10 and 11–50. The experienced surgeons' averaged results are labeled a "quality reference" and regarded as a desirable goal for trainees. This method handles wide intra- and interindividual scattering of individual learning curves. (Nüssler EK et al. (2021))
- **Trials of surgical and other skill-delivered interventions should statistically investigate learning effects to aid interpretation and assess design success**: The article recommends that trialists explicitly explore residual learning even when design measures such as pre-trial training and surgeon-stratified randomisation are in place, because such measures "may not eradicate it entirely". The investigation combines visual methods (moving average and cusum plots, funnel plots) with multilevel models of a process indicator and a patient outcome. The authors state the method "could be used in future trials as an aid to explore whether learning is occurring in the trial and be valuable when interpreting trial results". (Conroy Elizabeth J et al. (2025))
- **Three surgeon-level experience variables and two learning measures for investigating surgical learning in trials**: The article defines three surgeon-level variables to represent pre-existing or changing experience: operation sequence (X1), a count of the order infants were treated within trial surgeon; technique experience (X2), a binary indicator of prior Sommerlad experience; and age experience (X3), the routine age of children at surgery at the surgeon's centre. Learning is measured through operation time (Y1), a continuous process indicator, and fistula occurrence (Y2), a dichotomous patient outcome. The authors note counting trial operations is "the closest proxy to a time-varying learning effect when no information on non-randomised cases is available". (Conroy Elizabeth J et al. (2025))
- **Three-phase model of the surgical learning curve: starting point, rate of learning, and expert level (asymptote)**: The review adopts a learning-curve model, attributed to Cook and colleagues, in which skill acquisition for a surgical procedure has three phases: "the starting point, where the surgeon begins performing a specific intervention; the rate of learning, which reflects the speed of skill acquisition for a surgeon to reach a certain level of performance; and the expert level or asymptote, where the surgeon's performance stabilizes." A learning curve is defined as the time and/or number of procedures an average surgeon needs to perform a procedure independently with a reasonable outcome. During the early phases, errors and adverse outcomes are more likely to occur. The review uses this model to frame expertise criteria for trial design. (Alsagheir Ali et al. (2021))
