# Behavioral coding methodology for identifying epistemological framing in group video

> **Research Method** · [All research methods](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 11 claims (11 for) · 7 studies (5 qualitative, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q2` · 0 of 7 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

## Description
A real-time observational coding protocol in which coders watch video of small groups without a transcript and note the exact time of changes in vocal register, affect, grammar, gesture production, and body language. Clusters are labeled with meaning-neutral labels (colors), at least two researchers code independently, and whole groups can be coded reliably because "peers typically share the same behavioral cluster." The method achieved 90% inter-rater reliability before discussion, to five seconds' accuracy.

- **Behavioral coding methodology for identifying epistemological framing in group video**: A real-time observational coding protocol in which coders watch video of small groups without a transcript and note the exact time of changes in vocal register, affect, grammar, gesture production, and body language. Clusters are labeled with meaning-neutral labels (colors), at least two researchers code independently, and whole groups can be coded reliably because "peers typically share the same behavioral cluster." The method achieved 90% inter-rater reliability before discussion, to five seconds' accuracy. (Rachel E. Scherr et al. (2007))
- **A four-cluster taxonomy of tutorial behavior — blue, green, red, yellow — mapped to worksheet, discussion, TA, and joking frames**: The article identifies four recurring clusters of physical and paraverbal behavior, labeled with meaning-neutral colors. Blue (eyes on paper, leaning forward, quiet reading) indicates a worksheet frame; green (sitting up, eye contact, prolific gestures, clear loud original speech) a discussion frame; red, attending to the TA; yellow (giggling, fidgeting) a joking frame. Blue and green "indicate very different framings of the tutorial activity". (Rachel E. Scherr et al. (2007))
- **Track students' warrants in mathematical arguments as a window to their epistemic framing**: The paper's recommendation is to identify students' epistemic framing through the warrants of their mathematical arguments, the often unspoken bridges between data and claim. The authors "suggest: look for the (sometimes implicit) warrants they use in their mathematical arguments," because warrants are "a powerful, concise piece of evidence" of the nature students currently attribute to the math in play. (Thomas J. Bing et al. (2008))
- **Epistemological framing coding scheme splitting Discussion into Procedural and Conceptual frames, with sensemaking operationalized as Conceptual Discussion**: The article uses epistemological framing, "a person’s implicit expectations about knowledge and learning in their current activity and context," shared by members of a collaborative group. It modifies Scherr and Hammer's scheme in two ways: coding from audio alone matched video-plus-audio coding more than 80% of the time, and the Discussion frame was split into Procedural Discussion ("figuring out what to do") and Conceptual Discussion ("figuring out what is happening" in the physics). Sensemaking is operationalized as engaging in Conceptual Discussion. (Martinuk et al. (2010))
- **Behavior modes as indicators of epistemological framing in collaborative tutorials**: This framework holds that students' observable behaviors cluster into locally stable modes—green (discussion), blue (worksheet), yellow (socializing), red (receptive to TA)—separated by sharp transitions, and that these modes indicate how students frame the activity, i.e., their sense of "what is going on here". The article adopts Scherr's methodology, coding modes holistically from posture, gesture, vocal register, and visual focus, and uses the modes to analyze when mechanistic reasoning occurs. (Conlin et al. (2006))
- **Four identifiable epistemological framings that lead students astray: one-step thinking, p-priming, rote reasoning, and disciplinary siloing**: The lecture enumerates four observable epistemological framings that recur in classroom video data: "One-step thinking", "P-priming", "Rote reasoning", and "Disciplinary siloing". Each is a characteristic answer to the student's implicit question about what kind of knowledge and effort the task requires, and each is sometimes perfectly appropriate but leads students into trouble in some situations. The pattern gives instructors a vocabulary for diagnosing errors as framing mismatches rather than knowledge deficits. (Edward F. Redish (2013))
- **Math-physics-algorithmic-conceptual framework of epistemological framing with four quadrants**: The article develops a theoretical framework with two orthogonal axes, algorithmic versus conceptual and math versus physics, whose quadrants are four epistemological frames: algorithmic math, conceptual math, algorithmic physics, and conceptual physics. The authors state: "We investigate four frames: algorithmic math, conceptual math, algorithmic physics, and conceptual physics, looking for moments where students’ problem solving is impeded because they are in an unproductive frame." No frame is inherently good or bad; frames are quasi-stable states between which students move during problem solving, and the axes are chosen for practical reasons rather than as a formal metric. (Bahar Modir et al. (2017))
- **Three-type taxonomy of student-initiated frame shifts: alpha, beta, gamma**: The article classifies student-initiated shifts into three named types. An "Alpha shift" is "A shift to another activity in another frame after students have completed the previous activity" with no explicit disagreement. A Beta shift is similar "however, members of the group disagree or make a bid for a new frame". A Gamma shift is a shift "to or from an activity outside of our four quadrants of interest (represented by an O code)", such as joking. This taxonomy lets researchers track whether changes in problem solving are driven by consensus or by dissent/confusion. (Hai D. Nguyen et al. (2016))
- **Math-in-physics epistemic framing framework with two axes (math-to-physics, conceptual-to-algorithmic) defining four frames**: The article develops and applies a framework for analyzing epistemic framing in math-rich physics problem solving along "two axes: math to physics and conceptual to algorithmic." The axes create four quadrants: conceptual physics (discussing physical scenarios and symmetry), algorithmic physics (recalling or rearranging physics equations), algorithmic math (following established computational protocols), and conceptual math (exploiting properties of mathematical constructs to obtain results quickly). Unlike prior schemes (Scherr and Hammer; Bing and Redish; Irving et al.), it applies equally to students and instructors and distinguishes conceptual from algorithmic math. (Deepa N. Chari et al. (2019))
- **Math-in-physics epistemological framing framework with four interrelated frames**: The article develops and applies a set of four interrelated epistemological frames around math-in-physics, previously built from video data in upper-division quantum mechanics and E&M courses. The framework treats student thinking as locally activated frames rather than stable misconceptions: "The four frames are: algorithmic math, conceptual math, algorithmic physics, and conceptual physics." Frames are diagnosed from student behaviors, gaze, and discourse, and "Being in the appropriate frame and shifting between frames are determining factors in students' success at problem solving." (Bahar Modir et al. (2019))
- **Two-axis epistemic framing framework with four frames (CP, AP, AM, CM)**: The article applies a two-dimensional epistemic framing framework to group problem solving in upper-division physics. Its "horizontal axis describes students' activities towards the conceptual or algorithmic ends, while the vertical axis extends towards the physics and math ends", dividing the space into four quadrants: Conceptual Physics, Algorithmic Physics, Algorithmic Math, and Conceptual Math. Each quadrant corresponds to characteristic student activities, e.g. in the CP frame students discuss "conceptual ideas of physics or properties of physics quantities /systems/scenarios", and in the CM frame students apply mathematical rules such as orthogonality to avoid lengthy manipulation. (Hai D. Nguyen et al. (2016))
- **Three-frame taxonomy of student framing in laboratory work: confirmation, inquiry-based, and hoops**: The article identifies three broad epistemological frames capturing how students understand what is taking place in lab. Confirmation or model-verifying frames "occur when students view the goal of the lab as collecting data to conﬁrm or support a known or provided physical model," while an inquiry-based frame views lab as a place to explore concepts through authentic experimentation. A third hoops frame views the lab as tasks to get through to leave the room. The authors apply this taxonomy to transcript and behavior evidence to explain why some groups did not problematize. (Anna McLean Phillips et al. (2021))
- **Taxonomy of six student epistemic frames in nontraditional physics labs defined by prediction and epistemic agency**: From video analysis of seven lab groups, the authors inductively identify six epistemic frames organized along two dimensions: whether students hold a prediction (confirmation or falsification) and the extent of epistemic agency. As printed: "We identify six different epistemic frames (Fig. 3) and observe that each lab group takes up and shifts between these epistemic frames differently throughout the activity (Fig. 4)." The frames are Low Agency-Confirmation, Low Agency-Falsification, High Agency-Confirmation, High Agency-Falsification, Hoops, and Discovery, each with observed behaviors such as manipulating data or drawing conclusions supported by data. (Meagan Sundstrom et al. (2023))
