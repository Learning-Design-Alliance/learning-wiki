# Messick's five-source validity framework for surgical assessment tools

## Description
The review applies a scoring tool based on the five sources of validity originally described by Messick: content, response process, internal structure, relation to other variables, and consequences. Assessment tools should ideally be supported by validity evidence from each of the five sources, though not necessarily within the same paper. The review used Ghaderi's toolbox, with scores from 0 to 15 points, to assess the validity evidence reported for each of the 31 identified assessment tools.

The review used Ghaderi's toolbox, with scores from 0 to 15 points, to assess the validity evidence reported for each of the 31 identified assessment tools. (Hertz P et al. (2024))
- **Contemporary five-aspect validity framework (adapted from Messick) for appraising surgical assessment tools**: The review applies a contemporary framework of validity, adapted from Messick's validity framework, with five aspects: content, response process, internal structure, relationship to other variables, and consequences. Each aspect is scored 0–3, giving a maximum of 15, banded as limited (1–5), moderate (6–10) or substantial (11–15) validity. The scoring system was adapted from Beckman, Ghaderi, Haug and Grüter and modified for this review; separate scoring definitions are provided for manual tools and APMs, e.g. for APMs "Performance metrics were recorded by the simulator (i.e., eliminating rater bias)". The framework structures the review's per-tool validity tables. (Tesfai FM et al. (2024))
- **Five-domain validity evidence framework for appraising surgical assessment tools**: The review applies a five-domain validity framework, based on Messick's unitary view of validity and operationalized by Ghaderi et al, in which each assessment tool is scored on content, response process, internal structure, relation to other variables, and consequences, with a maximum score of 15. Two independent reviewers scored each of the 24 tools on these domains, with disagreements resolved by consensus. The framework is presented as having "good interrater reliability" when used in prior surgical specialties' reviews. (Li Yibo et al. (2024))
- **Messick's unified construct validity framework for evaluating robotic VR simulators as assessment and selection tools**: The article applies Messick's framework, which "conceptualizes all validity under one overarching framework of construct validity", to robotic VR simulators. Within it, concurrent validity asks how accurately simulator performances predict current intraoperative performance, informing credentialing, while predictive validity asks how accurately they predict future performance, informing selection of future robotic surgeons. The review uses this framework to organize its predictability studies and to argue that terminology around validity is often misunderstood and misused. (Schmidt MW et al. (2021))
- **Messick's modern validity framework with Beckman ratings and McGaghie-style translational outcomes (LoE 1-5) for evaluating surgical simulators**: The review applies a two-part evaluation framework to vascular surgery simulators. Validity evidence is judged against Messick's five sources — content, response process, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences — each rated 0-2 with Beckman's scale, where 2 means "Data strongly supports the source of validity". Effectiveness is scored with an adapted McGaghie translational outcomes hierarchy from LoE 1 (trainee satisfaction) to LoE 5 (collateral effects such as "Cost saving; skill retention"). The authors argue modern unitary validity definitions are "vital in the in-depth assessment of simulators" and recommend the framework as standard practice. (Haiser Alexander et al. (2022))
- **Messick Validity Framework applied to surgical simulation assessment**: The Messick Validity Framework proposes "a five-component framework exploring different sources of validity: Content, Response Process, Internal Structure, Relations with Other Variables and Consequences". The article uses it to structure validity assessment of the HelpMeSee MSICS module, with score differences across experience levels providing 'relations to other variables' evidence. Prior work cited in the article mapped content validity (Nair et al.), pass-fail consequences and reliability (Hutter et al.) onto the same framework. (Dormegny L et al. (2026))
- **Messick's contemporary framework of validity evidence as an organizing structure for simulation-based test validation**: Messick's contemporary framework structures validity argumentation around five sources of evidence: "content, response process, internal structure, relationship with other variables, and consequences". The study uses it to organize all validation work for the camera navigation test: expert consensus for content, standardized automated metrics for response process, reliability analyses for internal structure, group comparisons for relationships to other variables, and contrasting groups' standard setting for consequences. (Yu Jiongbiao et al. (2025))
- **Messick's five-source validity framework applied to simulation-based test development**: The study organizes its validation process around Messick's framework, which "includes 5 different sources of validity evidence: content, response process, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences". Content evidence came from specialist-selected metrics, response process from standardized instruction and objective scoring, internal structure from test–retest reliability, relations to other variables from expert–novice comparisons, and consequences from whether all mastery novices could meet the standard. (Gustafsson A et al. (2024))
- **Messick's five-source validity framework applied to a clinical skills test**: The article organises its validation around Messick's framework, which "gathers validity evidence from five sources": content validity, response process, internal structure, relationship to other variables, and consequences. Each source was operationalised for the OSCE — content via expert and ERS task-force review, response process via standardised examiner instructions, internal structure via Cronbach's alpha and item analysis, relationships via comparison across experience groups, and consequences via contrasting-groups standard setting with false-positive/false-negative analysis. (Pietersen Pia Iben et al. (2023))
- **Messick's validation framework as the organizing framework for establishing construct validity of a simulation-based surgical skills assessment**: The article adopts Messick's validation framework to structure its validity argument for the EMIG simulation systems, chosen because it is advocated by the American Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association and the National Council on Measurement in Education. Under this framework, construct validity is supported by multiple sources of evidence: the study reports content validity (gynecologic surgery-specific tasks), response process validity ("a rigorous review process of the tasks incorporating quality control and an evaluation of rater discrepancies"), and notes that consequence validity evidence remains unevaluated. The framework frames how assessment interpretations can support defensible decisions. (Essentials in Minimally Invasive Gynecology Manual Skills Construct Validation Trial.)
