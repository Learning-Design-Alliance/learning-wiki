## Description
Legitimation Code Theory (LCT) is an explanatory framework for analysing the basis of achievement underlying practices. Its specialisation dimension analyses epistemic relations (ER) — the value placed on specialised knowledge — and social relations (SR) — the value placed on 'ways of being' such as resilience and teamwork — yielding knowledge, knower, elite and relativist codes. The authors used it as a "grounding framework in a fully theory-informed inductive approach" with a translation device to code qualitative data and plot themes on a Specialisation Plane.

- **Specialisation dimension of Legitimation Code Theory: epistemic and social relations combining into four specialisation codes**: The Specialisation dimension of Legitimation Code Theory is a toolkit for analysing knowledge-knower structures in social fields of practice. Its organising principles are epistemic and social relations, where "Epistemic relations (ER) refer to the strength of the relationship between a practice and its object" and social relations refer to the strength of the relationship between practices and their subject. Their strengths combine into four specialisation codes: knowledge, knower, elite and relativist. The article applies these concepts, via a translation device, to autoethnographic data on theoretical physicist education. (Alan S. Cornell et al. (2020))
- **LCT Specialization epistemic plane as a framework for analysing assessment tasks**: The article applies the Specialization dimension of Legitimation Code Theory, which reveals "the extent to which the basis of achievement in a practice or intellectual field lies in the mastery of a body of specialised knowledge and/or the acquisition of specialised dispositions." Epistemic relations combine ontic relations (the "what" of knowledge practices) and discursive relations (the "how"), yielding four epistemic codes on the epistemic plane: purist, doctrinal, situational and knower/no insights. The authors use this framework to classify summative assessments at each level of a physics programme via translation devices. (Alan S. Cornell et al. (2021))
