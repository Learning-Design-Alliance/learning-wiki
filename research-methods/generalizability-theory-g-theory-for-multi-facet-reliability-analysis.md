## Description
Generalizability theory (G-theory), developed by Cronbach, models an observed score as a universal score plus multiple sources of variation called facets. Unlike Cronbach's alpha, which "estimates only one facet as a source of measurement error", G-theory "enables the calculation of multiple sources of error with a single measurement" using ANOVA techniques, yielding a G coefficient for relative reliability and a dependability (D) index for absolute reliability. In OSCEs the facets can include stations, standardized patients, examiners, scenarios/sites, and occasions.

- **Generalizability theory as a multi-facet framework for OSCE reliability analysis**: Generalizability theory (G-theory), developed by Cronbach, models an observed score as a universal score plus multiple sources of variation called facets. Unlike Cronbach's alpha, which "estimates only one facet as a source of measurement error", G-theory "enables the calculation of multiple sources of error with a single measurement" using ANOVA techniques, yielding a G coefficient for relative reliability and a dependability (D) index for absolute reliability. In OSCEs the facets can include stations, standardized patients, examiners, scenarios/sites, and occasions. (Trejo-Mejía Juan Andrés et al. (2016))
- **Interpret teaching evaluations via variance partitioning, not as pure professor effects**: Because students' ratings contain rater bias and relationship effects as well as professor effects, evaluations used for personnel decisions should be partitioned with generalizability theory or the social relations model. The article warns that 'the presence of strong rater and relationship effects opens the door for decision errors when teaching evaluations are treated as though they reflect only professor effects.' For example, rater bias correlated with major could inflate a department's evaluations. (Gross Jennifer et al. (2015))
- **Generalizability Theory as a multi-faceted framework for decomposing score reliability into items, raters, and their interactions**: Generalizability Theory (G Theory) treats measurement error as sampling variability from a universe of admissible observations, estimating variance components for persons, items, raters, and their interactions in a fully crossed p x i x r G Study. It yields two reliability coefficients: the generalizability coefficient for relative (norm-referenced) decisions and the dependability coefficient for absolute (criterion-referenced) decisions, the latter generally lower because it takes into account "the error associated with each facet of measurement." Decision Studies then model how reliability changes under different item/rater configurations. The article applies this framework to the FASCI to reveal where reliability gains can be realized. (Talbot et al. (2014))
