## Description
Generalizability theory (G-theory), developed by Cronbach, models an observed score as a universal score plus multiple sources of variation called facets. Unlike Cronbach's alpha, which "estimates only one facet as a source of measurement error", G-theory "enables the calculation of multiple sources of error with a single measurement" using ANOVA techniques, yielding a G coefficient for relative reliability and a dependability (D) index for absolute reliability. In OSCEs the facets can include stations, standardized patients, examiners, scenarios/sites, and occasions.

- **Interpret teaching evaluations via variance partitioning, not as pure professor effects**: Because students' ratings contain rater bias and relationship effects as well as professor effects, evaluations used for personnel decisions should be partitioned with generalizability theory or the social relations model. The article warns that 'the presence of strong rater and relationship effects opens the door for decision errors when teaching evaluations are treated as though they reflect only professor effects.' For example, rater bias correlated with major could inflate a department's evaluations. (Gross Jennifer et al. (2015))
