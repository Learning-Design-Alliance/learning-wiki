Trustworthiness lens for evaluating LLMs in education¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 2 studies (1 design, 1 theoretical), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article proposes adapting trustworthiness criteria from the ML community as a structured lens for LLM evaluation in education. Trustworthiness functions as an anchor that connects high-level ethical concerns (fairness, safety) to concrete, quantifiable measurements that learning engineers can apply per response. The authors argue this lens helps "bridge the LLM evaluation gap" by providing measurements that help learning engineers align on education-specific challenges and systematically identify potential pedagogical disruptions before deployment.

  • Five trustworthiness metrics comprising 20 measures for evaluating LLM trustworthiness in education: The article co-constructed a set of five trustworthiness metrics comprising 20 quantitative measures tailored to pedagogical use. Metrics visible in the article include Misinformation, Sycophancy, Diplomacy, Disposition, Learning Method, and Response Quality, each defined by criteria such as "The LLM only generates true, substantiated factual claims" and "The LLM response follows established principles of learning." Measures were sourced from ML trustworthiness literature, education literature on pedagogical prompt patterns, and task-based response quality criteria, then refined through collaborative whiteboarding sessions with learning engineers. (Adam Coscia et al. (2026))

Claims¶

  • Making trustworthiness metrics and visualizations explicit increased inter-rater reliability among learning engineers evaluating LLM responses [+W]
  • Trustworthiness could not be reduced to a fully automated objective; human judgment remains necessary for resolving complex or context-dependent cases [~W]
  • Automated metrics and LLM judges can misalign with expert human preferences in non-verifiable educational evaluation [~W]
