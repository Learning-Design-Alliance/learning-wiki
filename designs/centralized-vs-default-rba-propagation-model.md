# Centralized versus default (decentralized) propagation models for research-based assessments

> **Design** · [All designs](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article contrasts two models for propagating research-based assessments. In the historical default model, individual instructors are solely responsible for administering, scoring, and analyzing assessments, with little centralized aggregation across courses. The alternative 'localizes administration, data collection, and analysis to a single centralized system': a central administrator distributes the assessment, aggregates responses tagged with course meta-data, and returns individualized comparison reports to instructors.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructors provide course information to a central administrator, and the administrator needs Institutional Review Board approval to use student responses for research purposes.
- Responses from multiple courses are tagged with course-specific meta-data (e.g., class size, level, type of instruction) so appropriate comparison data can be provided.
#### Constraints
- The systems discussed have been tested only with multiple-choice assessments; the article states centralized systems 'may be feasible only for RBAs with forced-choice question formats'.
- Development of these systems is 'time consuming and expensive', and even automated systems require some funding to support a part-time administrator indefinitely.

### Target Learners
- undergraduate physics students in laboratory courses

### Learning Goals
- measuring students' epistemologies and expectations about experimental physics via research-based assessments
