# For chronically sleep-restricted learners, scheduling a daytime nap as part of a split sleep schedule can harness afternoon learning without impairing morning learning

> **Design** · [All designs](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), `q3` · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Description
This principle holds that when adolescents cannot obtain adequate nocturnal sleep, dividing the available sleep between a shortened night and a mid-afternoon nap supports memory better than concentrating the same total sleep at night, particularly for afternoon learning. The article states that "a split sleep schedule appears to be preferable for harnessing the cognitive functions that underpin learning in the afternoon, without negatively impacting upon morning learning", and that "facilitating naps in schools may be an effective measure to improve learning outcomes". It is grounded in the experimental comparison of split versus continuous restricted sleep on picture encoding and factual knowledge.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A mid-afternoon nap opportunity (1.5 h in the study, chosen to allow a full cycle of N2, SWS and REM sleep) in addition to nocturnal sleep
#### Constraints
- The evidence applies under sleep restriction below the recommended 8–10 h; whether splitting sleep advantages adolescents with adequate habitual sleep was not tested
- The 90-min nap "is toward the upper limits of nap durations that may be practical to implement in schools"; shorter naps of 30–60 min have shown benefits but were not tested in this schedule
- Splitting sleep reduced total sleep time and REM sleep, and REM-linked faculties (procedural memory, creative problem solving, emotional regulation) may have been affected

### Target Learners
- Chronically sleep-restricted adolescents (15–19 years old)

### Learning Goals
- Long-term memory for school learning material encoded across the school day (factual knowledge and picture recognition)
