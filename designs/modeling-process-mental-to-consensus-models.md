# Modeling-based inquiry as a classroom process running from mental models to consensus models and experimental testing

> **Design** · [All designs](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 causal), `q3` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article organizes model-based science education around Gilbert's (2005) categories — "mental models, expressed models, consensus models, scientific models and teaching models" — and a modeling process that moves from encountering a problem, through forming and sharing temporary models, to systematic observation, model evaluation and revision. In the study's classroom version, students form and share mental models, choose a consensus model, assess it for usefulness, predictive power, coherence and testability, then experimentally test, accept, revise or reject it. The in-class process was deliberately stopped at the consensus stage.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Problem situations drawn from the curriculum subject titles, here the phenomena of electrification and electrical current
- Experimental materials attainable enough for students to test their models empirically
#### Constraints
- The in-class modeling process was designed only up to the consensus stage because the next step would require participants to act as scientists

### Target Learners
- 7th grade lower-secondary science students

### Learning Goals
- Understanding electrification and electrical current
- Critical thinking skills such as weighing evidence and creating cause-and-effect relations
