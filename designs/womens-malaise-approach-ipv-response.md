# Frame IPV response through a women's malaise approach to ease detection and support team learning

> **Design** · [All designs](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
The women's malaise approach treats non-specific somatic and emotional symptoms as related to contextual, subjective and gender-related factors, using a gendered biopsychosocial, person-centred lens. The article found it 'can smooth team learning on how to respond to IPV and make such responses more comprehensive and woman-centred', and that it 'might be easier to detect women's malaise as compared to IPV', helping when women do not recognise their situation as IPV. Supporting structures include monthly professionals' case-discussion meetings and a women's therapeutic group to which providers can refer patients.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires health professionals trained in the biopsychosocial approach
- Requires management permitting time for meetings and therapeutic spaces (e.g. one morning per week for the women's group)
#### Constraints
- Studied in one Spanish primary health care centre; the article states it found no other studies linking the approach with improved IPV detection and management
- WHO recognises the evidence for women-centred care is only indirect and its definition vague

### Target Learners
- primary health care teams
- health professionals in interdisciplinary teams

### Learning Goals
- detecting and responding to intimate partner violence
- adopting a person-centred, gender-sensitive consultation style
