# Promising county-level coordination practices: centralized eligibility lists, streamlined multi-agency collaboration, and pooled QRIS resources

> **Design** · [All designs](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Across its county case studies, the report identifies a recurring pattern of local practices that partially offset state-level fragmentation. San Mateo and San Luis Obispo counties "coordinate resources and have streamlined processes for families and providers"; San Francisco and Sacramento counties "maintain centralized waiting lists for ECE programs to efficiently link eligible families with available slots"; and seven Northern California counties "have pooled resources to develop a more comprehensive QRIS and offer stronger incentives and training than any county could offer on its own." These practices emerge where local agencies fund coordination themselves despite lacking state support.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Local funding sources to sustain coordination activities the state no longer funds, such as centralized eligibility lists
#### Constraints
- State and federal administrative structures still create the underlying tangle; local administrators "struggle to undo the administrative tangle created by the state and federal governments"

### Target Learners
- Families and children birth to age 5 navigating county ECE systems in California

### Learning Goals
- Access to subsidized early care and education and connection to programs matching family needs
