# Whole-task performance

## Description
Whole-task performance engages learners in authentic, complex tasks that require integrating multiple knowledge types and skills simultaneously, mirroring the real conditions of professional practice. Rather than isolating sub-skills for sequential mastery, this element asks learners to coordinate across domains from the outset — making decisions, managing uncertainty, and applying judgment as a practitioner would. Learners begin with supported versions of the whole task (high scaffolding, lower complexity) and progressively work toward independent performance on increasingly realistic instances.

## Design Implications

Embedding practice within whole, authentic tasks accelerates skill integration and transfer by preventing the fragmentation that decontextualized sub-skill training creates [[claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer]] [+S]. When learners practice sub-skills in isolation, the cues that normally signal when to apply each skill — and how they interact — are absent; this is a central mechanism behind the "transfer problem," the persistent difficulty of applying learned knowledge to real-world situations. Effective whole-task design sequences tasks from simple-to-complex rather than part-to-whole: learners perform a complete, authentic task from the beginning, but with scaffolding that fades as competence grows [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [+M]. Part-task practice may still be warranted for sub-skills that are too routine or too demanding for learners to acquire incidentally [[claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices]] [~M], but whole-task performance forms the backbone of the sequence.

### Context
#### Requirements
- High-fidelity environments or carefully constructed simulations that permit genuine decision-making and reflect the cognitive and social complexity of professional practice
- Instructional scaffolds — including [[elements/coaching|coaching]], [[elements/demonstration|worked examples]], and [[elements/supportive-information|supportive information]] — to manage cognitive load during initial task performance
- Explicit criteria aligned with professional standards, not arbitrary classroom benchmarks, so learners can calibrate their performance against meaningful targets

#### Constraints
- Absolute novices lacking prerequisite procedural knowledge may be cognitively overwhelmed; initial part-task practice on heavily automated sub-skills may be required before the whole task is accessible [[claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices]] [~M]
- High implementation cost: whole-task environments often require specialized equipment, expert coaching, or extended time commitments that institutional settings struggle to sustain
- Premature removal of scaffolding creates disorientation; the transition from supported to independent whole-task performance must be managed carefully [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [-M]

### Target Learners
- Professional practitioners-in-training in fields defined by genuine complexity and real consequences: medicine, law, engineering, teaching, military
- Advanced learners who possess sufficient domain background to engage with multiple knowledge types simultaneously without cognitive overload
- Learners developing "adaptive expertise" — the capacity to manage complexity and novel variation, not just execute known procedures reliably

### Target Learning Goals
- Complex skill integration: coordinating procedural, conceptual, and situational knowledge within a single authentic workflow
- Transfer: applying learned competencies to novel, ill-defined problems that differ from training instances in consequential ways
- Professional judgment and metacognitive monitoring: recognizing decision points, evaluating options under uncertainty, and regulating one's own performance in real time

### Affordances
- [[principles/scaffolding-and-fading|Scaffolding and Fading]] — manages the inherent cognitive load of whole tasks by providing temporary supports that allow learners to experience the complete task before they have automated every sub-skill; fading is built into the sequence, not optional
- [[principles/worked-examples|Worked Examples]] — provides the initial model of expert whole-task performance that novices can study before attempting the task themselves, reducing unproductive search during early practice
- [[principles/explaining-their-thinking|Explaining Their Thinking]] — requires learners to articulate the rationale behind their integrative decisions, making metacognitive monitoring explicit and deepening schema formation for complex task structures

## Related Elements
- [[elements/supportive-information|Supportive information]] — provides the conceptual frameworks and mental models learners need to make sense of the non-routine decisions the task requires
- [[elements/part-task-practice|Part-task practice]] — the complement: routine sub-skills that must be automated to free up working memory for the higher-order reasoning whole-task performance demands
- [[elements/coaching|Coaching]] — provides targeted guidance as learners navigate integrated challenges, adjusting the level of support to the learner's current performance
- [[elements/demonstration|Demonstration]] — models expert whole-task performance before learners attempt it, making the integration of sub-skills visible
- [[elements/debriefing|Debriefing]] — structured reflection after whole-task performance consolidates learning, helping learners identify what worked, what did not, and why

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [[patterns/4cid-four-component-instructional-design|4C/ID]] — makes learning tasks (whole tasks, sequenced from simple to complex) the organizing backbone of the entire instructional design
- [[patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship|Cognitive Apprenticeship]] — sequences learning globally (observing whole-task expert performance) to locally (practicing sub-skills) and back to globally (independent whole-task performance)
- [[patterns/merrills-first-principles-of-instruction|Merrill's First Principles]] — centers all instruction on a series of real-world whole tasks, treating task-centeredness as the master principle

## Examples
- **Medical Clerkship (Third-Year Medicine)** — Students manage real patient care in hospital settings, integrating diagnostic reasoning, communication, physical examination, and procedural skills under supervised conditions. Complexity is managed through supervision intensity, not task simplification.
- **Georgia Tech Capstone Design** — Students execute a full engineering design cycle, balancing thermal, structural, and financial constraints through iterative, interdependent decision-making — a structured whole task that no single sub-course could replicate.
- **Law School Clinical Programs** — Students handle real client matters requiring legal research, negotiation, drafting, and ethical judgment in a single workflow; the whole-task context makes abstract legal principles consequential in ways that case-reading alone cannot.
