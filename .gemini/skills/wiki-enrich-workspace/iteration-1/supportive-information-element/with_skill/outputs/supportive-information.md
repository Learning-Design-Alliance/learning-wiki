# Supportive information

## Description
Supportive information provides the cognitive strategies, mental models, and domain-specific conceptual frameworks learners need to tackle the non-routine, problem-solving aspects of complex tasks. Unlike [[elements/procedural-information|procedural information]] — which specifies fixed steps for recurrent sub-skills — supportive information is made available throughout practice and equips learners to navigate the ill-defined decisions that expert performance requires. It makes tacit expertise visible: the conceptual "why" behind professional reasoning, not just the sequential "how."

## Design Implications

Supportive information reduces cognitive overhead during complex problem-solving by externalizing the domain structures learners would otherwise have to construct through unguided search [[theories/cognitive-load-theory]] [+M]. When learners arrive at a task with an accurate mental model in hand, working memory is available for adaptive decision-making rather than figuring out the basic structure of the problem. Evidence that scaffolded support timed to need improves both immediate performance and transfer [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [+M] strengthens the rationale for providing mental models early — and keeping them accessible — rather than withholding them. Prompting learners to explain the concepts embedded in supportive information deepens encoding substantially [[claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding]] [+S], which argues for pairing this element with reflection or articulation tasks rather than using it as a passive read-before-you-start text.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A completed task analysis that identifies the non-routine decisions professionals actually make — the mental models must match genuine cognitive demands, not idealized procedures or surface-level content outlines
- Delivery before or alongside the learning task, not after — information provided after learners have attempted a task without it arrives too late to shape initial schema formation
- Worked cases or scenarios that give learners an opportunity to apply the mental models in context; abstract frameworks without application produce inert knowledge

#### Constraints
- Information delivered without application or practice leads to shallow encoding; learners can reproduce the framework but not deploy it under novel conditions [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [-M]
- Advanced learners may find explicit mental models redundant; over-provision to experienced practitioners creates the redundancy effect and can reduce engagement [[theories/cognitive-load-theory]] [~M]
- Expert blind spot makes accurate task analysis difficult — domain experts often fail to articulate the foundational reasoning novices need because they have already automated it

### Target Learners
- Novice to intermediate learners entering domains with genuine ill-structure: clinical medicine, law, systems engineering, teaching, organizational leadership
- Learners transitioning from rule-based execution to adaptive, principled decision-making in complex, open-ended domains
- Professionals re-skilling across fields where existing mental models are partially applicable but require elaboration

### Target Learning Goals
- Schema formation: building accurate mental models of system behavior, causal structure, and decision logic in the target domain
- Transfer: acquiring generalizable heuristics that function across novel instances of complex task classes, not only the training cases
- Metacognitive awareness: learning to recognize which type of reasoning a given problem demands and when to consult domain frameworks

### Affordances
- [[principles/scaffolding-and-fading|Scaffolding and Fading]] — supportive information is external cognitive scaffolding; as learners internalize the mental models, access can be progressively reduced, shifting the locus of expertise from the material to the learner
- [[principles/worked-examples|Worked Examples]] — provides the conceptual framework that makes the decisions within a worked example interpretable; the two elements are mutually reinforcing and are frequently deployed together in 4C/ID-designed courses
- [[principles/explaining-their-thinking|Explaining Their Thinking]] — prompting learners to articulate the reasoning embedded in supportive information converts declarative exposure into transferable procedural knowledge, amplifying the default benefit of exposure alone

## Related Elements
- [[elements/procedural-information|Procedural information]] — the counterpart for routine task components; where supportive information equips learners for non-routine decisions, procedural information specifies the fixed steps that can be executed without deliberation
- [[elements/demonstration|Demonstration]] — provides a concrete instance of the expert reasoning described in supportive information; often deployed as a pair, with the worked case making the mental model tangible
- [[elements/whole-task-performance|Whole-task performance]] — the authentic context in which supportive information is meant to function; mental models become meaningful only when learners encounter the genuine complexity of a real task
- [[elements/part-task-practice|Part-task practice]] — the complementary element for routine sub-skills that should be automated and no longer require the deliberate reasoning supportive information supports

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [[patterns/4cid-four-component-instructional-design|4C/ID]] — defines supportive information as one of four component types; it is delivered non-recurrently across a sequence of learning tasks and available throughout practice rather than tied to specific task moments
- [[patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship|Cognitive Apprenticeship]] — the modeling and coaching phases supply conceptual scaffolding that functions as supportive information, making expert reasoning visible before learners attempt the task independently

## Examples
- **4C/ID: Spreadsheet Training** — A course on financial modeling delivers the conceptual logic of absolute vs. relative cell referencing as supportive information before learners build complex formulas. The framework remains accessible throughout; learners consult it when they encounter novel formula types rather than on a fixed schedule.
- **Harvard Business School Case Method** — Analytical frameworks such as Porter's Five Forces are introduced as supportive information before students confront open-ended business cases. The framework does not produce the answer; it provides the cognitive structure that makes the case tractable.
- **Problem-Based Learning in Medical Education** — Diagnostic reasoning frameworks (hypothesis-driven examination, likelihood ratios) are provided as supportive information before students work through undifferentiated patient presentations. Students are not expected to derive these frameworks; they are expected to learn to use them adaptively.
