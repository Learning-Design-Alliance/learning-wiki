## Discussion

Persistence is the key question for SEL program evaluation: immediate post-test gains can reflect novelty, demand characteristics, or recent practice, while durable effects require skills to be consolidated, generalized to new settings, and maintained without program support. Follow-up designs (typically 6 months to several years post-intervention) are the appropriate test, and they are much rarer than immediate post-tests, so the evidence base for persistence is thinner than for immediate effects. Until follow-up studies are added to the Evidence section, this claim should be treated as a hypothesis awaiting synthesis rather than an established finding.

Several moderators plausibly govern whether gains persist. Programs that teach concrete, practicable skills and provide opportunities for application across contexts should consolidate better than awareness-raising formats; classroom-teacher-delivered programs embed skills in daily routines, which may support maintenance after the program ends; and dosage and fidelity during implementation plausibly predict durability. Conversely, brief one-off interventions, programs delivered by external providers with no classroom follow-through, and effects measured only on attitudes (rather than practiced skills) are the conditions under which fade-out is most likely. Academic outcomes may show delayed or lagged benefits even when behavioral effects plateau, so follow-up windows matter for interpreting null results.

The persistence question parallels findings from adjacent literatures. Brief social-psychological interventions such as [belonging interventions](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) have shown surprisingly durable effects from short treatments, suggesting that persistence does not always require high dosage — but SEL skill-building programs differ in mechanism, since they depend on practiced skills rather than reappraisal. Maintenance plausibly depends on continued opportunities to exercise social-emotional skills in ordinary classroom structures such as [collaborative learning](collaborative-learning-improves-outcomes.md), and on motivational conditions like [autonomy support](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) that keep students engaged in using those skills. Ongoing [assessment for learning](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) can supply the feedback loops that support consolidation.

Open questions: how long effects persist beyond the typical 1–2 year follow-up window, whether booster sessions are needed for maintenance, and whether effects persist equally across demographic groups and school contexts.
