## Evidence

### Jerrim 2017

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Pre-specified subgroup analyses in the RCT: boys' point estimate was -0.03, girls' +0.03 (p = 0.76), FSM +0.01. "We find no evidence that estimates differ between boys, girls and children who are eligible for Free School Meals (FSM)." A formal gender-by-treatment interaction test was non-significant.

> "We find no evidence that estimates differ between boys, girls and children who are eligible for Free School Meals (FSM)."

### Jerrim 2017 (2)

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Quantile regression estimates of the treatment effect at each decile of the Key Stage 2 distribution, testing whether effects at the mean masked offsetting gains and losses: "the effect size is below 0.05 standard deviations in magnitude" at every decile.

> "At each decile, the effect size is below 0.05 standard deviations in magnitude and is never significantly different from zero at even the 10 percent level."
