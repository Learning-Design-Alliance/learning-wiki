# Female introductory physics students who agree with the gender stereotype perform worse on the FCI than female students who disagree, especially in calculus-based courses

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` On the FCI pre-test, female students who agreed with the stereotype performed significantly worse than female students who disagreed, in both calculus-based (d = 0.69) and algebra-based (d = 0.36) courses. [→ Alexandru Maries 2017](#alexandru-maries-2017)
`q3 i1` On the calculus-based post-test, female agreeers continued to perform worse (effect size 0.39) but group sizes were too small for a t-test; in algebra-based post-test there was no difference. [→ Alexandru Maries 2017 (2)](#alexandru-maries-2017-2)

## Evidence

### Alexandru Maries 2017

Alexandru Maries, Nafis I. Karim, and Chandralekha Singh. (2017). The impact of stereotype threat on gender gap in introductory physics. 2017 PERC Proceedings. https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2017.pr.059

`q3 · i2` · `associational · r2`

Comparison of FCI pre-test scores among over 1100 introductory physics students grouped by stereotype agreement, analyzed with t-tests. The calculus-based pre-test comparison of female agreeers versus disagreeers yielded p = 0.003 and effect size 0.69; the algebra-based pre-test difference (about 4%) had p = 0.049, effect size 0.36.

> "female students who agreed with the stereotype performed significantly worse than female students who disagreed with the stereotype (p value = 0.003, effect size = 0.69)."

### Alexandru Maries 2017 (2)

Alexandru Maries, Nafis I. Karim, and Chandralekha Singh. (2017). The impact of stereotype threat on gender gap in introductory physics. 2017 PERC Proceedings. https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2017.pr.059

`q3 · i1` · `associational · r1`

Calculus-based FCI post-test comparison of female agreeers (N=10) versus disagreeers: descriptive difference with effect size 0.39, not testable. In the algebra-based post-test "there was no difference in performance" between female agreeers and disagreeers (effect size 0.00).

> "female students who agreed with the stereotype continued to perform worse than female students who disagreed, although the numbers are too small to perform a t-test. However, the effect size (0.39) suggests that if the number of female students was larger, the difference may have possibly become statistically significant."
