Personal connection to data supports critical data-literacy stance-taking¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · design r2 · q2 · n=27 prospective secondary teachers across two teacher-education courses (Mathematics Literacies, Human Geography)

When learners recognize a personal or moral stake in what a dataset represents — rather than treating it as an abstract external phenomenon — they are more likely to question what the data selects, aggregates, and omits, and to narrate their model-building choices in ways that invite an audience to do the same.

Evidence¶

Kahn & Hall 2026¶

Kahn, J., & Hall, R. (2026). An oldtimer and newcomers telling stories about models using open large datasets. Journal of the Learning Sciences. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2026.2624531

q2 · design study using interaction analysis of video-recorded performances across two classroom iterations, no comparison group · i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may · n=27 prospective secondary teachers across two teacher-education courses (Mathematics Literacies, Human Geography) · design · r2

Prospective teachers used the Gapminder open dataset and visualization tool to build and perform data stories about global health and wealth, following a forage-remix-DIY pattern. Interaction analysis of the performances found moments where learners "got personal" with the data — for example, questioning whether national-level CO2 measures adequately assigned responsibility to consumers like themselves — and in doing so shifted from presenting the model as a neutral display of facts to inviting peers into a critical, sometimes moral, discussion of what it meant. This pattern appeared in all groups in the Human Geography course but only 1 of 6 groups in Mathematics Literacies, tracking differences in how explicitly each course's instructor foregrounded social-justice framing.

Discussion¶

This is a single design study in two university teacher-education courses, with no comparison condition and no measure of whether the critical stance-taking persisted or transferred beyond the performance itself. The large difference in frequency between the two courses (6/6 groups vs. 1/6) suggests the effect may depend heavily on how much the instructor already foregrounds social and ethical framing of data — the claim should not be read as "personal connection alone is sufficient," but as a design lever more likely to matter when the surrounding course already values that framing. The authors themselves note a tension: privileging learners' personal experience can be read by others as bias that undermines data-driven reasoning, so this is not a claim that personal connection straightforwardly improves data literacy in every context.
