## Subclaims

`q3 i?` Oral, written, and video/audio/computer-assisted feedback channels did not differ significantly in average effect, so prior findings that written feedback outperforms oral were not confirmed. [→ Wisniewski B 2020](#wisniewski-b-2020)

`q3 i?` Wisniewski et al. (2020)'s meta-analysis of educational feedback research concluded that the feedback channel or medium itself is non-significant for effectiveness. [→ Istenič 2021](#istenic-2021)

## Evidence

### Wisniewski B 2020

Wisniewski B, Zierer K and Hattie J (2020) The Power of Feedback Revisited: A Meta-Analysis of Educational Feedback Research. Front. Psychol. 10:3087. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087

`q3 · i?` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

Mixed-effects moderator analysis across 994 effect sizes (Tables 2–3); feedback channel showed QB = 5.12 (df = 2), p > 0.05. The authors state that "Findings by Biber et al. (2011) that written feedback is more eﬀective than oral feedback could not be conﬁrmed."

> "Five of six moderators (research design, publication type, outcome measure, type of feedback, and feedback direction) proved to be statistically signiﬁcant, the feedback channel proved to be a non-signiﬁcant moderator."

### Istenič 2021

Istenič, A. (2021). Online learning under COVID-19: re-examining the prominence of video-based and text-based feedback. Education Tech Research Dev, 69, 117–121. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-021-09955-w

`q3 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

The editorial cites Wisniewski et al. (2020)'s meta-analysis of educational feedback research, which "concluding that feedback channel per se is non-significant". No effect size is printed in the editorial.

> "Wisniewski et al. (2020), in a large meta-analysis, explored the effectiveness of feedback channels or media use and among them of audio-, video, or computer-assisted instructional feedback, concluding that feedback channel per se is non-significant."
