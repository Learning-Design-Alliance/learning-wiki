# Expert performance rests on a large base of domain-specific patterns accessed by recognition, not on general reasoning alone

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Chess grand masters recall game-position layouts far better than random layouts, while novices do not, indicating a vast repertoire of chess-specific configurations. [→ PERKINS 1989](#perkins-1989)
`q2 i?` Across domains, experts use rapid pattern recognition and forward reasoning from givens, whereas novices reason backward from the unknown and rely on superficial features. [→ PERKINS 1989 (2)](#perkins-1989-2)

## Evidence

### PERKINS 1989

D. N. Perkins and Gavriel Salomon. (1989). Are Cognitive Skills Context-Bound? Educational Researcher, 16-23. https://www.jstor.org/stable/1176259

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

The review reports the classic chess memory experiments (Chase & Simon, 1973; de Groot, 1965): "experts could indeed do this" only for natural game positions, and grand masters were estimated to hold something like fifty thousand chess-specific configurations, or schemata.

> "The experiments showed that experts could indeed do this-but only if the chess pieces' positions had emerged in the natural course of play, not if the same pieces were arranged randomly on the board."

### PERKINS 1989 (2)

D. N. Perkins and Gavriel Salomon. (1989). Are Cognitive Skills Context-Bound? Educational Researcher, 16-23. https://www.jstor.org/stable/1176259

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review synthesizes the expertise-research profile across physics, mathematics, programming, and medicine, contrasting experts' forward reasoning with novices' backward reasoning; no effect sizes are printed, so impact is coded i? (no magnitude reported; not a null effect).

> "Expert performance entailed (a) a large knowledge base of domain-specific patterns (for example, typical configurations of pieces in chess, typical uses of conservation laws in physics); (b) rapid recognition of situations where these patterns apply"
