Evidence¶

Winch 1913¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part I. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(9), 525–537. doi:10.1037/h0075675

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Table II groups the 53 boys into seven ability sections based on preliminary test scores. In every section, the average number of words correct after learning was higher for Set A (combined method) than Set B (visual silent method), from the top group (29.7 vs 29.3) to the bottom (5.5 vs 5.3).

"The regularities of this table are quite beyond the realm of 'chance,' and show a clear victory for the method employed for Set A all along the line."

Winch 1913 (2)¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(10), 579–592. doi:10.1037/h0076021

q2 · i? · causal · r1

Grouped correlation table (Table IV) from the Standard IV experiment, sectioning boys by pre-learning Set A score; post-learning averages favor Set A in every section (e.g., 26.3 vs 24.3 in the top section, 8.7 vs 6.7 in the lowest). The author concludes these are "evidently not 'chance' results."

"All along the line, for the weaker pupils as well as for the abler ones, the simul- taneous combined method with attention directed by the ex- perimenter has shown itself victorious."

Related Claims¶

  • A combined visual-motor-articulatory-auditory spelling method with teacher-directed attention produces better spelling than a predominantly visual silent method in young low-ability boys — related
  • Both directed multisensory and silent visual spelling methods produced large improvement from baseline after 40 minutes of practice spread over four days — related
  • For Standard IV boys (average age 11), a combined articulatory-auditory-visual spelling method with directed attention yields higher dictation scores than a silent visual method without directed attention — related
  • Across Standards II and IV (ages 8 to 11) in the same school, the spelling method with pronounced auditory, articulatory, and visual factors plus directed attention proved definitely superior to a more purely visual method with less directed attention — a broader claim this one bears on
  • In-class flash-card visual study with writing from memory raised Standard I dictation averages to 20.8, with every boy spelling some words correctly, though the method was time-costly and complicated — related
  • A head-to-head teaching contest between a primarily visual method and a combined method produced nearly identical averages (22.9 vs 23.0) and could not decide between the methods because of uncontrolled confounds — reports the opposite
  • Reviewer credits the method with directness, concentration from flash-cards, valuable action responses, and stimulating effect — related
