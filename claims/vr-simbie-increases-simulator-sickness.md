## Evidence

### Khuansiri Narajeenron et al 2025

Khuansiri Narajeenron et al. (2025). Enhancing Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety Performance Through Medical Movies, Massive Open Online Courses, and 3D Virtual Simulation–Based Interprofessional Education: Mixed Methods Double-Blind Quasi-Experimental Study. Journal of Medical Internet Research. https://doi.org/10.2196/67001

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

SSQ (range 0-235.62) measured before and after the initial game session in the quasi-experimental study; the VR arm showed a "significant increase in SSQ scores" (Δ=21.02, P=.001) while the computer-based arm's change of 5.28 was insignificant (P=.44).

> "Posttraining results indicated a significant increase in SSQ scores for arm C, which used a VR headset, showing the highest change in SSQ score of 21.02 (95% CI 10.67-31.37; P=.001). In contrast, arm B, which used computer-based games, exhibited an insignificant change in SSQ scores, with a difference of 5.28 (95% CI –8.24 to 18.80; P=.44)."

### Khuansiri Narajeenron et al 2025 (2)

Khuansiri Narajeenron et al. (2025). Enhancing Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety Performance Through Medical Movies, Massive Open Online Courses, and 3D Virtual Simulation–Based Interprofessional Education: Mixed Methods Double-Blind Quasi-Experimental Study. Journal of Medical Internet Research. https://doi.org/10.2196/67001

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Table 4 oculomotor subscale differences: arm A 2.64 (P=.58), arm B 8.03 (P=.15), arm C 21.43 (P<.001); the VR arm's oculomotor increase was significant while the other arms' were not. Nausea (arm C 12.50, P=.007) and disorientation (arm C 20.64, P=.003) also rose significantly in the VR arm.
