## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Strong ties with academic prestige positively predict mathematics grades (coefficients .47 and .5, p < .01), yet their effect is null on physics. [→ Javier Pulgar 2021](#javier-pulgar-2021)

## Evidence

### Javier Pulgar 2021

Javier Pulgar, Diego Ramírez, Abigail Umanzor, Cristian Candia, and Iván Sánchez. (2021). Student networks on online teaching due to COVID-19: Academic effects of strong friendship ties and perceived academic prestige in physics and mathematics courses. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2109.06245](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2109.06245)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Mathematics regression models C and D added academic prestige to the strong-tie measure, yielding "coefﬁcients of .47 and .5 (p < . 01)". No standardized effect size printed.

> "The bene-ﬁts of strong ties strengthen when adding academic prestige into the dyadic relationship, with coefﬁcients of .47 and .5 (p < . 01) on models C and D respectively."

### Javier Pulgar 2021 (2)

Javier Pulgar, Diego Ramírez, Abigail Umanzor, Cristian Candia, and Iván Sánchez. (2021). Student networks on online teaching due to COVID-19: Academic effects of strong friendship ties and perceived academic prestige in physics and mathematics courses. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2109.06245](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2109.06245)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Physics regression models C and D from the same OLS analysis showed no significant gains from collaborating with peers perceived as good physics students. The null was not tested for equivalence.

> "When adding academic prestige, models C and D show no signiﬁcant gains of working alongside others who are perceived as good physics stu- dents."
