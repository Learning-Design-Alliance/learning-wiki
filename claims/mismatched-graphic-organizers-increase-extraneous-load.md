# Mismatched Graphic Organizers Increase Extraneous Load

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q3` peer-reviewed experiment · `i2` medium · n=157 (Experiment 1) and n≈134 (Experiment 2), German university students

A graphic organizer helps learning only when its structure matches the structure of the content and the task; a mismatched organizer forces learners to reconcile two incompatible representations, consuming working-memory resources that do not contribute to schema construction.

## Subclaims

`q3 i2` When the surface structure of a graphic (its visual format) mismatches the structure a task or later recall demands, comprehension/recall accuracy drops relative to a matched format — but this mismatch penalty is modest and can be outweighed by which content is thematically central to the material. [→ Schnotz & Baadte 2015](#schnotz-baadte-2015)

## Evidence

### Schnotz & Baadte 2015

Schnotz, W., & Baadte, C. (2015). Surface and deep structures in graphics comprehension. *Memory & Cognition, 43*(4), 605–618. [doi:10.3758/s13421-014-0490-2](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-014-0490-2)

`q3 · peer-reviewed lab experiments (two studies)` · `i2 · medium effect on schema-interference contrasts, d=0.51–0.54; smaller format-match interaction, η²=.03–.04` · `n=157 (Experiment 1) and n≈134 (Experiment 2), German university students`

Two between-subjects experiments had students study bar graphs of (fictional-data) US election results presented in one of two informationally-equivalent visual formats ("party" graphs vs. "religion" graphs), then recall the content by filling in blank graphs of either the studied or a differently-formatted item. In both experiments a significant graph-format × recall-item-format interaction showed recall was more accurate when the recall format matched the studied format (F(1,151)=6.52, p=.006, η²=.04 in Exp. 1; F(1,128)=4.22, p=.021, η²=.03 in Exp. 2), and cueing an unfamiliar, incongruent processing perspective by instruction significantly *degraded* recall of the previously-studied content (t(151)=2.03, p=.022, d=0.54, and t(151)=1.99, p=.025, d=0.52) — consistent with the authors' framing that an incongruent schema "impose[s] a higher cognitive load on working memory," producing interference. The important qualifier: participants also recalled content more accurately from whichever perspective was *thematically* dominant in the material regardless of which graphic format they had studied — in Experiment 2 they recalled the non-studied format's items *better* than the studied one (t(66)=3.83, p<.001, d=0.51). So format (surface-structure) mismatch imposes a real but modest processing cost, which the content's underlying task-relevance can override.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Within [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md), extraneous load arises from instructional design that requires processing not directed at learning. A well-matched organizer (e.g., a compare/contrast matrix for comparative content, a cycle diagram for cyclical processes) externalizes relationships so learners need not hold them in working memory — consistent with the claim that [chunking reduces working-memory load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. A mismatched organizer adds a second, partially inconsistent representation: learners must map organizer slots onto content that does not fit them, splitting attention between the two structures — a condition analogous to the split-attention effect. The result is the same outcome described in [cognitive overload degrades learning](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]: resources spent on reconciliation rather than schema construction.

**Constraints.** The claim is about *mismatched* organizers, not organizers generally — the penalty applies only under specific conditions:

- **Imposed rather than learner-generated.** An organizer assigned without reference to the content's actual structure forces reconciliation work on the learner [-M].
- **Unfamiliar content structure.** Novices lack the expertise to detect and repair an ill-fitting frame, so they process the mismatch rather than the content [-M].
- **Advanced learners.** For experts, any imposed organizer may simply be redundant — consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S].
- **High element interactivity.** When the content is already near working-memory capacity, the added reconciliation load is most likely to push learners past capacity [-M].

Well-aligned organizers and advance organizers can support learning — see [Advance organizers improve learning](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. The penalty also parallels the [coherence principle](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+S]: like decorative but irrelevant material, an ill-fitting organizer is a design feature that invites processing unrelated to the learning goal.

**Design implications.** Before selecting an organizer, analyze the content's underlying structure — sequence, hierarchy, comparison, cycle, cause–effect — and choose or adapt a frame that mirrors it. Where no standard frame fits, a sparser outline or learner-constructed map is safer than forcing content into a borrowed template. This aligns with the broader guidance in [cognitive load reduction](../principles/cognitive-load-reduction.md) and [clear structure](../principles/clear-structure.md).

**Open questions.** How much structural mismatch is tolerable before load costs outweigh organizational benefits, and whether learner-constructed organizers avoid the penalty, remain under-researched. Studies are needed to establish this claim empirically.
