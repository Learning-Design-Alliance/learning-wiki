Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis r? · q3–q4 · i1 small

Learners who take part in school peer-tutoring programs, as tutees or as tutors, tend to show higher achievement than comparable learners without tutoring. One meta-analysis (65 evaluations) found gains for tutees and the same pattern of gains for tutors, without a pooled effect size in the abstract read; another (50 mathematics studies) found a small average effect for participants (g = 0.333). Neither recorded entry shows that tutors gain more than tutees.

Evidence¶

Cohen et al. 1982¶

Cohen, P. A., Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1982). Educational Outcomes of Tutoring: A Meta-analysis of Findings. American Educational Research Journal, 19(2), 237–248. doi:10.3102/00028312019002237

q3 · meta-analysis · i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract · n=65 independent evaluations · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programs, spanning tutee and tutor outcomes. Tutored students outperformed control students on examinations and developed more positive attitudes toward the subject matter; students who served as tutors showed the same pattern of academic and attitudinal gains. Participation had little or no effect on the self-esteem of either tutors or tutees. Only the abstract was read (the full article is paywalled and no open-access copy was found), so no pooled effect-size statistic could be confirmed from the text — the abstract itself states the finding only in directional terms.

Alegre-Ansuategui et al. 2017¶

Alegre-Ansuategui, F. J., Moliner, L., Lorenzo, G., & Maroto, A. (2017). Peer Tutoring and Academic Achievement in Mathematics: A Meta-Analysis. EURASIA Journal of Mathematics, Science and Technology Education, 14(1). doi:10.12973/ejmste/79805

q4 · meta-analysis · i1 · small effect, Hedge's g = 0.333 · n=50 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of 50 independent studies of peer-tutoring programs in mathematics across multiple educational stages found that 88% of the programs had a positive effect on participants' academic performance, with a pooled Hedge's g of 0.333. Moderator analyses found tutee/tutor age, assigned roles, tutee ability level (disabled/at-risk vs. not), session length and frequency were not significant moderators, while educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day (in-school vs. out-of-school) and sample size were significant moderators of the effect. Crossref carries no page range for this record; the article's own header gives pp. 337–354 (2018;14(1)), which is noted here but not asserted as the Crossref-verified value.

Discussion¶

Tutor gains. Cohen et al. (1982) report that tutors showed the same pattern of academic and attitudinal gains as tutees; the recorded entries do not compare the size of the two, so the claim often made in the peer-tutoring literature that the tutor benefits as much as or more than the tutee is not established here. Preparing to explain, generating explanations, and detecting and repairing one's own knowledge gaps during tutoring are forms of elaborative retrieval and self-explanation, which plausibly explains why tutoring functions as a powerful learning activity for the tutor rather than merely a delivery mechanism. This aligns with the broader "protégé effect" documented for teaching-related activities — see Teaching as learning.

Moderators to establish. Two meta-analyses are recorded, one read only as an abstract; Alegre-Ansuategui et al. (2017) found educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size moderated the effect in mathematics. Open questions the recorded evidence does not settle include: structured versus unstructured tutoring formats, same-ability versus cross-ability pairing, age and domain of learners, and whether effects persist on delayed or transfer measures rather than only immediate post-tests. Reciprocal tutoring designs, where learners alternate roles, are one common attempt to capture both tutor and tutee benefits.

Boundary conditions. Peer tutoring is not a substitute for expertise: tutors with inaccurate understanding can propagate errors, and benefits depend on tutors having sufficient mastery of the material they explain. Implementation quality — training tutors, providing structured materials, and monitoring sessions — is likely to moderate outcomes substantially. From a cognitive load theory perspective, explaining to a peer can benefit the tutor through germane elaboration, but an overloaded or under-prepared tutor may fail to generate useful explanations at all.

Interpreting against the right baseline. Because tutoring is an active, interactive format, any comparison against passive instruction conflates tutoring-specific effects with the general advantage of active learning. Well-designed studies compare tutoring against other active formats, not only lecture.

Role assignment and structure. Because tutor and tutee roles engage different mechanisms — elaborative explanation for the tutor, guided practice with feedback for the tutee — designs that fix one learner in each role may systematically favor one party. Reciprocal or role-swapping designs, and structured protocols that script the explanation and questioning moves, are the main implementation responses; neither is tested by the evidence recorded here.
