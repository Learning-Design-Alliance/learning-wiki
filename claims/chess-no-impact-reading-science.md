# The chess intervention showed no evidence of positive impact on Key Stage 2 reading and science scores

> **Currency** · newest study 2017 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

## Subclaims

`q3 i?` Secondary outcomes of KS2 reading and science showed no evidence of positive impact, the same as for mathematics. [→ Jerrim et al. 2016](#jerrim-et-al-2016)

`q4 i?` The estimated impact on Key Stage 2 reading scores was -0.06 standard deviations and not statistically significant. [→ Jerrim 2017](#jerrim-2017)

`q4 i?` The estimated impact on science scores was -0.03 and not statistically significant; science scores were based on teachers' judgements rather than external examinations. [→ Jerrim 2017 (2)](#jerrim-2017-2)

## Evidence

### Jerrim et al. 2016

John Jerrim, Lindsey Macmillan, John Micklewright, Mary Sawtell and Meg Wiggins. (2016). Chess in Schools: Evaluation report and executive summary. Education Endowment Foundation. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/library/chess-in-schools-protocol/

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Secondary outcome analysis of the clustered RCT covering Key Stage 2 English (reading) and science tests, plus mathematics sub-domains (paper A, paper B, mental arithmetic), measured one year after the intervention finished.

> "There is no evidence that the intervention had a positive impact on mathematics attainment for the children in the trial, as measured by Key Stage 2 scores one year after the intervention ended. The same is true for science and reading."

### Jerrim 2017

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

ITT estimates from the same clustered RCT for secondary outcomes: "-0.06 standard deviations in reading (95 percent confidence interval from -0.21 to +0.09)". Reading was measured by externally marked national examination.

> "The estimated impact upon children's post-test (Key Stage 2) scores was -0.06 standard deviations in reading (95 percent confidence interval from -0.21 to +0.09) and -0.03 in science (95 percent confidence interval from -0.13 to +0.08)."

### Jerrim 2017 (2)

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Same trial, science outcome: "-0.03 in science (95 percent confidence interval from -0.13 to +0.08)". The article notes science scores were based upon teachers' judgements, unlike the examined reading and mathematics scores.

> "The estimated impact upon children's post-test (Key Stage 2) scores was -0.06 standard deviations in reading (95 percent confidence interval from -0.21 to +0.09) and -0.03 in science (95 percent confidence interval from -0.13 to +0.08)."
