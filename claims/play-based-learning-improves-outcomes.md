# Guided play outperforms direct instruction or free play on a few early-childhood outcomes, with no reliable difference on most others, in one meta-analysis

Guided play and structured play-based approaches can improve academic, social, and self-regulation outcomes relative to didactic instruction, though the strength of the effect depends heavily on how much adult scaffolding is built into the play.

## Evidence

### Skene et al. 2022

Skene, K., O'Farrelly, C. M., Byrne, E. M., Kirby, N., Stevens, E. C., & Ramchandani, P. G. (2022). Can guidance during play enhance children's learning and development in educational contexts? A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Child Development, 93*(4), 1162–1180. [doi:10.1111/cdev.13730](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13730)

`q3 · systematic review and meta-analysis of quasi-experimental and randomized studies` · `i1 · small to large effects, g=0.24–0.93 across outcomes` · `n=39 studies (3,893 children in all; 17 pooled)` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

This systematic review searched for studies (1977–2020) comparing guided play to free play or direct instruction on children's academic and developmental outcomes. Thirty-nine studies (from 38 papers) met inclusion criteria and were reviewed; 17 were pooled into 12 meta-analyses across four outcome domains (literacy, numeracy, executive function, socioemotional). The 39 studies involved 3,893 children aged roughly 1–8 years in all, and the meta-analysed samples varied by outcome. Guided play showed a greater positive effect than [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) on early maths skills (Hedges' g=0.24, 95% CI [0.12, 0.35]), shape knowledge (g=0.63, CI [0.17, 1.09]) and task switching (g=0.40, CI [0.05, 0.74]), and a greater effect than free play on spatial vocabulary (g=0.93, CI [0.10, 1.75], which the authors call a large effect, but with high heterogeneity, I²=80.70%). No reliable differences were found for most other outcomes compared, and the authors caution that many effects, especially the free-play comparison, rest on very small samples and substantial heterogeneity in how guided play was conceptualized and implemented across studies.

## Discussion

**Guided play vs. free play.** The literature distinguishes free play, guided play, and direct instruction. Guided play — where adults design the environment and co-participate with learning goals in mind — shows the most consistent positive findings for early literacy, mathematics, and executive-function outcomes, while wholly unstructured free play shows weaker and less consistent academic effects. The mechanism proposed is that play sustains engagement and autonomy while adult guidance keeps activity oriented toward the target learning goal. This scaffolding role parallels the adult structuring described in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) and [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md): unguided activity can leave novices without the support they need.

**Domain and age boundaries.** Most supporting evidence comes from early childhood (preschool through early primary). Extrapolating play-based claims to older learners is not supported by the current evidence base, and academic content that requires explicit, sequenced instruction may be poorly served by discovery-through-play alone — a boundary consistent with the general pattern that [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) mainly when activities are well structured.

**Constraints on effectiveness.** Play-based approaches underperform when the play is purely child-directed with no adult co-regulation toward a learning goal, when outcome content demands explicit sequencing (e.g., systematic phonics) that play alone does not reliably deliver, and when implemented with learners beyond early childhood, where the evidence base thins out entirely. Comparisons against weak or poorly implemented direct instruction inflate apparent benefits; against well-implemented instruction, guided play's advantage narrows or disappears.

**Open questions.** The field lacks large-scale randomized trials with long-term follow-up, and outcome measures often differ across studies (observer ratings vs. standardized assessments), making effect-size synthesis difficult. Studies comparing guided play with well-implemented direct instruction — rather than weak instruction — are especially needed.

*Merged from “Guided Play Improves Academic Outcomes” (guided-play-improves-academic-outcomes):* **Positioning on the play–instruction continuum.** Guided play sits between free play and direct instruction: the adult designs the environment and frames the activity around a learning goal, but children retain meaningful choices about how to pursue it. The claim is strongest for this middle position — neither pure discovery nor fully scripted teaching. This mirrors the broader pattern in which minimally guided approaches underperform for novices while heavy-handed instruction can forfeit engagement; guided play is best understood as a scaffolded middle path rather than pure discovery, consistent with [Cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) [~M].

**Likely moderators.** Expected boundary conditions include learner age (effects are best documented in preschool and early primary years), domain (early literacy, numeracy, and spatial skills are the most-studied outcomes), and the quality of adult scaffolding — guidance that is too light collapses into free play, while guidance that is too heavy collapses into direct instruction and may forfeit the motivational and self-regulation benefits that distinguish guided play [~W]. Open questions include durability of effects over time and transfer beyond the trained domain; the evidence base remains thinner than for instruction-centered claims such as [Direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) or [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md).

**Measurement cautions.** Much of the guided-play literature relies on small samples, researcher-designed interventions, and immediate post-tests, so effect sizes should be read cautiously until replicated at scale [-W]. Outcomes such as self-regulation and executive function are often measured alongside academics, and gains in those proximal skills may not translate into durable academic achievement.

**Relation to constructivist claims.** Guided play is often cited as a practical instantiation of [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) with scaffolding, and it connects to [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md): the child's sense of volition is treated as a mechanism, not a side effect [+M]. It also aligns with [Conflict-based instruction improves science conceptual learning, though staged contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused, and no study isolates disequilibrium as the mechanism](cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) — well-designed play creates productive challenges that the adult then helps the child resolve.

**Design implication.** Practitioners should treat guided play as an intentional design act, not an unstructured activity: select materials and play scenarios that make the target concept salient, plan open-ended prompts rather than directives, and observe before intervening. The adult's role is closer to that of a [cognitive apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) coach — modeling and hinting within the child's activity — than a lecturer.
