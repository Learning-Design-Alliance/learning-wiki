## Evidence

### Paré 2017

Paré, D. & Quirk, G. J. (2017). When scientific paradigms lead to tunnel vision: lessons from the study of fear. npj Science of Learning. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-017-0007-4

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Unit recordings from the basolateral amygdala in the Robogator foraging task (Amir et al., as reported by this perspective). The authors state Type-1 firing rates were "markedly reduced, becoming nearly silent near the predator", the reverse of threat-coding predictions, and Type-2 activity was also unrelated to threat.

> "Yet, during foraging, the activity of Type-1 cells was opposite to that predicted by the threat-coding model: their FRs were markedly reduced, becoming nearly silent near the predator"

### Paré 2017 (2)

Paré, D. & Quirk, G. J. (2017). When scientific paradigms lead to tunnel vision: lessons from the study of fear. npj Science of Learning. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-017-0007-4

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Amir et al. compared basolateral amygdala activity in the foraging task and two control tasks with no explicit threats or rewards. The article reports "neither threat proximity nor reward availability predicted BL activity"; instead Type-1 firing varied with movement, rising when behavioral output was suppressed and falling during exploration or food retrieval.

> "Unexpectedly, they found that across the three tasks, neither threat proximity nor reward availability predicted BL activity"
