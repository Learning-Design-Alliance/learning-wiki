# Considering The Opposite Reduces Bias

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Deliberately reasoning about hypotheses and evidence that contradict one's initial belief reduces confirmation bias in judgment and evaluation. The strategy operates at the moment of judgment: learners are prompted to ask how the evidence might support the rival view rather than whether it supports their own.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The mechanism most commonly proposed is that "consider the opposite" works by making disconfirming evidence cognitively available at the moment of judgment: instead of asking learners whether the evidence supports their view (which biases memory search toward confirming cases), they are prompted to ask how the evidence might support the rival view. This reframing is what distinguishes it from generic instructions to "be unbiased," which tend to fail because people believe they are already objective.

**Boundary conditions.** The strategy should work best when learners have at least some access to disconfirming information — if the evidence base presented is one-sided, considering the opposite has nothing to work with. It also presupposes that learners can generate a plausible opposite; for novices in a domain, the rival hypothesis may be unavailable without scaffolding, suggesting pairing with structured [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) or [case-based learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md). Whether the bias reduction transfers beyond the specific judgment task, or persists once the prompt is removed, remains an open question that the evidence section does not yet address.

**Design implications.** Designers should treat this as a judgment-stage intervention: it is most naturally embedded in activities where learners must evaluate evidence or reach a verdict — [debates](../patterns/debate.md) (especially formats that force learners to argue an [assigned position](../elements/assigned-positions.md)), case analyses, and source evaluation — rather than as a standalone lesson on bias. Prompts should be specific ("list three reasons the opposing interpretation could be correct") rather than general exhortations to objectivity, since the mechanism depends on redirecting the memory search itself.
