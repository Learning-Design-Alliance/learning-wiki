## Evidence

### Maëlle Neveu 2023

Maëlle Neveu, Marie Geurten, Nancy Durieux, Laurence Rousselle. (2023). Finger Use and Arithmetic Skills in Children and Adolescents: a Scoping Review. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09722-8

`q3 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports five non-randomized controlled trials in TD primary school children; in four, explicit finger-strategy training for addition and subtraction was followed by improvement, as "A significant improvement in calculation performance was consistently observed after training." No effect size printed.

> "In four of them, participants were trained to explicitly use finger strategies to solve addition and subtraction problems (Fuson, 1986; Fuson & Secada, 1986; Fuson & Willis, 1988; Ollivier et al., 2020). A significant improvement in calculation performance was consistently observed after training."

### Maëlle Neveu 2023 (2)

Maëlle Neveu, Marie Geurten, Nancy Durieux, Laurence Rousselle. (2023). Finger Use and Arithmetic Skills in Children and Adolescents: a Scoping Review. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09722-8

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

Cluster-randomized controlled trial (Chao et al., 2000) in TD kindergartners contrasting finger-users and non-finger-users trained with abstract versus concrete manipulatives; among finger-users, "training with concrete manipulatives was more effective." No effect size printed.

> "The results showed that, among finger-users, training with concrete manipulatives was more effective in increasing their calculation performance than the use of abstract manipulatives."

### Maëlle Neveu 2023 (3)

Maëlle Neveu, Marie Geurten, Nancy Durieux, Laurence Rousselle. (2023). Finger Use and Arithmetic Skills in Children and Adolescents: a Scoping Review. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09722-8

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

Non-randomized trial of the Chisanbop finger counting method (Stegemann, 2014); the review reports inconclusive results, with no significant improvement for trained second-graders and control fifth-graders outperforming the training group.

> "This training yielded inconclusive results as second-graders did not show a significant improvement in their calculation performance, whereas fifth-graders in the control group improved their performance but not those in the training group."
