# The Chess in Primary Schools programme showed no evidence of a positive impact on Key Stage 2 mathematics attainment one year after the intervention ended

## Subclaims

`q4 i?` A large clustered RCT found no evidence that one year of chess lessons raised KS2 maths scores, with an effect size of 0.01 and a confidence interval spanning zero. [→ Jerrim et al. 2016](#jerrim-et-al-2016)

`q4 i?` In a pre-registered clustered RCT of over 4,000 pupils, the intention-to-treat impact of the CSC chess programme on Key Stage 2 mathematics scores one year after the intervention was essentially zero and not statistically significant. [→ Jerrim 2017](#jerrim-2017)

`q4 i?` Local Average Treatment Effect estimates correcting for non-compliance confirm the null mathematics result. [→ Jerrim 2017 (2)](#jerrim-2017-2)

## Evidence

### Jerrim et al. 2016

John Jerrim, Lindsey Macmillan, John Micklewright, Mary Sawtell and Meg Wiggins. (2016). Chess in Schools: Evaluation report and executive summary. Education Endowment Foundation. https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/library/chess-in-schools-protocol/

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Two-armed clustered RCT with 100 schools and 4,009 pupils across 11 English local authorities, analysed by intention to treat. The summary table reports an effect size of 0.01 (95% CI -0.15, 0.16), equivalent to 0 months' progress, with a high security rating.

> "There is no evidence that the intervention had a positive impact on mathematics attainment for the children in the trial, as measured by Key Stage 2 scores one year after the intervention ended."

### Jerrim 2017

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Intention-to-treat analysis of a pre-registered clustered RCT (4,009 pupils, 100 schools, 50 treatment/50 control) using externally marked Key Stage 2 mathematics scores from the National Pupil Database, standardised to z-scores. The estimate was "0.001 with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from approximately -0.149 to +0.151". Post-test scores were available for 3,865 (97 percent) pupils.

> "In both cases, the point estimate of the treatment is 0.001 with a 95 percent confidence interval ranging from approximately -0.149 to +0.151."

### Jerrim 2017 (2)

Jerrim, J., Macmillan, L., Micklewright, J., Sawtell, M., and Wiggins, M. (2017). Does teaching children how to play cognitively demanding games improve their educational attainment? Evidence from a Randomised Controlled Trial of chess instruction in England. UCL Institute of Education. https://controlled-trials.com/ISRCTN33648117

`q4 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Robustness analysis via Two-Stage Least Squares instrumental variables, using initial random allocation as an instrument for treatment receipt, correcting for six treatment schools dropping out and one control school delivering chess. The "LATE point estimate is also 0.001 standard deviations".

> "Specifically, the LATE point estimate is also 0.001 standard deviations, with 95 percent confidence interval running from -0.166 to +0.168."
