# Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r2`, 2 review `r2`, 1 causal `r?` · `q3`–`q4` · `i1` small
<!-- deprecated title (2026-09-30, overstated its evidence): Rewards Undermine Intrinsic Motivation -->

Extrinsic rewards — particularly tangible, expected rewards offered contingent on task engagement or completion — can reduce learners' intrinsic motivation for activities they initially found interesting.

## Subclaims

`q4 i1` A 128-study meta-analysis found that engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent tangible rewards significantly reduced learners' later free-choice engagement with the rewarded activity, though the pattern was contested by an earlier meta-analysis reaching the opposite overall conclusion. [→ Deci Koestner Ryan 1999](#deci-koestner-ryan-1999)

`q4 i?` A competing 96-study meta-analysis found no overall undermining effect of reward on free-choice intrinsic motivation, with a significant negative effect confined to expected tangible rewards given merely for doing a task. [→ Cameron Pierce 1994](#cameron-pierce-1994)

`q3 i?` Paying university students to do puzzle-solving and headline-writing tasks they already found interesting reduced their subsequent free-time engagement with those tasks relative to unpaid students, even though both groups started out equally interested. [→ Deci 1971](#deci-1971)

`q4 i?` The effect is substantially weaker or absent when pay is a flat, hourly rate rather than tied piecemeal to output, and when the task itself is well-defined with a clear standard for high-quality performance. [→ Cameron and Pierce 1994](#cameron-pierce-1994)

`q3 i?` A synthesis of the reward/intrinsic-motivation literature confirms the undermining effect occurs often enough that extrinsic rewards for already-interesting tasks need to be used selectively and thoughtfully, rather than never or always. [→ Deci Koestner and Ryan 2001](#deci-koestner-and-ryan-2001)

## Evidence

### Deci Koestner Ryan 1999

Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Psychological Bulletin, 125*(6), 627–668. [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i1 · small to medium effects, d=-0.28 (performance-contingent) to -0.40 (engagement-contingent); the printed range spans the i1–i2 boundary and is binned by its smaller end, so it covers all three contingencies the subclaim names` · `n=128 studies` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

A meta-analysis of 128 experiments compared free-choice persistence and self-reported interest in a rewarded activity, after the reward was withdrawn, between reward and no-reward groups. Engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent tangible rewards each significantly undermined free-choice behavior, and engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also undermined self-reported interest; all tangible rewards and all expected rewards undermined free-choice behavior overall. By contrast, positive (verbal) feedback significantly *enhanced* both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31), and tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students. The authors present this as a defense and extension of the overjustification/self-determination account against contemporaneous critiques, including Cameron & Pierce's own reanalyses.

### Cameron Pierce 1994

Cameron, J., & Pierce, W. D. (1994). Reinforcement, reward, and intrinsic motivation: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 64*(3), 363–423. [doi:10.3102/00346543064003363](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003363)

`q4 · meta-analysis of between-groups experiments (plus a secondary within-subject analysis)` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract; described as no overall effect and a minimal negative effect for expected tangible rewards` · `n=96 studies (main analysis), plus 5 studies in a secondary within-subject analysis` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A hierarchical meta-analysis of 96 between-groups experiments compared rewarded and non-rewarded participants on four measures of intrinsic motivation, with a secondary analysis of 5 within-subject studies. Contrary to the overjustification hypothesis, the authors report that reward does not decrease intrinsic motivation overall, and that verbal praise increases it; a negative effect appears only for expected tangible rewards given simply for performing a task, and only on the free-choice behavior measure (not on self-reported attitudes), with the effect described as minimal. (Read as an abstract; the abstract gives no effect sizes.) The flat-rate and well-defined-task moderators in the subclaims come from the second write-up of this study kept in the comment below; the abstract does not state them, so they rest on that write-up's reading of the full text and are unchecked here. The two write-ups coded the design q4 and q3; it is q4 here, a meta-analysis of 96 experiments, as Deci, Koestner & Ryan's (1999) 128-experiment meta-analysis is coded on this page and on [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md).

### Deci 1971

Deci, E. L. (1971). Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 18*(1), 105-115. [doi:10.1037/h0030644](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0030644)

`q3 · peer-reviewed lab experiment · i? · no source text available to check; the entry prints no effect size · n=modest (university sample)` · `causal · r?`

University students worked on interesting puzzle-solving and newspaper-headline-writing tasks across several sessions; one group was paid for the activity and another was not, though both groups reported equal initial interest. Students who had been paid spent measurably less free time on the same activities afterward than students who had never been paid — the original demonstration of the overjustification effect, and the source study behind the "hugging and bridging" caution against over-relying on extrinsic reward systems (see [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), [Reinforcement Theory](../theories/behaviorism.md)).

### Eisenberger and Cameron 1996

Eisenberger, R., & Cameron, J. (1996). Detrimental effects of reward: Reality or myth? *American Psychologist, 51*(11), 1153-1166. [doi:10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153)

`q3 · narrative synthesis of experimental literature · i? · no source text available to check; the entry prints no effect size · n=large (aggregated)` · `review · r?`

Reaching a similar conclusion to Cameron and Pierce (1994) from a partly overlapping literature, this synthesis argues the overjustification effect, while real, has often been overstated as a blanket argument against all extrinsic reward, and identifies boundary conditions (reward contingent on mere participation vs. on producing more of an activity) under which rewards are unlikely to undermine intrinsic motivation.

### Deci Koestner and Ryan 2001

Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (2001). Extrinsic Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation in Education: Reconsidered Once Again. *Review of Educational Research, 71*(1), 1-27. [doi:10.3102/00346543071001001](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543071001001)

`q3 · review of a meta-analysis (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999) · i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may · n=large (aggregated across studies)` · `review · r2`

Responding directly to Cameron and Pierce's (1994) conclusion that the undermining effect was minimal, this article reviews the results of the authors' 1999 meta-analysis, which it says showed Cameron and Pierce's analysis was seriously flawed and that tangible rewards do have a substantial undermining effect, supporting cognitive evaluation theory. It discusses what that means for rewards in classrooms: they should be used selectively and thoughtfully, not treated as a costless motivational tool.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The standard explanation draws on [self-determination theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md): rewards can shift a learner's perceived locus of causality from internal ("I do this because I enjoy it") to external ("I do this to get the reward"), undermining the needs for [autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) and competence that sustain intrinsic motivation. This is closely related to the claim that [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md).

**Moderators matter.** The undermining effect is not uniform across reward types. Tangible, expected, task-contingent rewards (given simply for doing the task) show the strongest undermining; verbal praise and unexpected rewards show weaker or no undermining; and rewards contingent on performance quality or achievement of standards can sometimes maintain or even enhance motivation, plausibly because they convey competence information rather than mere control. Rewards for tasks that are already intrinsically uninteresting pose little risk — there is little intrinsic motivation to undermine — and may be the appropriate tool there.

**Boundary conditions and open questions.** The effect is typically demonstrated on free-choice persistence and self-reported interest in novel, playful tasks; how strongly it generalizes to sustained engagement in structured academic settings remains debated. Designers should also weigh the risk against behaviorist uses of reinforcement — see [behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) — since reward systems that successfully shape behavior in the short term may simultaneously erode the interest that sustains behavior once rewards stop.

**Design implications.** Where learners already show interest, prefer informational feedback over controlling incentives; if rewards are necessary, make them unexpected, intangible, or tied to performance standards rather than mere task completion. Where interest is absent, rewards can bootstrap engagement, but designers should plan for fading them and building [autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md)-supportive structures before withdrawal.

**Evidence status.** Five entries are recorded: two competing meta-analyses (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, 128 experiments; Cameron & Pierce 1994, 96 experiments, read from its abstract), the original laboratory experiment (Deci 1971), and two reviews from either side of the debate (Eisenberger & Cameron 1996; Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001). Still missing: studies in authentic classroom settings, and full-text effect sizes for Cameron & Pierce (1994). <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale): This page currently has no curated evidence entries; studies still need to be added before the claim can be graded. -->

*Merged from “Extrinsic Rewards Undermine Intrinsic Motivation” (extrinsic-rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation):* **Scope and mechanism.** The claim is usually explained by the overjustification account: when an external reward supplies a sufficient justification for performing an already-interesting task, learners reattribute their engagement to the reward rather than to their own interest, and voluntary engagement drops once the reward is removed. This sits in tension with the reinforcement tradition of [behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), which predicts rewards should increase the rewarded behavior — the contradiction is sharpest for measures of free-choice persistence and self-reported interest rather than for task performance while rewards are still available.

**Likely moderators.** The effect is generally understood to depend on how the reward is framed and delivered: tangible versus verbal rewards, rewards promised in advance versus given unexpectedly, and rewards contingent merely on task engagement versus on performance quality. Rewards interpreted as informational — signaling competence or recognition — are less likely to undermine interest than rewards experienced as controlling [~M]. The two meta-analyses recorded above document several of these moderators, and they disagree about how much they matter: Deci et al. (1999) find undermining for engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards, while Cameron and Pierce (1994) find no overall effect and a minimal one only for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Both find that verbal praise does not undermine intrinsic motivation.

**Boundary conditions.** For tasks learners find uninteresting to begin with, there is little intrinsic motivation to undermine, and rewards may operate as straightforward incentives [~M]. This connects to the positive counterpart of the claim: supporting learners' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness sustains interest — see [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) and [Self-determination theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md).

**Design implications.** On the evidence recorded above, the practical guidance is conditional: <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale): "Pending sourced evidence, the practical guidance is conditional:" --> avoid promising tangible rewards for activities learners already find interesting; if rewards are used, prefer unexpected, performance- or progress-contingent, informational forms (feedback that signals competence) over expected, engagement-contingent, controlling ones [~M]. Gamification systems built on points and badges should be evaluated for whether they displace the interest they are meant to support — see [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md). Where rewards are unavoidable (grading systems, institutional incentives), framing them as feedback on competence rather than as payment for compliance is the safer design choice [~M].

**Open questions.** Studies in authentic educational settings, rather than laboratory tasks, are still not recorded here.

*Merged from “Rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivating activity can reduce future engagement with it” (overjustification-effect-reduces-intrinsic-motivation):* The 1990s-2000s literature split into a genuine, still-cited disagreement: Cameron and Pierce (and Eisenberger & Cameron) argue the undermining effect is smaller and more conditional than early studies like Deci (1971) suggested, while Deci, Koestner, and Ryan push back that even a bounded, condition-dependent effect is large and common enough in classrooms to warrant real caution. Both camps agree on the two clearest moderators: rewards delivered as a flat rate for participating (rather than piecemeal for output) and rewards attached to tasks with a well-defined, consistently-expected performance standard are least likely to backfire. This directly informs the constraint most learning-design guidance now gives on [Reinforcement Theory](../theories/behaviorism.md) and [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md): extrinsic reinforcement is a legitimate design lever, but layering it onto a task a learner is already intrinsically engaged with is exactly the case where it is most likely to reduce, not increase, future engagement.
