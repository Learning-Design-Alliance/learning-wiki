Evidence¶

Taylor et al. 2017¶

Taylor, R. D., Oberle, E., Durlak, J. A., & Weissberg, R. P. (2017). Promoting positive youth development through school-based social and emotional learning interventions: A meta-analysis of follow-up effects. Child Development, 88(4), 1156–1171. doi:10.1111/cdev.12864

q4 · well-powered meta-analysis · i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract · n=82 studies (97,406 students) · quant-synthesis · r?

This meta-analysis pooled 82 school-based, universal SEL interventions (kindergarten through high school, 38 outside the United States) and examined outcomes assessed after the program had ended, from 6 months to 18 years later. Participants who received SEL programming fared significantly better than controls on social-emotional skills, attitudes, and indicators of well-being at follow-up, and these benefits held regardless of students' race, socioeconomic background, or school location. Post-intervention social-emotional skill gains were the strongest predictor of later well-being, and rarer outcomes such as graduation and safe sexual behavior also showed improvement. Only the abstract (via PubMed/ERIC/Crossref) was accessible — the publisher full text (Wiley/Oxford University Press) is paywalled and not deposited in PMC — so no pooled effect-size statistic could be verified from what was read; the finding of statistically significant, durable benefits is stated directly in the abstract.

Bierman et al. 2026¶

Bierman, K. L., Heinrichs, B. S., Welsh, J. A., Jones, D. E., & Crowley, D. M. (2026). Promoting resilience with social–emotional learning: Young adult follow-up of a preschool randomized-controlled trial. American Psychologist. Advance online publication. doi:10.1037/amp0001765

q3 · randomized controlled trial, follow-up analyses pre-registered (Registry of Efficacy and Effectiveness Studies #16840) · i1 · small-to-moderate effect, d=.21 to .33 · n=356 randomized; 263 (74%) retained at follow-up · causal · r2

This RCT randomly assigned Head Start classrooms to receive an SEL- and language-enriched curriculum (Head Start REDI) or usual practice, enrolling 356 low-income preschoolers (mean age 4.49 years at entry). Outcomes were reassessed when participants were young adults, age 21–22 — roughly 17 years after the preschool intervention ended — with 74% of the original sample retained. The intervention group showed statistically significant advantages on 7 of 11 measures of young adult well-being (including conduct problems, depressive symptoms, loneliness, empathy, emotional support, and life satisfaction), with effect sizes in the small-to-moderate range, and the preschool intervention also buffered the negative impact of adverse childhood experiences on adult social adjustment.

Discussion¶

Persistence is the key question for SEL program evaluation: immediate post-test gains can reflect novelty, demand characteristics, or recent practice, while durable effects require skills to be consolidated, generalized to new settings, and maintained without program support. Follow-up designs (typically 6 months to several years post-intervention) are the appropriate test, and they are much rarer than immediate post-tests, so the evidence base for persistence is thinner than for immediate effects. Two follow-up studies are recorded: a meta-analysis of 82 universal school-based programs with follow-ups from 6 months to 18 years (Taylor et al. 2017, read at abstract level, so its pooled effect sizes are not recorded here) and one preschool RCT followed to age 21–22 (Bierman et al. 2026; 7 of 11 well-being measures favoured the intervention, d = .21 to .33). Both find gains that persist; what is missing is the size of the persisting effect across programs.

Several moderators plausibly govern whether gains persist. Programs that teach concrete, practicable skills and provide opportunities for application across contexts should consolidate better than awareness-raising formats; classroom-teacher-delivered programs embed skills in daily routines, which may support maintenance after the program ends; and dosage and fidelity during implementation plausibly predict durability. Conversely, brief one-off interventions, programs delivered by external providers with no classroom follow-through, and effects measured only on attitudes (rather than practiced skills) are the conditions under which fade-out is most likely. Academic outcomes may show delayed or lagged benefits even when behavioral effects plateau, so follow-up windows matter for interpreting null results.

The persistence question parallels findings from adjacent literatures. Brief social-psychological interventions such as belonging interventions have shown surprisingly durable effects from short treatments, suggesting that persistence does not always require high dosage — but SEL skill-building programs differ in mechanism, since they depend on practiced skills rather than reappraisal. Maintenance plausibly depends on continued opportunities to exercise social-emotional skills in ordinary classroom structures such as collaborative learning, and on motivational conditions like autonomy support that keep students engaged in using those skills. Ongoing assessment for learning can supply the feedback loops that support consolidation.

Open questions: how large effects remain beyond the typical 1–2 year follow-up window (the two entries reach 17–18 years but report direction more than size), whether booster sessions are needed for maintenance, and whether effects persist equally across demographic groups and school contexts.
