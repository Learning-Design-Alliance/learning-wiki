Evidence¶

Gilbert 1999¶

Gilbert, L. S. (1999). Where Is My Brain? Distributed Cognition, Activity Theory, and Cognitive Tools. Proceedings of the AECT National Convention. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED436153

q2 · i? · theoretical · r3

The paper quotes Salomon (1993c) on pedagogic computer tools in its areas-for-additional-research section, including the claim that if nothing significant changes in the classroom save the introduction of a tool, few important changes can be expected. This is a cited theoretical assertion, not a new empirical test.

"No tool is good or bad in and of itself; its effectiveness results from and contributes to the whole configuration of events, activities, contexts, interpersonal processes taking place in the context of which is used."

Gilbert 1999 (2)¶

Gilbert, L. S. (1999). Where Is My Brain? Distributed Cognition, Activity Theory, and Cognitive Tools. Proceedings of the AECT National Convention. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED436153

q2 · i? · theoretical · r2

The paper cites prior Logo research (Jonassen & Reeves, 1996; Pea, Kurland, & Hawkins, 1987) as confirming context-dependent effects, and suggests activity-theory perspectives may help identify the contextual factors behind the widely varying reported results. The underlying studies are not described here.

"Research confirms that tools like Logo can have widely different effects when used in varying contexts."
