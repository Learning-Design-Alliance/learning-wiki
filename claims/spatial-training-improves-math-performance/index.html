Spatial Training Improves Math Performance¶

Claim · All claims

Training learners' spatial skills — through mental rotation, spatial visualization, and similar tasks — transfers to improved performance in mathematics, particularly in areas with strong spatial components such as geometry, measurement, and graph interpretation.

Evidence¶

Hawes et al. 2022¶

Hawes, Z. C. K., Gilligan-Lee, K. A., & Mix, K. S. (2022). Effects of spatial training on mathematics performance: A meta-analysis. Developmental Psychology, 58(1), 112–137. doi:10.1037/dev0001281

q3 · systematic meta-analysis of controlled pre-post training studies · i1 · small-to-moderate effect, Hedges's g=0.28 (SE=0.07) · n=3,765 participants across 29 studies, k=89 effect sizes · quant-synthesis · r2

This meta-analysis pooled 29 controlled pre-post studies (N = 3,765, k = 89 effect sizes) testing whether training spatial skills (e.g., mental rotation, spatial visualization tasks) transfers to mathematics performance. Relative to control conditions, spatial training produced an average transfer effect on math of Hedges's g = .28 (SE = .07), alongside a larger direct effect on spatial thinking itself (g = .49, SE = .09). Effects were moderated by participant age (larger in older participants, ages 3–20), by use of concrete manipulatives (larger than computerized training), and by transfer distance ("near" transfer to spatially-aligned math content produced larger effects than "far" transfer); training dosage, spatial gains, posttest timing, control-group type, experimental design, and publication status did not moderate effects, and publication-bias/selective-reporting checks were nonsignificant.

Katie A. Gilligan-Lee 2022¶

Katie A. Gilligan-Lee, Zachary C. K. Hawes and Kelly S. Mix. (2022). Spatial thinking as the missing piece in mathematics curricula. npj Science of Learning 7:10. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-022-00128-9

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The most common explanation is that spatial and mathematical thinking share working-memory and representational resources: many mathematical concepts (number lines, place value, geometry, data displays) are inherently spatial, so strengthening spatial processing frees capacity or improves the quality of mental models used during math work. This places the claim in the same family as Cognitive Load Theory and Chunking reduces working memory load — the claim is about improving the efficiency of processing, not adding content.

Transfer is selective. Transfer from spatial training to math is strongest when the target math content has an obvious spatial structure (geometry, graphing, estimation of magnitude) and weaker for purely symbolic or procedural content. Practitioners should match the type of spatial training (e.g., mental rotation vs. spatial visualization) to the spatial demands of the target math topic rather than expecting uniform gains. This selectivity is consistent with the general pattern that analogical reasoning improves transfer only when the trained and target tasks share deep structure — surface-level similarity between a rotation task and, say, fraction arithmetic is not enough.

Dosage and durability. Open questions remain about how much training is needed, how long gains persist, and whether embedded approaches — using spatially supportive tools such as block play, manipulatives, or gesture — outperform decontextualized spatial drills. Until evidence entries are added, effect sizes and boundary conditions on this page should be treated as provisional.

Who benefits most. Learners with weak baseline spatial skills have the most room to grow, which makes spatial training a candidate for accommodations and targeted support rather than universal replacement of math instruction. It is best positioned as a supplement within cognitive load reduction strategies, not as a substitute for explicit math teaching.

Design implications. Where spatial training is used, it should be brief, frequent, and paired with explicit connections to the spatial structure of the math content being taught — for example, using mental rotation practice before a geometry unit, or number-line estimation activities alongside magnitude comparison. Embedding spatial language ("above," "rotate," "parallel") into math instruction is a low-cost complement that requires no separate training block.
