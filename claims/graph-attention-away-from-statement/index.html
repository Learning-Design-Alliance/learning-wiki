Evidence¶

Ögren 2017¶

Ögren, M., Nyström, M., & Jarodzka, H. (2017). There's more to the multimedia effect than meets the eye: is seeing pictures believing? Instructional Science, 45, 263–287. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-016-9397-6

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Eye-tracking AOI analysis of 36 participants randomly assigned to solve the problems with graphs (N = 20) or without (N = 16); dwell on the input and statement areas was compared across the two groups, and the 20 students with graphs spent 19.0 ± 10.2% of total time viewing the graph, taken about equally from input and statement areas. The quotient between input and statement did not differ significantly (p > 0.05).

"The proportion of total dwell time on both the input and problem statement was signiﬁcantly shorter when the graph was present, according to a two sample t test (p<0:001)"

Related Claims¶

  • Performance was unrelated to dwell time on the graph but increased with dwell time on the problem statement — related
  • Students allocated roughly equal attention to the graph and the molecular model, both above chance, while attending little to the equations — related
  • Solving problems with graphs increased mental effort, shown by a higher proportion of silence in think-aloud data — related
  • Picture bias: graphs made students more likely to confirm problem statements, irrespective of whether the statements were true — related
  • Frequent eye transitions between graph and problem statement were marginally associated with correct answers — related
  • Contrasting problems show a graph can help on one problem and harm on another: verbal data reveal both more active graph use and more shallow, confident confirmation when graphs were present — related
