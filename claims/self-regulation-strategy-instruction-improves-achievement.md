# Self-regulation strategy instruction improves achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Explicitly teaching learners strategies for goal-setting, planning, monitoring, and reflecting on their own learning raises academic achievement across domains and age groups.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Self-regulation strategy instruction works by making learners active managers of their own cognition: they set goals, select strategies, monitor comprehension and progress, and adjust effort or approach when feedback indicates a mismatch. This aligns with the broader model described in [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). Strategy instruction is closely related to [metacognitive prompts and reflection](../claims/metacognitive-prompts-improve-learning.md), which operationalize the monitoring step.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Effects are generally strongest when strategies are taught explicitly, modeled, and practiced within subject-matter content rather than as generic study-skills sessions detached from coursework. Benefits also depend on learners having enough prior knowledge to monitor meaningfully — novices who lack domain knowledge may misjudge their understanding, and strategy instruction cannot substitute for [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) during initial skill acquisition. Younger learners and low-achieving students typically need more scaffolding and external prompts before self-regulation becomes self-sustaining.

**Open questions.** How durable strategy gains are after instruction ends, and how much training is needed before learners transfer strategies to new domains, remain under-established in the current evidence base for this claim. Because no Evidence entries have yet been added, the overall strength of this claim should be treated as provisional until studies are sourced and coded.
