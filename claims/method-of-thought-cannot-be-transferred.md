# Mead holds that a method of thought cannot be transferred but must be gained through the learner's own abstractions

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q1` argument or single case

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` In the reconstructed course, Mead defines the problem of education as introducing a method of thought — problem, hypothesis, and testing — while insisting the method cannot simply be transferred and that abstractions must be made by the child himself. [→ Biesta 1997](#biesta-1997)

## Evidence

### Biesta 1997

Biesta, Gert J. J. (1997). George Herbert Mead's Lectures on Philosophy of Education at the University of Chicago (1910-1911). Paper presented at the Annual Meeting of the American Educational Research Association, Chicago, IL. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED422199

`q1 · i?`

Reconstruction from lecture XXXIII of Mead's Course 50, in the article's section on the social origin of thought. The article reports Mead's three-stage account of thinking (problem, hypothesis, testing) and his insistence that "For a child to make use of abstraction they must have been made by himself", with training arising out of problems within the child. This is a philosophical position from the lectures, not an empirical finding.

> "For a child to make use of abstraction they must have been made by himself."
