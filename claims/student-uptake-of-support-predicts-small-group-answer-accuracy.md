# Students' application of teacher support predicts accurate answers in small-group work

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r1` · `q3` · n=7 eighth-grade Dutch social studies teachers, 28 students (7 groups of 4), 35 lessons, 13,316 coded interaction turns

A mediation analysis of 35 secondary lessons found that students who applied (rather than ignored or merely copied) a teacher's scaffolding went on to give significantly more accurate answers in subsequent small-group work, but how well-calibrated (contingent) the teacher's support was did not itself predict whether students applied it; an exploratory qualitative analysis suggested untimely fading (withdrawing before students' understanding was checked or shown) as a possible explanation.

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Student uptake (applying rather than ignoring or copying teacher support) significantly predicted the accuracy of students' subsequent small-group answers (b=0.25, SE=0.11, p=.017, odds ratio=1.29), in a logistic multilevel mediation model. [→ van de Pol et al. 2019](#van-de-pol-et-al-2019)

`q3 i?` The contingency (calibration to student understanding) of teachers' support did not significantly predict student uptake (b=-2.97, SE=5.19, p=.567), and the hypothesized indirect path from contingency through uptake to accuracy was not significant (b=-0.75, SE=1.43, p=.599) — contingency alone, without accounting for how support ended, was not sufficient to produce uptake in this sample. [→ van de Pol et al. 2019](#van-de-pol-et-al-2019)

`q3 i?` Qualitative analysis of cases where contingent support was ignored found that in 5 of 7 such cases the teacher seemed to fade support too soon (indicated by no check of students' understanding and/or no demonstration of understanding before the teacher left), and students subsequently often showed frustration or a lack of understanding; in none of the 7 cases where contingent support was applied was support faded prematurely, as indicated by the teacher checking and students demonstrating understanding before the teacher left and/or students being able to proceed afterwards. The authors offer untimely fading as a possible explanation, not a tested cause. [→ van de Pol et al. 2019](#van-de-pol-et-al-2019)

`q3 i?` Whether teacher support was relevant to the task students were currently working on strongly predicted uptake (b=6.37, SE=1.94, p=.001), independent of contingency. [→ van de Pol et al. 2019](#van-de-pol-et-al-2019)

`q3 · peer-reviewed observational study coded from audio-recorded lesson transcripts with logistic multilevel mediation analysis; high inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's α .73-.87 across coded variables); not experimental — teacher behavior was observed, not manipulated` · `i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may` · `n=7 eighth-grade Dutch social studies teachers, 28 students (7 groups of 4), 35 lessons, 13,316 coded interaction turns` · `associational · r1`

Audio-recorded and transcribed interactions from 35 lessons (seven teachers, each with one target group of four students) were coded for teacher control level, student understanding, support contingency, student uptake (ignore/copy/apply), and subsequent small-group answer accuracy. A logistic multilevel mediation model tested whether contingent support's effect on accuracy was mediated by uptake; the direct uptake-accuracy link held, but the contingency-uptake link did not. Qualitative analysis of the specific cases driving this null result suggested untimely fading in [Scaffolding and Fading](../principles/scaffolding-and-fading.md) — the teacher withdrawing before students' understanding was checked or demonstrated — as a possible explanation for why contingent support was sometimes ignored; it was not observed where contingent support was applied.

## Discussion
This study's honest null result (contingency did not predict uptake) is as important as its positive one: it directly complicates a common assumption in the scaffolding literature that well-calibrated support is close to sufficient for effectiveness, showing instead that *how support ends* matters as much as how well it was pitched. The qualitative timely-fading mechanism is drawn from a small number of illustrative cases (7 ignored, 7 applied) within the larger dataset and should be read as a well-grounded explanatory account rather than a separately quantified effect. The study is observational, not an experiment on fading timing, so a causal claim that teaching fading-checks would improve uptake is plausible but untested here.
