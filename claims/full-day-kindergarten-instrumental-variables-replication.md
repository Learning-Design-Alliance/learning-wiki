# Instrumental variables estimates using state full-day policy broadly replicate the baseline pattern, though the instrument's validity is uncertain

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` State full-day policy is a strong positive predictor of full-day attendance (significant at the 1 percent level), and IV second-stage estimates in kindergarten differ little from OLS/probit estimates. [→ Jill S. Cannon 2006](#jill-s-cannon-2006)
`q3 i?` The instrument's exogeneity is doubtful because state policies may reflect constituent desires, so the IV results should be viewed with caution. [→ Jill S. Cannon 2006 (2)](#jill-s-cannon-2006-2)

## Evidence

### Jill S. Cannon 2006

Jill S. Cannon, Alison Jacknowitz, Gary Painter. (2006). Is Full Better than Half? Examining the Longitudinal Effects of Full-day Kindergarten Attendance. Journal of Policy Analysis and Management, Vol. 25, No. 2. https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.20174

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

First-stage 2SLS participation equation on the ECLS-K sample using state full-day policy as the instrument (Table 2, spring kindergarten math scores sample). The article reports the instrument was always significant at the 1 percent level.

> "The coefficient on the instrument was a strong positive predictor of full-day attendance and was always significant at the 1 percent level."

### Jill S. Cannon 2006 (2)

Jill S. Cannon, Alison Jacknowitz, Gary Painter. (2006). Is Full Better than Half? Examining the Longitudinal Effects of Full-day Kindergarten Attendance. Journal of Policy Analysis and Management, Vol. 25, No. 2. https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.20174

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

The article's assessment of instrument validity: states without full-day requirements spent significantly more on K-12 expenditures in 1998, but the policy may be endogenous because parents who prefer full-day kindergarten may lobby for it.

> "Given the mixed results from these two tests, the instrumental variables results should be viewed with caution."
