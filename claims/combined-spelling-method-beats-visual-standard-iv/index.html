Evidence¶

Winch 1913¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(10), 579–592. doi:10.1037/h0076021

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Controlled experiment with 54 Standard IV boys in a London school; each boy learned one 30-word list by a combined method with directed attention and the other by a silent visual method. The author reports "the difference between the means, namely, 2.8" as eight times its probable error; no standardized effect size is printed.

"The difference between the means, namely, 2.8, is 8 times its 'probable error,' and is therefore a highly 'significant' differ ence."

Winch 1913 (2)¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(10), 579–592. doi:10.1037/h0076021

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Preliminary dictation test in the same Standard IV experiment, used to check that the two 30-word lists were of similar difficulty before learning; the author reports the correlation between lists as +.81, a probable error of the difference of .20, and "no 'significant' difference between the two lists before learning." A non-significant difference does not prove exact equality. The author also cautions that the later advantage of Set A may come from directed attention rather than from the combination of motor, auditory and visual memories, and Set A's lessons fell more often in the morning (judged by the author a very slight advantage).

"The average number of words correctly spelt in Set A before learning was 4.3, for Set B it was 4.4."

Related Claims¶

  • A combined visual-motor-articulatory-auditory spelling method with teacher-directed attention produces better spelling than a predominantly visual silent method in young low-ability boys — related
  • Across Standards II and IV (ages 8 to 11) in the same school, the spelling method with pronounced auditory, articulatory, and visual factors plus directed attention proved definitely superior to a more purely visual method with less directed attention — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Both directed multisensory and silent visual spelling methods produced large improvement from baseline after 40 minutes of practice spread over four days — related
  • The two word lists were confirmed to be of equal difficulty before practice, with a correlation of +.93 between performance on the two lists — related
  • The superiority of the combined directed method held across all ability levels, with the advantage present in every section of a grouped correlation table — related
  • In-class flash-card visual study with writing from memory raised Standard I dictation averages to 20.8, with every boy spelling some words correctly, though the method was time-costly and complicated — related
  • Twelve weeks of copy-and-home-study spelling practice with next-day dictation produced no improvement in Standard I boys' ability to acquire new spellings — related
  • A head-to-head teaching contest between a primarily visual method and a combined method produced nearly identical averages (22.9 vs 23.0) and could not decide between the methods because of uncontrolled confounds — reports the opposite
  • Reviewer credits the method with directness, concentration from flash-cards, valuable action responses, and stimulating effect — related
  • The journal prints a correction to the probable-error formula in W. H. Winch's article on learning to spell — related
