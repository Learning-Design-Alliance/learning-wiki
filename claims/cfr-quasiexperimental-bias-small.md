## Evidence

### Koedel 2015

Koedel, C., Mihaly, K., & Rockoff, J. E. (2015). Value-Added Modeling: A Review. Preprint, forthcoming at Economics of Education Review. https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Value-Added+Modeling+A+Review+Koedel

`q3 · i?` · `review · r3`

Observational analysis of a 21-year administrative panel merged with IRS tax data. The review attributes to Chetty, Friedman and Rockoff (2014a) the finding that bias from unobserved parental characteristics is "0.2 percent", because standard controls capture much of the variation and residual variation is uncorrelated with teacher value-added.

> "They estimate that the magnitude of the bias in value-added estimates in typical circumstances when these parental characteristics are unobserved is 0.2 percent, with an upper-bound at the edge of the 95-percent confidence interval of 0.25 percent."

### Koedel 2015 (2)

Koedel, C., Mihaly, K., & Rockoff, J. E. (2015). Value-Added Modeling: A Review. Preprint, forthcoming at Economics of Education Review. https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Value-Added+Modeling+A+Review+Koedel

`q3 · i?` · `review · r3`

Quasi-experimental analysis comparing achievement across cohorts passing through the same school-by-grade cells under staffing changes. The review reports forecasting bias of "2.6 percent and not statistically significant", with the 95-percent upper bound under 10 percent; similar null results are reported by Bacher-Hicks, Kane and Staiger (2014) in Los Angeles.

> "Using their second method, Chetty, Friedman and Rockoff (2014a) estimate that the forecasting bias from using their observational value-added measures to predict student achievement is 2.6 percent and not statistically significant (the upper bound of the 95-percent confidence interval on the bias is less than 10 percent)."
