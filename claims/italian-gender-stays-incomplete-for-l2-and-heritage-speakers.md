# Italian grammatical gender stays incompletely mastered by L2 learners and heritage speakers alike, and a gendered first language helps only conditionally.

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q2` quasi-experiment · n=not reported here

Having gender in your first language does not transfer as a rule. The advantage it confers is
specific to particular item configurations, and it does not appear in every task.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` Across every configuration tested, gender knowledge on third-person singular and plural object clitics was incomplete for both L2 learners and heritage speakers. [→ Bäck Romano et al. 2025](#back-romano-et-al-2025)

`q2 i?` Typological proximity helped only conditionally: Spanish–Italian groups were advantaged where the gender-assigning nouns were non-cognate and gender-incongruent, Swedish–Italian groups where items were gender-incongruent cognates. [→ Bäck Romano et al. 2025](#back-romano-et-al-2025)

`q2 i?` The advantages were moderated by intrinsic differences between heritage and L2 speakers, and appeared in production only. [→ Bäck Romano et al. 2025](#back-romano-et-al-2025)

## Evidence

### Bäck Romano et al. 2025

Bäck Romano, S., Guijarro-Fuentes, P., Calpe-Álvarez, V., & Rivera, J. (2025). Gender in L2 and heritage Italian by Swedish and Spanish dominant speakers. *Italiano LinguaDue, 17*(2), 27–48. [doi:10.54103/2037-3597/30422](https://doi.org/10.54103/2037-3597/30422)

`q2 · between-groups comparison across two language pairings and two speaker types` · `i? · direction of advantage reported, error percentages not given` · `n=not reported here`

Spanish–Italian and Swedish–Italian bilinguals — L2 learners and heritage speakers in each pairing — were compared on gender assignment to third-person singular and plural object clitics, crossing cognate status against gender congruency. The paper reports which configurations favoured which group and that the effects were production-only; it does not report error rates, so the study establishes that the difficulty persists rather than how large it is.

## Discussion

**Gender does not generalise from a rule, even for learners whose first language has gender.** It
behaves like item knowledge — a fact about a word rather than a procedure applied to it — which
argues for holding it per lexeme rather than as one grammar objective averaging hundreds of
independent facts.

**Heritage and L2 speakers are different populations, not points on one scale.** A heritage speaker
may assign gender correctly by ear while unable to state a rule, and an L2 learner the reverse. A
single mastery number over both channels describes neither. The wiki's existing treatment of
heritage language is about its value rather than its structure — see
[Preserving a bilingual student's heritage language supports rather than hinders English
acquisition](heritage-language-preservation-supports-english-acquisition.md) [+M] — and this claim
is the complement: same population, a place where the two channels come apart.

**Same shape as the phonology finding, one level up.** In
[A learner's first language predicts L2 phoneme perception more strongly than their proficiency
level does](l1-predicts-l2-phoneme-perception-more-than-proficiency.md) [+S] the L1 helps when it
encodes the relevant distinction. Here it helps only in some item configurations, which is a weaker
and more awkward result to design around: you cannot read the advantage off the learner's L1 alone.

**Limits.** Object clitics specifically, two language pairings, production-only effects, no error
percentages reported.
