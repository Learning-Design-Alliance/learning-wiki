# Minority teachers' lower observation scores are largely attributable to school characteristics, and no race/ethnicity differences appear on value-added scores

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · associational `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` African American, Latino, and other minority teachers average lower observation scores than white teachers, but for African American teachers most of this difference appears due to the relationship between observation scores and school characteristics such as school-level poverty. [→ Jennie Y. Jiang 2016](#jennie-y-jiang-2016)

## Evidence

### Jennie Y. Jiang 2016

Jennie Y. Jiang, Susan E. Sporte. (2016). Teachers with lowest evaluation scores are overrepresented in high-poverty schools. University of Chicago Consortium on School Research. http://consortium.uchicago.edu/page/teacher-evaluation

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

The REACH data analysis finds average observation-score differences by race/ethnicity. For African American teachers, "who are overrepresented in the highest-poverty schools, most of this difference seems to be due to the relationship between observation scores and school characteristics."

> "on average, African American, Latino, and other minority (i.e. Asian, Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Native American, and multi-racial), teachers’ observation scores are lower than white teachers’ observation scores"

### Jennie Y. Jiang 2016 (2)

Jennie Y. Jiang, Susan E. Sporte. (2016). Teachers with lowest evaluation scores are overrepresented in high-poverty schools. University of Chicago Consortium on School Research. http://consortium.uchicago.edu/page/teacher-evaluation

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Value-added scores showed no significant differences by teacher race/ethnicity on either reading or math. Equivalence beyond the non-significant tests was not reported; no effect size printed.

> "There were no significant differences by teacher race/ethnicity on either reading or math value-added scores."
