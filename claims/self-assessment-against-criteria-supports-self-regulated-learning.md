# Self Assessment Against Criteria Supports Self Regulated Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i1` small · n=2,305 students across 19 studies

When learners evaluate their own work against explicit criteria (rubrics, checklists, exemplars), they develop the monitoring and judgment skills that underpin self-regulated learning. The claim concerns self assessment that is structured by external quality standards — not unguided self-rating, which tends to be inaccurate and self-serving.

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Panadero et al. 2017

Panadero, E., Jonsson, A., & Botella, J. (2017). Effects of self-assessment on self-regulated learning and self-efficacy: Four meta-analyses. *Educational Research Review, 22*, 74–98. [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2017.08.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.08.004)

`q4 · random-effects meta-analysis (four sub-analyses)` · `i1 · small effect on Learning SRL, d=0.23; medium/non-significant on the other two SRL measures; large on self-efficacy, d=0.73` · `n=2,305 students across 19 studies`

Four random-effects meta-analyses (Hedges & Olkin method) pooled 19 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of self-assessment interventions in K-12 and higher-education classrooms. For the "Learning SRL" outcome — measures of strategy use such as planning, monitoring and self-regulatory strategy deployment — 12 effect sizes from 369 students produced a combined effect of d=0.23, 95% CI [0.08, 0.39], a small but statistically significant positive effect. A second meta-analysis on "Negative SRL" (self-regulatory behaviors detrimental to learning, e.g. procrastination) found d=−0.65 (k=6, n=347; CI crossed zero), meaning self-assessment reduced these behaviors; a third on SRL measured qualitatively found d=0.43 (k=3, n=338; CI crossed zero, underpowered). A separate meta-analysis of 27 effect sizes on self-efficacy found a larger effect, d=0.73, 95% CI [0.39, 1.08], moderated by gender (larger for girls) and by whether the self-assessment intervention included a monitoring component.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Self-regulated learning theory describes a cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection (Zimmerman, 2002; see [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)). Self assessment against criteria operationalizes the self-reflection phase: criteria give learners a standard to compare their performance against, which makes self-monitoring accurate rather than impressionistic [+M]. Without explicit criteria, learners tend to overestimate their competence, and the resulting miscalibration undermines planning and effort allocation [~M]. Andrade (2010) frames self assessment as the mechanism by which students become "the definitive source of formative assessment" — internalizing the evaluative criteria that teachers otherwise apply for them. Rubric-based self assessment has been associated with improved learning and with more accurate self-regulatory judgments, particularly when students use the criteria before and during drafting rather than only after completion [+M] (Andrade & Valtcheva, 2009; Panadero & Alonso-Tapia, 2013). This positions self assessment as a form of [assessment for learning](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) rather than merely a grading convenience.

**Boundary conditions.** The quality of the criteria matters. Vague or holistic criteria invite leniency bias and self-serving judgments [~M]; specific, task-relevant criteria (e.g., analytic rubrics, quality checklists, comparison to annotated exemplars) support more accurate self-evaluation [+M]. Accuracy also improves when learners have prior exposure to exemplars of the target quality, and when self assessment is practiced repeatedly rather than used once [+M]. There is a known tension with feedback: if self assessment replaces external feedback entirely, learners may lack the information needed to close the gap between their judgment and the standard — self assessment works best alongside, not instead of, teacher or peer feedback [~M] (see [Action-oriented feedback](../strategies/action-oriented-feedback.md)). A further boundary condition is that checklists used as evaluation tools can fail in certain online contexts (see [Checklist evaluation ineffective online](checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md)) [-M], so the format of the criteria is not neutral. Novices and younger learners are also more prone to leniency and miscalibration, so structured criteria and modeling of the self-assessment process matter most where self-regulation is weakest [~M].

**Design implications.** To realize the self-regulatory benefit, criteria should be introduced early enough to guide forethought — not only consulted after work is complete — and the self-assessment step should ask learners to identify evidence in their own work against each criterion, not merely assign a score. Co-constructing criteria with students is a promising but under-documented variant. Where self assessment is used, pair it with a mechanism for acting on the judgment (revision, goal-setting) so that accurate monitoring translates into regulated effort.

**Open questions.** Most of the literature base for this claim still needs to be added (see Evidence). Key moderators to document include learner age and domain expertise, whether criteria are co-constructed with learners, and whether effects on self-regulation transfer beyond the assessed task.
