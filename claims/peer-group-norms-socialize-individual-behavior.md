# Peer group descriptive norms and social cohesion socialize individual children's prosocial and aggressive behavior over time

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · review `r2`–`r3` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Individuals tended to become more prosocial over time when the classroom descriptive norm for prosocial behavior was initially high, and a similar mechanism was found for aggressive behavior. [→ Krijnen 2024](#krijnen-2024)
`q2 i?` High density and low centrality benefit individual citizenship: aggressive boys became less accepted over time in high-density or low-centrality classrooms. [→ Krijnen 2024 (2)](#krijnen-2024-2)

## Evidence

### Krijnen 2024

Krijnen, M. A., Wansink, B. G. J., van den Berg, Y. H. M., van Tartwijk, J., & Mainhard, T. (2024). Citizenship in the Elementary Classroom Through the Lens of Peer Relations. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-024-09870-5

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review reports, citing a longitudinal study among adolescents (Laninga-Wijnen et al., 2018), that "individuals tended to become more prosocial over time when the descriptive norm for prosocial behavior was initially high," with a similar mechanism found for aggression in several cited studies.

> "individuals tended to become more prosocial over time when the descriptive norm for prosocial behavior was initially high (Laninga-Wijnen et al., 2018)"

### Krijnen 2024 (2)

Krijnen, M. A., Wansink, B. G. J., van den Berg, Y. H. M., van Tartwijk, J., & Mainhard, T. (2024). Citizenship in the Elementary Classroom Through the Lens of Peer Relations. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-024-09870-5

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports, citing Ahn and Rodkin (2014), that "in high-density or low-centrality classrooms, they became less accepted over time," suggesting social cohesion shapes peaceful behavior; Zwaan et al. (2013) found well-included boys in high-centrality classrooms showed less aggression.

> "In low-density or high-centrality classrooms, aggressive boys were more accepted, whereas in high-density or low-centrality classrooms, they became less accepted over time (Ahn & Rodkin, 2014)."
