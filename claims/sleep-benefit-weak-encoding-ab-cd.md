# With weak encoding and no retroactive interference (A-B, C-D), sleep enhanced retention of both lists to a similar extent; with intense encoding, no sleep benefit appeared for either list

## Evidence

### Drosopoulos 2007

Drosopoulos, S., Schulze, C., Fischer, S., & Born, J. (2007). Sleep's Function in the Spontaneous Recovery and Consolidation of Memories. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.136.2.169

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r2`

Experiment 2 (50 participants, A-B, C-D paradigm, 60% learning criterion for weak encoding): sleep group retained 68.50%/75.50% versus wake 59.50%/59.00% for A-B/C-D; "retention was better in the sleep group than in the wake group", ηp2 = .29, with no list difference.

> "For weakly encoded word pairs, retention was better in the sleep group than in the wake group, F(1, 18) ⫽ 7.33, p ⫽ .014, ␩p2 ⫽ .29, for main effect for group. The magnitude of this improving effect of sleep on memory after weak encoding was the same in both lists"

### Drosopoulos 2007 (2)

Drosopoulos, S., Schulze, C., Fischer, S., & Born, J. (2007). Sleep's Function in the Spontaneous Recovery and Consolidation of Memories. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.136.2.169

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Experiment 2 intense-encoding condition (90% criterion): retention was high in both sleep (84.00%/85.00%) and wake (88.50%/83.50%) groups; "no effect reached significance" for sleep versus wake, indicating no sleep benefit for strongly encoded associations.

> "For intense encoding, no effect reached significance, F(1, 18) ⬍ 1, for sleep versus wake main effect and for both the list main effect and Group ⫻ List interaction"
