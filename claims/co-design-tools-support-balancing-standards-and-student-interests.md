# Co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data, and launch rehearsal support balancing standards alignment with student interests

Across a two-year co-design partnership, teachers and researchers reported that specific tools — unpacking standards, writing target explanations, collecting disaggregated student-interest data, and rehearsing unit launches with peers — each directly supported balancing the dual goals of standards coverage and student/community interest, while also producing new critical perspectives on how the process itself should change.

## Subclaims
`q2 i2` Unpacking standards and writing target student explanations were most directly credited by participants with clarifying science learning goals; one teacher reported: "Unpacking was when I learned some really important chemistry ideas... it forced me to realize when I was just using a word as a stand-in for an idea I hadn't really 'figured out.'" [→ Penuel et al. 2022](#penuel-et-al-2022)

`q2 i2` Disaggregated student-interest survey data revealed that question wording could shift which demographic groups rated a phenomenon highly (an active-voice phrasing of a nuclear-weapons question was rated highly by Latinx, African American, and Native American students, while a passive-voice version of the same underlying question ranked in the middle), leading the team to conclude that surveys alone were insufficient and should be supplemented with focus groups or interviews. [→ Penuel et al. 2022](#penuel-et-al-2022)

`q2 i2` Rehearsing a unit's launch with co-designers from other disciplines and grade levels "in student hat" was reported as most useful for identifying whether a phenomenon actually generated standards-aligned questions or led into unproductive "rabbit holes"; one participant noted that testing chemistry material with biology teachers "makes it a little more authentic when the audience is in student hat." [→ Penuel et al. 2022](#penuel-et-al-2022)

`q2 i1` The team's own demographic homogeneity (25 of 27 participants identified as white) was identified by the team itself as a constraint on which phenomena could be imagined as compelling to students of color, since the interest survey could only test phenomena the team had already thought to propose. [→ Penuel et al. 2022](#penuel-et-al-2022)

Reflective surveys (59% response rate on process feedback; 89% response rate on the learning survey), design artifacts, and structured team reflections were analyzed using an iterative, participatory evaluation approach in which the design team itself helped interpret and validate the findings.

## Discussion
This is a qualitative case study of a single, well-resourced co-design partnership; the tools' effectiveness is documented through participant self-report and design artifacts, not an outcome measure comparing units produced with and without these tools. The team's own acknowledged demographic homogeneity is a meaningful limitation for a strategy explicitly aimed at balancing standards with diverse student interests — the strategy surfaced this gap but did not resolve it within the study period. Findings on specific tool effectiveness draw on retrospective participant reflection, which may be shaped by social-desirability or recency effects rather than an independent measure of what actually drove design decisions.
