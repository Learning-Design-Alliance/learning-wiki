## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Qualitative analysis of protocols suggested that problem-solving routines differed between the natural and formal situations: children chained successive additions on quantities in the street but attempted school-prescribed routines on paper in the formal test. [→ Carraher 1985](#carraher-1985)

## Evidence

### Carraher 1985

Carraher, T. N., Carraher, D. W., & Schliemann, A. D. (1985). Mathematics in the streets and in schools. British Journal of Developmental Psychology, 3, 21-29. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-835x.1985.tb00951.x

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Qualitative analysis of taped and observed protocols from the informal and formal tests, illustrated with five worked examples (e.g. a 12-year-old solving 35 x 10 by repeated chunked addition of 105s, then failing 35 x 4 with the written algorithm). The authors report strategies, not tested effects.

> "In the informal test, children rely upon mental calculations which are closely linked to the quantities that are being dealt with. The preferred strategy for multiplication problems seems to consist in chaining successive additions."

### Carraher 1985 (2)

Carraher, T. N., Carraher, D. W., & Schliemann, A. D. (1985). Mathematics in the streets and in schools. British Journal of Developmental Psychology, 3, 21-29. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-835x.1985.tb00951.x

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Protocol analysis of the formal-test examples: for instance, a child computing 420 + 80 as 130 by applying a carry step from a multiplication routine. The authors describe error sources and absent plausibility checking from the printed examples rather than from tested measures.

> "Mistakes often occur as a result of confusing addition routines with multiplication routines, as is clearly the case in examples (I) and (5). Moreover, in all the cases, there is no evidence, once the numbers are written down, that the children try to relate the obtained results to the problem at hand in order to assess the adequacy of their answers."
