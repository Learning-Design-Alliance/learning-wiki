# Deaf children produce shorter conventional number strings in abstract counting than hearing children of the same school level, with a roughly 2-year delay

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r1` · `q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Hearing children's mean abstract counting (strict and lenient scores) was higher than deaf children's, and the deaf group's highest number did not increase linearly with age. [→ Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem 2002](#jacqueline-leybaert-and-marie-noelle-van-cutsem-2002)

## Evidence

### Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem 2002

Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem. (2002). Counting in Sign Language. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1006/jecp.2002.2660

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Group comparison ANOVAs on abstract counting in a study of 21 deaf and 28 hearing children matched by school year. Hearing children outperformed deaf children on both strict and lenient scores, F(1, 43) = 25.20 and F(1, 43) = 24.18, both p < .001; no effect size is printed.

> "revealed significant effects of hearing status on the strict score, F(1, 43) 5 25.20; p , .001, as well as on the lenient score, F(1, 43) 5 24.18; p , .001. Hearing children's mean abstract counting was higher than that of deaf children, whatever the score."
