Explicit contextualization instruction improves procedural aspects of historical writing without improving contextualization scores themselves¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r2 · q3 · i2 medium · n=140 undergraduate L2 English learners (B2 CEFR level) at a university in Istanbul; experimental n=60, control n=80

In a quasi-experiment with 140 university L2 history students, explicit instruction in historical contextualization did not raise contextualization rubric scores relative to a content-matched active control, but it unexpectedly improved claim-writing and shifted how (not how much) students used and placed contextualization in their essays.

Evidence¶

Sendur et al. 2021¶

Sendur, K. A., van Drie, J., & van Boxtel, C. (2021). Historical contextualization in students' writing. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 30(4-5), 797-836. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2021.1939029

q3 · quasi-experimental pre/post design with an active, content-matched control group (13 intact classes, 11 instructors), non-parametric hypothesis testing throughout; not randomized at the student level, not pre-registered · i2-i3 · large, well-quantified overall improvement in both conditions (r=-.61); the targeted contextualization-score comparison is a clear, precisely reported null (r=.09); the claim-writing transfer effect is small-to-medium (r=.26) but well quantified; the connection-frequency and placement findings are descriptive only · n=140 undergraduate L2 English learners (B2 CEFR level) at a university in Istanbul; experimental n=60, control n=80 · causal · r2

140 undergraduate L2 English learners across 13 intact classes received a 28-hour CLIL history course; 5 classes (n=60) received Explicit Instruction in Historical Contextualization targeting the Historical Reasoning Framework's contextualization aspect specifically, while 8 classes (n=80) received the same CLIL course without that focus. Pre/post document-based-question essays were scored on a 5-point rubric across five reasoning aspects (Cohen's kappa .64-1.0 pretest, .66-.82 posttest). The central, well-powered comparison — contextualization rubric score — is a clean null result; the paper's positive findings are either an unplanned transfer effect (claim quality) or descriptive qualitative patterns (connection frequency, placement) without inferential statistics or reported inter-rater reliability for those specific codes.

Discussion¶

This is a genuine, informative null result on the paper's primary target and should not be overstated into a general endorsement of the instructional pattern. The unexpected claim-writing transfer effect and the descriptive placement/connection patterns are plausible but weaker-tier evidence (q1-q2, no inferential statistics for the qualitative codes) that the intervention changed how students write even when the primary rubric score didn't move — a genuine dissociation between procedural/discourse-level change and holistic rubric scoring that is itself worth documenting, but which the study cannot explain mechanistically. Findings are specific to L2 learners at B2 English proficiency; generalization to L1 history students is untested.
