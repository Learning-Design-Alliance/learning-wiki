Elaborative Interrogation Improves Learning¶

Claim · All claims

Elaborative interrogation — prompting learners to answer "why is this true?" or "why does this make sense?" about factual statements — supports learning by connecting new material to prior knowledge.

Dunlosky et al. 2013¶

Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. doi:10.1177/1529100612453266

q3 · systematic narrative review · i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract · n=10 learning techniques reviewed · review · r?

A monograph reviewing ten learning techniques and judging how far each one's benefits generalise across learning conditions, student characteristics, materials and criterion tasks. Elaborative interrogation received a moderate utility rating, alongside self-explanation and interleaved practice. The authors held it back from a high rating because the evidence for its efficacy is limited, and in particular because it had not been adequately evaluated in educational contexts. Only the abstract was read, so the review's per-study findings and moderators are not reported here.

Seifert 1993¶

Seifert, T. L. (1993). Effects of elaborative interrogation with prose passages. Journal of Educational Psychology, 85(4), 642–651. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.85.4.642

q3 · peer-reviewed randomised experiment · i? · no effect size in the abstract · n=114 students · causal · r?

114 students in grades 6 and 7 were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: underline only, underline with elaboration, generate elaboration, and elaboration with a study sheet. Generating an elaboration led to better memory for main ideas than underlining alone, and the underline-with-elaboration group did better on an inference problem. The characteristics of students' elaborations did not predict whether they learned the target fact. Only the abstract was read.

Kühl and Bertrams 2019¶

Kühl, T., & Bertrams, A. (2019). Is Learning With Elaborative Interrogation Less Desirable When Learners Are Depleted? Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 707. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00707

q3 · peer-reviewed randomised experiment, 2 × 2 between-subjects · i? · null result, no effect size reported for learning condition: no effect on retention, F(1, 91) = 0.44, p = 0.51, or on transfer, F(1, 91) = 0.20, p = 0.66 · n=97 participants · causal · r2

97 participants were randomly assigned by ego depletion (yes or no) and learning condition. In the elaborative interrogation condition some segments of a text about how planes fly were removed and replaced with prompts asking for that information. The control group read the complete text with no prompts. ANCOVAs found no effect of learning condition on retention or transfer. Learners given prompts reported more mental effort and spent far longer with the material (about 360–390 s against 118–130 s). This operationalisation (generating missing text) differs from the classic "why is this true?" prompt, so it qualifies the claim rather than refuting it. The full text was read.

Discussion¶

Elaborative interrogation works through elaborative processing: generating an answer to a "why" question forces the learner to search prior knowledge for a causal or explanatory link, which integrates the new fact into existing schemas. This mechanism places it in the same family as self-explanation and analogical reasoning, and it presupposes that relevant prior knowledge is available — see Activation improves learning.

Expected moderators, to be confirmed once evidence entries are added: the technique appears most effective for learners with sufficient domain knowledge to generate plausible answers, and for fact-dense material where individual statements can be interrogated one at a time. Learners with sparse prior knowledge may produce weak or incorrect explanations, and the technique is less suited to procedural skills or complex tasks where the unit of analysis is a whole procedure rather than an isolated fact. Generating explanations also consumes working-memory resources, so it should be considered alongside cognitive load theory and chunking when material is high in element interactivity.

Boundary conditions for design practice: prompts should be attached to specific, discrete factual claims rather than to entire passages, since a vague "why does this matter?" prompt invites generic rather than integrative responses. Where learners cannot generate a plausible answer, providing the explanatory link after the prompt (a feedback-supported variant) is likely safer than leaving the learner with an unresolved or misconceived explanation — a design tension shared with advance organizers improve learning, where the organizer must actually connect to what the learner knows.

A further design consideration is response quality: because learners generate their own answers, incorrect or superficial explanations can be encoded as confidently as correct ones. Pairing interrogation prompts with verification (checking the answer against the text or receiving corrective feedback) mitigates this risk, and distinguishes productive elaborative interrogation from unguided "think about why" instructions that learners may satisfy with low-effort responses.

Open questions for future enrichment: effect sizes relative to other learning techniques, durability of gains over delay, and whether prompted interrogation transfers to spontaneous questioning during self-study. Until evidence entries are added, this page should be treated as a mechanism-level claim awaiting empirical grounding.
