## Evidence

### Arthur 1998

Arthur, W., Jr., Bennett, W., Jr., Stanush, P. L., & McNelly, T. L. (1998). Factors That Influence Skill Decay and Retention: A Quantitative Review and Analysis. Human Performance, 11(1), 57-101. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327043hup1101_3

`q3 · i?` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Meta-analytic moderator analysis (Table 4) comparing speed-criterion and accuracy-criterion task subsets. The review attributes the pattern to accuracy being a deficient criterion that asymptotes rapidly, potentially giving a false impression of mastery.

> "The results of the moderator analysis presented in Table 4 indicate that across all retention intervals, the amount of skill decay for accuracy tasks was over three times higher than that of speed tasks (i.e., 8-1.00 and -0.32, respectively)."

### Tatel 2025

Tatel, C. E., & Ackerman, P. L. (2025). Procedural Skill Retention and Decay: A Meta-Analytic Review. Psychological Bulletin. https://dx.doi.org/10.1037/bul0000481

`q2 · i1` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Secondhand moderation result from Arthur et al. (1998) narrated by this review: "lower levels of decay for speed-based tasks" than accuracy-based tasks, with printed effect sizes d = -0.33 (speed) and d = -1.02 (accuracy), per the article's footnote identifying δ as a meta-analytic Cohen's d. The review cautions speed and accuracy may not be directly comparable.

> "Arthur et al. (1998) found lower levels of decay for speed-based tasks (ð = -0.33) than for accuracy-based tasks (ð = -1.02)."
