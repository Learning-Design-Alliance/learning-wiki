## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In Experiment 1, enjoyment positively predicted comprehension (β = 0.09, p = 0.001) with reading ability controlled (r = 0.36 with comprehension). [→ Amrita Bains 2023](#amrita-bains-2023)

## Evidence

### Amrita Bains 2023

Amrita Bains, Carina Spaulding, Jessie Ricketts and Saloni Krishnan. (2023). Using a willingness to wait design to assess how readers value text. npj Science of Learning 8:17. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-023-00160-3

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Mixed logistic regression on comprehension of two multiple-choice questions per synopsis (N = 40), controlling reading ability measured by a sentence verification task; the article states that a 1 SD enjoyment increase made participants "12 times more likely to correctly answer". That figure does not match the printed log-odds coefficient (β = 0.09): the article's other per-SD figures equal e^β (2.57 for β = 0.94; 1.11 for β = 0.10), which for β = 0.09 gives about 1.09, so "12 times" is probably a misprint. No standardised effect size comparable to d is printed, so impact is not coded.

> "Further, as hypothesised, enjoyment of a synopsis positively predicted comprehension in Experiment 1, β = 0.09, SE = 0.03, z = 3.25, p = 0.001"

### Amrita Bains 2023 (2)

Amrita Bains, Carina Spaulding, Jessie Ricketts and Saloni Krishnan. (2023). Using a willingness to wait design to assess how readers value text. npj Science of Learning 8:17. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-023-00160-3

`q3 · i0` · `associational · r2`

Direct replication (N = 40); reading ability was excluded because it "was not significantly associated with comprehension, r = −0.021, p = 0.9"; the enjoyment effect was smaller in odds terms (1.11).

> "We replicated this finding in Experiment 2, β = 0.10, SE = 0.04, z = 2.71, p = 0.007 (Fig. 2d). A one standard deviation increase in enjoyment made a participant 1.11 times more likely to correctly answer the comprehension questions."
