# Peer Feedback Improves Work Quality

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i1` small · n=54 studies (k=141 effect sizes)

Feedback provided by peers on drafts or work-in-progress can improve the quality of the work produced, both for receivers (who act on the feedback) and often for givers (who learn by evaluating others' work against criteria). [+W]

## Subclaims

`q4 i1` A meta-analysis of 54 experimental/quasi-experimental control-group studies (k = 141) finds peer assessment produces a small-to-medium improvement in students' academic performance (essay writing, tests, and practical skills) relative to no assessment, and a comparable improvement relative to teacher assessment. [→ Double et al. 2020](#double-et-al-2020)

## Evidence

### Double et al. 2020

Double, K. S., McGrane, J. A., & Hopfenbeck, T. N. (2020). The impact of peer assessment on academic performance: A meta-analysis of control group studies. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(2), 481–509. [doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3)

`q4 · meta-analysis of experimental/quasi-experimental control-group studies` · `i1 · small-to-medium effect, g=0.31` · `n=54 studies (k=141 effect sizes)`

A meta-analysis of 54 control-group studies (141 effect sizes) spanning primary, secondary and tertiary students across subjects, comparing peer-assessment interventions (grading, peer dialogue, written feedback) against no-assessment and teacher-assessment control conditions on academic performance (test scores, essay/writing quality, practical skills). Peer assessment improved performance relative to no assessment (g = 0.31, p = .004) and relative to teacher assessment (g = 0.28, p = .007), and its effect did not differ significantly from self-assessment (g = 0.23, p = .209). Meta-regressions found the effect was robust across moderators such as online/offline delivery, assessment frequency and education level, i.e., no single implementation detail was required for the effect to hold.

## Discussion

**Dual benefit.** Peer feedback is often framed as benefiting the receiver, but the act of reviewing a peer's work — diagnosing problems against criteria and formulating actionable comments — is itself a form of elaborative practice and can improve the reviewer's own subsequent work. [+W] This "giver gains" effect suggests peer feedback activities should be designed as learning experiences for both roles, not merely as a scalable substitute for instructor feedback. Reviewing also builds evaluative judgement: the capacity to judge quality in one's own work, which is arguably the more durable outcome. This connects peer feedback to [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md), where students act as agents rather than recipients of assessment.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The quality of peer feedback depends heavily on scaffolding: without [rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md), exemplars, or training in how to critique, peer comments tend to be vague ("good job") or purely affective, and receivers may ignore or misapply them. [~W] Structured protocols — specifying what to look for, requiring specific and actionable comments, and pairing review with revision — are what convert peer commentary into improved work. [Rubrics improve student work quality.](rubrics-improve-student-work-quality.md) partly because they supply the shared criteria that make peer commentary specific and actionable. Anonymity, trust, and psychological safety shape whether reviewers give honest critique and whether receivers act on it. [~W] Domain expertise matters — peers may lack the knowledge to detect substantive errors, so peer feedback is best suited to clarity, structure, and audience-fit dimensions of work rather than deep correctness. [~W] Draft-stage timing also matters: feedback on work-in-progress can still change the product, whereas feedback on finished work rarely does [~M] — a point that aligns peer feedback with the formative logic of [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) and with the audience effects described in [Authentic audiences improve student work.](authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md).

**Constraints.** Peer feedback fails or backfires under identifiable conditions. When reviewers lack domain expertise and the work's quality hinges on factual or technical correctness, peer comments can be confidently wrong and receivers may "fix" non-problems or introduce errors. [~W] When feedback is unscaffolded and purely affective, it consumes class time without improving the product — and can erode trust if reviews feel arbitrary or unfair. [~W] Low psychological safety (competitive climates, non-anonymous review of high-stakes work) suppresses honest critique, producing inflated praise that receivers cannot act on. [~W] Finally, feedback delivered after submission rarely improves the work it addresses; without a built-in revision cycle, the activity yields little product improvement even if learning occurs. [~M]

**Relation to collaboration.** Peer feedback is a structured, role-differentiated form of the interactions studied under [Collaborative learning improves outcomes.](collaborative-learning-improves-outcomes.md); its distinctive feature is that the product being critiqued belongs to an individual, so accountability and revision pathways are clearer than in group work.

**Open questions.** How much training is needed before peer feedback reaches useful quality, how receiver uptake should be assessed, and how peer feedback compares to instructor feedback across domains remain open. The one meta-analysis recorded above measured academic performance (tests, writing, practical skills) rather than the quality of the revised work itself, and found no moderator it tested, including delivery mode, frequency and education level, changed the effect. The scaffolding moderators described above are not among those it tested.
