# Rapid prototyping methods can amplify novice designers' tendency to commit to a solution too early.

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 source · `q1` theoretical argument · no effect size

The claim concerns novice designers learning design, not experienced designers. For them, the chapter treats early prototyping as the strength of agile methods.

## Subclaims

`q1 i?` Novice designers tend to jump to solutions too quickly, and agile methods, which ask for a tentative solution right away and feedback on a low-fidelity prototype, could exacerbate this; once a solution is found, alternatives are hard to take seriously. [→ Svihla 2018](#svihla-2018)

`q1 i?` Teaching only ADDIE is not the remedy, because it signals that design is linear and tidy; the chapter proposes scaffolds that support novices in agile, human-centred work instead. [→ Svihla 2018](#svihla-2018)

`q1 · theoretical argument drawing on design research` · `i? · no effect size` · `n=N/A`

A chapter situating design thinking in the design research field, from which it draws lessons for instructional designers. On teaching design, it argues that learning to design is often "a single pass, with no or few opportunities to iterate". It also notes that schooling rewards getting to the right answer efficiently. Agile methods that "encourage designers to come to a tentative solution right away" could therefore "exacerbate a new designer's tendency to leap to solutions". The author treats ADDIE's clarity as a useful scaffold for novices, argues that the field needs comparable scaffolds for agile and human-centred work, and offers her own Wrong Theory Design Protocol as one.

## Discussion

**What to do with it.** When novices use rapid prototyping, build in a step that forces alternatives before a prototype is chosen. Ask several structurally different designs, as the ideate move in [Design Thinking](../processes/design-thinking.md) does. Treat the first prototype as a probe of the *problem*, not a draft of the answer. The chapter's own case makes this point: the agile team succeeded because stakeholder reactions to the prototype changed how the team framed the problem.

**Status.** This is a reasoned argument, not a measured effect. Design research on fixation, which the chapter's field is part of, is where empirical tests would come from.
