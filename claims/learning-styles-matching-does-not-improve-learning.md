# Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 studies · 2 review `r1`–`r3`, 1 causal `r2` · `q2`–`q3`

Matching instruction to a learner's purported "learning style" (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, etc.) does not improve learning outcomes; learners learn best when content is matched to the *nature of the material*, not to a self-reported style preference.

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Pashler et al. 2008

Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9*(3), 105–119. [doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)

`q3 · systematic review of the learning-styles literature` · `i? · no aggregate effect size reported` · `n=N/A (narrative synthesis of the field)` · `review · r3`

The authors specify that validating learning-style-based instruction requires a "crossover interaction" — style A learners must do best under method 1 while style B learners do best under method 2, using a design where learners are classified by style, randomly assigned to matched or mismatched instruction, and given a common assessment. Searching the literature for studies meeting this design, they found only one that even potentially qualified, and its evidence was weak; several well-designed studies instead found evidence *contradicting* the hypothesis. They conclude that instruction should follow the content's demands (e.g., visual material for geometry, verbal for writing), not a learner's self-reported style.

### Rogowsky et al. 2020

Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2020). Providing Instruction Based on Students' Learning Style Preferences Does Not Improve Learning. *Frontiers in Psychology, 11*, 164. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00164](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00164)

`q3 · randomized experiment (crossover-interaction design following Pashler et al.'s own criteria)` · `i? · no standardised effect size reported; interaction F(1,38)=1.16, p=0.29` · `n=125 fifth-grade students (34 analyzed in the learning-style-group ANOVA)` · `causal · r2`

125 fifth graders (ages 10–11) in a Pennsylvania public school were classified as auditory or visual learners via a standardized learning-style inventory, then given listening- and reading-comprehension tests. A mixed-design ANOVA testing whether learning style predicted which modality a student comprehended better found no learning-style-by-modality interaction (F(1,38)=1.16, p=0.29) — visual learners scored significantly higher on *both* listening and reading comprehension (main effect of style, F(1,32)=12.92, p=0.001, η²=0.29) rather than showing the crossover the [meshing hypothesis](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) predicts. This replicates the earlier adult study (Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal, 2015) with a school-age population.

## Discussion

**The meshing hypothesis.** The claim at issue is the "meshing hypothesis": that presenting visual learners with visual formats and verbal learners with verbal formats improves outcomes. Studies that have directly tested meshing — assigning learners to matched versus mismatched formats based on a style inventory — consistently fail to find the predicted interaction [-S], and several find the opposite pattern (e.g., self-identified verbalizers sometimes learn *better* from visual presentations) [~M]. No published study using a sound experimental design has supported meshing [X].

**Why the myth persists.** Roughly 90% of educators and a majority of the public endorse learning styles [~W], likely because the underlying intuition — that people have preferences and differ from one another — is true, and because the theory offers an appealing explanation for why some learners struggle. Preferences are real; the claim that matching instruction to them improves learning is not.

**What actually matters.** The modality of instruction should follow the content: geometry is best taught visually, poetry aloud. Dual-format presentation can help when formats are complementary and integrated, consistent with [dual coding](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) [+S] — but this is a property of the material, not a learner trait. Individual differences that *do* moderate instruction include prior knowledge (see the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)) [+S] and working memory capacity [~M], both of which have far stronger empirical support than styles. Where format choices do affect outcomes, the mechanism is cognitive load, not preference fit — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md)

**Practical risk.** Time spent diagnosing styles and building parallel matched materials is time diverted from strategies with demonstrated effects, and labeling learners by style may encourage fixed-ability beliefs and limit learners' willingness to engage with unfamiliar formats [-W]. Adding redundant or "style-matched" material can also degrade learning for the same reason irrelevant material does — see [Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning.](coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [-S]

**Constraints on any residual benefit.** Style-matched instruction fails under the conditions that matter most in practice: when formats are assigned by inventory rather than by content demands, the predicted match × outcome interaction does not appear [-S]; when "matched" material is redundant with the primary format, it adds extraneous load and depresses performance [-S]; and when learners are labeled by style, they may avoid formats outside their label, narrowing the range of representations they practice [-W]. Even where learners *prefer* a format, preference predicts satisfaction, not achievement [~M].

**Open questions.** The strongest remaining research need is not further tests of meshing, which have been repeatedly falsified, but studies of *why* the belief persists and how best to correct it in teacher education. A secondary open question is whether style inventories predict anything at all beyond stable preference — current evidence suggests they show poor reliability and little relation to learning outcomes [-M].

*Merged from “Learning Styles Matching Yields No Benefit” (learning-styles-matching-yields-no-benefit):* **The matching hypothesis.** The claim under test is the *meshing hypothesis*: that learners learn better when instruction is presented in their preferred modality (e.g., visual instruction for "visual learners"). Reviews of the literature have repeatedly found that almost no studies use the crossover experimental design needed to test this — learners must be sorted by style, randomly assigned to matched or mismatched instruction, and tested on equivalent outcomes. The few studies that do meet this standard consistently find no interaction between learner style and instructional modality. [-S]

**What the evidence does support.** Modality of instruction matters, but as a property of the *content*, not the learner. [Dual coding theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) supports combining verbal and visual representations for all learners [+S], and the modality principle from [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) favors audio-plus-visual presentation under specific load conditions [~S]. These content-driven design principles should not be confused with learner-style matching. Similarly, [cognitive load reduction](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) and [chunking](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) address the real constraint — working memory capacity, which is limited for everyone — rather than a supposed individual difference in modality preference.

**Persistence of the myth.** Belief in learning styles remains widespread among educators and the public despite the absence of supporting evidence. This matters practically: time spent diagnosing styles and building parallel materials is time diverted from strategies with demonstrated benefit, such as [retrieval practice](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md), [worked examples](worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md), and [spacing](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md). Style labels can also do harm indirectly — by encouraging learners to attribute difficulty to a poor "match" rather than to the need for more effortful practice, undermining productive beliefs about struggle and [desirable difficulty](desirable-difficulties-enhance-learning.md). [-M]

**Constraints on the claim's scope.** The claim applies to *matching instruction to measured styles to improve learning outcomes*; it does not deny that learners have preferences, nor that learners differ in prior knowledge, ability, or working memory capacity — differences that do moderate instructional effectiveness (see [expertise reversal](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)). Nor does it imply that single-modality instruction is always adequate: content that is inherently spatial or auditory should be presented accordingly, for all learners. Treating the claim as "modality doesn't matter" would be a misreading; the discredited element is the learner–instruction interaction, not modality as a design variable.

**Open questions.** Some researchers argue that *preferences* are real and measurable even if matching them does not aid learning; whether preference data could serve motivational or metacognitive purposes remains underexplored [~W]. The core claim here — that matching yields no learning benefit — is not in dispute in the experimental literature.
