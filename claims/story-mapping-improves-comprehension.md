# Story Mapping Improves Comprehension

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Story mapping — a graphic organizer that makes a narrative's structure explicit (setting, characters, problem, goal, events, resolution) — supports learners in building a coherent situation model of a text. The claim applies to narrative text; it is not a claim about graphic organizers generally.

## Discussion

Story mapping is a form of graphic organizer that externalizes the canonical structure of narrative text (story grammar). Its proposed mechanism is twofold: it reduces the working-memory burden of tracking story grammar during reading, and it directs attention to the causal and temporal relations that carry meaning in narratives — consistent with the reasoning behind [chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) and [advance organizers improve learning](advance-organizers-improve-learning.md).

Boundary conditions follow from this mechanism. Story maps should help most when the text actually has a narrative structure and when learners are young, struggling, or unfamiliar with story grammar; for skilled readers or for expository text, a story map adds little and may become redundant — a moderation pattern analogous to the expertise reversal documented for other scaffolds (see [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)). The map is also a scaffold, not a substitute for reading: learners who fill in a map without genuinely processing the text can show illusory comprehension. Like other visual scaffolds, its value depends on whether it prompts active integration of ideas rather than passive copying — a moderation pattern shared with [annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md).

Design implications: teacher-constructed maps presented before reading function as advance organizers; student-constructed maps during or after reading function as generative processing supports. The two uses likely differ in effect and in the expertise level at which they pay off, but the wiki lacks evidence entries to adjudicate between them yet.

Fading matters. Because the map is a scaffold for building an internalized sense of story grammar, the long-term goal is independent comprehension; keeping learners on fully completed or heavily prompted maps past the point of need risks the same dependency documented for other structured supports. A practical sequence moves from teacher-modeled maps, to collaboratively completed maps, to student-generated maps, to no map.

Open questions include how much fading the scaffold requires over time, whether teacher-constructed versus student-constructed maps differ in effect, and how story maps compare with other structural supports such as retelling and question generation.
