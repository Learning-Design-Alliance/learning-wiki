# Story Mapping Improves Comprehension

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q1`–`q2`

Story mapping — a graphic organizer that makes a narrative's structure explicit (setting, characters, problem, goal, events, resolution) — supports learners in building a coherent situation model of a text. The claim applies to narrative text; it is not a claim about graphic organizers generally.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` Teaching heterogeneous groups of third/fourth graders (including learning-disabled and low-achieving students) to use a story map produced a significant, maintained rise in reading-comprehension question accuracy across a multiple-baseline design, though no standardized effect size was reported. [→ Idol 1987](#idol-1987)

`q1 i?` In a single-subject ABC design, six third- and fourth-grade students with specific learning disabilities more than doubled their correct identification of story-grammar elements after story-map instruction, and the gain was maintained once the map was withdrawn; no standardized effect size was reported. [→ Boulineau et al. 2004](#boulineau-et-al-2004)

## Evidence

### Idol 1987

Idol, L. (1987). Group story mapping: A comprehension strategy for both skilled and unskilled readers. *Journal of Learning Disabilities, 20*(4), 196–205. [doi:10.1177/002221948702000401](https://doi.org/10.1177/002221948702000401)

`q2 · quasi-experimental multiple-baseline-across-groups design` · `i? · no standardized effect size reported (F(4,21)=4.45, p<.05, on repeated-measures ANOVA of raw comprehension-question percentages)` · `n=22 (heterogeneous third/fourth-grade groups, including 5 identified learning-disabled or low-achieving students)`

Heterogeneous groups of third/fourth graders were taught a story-mapping strategy (identifying setting, characters, problem, goal, events, resolution) using a model-lead-test paradigm, with a multiple-baseline design across two groups. Daily comprehension-question accuracy rose from baseline and was maintained above 80% correct once teacher support and the maps themselves were withdrawn; the low-achieving and learning-disabled students in the groups also improved and largely maintained gains. Improvements generalized to listening comprehension and spontaneous story writing but not consistently to standardized reading-test subtests.

### Boulineau et al. 2004

Boulineau, T., Fore, C., III, Hagan-Burke, S., & Burke, M. D. (2004). Use of story-mapping to increase the story-grammar text comprehension of elementary students with learning disabilities. *Learning Disability Quarterly, 27*(2), 105–121. [doi:10.2307/1593645](https://doi.org/10.2307/1593645)

`q1 · single-subject descriptive ABC design (baseline/intervention/maintenance) with interrater and procedural reliability` · `i? · no standardized effect size reported (raw percent-correct scores: group mean rose from 31% at baseline to 84% during intervention)` · `n=6 (third- and fourth-grade students identified with specific learning disabilities)`

Six students with specific learning disabilities were taught explicit story-grammar instruction using a story-grammar map (setting, time, characters, problem, solution, outcome, reaction, theme) across a baseline–intervention–maintenance sequence. Mean correct identification of story-grammar elements rose from 31% at baseline (range 25–35%) to 84% during story-map instruction (range 67–96%), with gains largely sustained once explicit instruction was withdrawn and non-overlapping data points between baseline and later phases for every student reported individually.

## Discussion

Story mapping is a form of graphic organizer that externalizes the canonical structure of narrative text (story grammar). Its proposed mechanism is twofold: it reduces the working-memory burden of tracking story grammar during reading, and it directs attention to the causal and temporal relations that carry meaning in narratives — consistent with the reasoning behind [chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) and [advance organizers improve learning](advance-organizers-improve-learning.md).

Boundary conditions follow from this mechanism. Story maps should help most when the text actually has a narrative structure and when learners are young, struggling, or unfamiliar with story grammar; for skilled readers or for expository text, a story map adds little and may become redundant — a moderation pattern analogous to the expertise reversal documented for other scaffolds (see [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)). The map is also a scaffold, not a substitute for reading: learners who fill in a map without genuinely processing the text can show illusory comprehension. Like other visual scaffolds, its value depends on whether it prompts active integration of ideas rather than passive copying — a moderation pattern shared with [annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md).

Design implications: teacher-constructed maps presented before reading function as advance organizers; student-constructed maps during or after reading function as generative processing supports. The two uses likely differ in effect and in the expertise level at which they pay off, but the wiki lacks evidence entries to adjudicate between them yet.

Fading matters. Because the map is a scaffold for building an internalized sense of story grammar, the long-term goal is independent comprehension; keeping learners on fully completed or heavily prompted maps past the point of need risks the same dependency documented for other structured supports. A practical sequence moves from teacher-modeled maps, to collaboratively completed maps, to student-generated maps, to no map.

Open questions include how much fading the scaffold requires over time, whether teacher-constructed versus student-constructed maps differ in effect, and how story maps compare with other structural supports such as retelling and question generation. Both studies recorded above are small designs (22 and 6 students) that report no standardized effect size.
