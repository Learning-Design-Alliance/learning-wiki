# Review scheduled by a model of each student's memory improved end-of-semester retention in a middle-school language course more than massed or uniformly spaced review of the same duration

The personalized method decides which material each student should review and when, by combining a psychological theory of memory with statistical estimates of how individual students and items differ. It ran inside retrieval-practice software used in the course.

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` On a cumulative exam given after the end of the semester, time-matched personalized review yielded a 16.5% boost in course retention over massed study (described by the authors as current educational practice) and a 10.0% improvement over a one-size-fits-all spaced review schedule. Lindsey, R. V., Shroyer, J. D., Pashler, H., & Mozer, M. C. (2014). Improving students' long-term knowledge retention through personalized review. *Psychological Science, 25*(3), 639–647. [doi:10.1177/0956797613504302](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613504302)

`q2 · comparison of time-matched review strategies within one course; the abstract does not describe how strategies were assigned` · `i? · results reported as percentage improvements in retention, not as a standardised effect size` · `n=not stated in the abstract` · `causal · r?`

The authors developed a method for systematic, personalized [spaced review](../elements/spaced-repetition.md) that combines statistical techniques for inferring individual differences with a psychological theory of memory, and built it into [retrieval-practice](../elements/retrieval-practice.md) software used throughout a semester-long middle-school foreign-language course. A cumulative exam given after the semester's end compared three review strategies matched for time: massed study, a one-size-fits-all spaced schedule, and personalized review. This entry was written from the published abstract: the full text was not available to this ingest, so the number of students, how strategies were assigned to students and material, the delay to the exam, the exact meaning of the percentage improvements and the authors' stated limitations are not recorded here.

## Discussion

The result supports scheduling review by a learner model rather than a fixed schedule, in one course and one subject. Whether "16.5%" is a relative or a percentage-point improvement should be read from the full article before the number is used to size a design decision.
