Evidence¶

Cookson 2022¶

Cookson, P. W., Jr., & Darling-Hammond, L. (2022). Building school communities for students living in deep poverty. Learning Policy Institute. https://doi.org/10.54300/121.698

q2 · i? · review · r1

The report cites a 2016 study by economists Kirabo Jackson, Rucker Johnson, and Claudia Persico linking school spending and finance-reform data to long-term outcomes for a national sample of children born between 1955 and 1985 and followed through 2011, finding "a 3.2 percentage point reduction in the annual incidence of poverty".

"a 10% increase in per-pupil spending each year for all 12 years of public school leads to 0.31 more completed years of education, about 7% higher wages, and a 3.2 percentage point reduction in the annual incidence of poverty; effects are much more pronounced for children from low-income families."

Related Claims¶

  • Research on school finance reforms finds increased spending improves educational and life outcomes, with larger effects for students from low-income backgrounds, including causal evidence from California's Local Control Funding Formula — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • A school finance reform analysis found that a 20% increase in per-pupil spending for a child's k-12 schooling raised high school completion and adult earnings — related
  • Increases in per-pupil spending improve educational attainment, wages, and adult poverty outcomes, with stronger effects for low-income children — possibly the same claim (merge candidate)
  • Tamarack Institute's collective impact approach reported 10 percent poverty reductions in cities and positive impact for 202,931 low-income Canadians over 10 years — related
  • A $1,000 increase in district per-pupil revenue in grades 10–12 was associated with a 5.3 percentage-point increase in high school graduation rates in California — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • A $1,000 per-pupil spending increase across high school raised graduation likelihood by 8.2 percentage points, and 3 consecutive years of increased high school spending raised college-readiness standards attainment in math and reading — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • LCFF-induced per-pupil spending increases caused improvements in math and reading achievement in districts receiving concentration grants — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Student outcome improvements from LCFF funding were more pronounced for cohorts exposed to funding increases for more of their school-age years — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • LCFF-induced spending increases of $1,000 per pupil for 3 consecutive years produced roughly a full grade-level increase in math and reading achievement in grades 3–8 — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
