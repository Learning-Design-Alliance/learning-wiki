## Evidence

### Natesan S 2020

Natesan S, Bailitz J, King A, Krzyzaniak S, Kennedy S, Kim A, Byyny R, Gottlieb M. (2020). Clinical Teaching: An Evidence-based Guide to Best Practices from the Council of Emergency Medicine Residency Directors. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine. https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2020.4.46060

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review's graded recommendation drawing on cognitive load theory, which holds that the brain "can process only a finite amount of information at one given time," plus interleaving and spaced repetition/retrieval practice strategies applied to clinical teaching.

> "Consider reducing cognitive load, interleaving, using spaced/retrieval practice, and increasing wait times after asking questions to allow the learner time to process and respond (Level 2a, Grade B)."

### Natesan S 2020 (2)

Natesan S, Bailitz J, King A, Krzyzaniak S, Kennedy S, Kim A, Byyny R, Gottlieb M. (2020). Clinical Teaching: An Evidence-based Guide to Best Practices from the Council of Emergency Medicine Residency Directors. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine. https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2020.4.46060

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

Evidence cited in the review's wait-time section: instructors typically wait an average of only 1.5 seconds before interjecting, whereas waiting three seconds or longer produced the reported response and engagement benefits. No effect sizes are printed.

> "studies have found that waiting three seconds or longer (especially after "wait time 2") allowed the learner time to process the question and decreased failure-to-respond rates, increased perception of caring (thereby encouraging the learner to engage more actively), and increased the total number of responses received."
