# In Experiment 2, making metacognitive predictions alone did not improve calibration, performance feedback was sufficient, and explicit metacognitive feedback added no significant benefit

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r3` · `q3` · `i2` medium

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` The key transition in absolute metacognitive bias occurred between the prediction-only group and the prediction-plus-performance-feedback group; adding metacognitive feedback produced no further significant reduction. [→ Ceri Ngai and Sam J. Gilbert 2026](#ceri-ngai-and-sam-j-gilbert-2026)

## Evidence

### Ceri Ngai and Sam J. Gilbert 2026

Ceri Ngai and Sam J. Gilbert (2026). Metacognitive training facilitates optimal cognitive offloading. Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications. https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-026-00714-0

`q3 · i2` · `causal · r3`

Pairwise one-tailed t-tests across the additive four-group design in Experiment 2 (N = 416). Significant reductions appeared for Group 1 versus Group 3 (d = 0.47), Group 2 versus Group 3 (d = 0.60), and Group 2 versus Group 4 (d = 0.51); Group 1 versus Group 2 and Group 3 versus Group 4 were not significant.

> "Second, making metacognitive predictions alone did not significantly improve metacognitive calibration (Group 1 versus Group 2). Third, although performance feedback was sufficient to improve metacognitive calibration (Group 3 versus Group 2), metacognitive feedback (i.e. explicitly highlighting over- or underconfidence) did not lead to any significant additional effect (Group 4 versus Group 3)."
