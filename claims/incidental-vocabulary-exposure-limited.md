## Discussion

**The mechanism.** Incidental learning from context depends on the learner noticing an unknown word, inferring meaning from surrounding text, and retaining that inference. Each step is unreliable: readers frequently skip unknown words, context often supports only a vague meaning hypothesis, and a single encounter rarely produces durable retention. The widely cited estimate that word meanings are acquired after roughly 8–10 meaningful encounters reflects the rarity of strong single-exposure gains, not their absence.

**Moderators.** Gains are larger when the word appears repeatedly, when context is highly informative, when the learner has strong existing vocabulary and comprehension skills, and when the learner is actively engaged (e.g., through prediction or discussion). Rich instruction — explicit definition, morphology, and spaced review — reliably outperforms exposure alone for targeted words.

**Design implication.** Incidental exposure should be treated as a complement to, not a substitute for, explicit vocabulary instruction. Texts can be engineered to increase encounter frequency and contextual support, but designers should not assume learners will acquire critical vocabulary without deliberate teaching and retrieval practice.

**Constraints on relying on incidental exposure.** Wide free reading alone is an inefficient route to vocabulary growth: acquisition rates per encounter are so low that learners would need to read vastly more text than curricula allow to close vocabulary gaps through exposure alone [-M]. Learners with weak [prior knowledge](activation-improves-learning.md) or limited comprehension skills make the poorest meaning inferences from context, so incidental exposure widens rather than narrows vocabulary differences between strong and weak readers [~M]. Highly predictable or narrative-embedded text can also let readers comprehend the passage without fully resolving the target word's meaning, producing illusory learning [~M].

**Open questions.** Most evidence comes from L1 reading research with school-age learners; the number of encounters needed may differ for L2 learners, multimedia environments, and listening comprehension. The Evidence section currently has no verified entries; studies quantifying per-encounter acquisition rates and the effects of encounter frequency still need to be added.
