# Parent Implemented Intervention Improves Outcomes

Parent implemented intervention refers to programs in which parents are trained to deliver instructional or behavioral support to their own children, typically in home or community settings. The claim concerns whether such parent-delivered programs improve child outcomes relative to usual care or comparison conditions.

## Discussion

This claim page currently lacks evidence entries; studies still need to be added before the claim can be rated. In the literature on parent implemented intervention — most extensively studied in early intervention for children with autism and related developmental disabilities — the central empirical question is whether parent-delivered procedures produce child outcomes comparable to specialist-delivered intervention, and whether parent training itself improves parent fidelity, confidence, and stress. Published syntheses generally report positive but variable child outcomes, with effects moderated by the dosage and quality of parent coaching, the fidelity with which parents implement the procedures, and the intensity of specialist support provided alongside parent delivery.

Boundary conditions to document when evidence is added include: (a) interventions requiring precise procedural fidelity may show weaker outcomes when delivered by parents without ongoing coaching; (b) family circumstances — time, resources, stress — constrain implementation dosage; and (c) outcomes measured immediately after training often differ from those maintained over time. Designers of parent mediated programs should treat parent training as an instructional design problem in its own right, applying principles from [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) (modeling, guided practice, feedback) when coaching parents. Coaching formats that model techniques, rehearse them with feedback, and fade support over time mirror the scaffolding logic described in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md), and the risk of overloading parents with too many simultaneous techniques parallels [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md)

Open questions for future enrichment: whether parent-delivered and professional-delivered formats differ in magnitude of child outcomes or only in cost and reach; how coaching dosage maps to implementation fidelity; and which outcome domains (language, adaptive behavior, behavior regulation) are most sensitive to parent delivery.
