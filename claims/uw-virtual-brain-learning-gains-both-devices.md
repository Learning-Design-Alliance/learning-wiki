## Evidence

### Karen B. Schloss 2021

Karen B. Schloss, Melissa A. Schoenlein, Ross Tredinnick, Simon Smith, Nathaniel Miller, Chris Racey, Christian Castro, and Bas Rokers. (2021). The UW Virtual Brain Project: An immersive approach to teaching functional neuroanatomy. Preprint. [doi:10.1037/tps0000281](https://doi.org/10.1037/tps0000281)

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r2`

Experiment 1A, a within-subjects lab experiment with 60 undergraduates comparing VR and PC lessons with paper drawing/labeling pre-/post-tests, found "change scores were signiﬁcantly positive for both PC and VR" with d = 1.68 (PC) and d = 1.51 (VR).

> "t-tests against zero showed that change scores were signiﬁcantly positive for both PC and VR ( t(59) = 13.05, p < .001, d = 1.68, and t(59) = 11.69, p < .001, d = 1.51, respectively), indicating that participants learned from the Virtual Brain lessons on both devices."

### Karen B. Schloss 2021 (2)

Karen B. Schloss, Melissa A. Schoenlein, Ross Tredinnick, Simon Smith, Nathaniel Miller, Chris Racey, Christian Castro, and Bas Rokers. (2021). The UW Virtual Brain Project: An immersive approach to teaching functional neuroanatomy. Preprint. [doi:10.1037/tps0000281](https://doi.org/10.1037/tps0000281)

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r2`

Experiment 1B replicated Experiment 1A with 120 undergraduates; "change scores were significantly greater than zero for PC" (d = 1.41) and VR (d = 1.27). Experiment 2 (n = 48, 3D Looking Glass testing) also showed significant gains for both devices.

> "As in Exp. 1A, change scores were significantly greater than zero for PC ( t(119) = 15.46, p < .001, d = 1.41) and VR ( t(119) = 13.94, p < .001, d = 1.27), indicating that participants learned on both devices (Figure 4A)."
