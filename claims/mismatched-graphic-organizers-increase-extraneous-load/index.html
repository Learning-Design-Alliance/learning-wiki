Recall from bar graphs is more accurate when the recall format matches the studied graph format, a small effect that the content's thematic emphasis can override¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r? · q3 · i2 medium · n=157 (Experiment 1) and n≈134 (Experiment 2), German university students

The one study recorded here tested bar graphs, not graphic organizers, and did not measure cognitive load. In two experiments, university students recalled election data more accurately when the recall graph matched the format they had studied (η² = .03–.04), and cueing an incongruent perspective degraded recall (d = 0.52–0.54). But students also recalled better from whichever perspective was thematically dominant in the material, regardless of the format studied. The authors interpret the cost as load from an incongruent schema; extending it to graphic organizers, and to extraneous load as such, is this page's inference.

Evidence¶

Schnotz & Baadte 2015¶

Schnotz, W., & Baadte, C. (2015). Surface and deep structures in graphics comprehension. Memory & Cognition, 43(4), 605–618. doi:10.3758/s13421-014-0490-2

q3 · peer-reviewed lab experiments (two studies) · i2 · medium effect on schema-interference contrasts, d=0.51–0.54; smaller format-match interaction, η²=.03–.04 · n=157 (Experiment 1) and n≈134 (Experiment 2), German university students · causal · r?

Two between-subjects experiments had students study bar graphs of (fictional-data) US election results presented in one of two informationally-equivalent visual formats ("party" graphs vs. "religion" graphs), then recall the content by filling in blank graphs of either the studied or a differently-formatted item. In both experiments a significant graph-format × recall-item-format interaction showed recall was more accurate when the recall format matched the studied format (F(1,151)=6.52, p=.006, η²=.04 in Exp. 1; F(1,128)=4.22, p=.021, η²=.03 in Exp. 2), and cueing an unfamiliar, incongruent processing perspective by instruction significantly degraded recall of the previously-studied content (t(151)=2.03, p=.022, d=0.54, and t(151)=1.99, p=.025, d=0.52) — consistent with the authors' framing that an incongruent schema "impose[s] a higher cognitive load on working memory," producing interference. The important qualifier: participants also recalled content more accurately from whichever perspective was thematically dominant in the material regardless of which graphic format they had studied — in Experiment 2 they recalled the non-studied format's items better than the studied one (t(66)=3.83, p<.001, d=0.51). So format (surface-structure) mismatch imposes a real but modest processing cost, which the content's underlying task-relevance can override.

Discussion¶

What is and is not shown. The paragraphs below apply the bar-graph finding to graphic organizers through cognitive load theory. No study recorded here tests a graphic organizer or measures extraneous load, and the strength tags below predate that reading.

Mechanism. Within Cognitive Load Theory, extraneous load arises from instructional design that requires processing not directed at learning. A well-matched organizer (e.g., a compare/contrast matrix for comparative content, a cycle diagram for cyclical processes) externalizes relationships so learners need not hold them in working memory — consistent with the claim that chunking reduces working-memory load [+M]. A mismatched organizer adds a second, partially inconsistent representation: learners must map organizer slots onto content that does not fit them, splitting attention between the two structures — a condition analogous to the split-attention effect. The result is the same outcome described in cognitive overload degrades learning [+S]: resources spent on reconciliation rather than schema construction.

Constraints. The claim is about mismatched organizers, not organizers generally — the penalty applies only under specific conditions:

  • Imposed rather than learner-generated. An organizer assigned without reference to the content's actual structure forces reconciliation work on the learner [-M].
  • Unfamiliar content structure. Novices lack the expertise to detect and repair an ill-fitting frame, so they process the mismatch rather than the content [-M].
  • Advanced learners. For experts, any imposed organizer may simply be redundant — consistent with the expertise reversal effect [~S].
  • High element interactivity. When the content is already near working-memory capacity, the added reconciliation load is most likely to push learners past capacity [-M].

Well-aligned organizers and advance organizers can support learning — see Advance organizers improve learning [+M]. The penalty also parallels the coherence principle [+S]: like decorative but irrelevant material, an ill-fitting organizer is a design feature that invites processing unrelated to the learning goal.

Design implications. Before selecting an organizer, analyze the content's underlying structure — sequence, hierarchy, comparison, cycle, cause–effect — and choose or adapt a frame that mirrors it. Where no standard frame fits, a sparser outline or learner-constructed map is safer than forcing content into a borrowed template. This aligns with the broader guidance in cognitive load reduction and clear structure.

Open questions. How much structural mismatch is tolerable before load costs outweigh organizational benefits, and whether learner-constructed organizers avoid the penalty, remain under-researched. Studies are needed to establish this claim empirically.
