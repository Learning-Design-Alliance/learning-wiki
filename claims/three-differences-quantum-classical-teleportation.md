# Quantum and classical teleportation differ in three stated respects: information required, measurability of the state, and entanglement without definite individual states

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` Teleporting a classical coin requires transmitting one bit matching the state's information content, whereas quantum teleportation conveys an infinite-information state using only two bits, because the parties remain ignorant of the coefficients. [→ Scott M. Cohen 2008](#scott-m-cohen-2008)
`q1 i?` A quantum coin cannot be 'looked at' without disturbing it, so the classical cheating strategy fails for quantum states. [→ Scott M. Cohen 2008 (2)](#scott-m-cohen-2008-2)
`q1 i?` In the quantum case the entangled coins have no definite individual states, so the question whether coins a and A are the same has no answer, unlike the classical protocol where Chloe can compare them. [→ Scott M. Cohen 2008 (3)](#scott-m-cohen-2008-3)

## Evidence

### Scott M. Cohen 2008

Scott M. Cohen. (2008). Visualizing Teleportation. https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.0051 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Section IV analytical comparison: "the two cases, classical vs. quantum, are dramatically diﬀerent in terms of the amounts of information involved" — one bit for a classical coin versus two bits carrying an arbitrarily specified quantum state.

> "it would require an inﬁnite amount of information for Alice to inform Bob of the state of coin A, whereas she only actually transmits two bits of information when informing him which of her four questions was the outcome of her measurement."

### Scott M. Cohen 2008 (2)

Scott M. Cohen. (2008). Visualizing Teleportation. https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.0051 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Section IV argument on measurement disturbance: looking at a quantum coin changes it, so the classical cheat of inspecting coin A will not work; the author draws the morals "don't bother trying to cheat" and that a do-over is pointless.

> "in contrast to a classical coin, when Alice looks at her quantum coin, she invariably disturbs it in the process. That is, no matter what state the coin wa s in before she looked at it, the state after she looks at it is with certainty given by the outcome of her measurement ."

### Scott M. Cohen 2008 (3)

Scott M. Cohen. (2008). Visualizing Teleportation. https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.0051 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0704.0051)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Section III/IV argument on entanglement: although classical and quantum correlations look similar (one coin H implies the other H), the entangled correlations "have no analog in the case of classical coins" because neither quantum coin has a state of its own.

> "in the quantum case coins a, b are entangled, which means that neither one has a deﬁnite state of its own. Since coin a does not have a deﬁnite state, the question whether coins a, A are the same (have the same state) has no answer !"
