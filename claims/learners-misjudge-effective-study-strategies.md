# Learners Misjudge Effective Study Strategies

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q2` quasi-experiment

Learners systematically prefer and rate as effective those study strategies that feel easy and fluent in the moment, while underusing strategies that produce durable learning but feel effortful.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` College students overwhelmingly favor rereading and rarely choose retrieval practice as a study strategy, and most who do self-test say they do it to check what they know rather than because it produces better learning. [→ Karpicke Butler Roediger 2009](#karpicke-butler-roediger-2009)

`q2 i?` A narrative review of the metacognition-and-studying literature concludes that most students believe rereading is a more effective learning strategy than self-testing, even though the reverse is true — a belief the review treats as a key driver of ineffective strategy choice. [→ Bjork Dunlosky Kornell 2013](#bjork-dunlosky-kornell-2013)

## Evidence

### Karpicke Butler Roediger 2009

Karpicke, J. D., Butler, A. C., & Roediger, H. L. (2009). Metacognitive strategies in student learning: Do students practise retrieval when they study on their own? *Memory, 17*(4), 471–479. [doi:10.1080/09658210802647009](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210802647009)

`q2 · survey study (open-ended and forced-choice questionnaires)` · `i? · no effect size reported (percentages of strategy endorsement, not a standardized effect)` · `n=177 college students (Question 1); 101 and 76 students respectively on the two versions of Question 2`

177 Washington University undergraduates were surveyed about their real-world study habits. On an open-ended free-report question, 83.6% listed rereading notes or a textbook as a strategy they use and 54.8% ranked it their #1 strategy, while only 10.7% (19 of 177) reported practising retrieval (self-testing) at all, and just 1.1% ranked it #1. On a forced-choice question asking whether they would reread, self-test, or do something else after reading a textbook chapter once, 57% chose to restudy and only 18% chose to self-test; of those who did choose self-testing, most explained they did it to check what they had learned rather than because testing itself produces better learning. The authors conclude students "lack metacognitive awareness of the testing effect."

### Bjork Dunlosky Kornell 2013

Bjork, R. A., Dunlosky, J., & Kornell, N. (2013). Self-regulated learning: Beliefs, techniques, and illusions. *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*(1), 417–444. [doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)

`q2 · narrative review of survey and laboratory studies` · `i? · no single effect size reported (synthesizes multiple studies' correlational and survey findings)` · `n=large (aggregated across the reviewed surveys and experiments, e.g. Kornell & Bjork 2007's 472 students and Karpicke et al. 2009's 177 students)`

This Annual Review of Psychology article synthesizes survey and experimental work on what students believe about how to study and how those beliefs relate to actual learning. It reports that when Kornell & Bjork (2007) asked 472 UCLA students why they used self-testing, only 18% said it was because they learn more from testing than rereading, versus about 70% who said they use it to check what they already know. The review states directly that "most students believe that rereading is a more effective strategy than is self-testing (McCabe 2011)," and frames this fluency-driven misjudgment, together with the dissociation between subjective ease and later retention, as a central reason self-regulated study choices go wrong.

## Discussion

This claim concerns a metacognitive mismatch rather than a specific intervention: the strategies learners *choose* and the strategies that *work* diverge. The mechanism most commonly proposed is that learners use subjective fluency — how smoothly processing feels right now — as a proxy for learning. Strategies that make information immediately accessible (rereading, highlighting, massed practice) feel productive, while strategies that introduce desirable difficulties (retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving) feel ineffective precisely because they slow performance during study, even though they improve retention and transfer.

A key boundary condition is that the misjudgment is strongest for judgments made *during or immediately after* study, when fluency cues dominate. Judgments made after a delay, or based on actual test performance rather than felt ease, align better with objective learning. This suggests the misjudgment is not a fixed trait but a consequence of the cues learners attend to — a scope question that remains open in the literature: how much of the effect reflects poor strategy knowledge versus poor monitoring despite adequate knowledge.

**Design implications.** Because learners will not reliably self-select high-utility strategies, learning designs should build those strategies into the structure of instruction — scheduled retrieval, spaced review, interleaved practice — rather than leaving the choice to learners. Feedback that recalibrates judgments of learning is the primary corrective, connecting to [Assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md). Where instruction reduces processing difficulty, designers should be alert to the flip side: reduced load can inflate fluency-based confidence without improving retention, so load reduction should be paired with retrieval demands rather than substituted for them — see [Cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) and [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md).

**Open questions.** The relative weight of strategy knowledge versus monitoring failure is unresolved, as is the durability of calibration training: learners can be taught that effortful strategies work, but whether they maintain use of those strategies once instruction ends is not well established. Evidence entries supporting this claim still need to be added.
