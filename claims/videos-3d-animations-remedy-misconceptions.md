## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Both groups' concept-test performance increased from pre-test to post-test, reported descriptively: in the homework group incorrect answers fell and correct answers rose in every unit, while in the classroom group some incorrect answers changed to correct or partially correct and the pattern varied by unit (in the post-test, correct answers outnumbered the rest except in the optical disk and ethernet card units). No within-group significance test is printed. [→ Unal Cakiroglu 2017](#unal-cakiroglu-2017)

## Evidence

### Unal Cakiroglu 2017

Unal Cakiroglu, Huseyin Yilmaz. (2017). Using Videos and 3D Animations for Conceptual Learning in Basic Computer Units. Contemporary Educational Technology, 8(4), 390-405. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1158165

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Quasi-experiment with two intact vocational high school classes, one using videos and 3D animations in class (CG, n = 15) and one as homework (HG, n = 19), and no untreated control group. Pre-to-post changes within each group are reported only as answer counts on a three-phase concept test, with no within-group significance test or effect size; the only inferential tests printed compare the two groups, which did not differ on the post-test (t(32) = 1.66, p = .10). The descriptive gains include CG incorrect answers on memory falling from 49 to 14 and hard disk correct answers rising from 5 to 29, and HG correct answers on processors rising from 13 to 51. The authors' discussion describes this as a "significant impact", but that wording is not backed by a printed test, and without an untreated group the gains cannot be attributed to the materials.

> "The change in pre -test and post-test results of the CG and HG students showed that us ing videos and 3D animations both in the classroom and in the homework activities provided significant impact in remedying misconceptions."
