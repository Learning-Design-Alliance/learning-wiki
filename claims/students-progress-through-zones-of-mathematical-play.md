# Middle-school students progress through zones of mathematical play in a designed digital game, taking varied conceptual paths to the same goal

Qualitative video/audio analysis of 14 middle-school students playing a designed fraction-based puzzle game found that nearly all progressed through at least three of the theory's five zones, that progression was consistently triggered by failure-feedback cycles, and that students used a variety of mathematical conceptualizations — not only the one the game was designed to evoke — to succeed.

## Subclaims
`q1 i1` Every participant reached at least Zone 3 (developing responsibility and causality), and all but one progressed further into Zones 4-5, illustrated by gameplay moments such as a student realizing "I chose 6. And six things came in" and forming an explicit hypothesis from it. [→ Williams-Pierce & Thevenow-Harrison 2021](#williams-pierce-thevenow-harrison-2021)

`q1 i1` Although the game was designed to evoke multiplication of fractions, players' actual reasoning varied to include proportional reasoning and other unstandardized strategies, and still succeeded — illustrative evidence for [Provocative Objects](../elements/provocative-objects.md)'s "legitimate alternative conceptual paths" characteristic. [→ Williams-Pierce & Thevenow-Harrison 2021](#williams-pierce-thevenow-harrison-2021)

`q1 i1` Across the analyzed vignettes, progression from one zone to the next was consistently associated with failure-feedback cycles (including a "permadeath" that forced a student to repeat an earlier puzzle), consistent with — but not an isolated causal test of — the theory's mechanism. [→ Williams-Pierce & Thevenow-Harrison 2021](#williams-pierce-thevenow-harrison-2021)

## Evidence

### Williams-Pierce & Thevenow-Harrison 2021

Williams-Pierce, C., & Thevenow-Harrison, J. T. (2021). Zones of mathematical play. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 30*(3), 509-527. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2021.1913167](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2021.1913167)

`q1 · illustrative qualitative case-study analysis of gameplay video/audio/screen-capture, iterative emergent coding; no control condition, no comparison games, no pre/post learning measures, small self-selected sample` · `i1 · descriptive, non-quantified pattern consistent with the proposed theory, useful for illustrating the zones construct but not for establishing a causal or generalizable effect` · `n=14 middle-school students (10 boys, 4 girls, self-selected volunteers), 14-30 minutes of gameplay each`

Fourteen middle-school students played *Rolly's Adventure*, a digital puzzle game designed as a [Provocative Objects](../elements/provocative-objects.md) instance targeting fraction multiplication, while researchers recorded video, audio, and screen capture. Coding was iterative and emergent rather than applying a pre-registered scheme, and the resulting zone-progression and conceptual-variety observations are illustrative vignettes (e.g., students "Ike," "Emmett," "Martin," and "Nathan") rather than a quantified analysis across the full sample.

## Discussion
The authors themselves are explicit that this is a conceptual/descriptive contribution rather than an experimentally validated one, and this claim should be read accordingly: it documents that the proposed zones and mechanism are *observable and illustratable* in one game with 14 students, not that the framework predicts outcomes, transfers to other games, or produces better mathematical learning than alternative designs. No pre/post mathematics assessment or transfer measure was collected, and the paper explicitly flags this as a limitation of its own design. Findings are best treated as motivating hypotheses for future, more rigorously designed studies rather than as established evidence.
