Evidence¶

Casey Inez Canfield & Baruch Fischhoff & Alex Davis 2019¶

Casey Inez Canfield & Baruch Fischhoff & Alex Davis. (2019). Better beware: comparing metacognition for phishing and legitimate emails. Metacognition and Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-019-09197-5

q2 · i? · associational · r2

Observational comparison in Experiment 2 linking laboratory metacognition scores to home-computer monitoring data from the Security Behavior Observatory, where malicious files were identified with VirusTotal.com aggregating over 70 anti-virus scanners.

"Most users (84/93 = 90%) had malicious files on their computer, which was more likely for those with poorer resolution, both overall, r(93) = −0.27, p = .009, and for phishing emails specifically, r(93) = −0.21, p = .04."

Casey Inez Canfield & Baruch Fischhoff & Alex Davis 2019 (2)¶

Casey Inez Canfield & Baruch Fischhoff & Alex Davis. (2019). Better beware: comparing metacognition for phishing and legitimate emails. Metacognition and Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-019-09197-5

q2 · i? · associational · r2

Logistic regression (Model 2, Table 3) predicting presence of malicious files in Experiment 2 (N = 91), controlling for total software downloads, which was a significant predictor in all models. Models 2 and 3 for all emails had the best fit and lowest AIC values.

"For all emails, overall resolution was a weakly significant predictor of the odds of malicious files. As resolution improved (i.e. increased), the odds of malicious files were lower."
