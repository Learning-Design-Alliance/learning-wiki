# End-of-program self-report surveys systematically flatter leadership programs via honeymoon and horizon effects

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` Participants report feeling most energized at a program's end, motivating positive self-reports (the honeymoon effect). [→ Hastings 2025](#hastings-2025)
`q1 i?` Pre/post self-report surveys act as a comfort blanket because participants most often say they are better than they were immediately after a program (the horizon effect). [→ Hastings 2025 (2)](#hastings-2025-2)

## Evidence

### Hastings 2025

Hastings, L. J., and D. Rosch. 2025. "Framing Metrics, Outcomes, and Assessment Overview in Leadership Programs." New Directions for Student Leadership 2025, no. 187: 85–91. https://doi.org/10.1002/yd.70007

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

The authors' retrospective interpretation of common LE assessment practice explains why end-of-program surveys yield positive data: "participants would feel the most energized by the program at the very end when they have an intrinsic motivation to report that their time was not wasted."

> "participants would feel the most energized by the program at the very end when they have an intrinsic motivation to report that their time was not wasted (e.g., the honeymoon effect, Rosch and Schwartz 2009)"

### Hastings 2025 (2)

Hastings, L. J., and D. Rosch. 2025. "Framing Metrics, Outcomes, and Assessment Overview in Leadership Programs." New Directions for Student Leadership 2025, no. 187: 85–91. https://doi.org/10.1002/yd.70007

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

The authors' interpretation of the field's reliance on pre/post self-report surveys notes that "people most often say they are 'better than they were' immediately following their participation in a formal program," with self-efficacy an aberration from this pattern.

> "the pre/post surveys became our "Linus blanket" because, other than some aberrations with self‐efficacy, people most often say they are "better than they were" immediately following their participation in a formal program (e.g., the horizon effect, Rosch and Schwartz 2009)."
