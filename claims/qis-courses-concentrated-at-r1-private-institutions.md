## Evidence

### Josephine C. Meyer 2024

Josephine C. Meyer, Gina Passante, and Bethany Wilcox. (2024). Disparities in access to US quantum information education. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.20.010131](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.20.010131)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Negative binomial regression of active QIS course counts across 456 US institutions (Type I NB model, Table II) found Carnegie classification the strongest predictor: students at typical non-research institutions have access to "nearly 9 times fewer QIS courses" than students at typical R1 universities.

> "all other factors being equal, a student at a typical non-research institution has access to nearly 9 times fewer QIS courses as a student at a typical R1 university, and 3 times fewer than a student at a typical R2."

### Josephine C. Meyer 2024 (2)

Josephine C. Meyer, Gina Passante, and Bethany Wilcox. (2024). Disparities in access to US quantum information education. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.20.010131](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.20.010131)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Descriptive analysis of the course census found QIS courses rare overall: the "median number of QIS courses identified at an institution is 0 and the mean is 0.63", indicating most institutions offer none.

> "It is worth noting that despite the recent surge in QIS coursework, the median number of QIS courses identified at an institution is 0 and the mean is 0.63, indicating that most institutions do not yet have any formalized QIS courses."
