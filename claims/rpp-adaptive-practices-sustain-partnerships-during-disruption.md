# Relational trust and rapid-response adaptation help research-practice partnerships survive acute disruption

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q1` argument or single case · `i1` small · n=21 contributors across 15 think-pieces, drawing on multiple named RPPs (e.g., Boston P-3 RPP, Houston Education Research Consortium)

When an acute disruption (e.g., a pandemic) hits, research-practice partnerships (RPPs) that had already built relational trust and embedded-researcher relationships could pivot to rapid, practically useful work — even at the cost of publishable rigor — and partners reported this strengthened rather than weakened the partnership.

## Subclaims
`q1 i1` Practitioners and researchers across 15 independently written reflections converged on relational trust, built before the disruption, as what "sustained" their partnerships when pre-planned research agendas had to be paused or abandoned. [→ Popa et al. 2023](#popa-et-al-2023)

`q1 i1` Partnerships that shifted to rapid-response formats (short surveys, dashboards) to answer practitioners' immediate questions reported this strengthened the partnership's perceived quality, even though the resulting work was not intended for peer-reviewed publication. [→ Popa et al. 2023](#popa-et-al-2023)

`q1 i0` Combining virtual meetings with in-person and asynchronous contact was reported as more effective for sustaining partnership relationships during disruption than defaulting to virtual-only communication. [→ Popa et al. 2023](#popa-et-al-2023)

## Evidence

### Popa et al. 2023

Popa, N., Anderson, E. R., Denner, J., McKenney, S., & Peurach, D. J. (2023). Belonging to a research-practice partnership: Lessons from 15 think-pieces about the COVID-19 pandemic and a call for action. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 32*(4-5), 682-704. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2023.2253529](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2023.2253529)

`q1 · qualitative synthesis of 15 practitioner/researcher think-pieces (no comparison group, no independent measurement)` · `i1 · descriptive, self-reported patterns across contributors, no quantified outcome` · `n=21 contributors across 15 think-pieces, drawing on multiple named RPPs (e.g., Boston P-3 RPP, Houston Education Research Consortium)`

This is a synthesis of independently written reflections from researchers and practitioners in established RPPs, describing how their partnerships adapted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Common threads included: pausing formal research agendas in favor of rapid surveys and dashboards that answered immediate district questions; relying on pre-existing [embedded researcher](../strategies/responsive-rpp-adaptation-during-disruption.md) relationships and trust rather than building new ones under pressure; and mixing communication channels (virtual, in-person, asynchronous) rather than defaulting to any single mode. The authors also propose "belonging" as a candidate unifying framework for why these adaptations worked, but explicitly flag this as a direction for future research rather than a tested finding.

## Discussion
This is the weakest-evidence claim in this ingest batch: a qualitative synthesis of self-selected practitioner reflections, with no comparison group, no independent verification, and no measurement of whether the reported "strengthened partnership quality" reflects an actual outcome change or a retrospective narrative constructed after the fact. The recommendations are plausible and consistent with prior RPP literature (Coburn, Penuel, Farrell), but this paper's own contribution is mainly a case-based illustration and a call to further study "belonging" as a construct, not new causal evidence. Treat the subclaims as reasonable practical guidance, not validated mechanisms.
