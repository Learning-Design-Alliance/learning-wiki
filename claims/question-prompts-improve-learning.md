# Question prompts improve learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Question prompts are questions embedded in learning materials, tasks, or dialogue that ask learners to respond before, during, or after instruction. They are hypothesized to improve learning by prompting active processing — retrieval, self-explanation, and connection to prior knowledge — rather than passive reading or listening.

## Discussion

**Proposed mechanisms.** Question prompts are typically justified on three grounds: they induce retrieval practice (answering requires reconstructing knowledge from memory), they trigger [activation](activation-improves-learning.md) of relevant prior knowledge, and they push learners toward elaborative processing similar to self-explanation. All three mechanisms are well established in the broader literature, but the strength of a prompt's effect depends heavily on whether learners actually generate a substantive answer rather than skipping or skimming the question.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Several conditions plausibly moderate the effect. Prompt type matters: questions requiring inference, application, or connection to prior knowledge are generally expected to do more work than verbatim recall questions, though recall questions may still aid retention. Timing matters: pre-questions can orient attention but may also act as advance organizers that narrow attention in ways that harm incidental learning of unprompted material — see [Advance organizers improve learning](advance-organizers-improve-learning.md); post-questions avoid this but arrive after the relevant text has been processed. Learner expertise matters: for novices, answering questions adds load to an already strained working memory, and poorly timed prompts could contribute to [cognitive overload](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md); for advanced learners, simple prompts may be redundant — a pattern consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md). Finally, prompts that go unanswered — because learners lack the knowledge, motivation, or feedback to answer them — likely produce little benefit and may inflate perceived difficulty.

**Open questions.** This page currently has no catalogued evidence entries. Key studies on pre-questions, post-questions, and elaborative prompts still need to be added before the claim's strength can be rated. Until then, treat the claim as a plausible synthesis rather than a validated effect. Priority questions for future evidence entries: (1) do pre-questions improve learning of the questioned material at the cost of incidental material? (2) do elaborative/why-questions outperform factual recall questions? (3) does requiring a written answer matter, or does merely encountering the question suffice?
