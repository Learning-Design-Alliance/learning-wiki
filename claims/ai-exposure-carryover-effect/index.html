Evidence¶

Sunny Yu 2026¶

Sunny Yu, Myra Cheng, Ahmad Jabbar, Ilia Sucholutsky, Katherine M. Collins, Dan Jurafsky, Robert D. Hawkins. (2026). The efficiency-gain illusion: People underestimate the rate of AI use and overestimate its benefits on simple tasks. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22687

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Pre-registered Study 3 (N=592) randomly assigned participants to complete two exposure tasks with AI, independently, or no tasks (control), then measured AI adoption on two easy test tasks. The effect held for both easy and difficult exposure variants.

"Participants who completed tasks with AI during the exposure phase chose AI assistance on 44.5% of subsequent tasks, compared to 27.7% among participants who completed the exposure tasks independently (β= 0.54,p <0.001)."

Sunny Yu 2026 (2)¶

Sunny Yu, Myra Cheng, Ahmad Jabbar, Ilia Sucholutsky, Katherine M. Collins, Dan Jurafsky, Robert D. Hawkins. (2026). The efficiency-gain illusion: People underestimate the rate of AI use and overestimate its benefits on simple tasks. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22687

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Study 3 measured time and effort calibration via 7-point agreement ratings after the test phase. AI exposure worsened time calibration but did not significantly affect effort calibration; qualitative analyses indicated time calibration drove choices.

"those who had previously completed tasks with AI reported lower agreement with the statement “It was faster to complete the tasks independently than using AI”. We reverse coded the value to operationalize the speedup illusion (β=−0.34,p<0.001; Fig. 4). We found no significant effect of previous completion mode on effort calibration (β= 0.08,p= 0.28)."
