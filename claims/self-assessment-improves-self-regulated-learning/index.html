Self Assessment Improves Self Regulated Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · quant-synthesis r? · q4 · i1 small · n=2,305 students across 19 studies

When learners appraise the quality of their own work against criteria, they gain the evaluative information needed to plan, monitor, and adjust their studying — the core operations of self-regulated learning.

Evidence¶

Panadero et al. 2017¶

Panadero, E., Jonsson, A., & Botella, J. (2017). Effects of self-assessment on self-regulated learning and self-efficacy: Four meta-analyses. Educational Research Review, 22, 74–98. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2017.08.004

q4 · random-effects meta-analysis (four sub-analyses) · i1 · small effect on Learning SRL, d=0.23; medium/non-significant on the other two SRL measures; large on self-efficacy, d=0.73 · n=2,305 students across 19 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

Four random-effects meta-analyses (Hedges & Olkin method) pooled 19 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of self-assessment interventions in K-12 and higher-education classrooms. For the "Learning SRL" outcome — measures of strategy use such as planning, monitoring and self-regulatory strategy deployment — 12 effect sizes from 369 students produced a combined effect of d=0.23, 95% CI [0.08, 0.39], a small but statistically significant positive effect. A second meta-analysis on "Negative SRL" (self-regulatory behaviors detrimental to learning, e.g. procrastination) found d=−0.65 (k=6, n=347; CI crossed zero), meaning self-assessment reduced these behaviors; a third on SRL measured qualitatively found d=0.43 (k=3, n=338; CI crossed zero, underpowered). A separate meta-analysis of 27 effect sizes on self-efficacy found a larger effect, d=0.73, 95% CI [0.39, 1.08], moderated by gender (larger for girls) and by whether the self-assessment intervention included a monitoring component.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. Self-assessment is theorized to support self-regulated learning by making performance standards explicit and by generating feedback that learners can act on without waiting for an external evaluator. It connects closely to assessment for learning, in which assessment information is used formatively rather than only to certify achievement. In self-regulated learning terms, self-assessment feeds the monitoring and reflection phases: learners who can accurately judge where their work stands relative to a standard are better positioned to set appropriate goals, select strategies, and persist or redirect effort — see self-regulated learning.

Boundary conditions to watch. Self-assessment quality depends heavily on the availability of clear criteria, exemplars, or rubrics; without external reference points, learners — especially novices — tend to overestimate their competence, and inaccurate self-estimates can lead to premature termination of study or misplaced practice effort. Training in self-assessment and calibration is generally treated in the literature as a prerequisite rather than an optional extra. Rubrics and worked exemplars help here by reducing the extraneous load of judging one's own work — see cognitive load reduction improves learning. Self-assessment also appears to benefit self-regulation more directly than it benefits task performance itself; gains in outcomes are typically mediated by improved strategy use rather than by direct effects on scores.

Calibration. A recurring theme in the literature is that self-assessment is only as useful as it is accurate. Miscalibrated learners — particularly low performers — may believe they have mastered material they have not, which undermines the monitoring function self-assessment is meant to serve. Interventions that pair self-assessment with external feedback or with comparison against exemplars tend to improve calibration, which in turn is expected to strengthen the downstream self-regulation benefits. This mirrors the broader pattern in which feedback improves learning only when it is actionable and tied to criteria learners understand.

Open questions. One synthesis is recorded: Panadero et al. (2017), whose four meta-analyses of 19 studies found a small positive effect on strategy use (d = 0.23, k = 12) and confidence intervals crossing zero for the other two SRL measures. No primary study and no study of calibration accuracy is recorded here yet. Open empirical questions include how durable the self-regulation gains are over time, whether effects differ across age groups and domains, and how self-assessment interacts with formative feedback practices.

Merged from “Self Assessment Against Criteria Supports Self Regulated Learning” (self-assessment-against-criteria-supports-self-regulated-learning): Mechanism. Self-regulated learning theory describes a cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection (Zimmerman, 2002; see Self-regulated learning). Self assessment against criteria operationalizes the self-reflection phase: criteria give learners a standard to compare their performance against, which makes self-monitoring accurate rather than impressionistic [+M]. Without explicit criteria, learners tend to overestimate their competence, and the resulting miscalibration undermines planning and effort allocation [~M]. Andrade (2010) frames self assessment as the mechanism by which students become "the definitive source of formative assessment" — internalizing the evaluative criteria that teachers otherwise apply for them. Rubric-based self assessment has been associated with improved learning and with more accurate self-regulatory judgments, particularly when students use the criteria before and during drafting rather than only after completion [+M] (Andrade & Valtcheva, 2009; Panadero & Alonso-Tapia, 2013). This positions self assessment as a form of assessment for learning rather than merely a grading convenience.

Boundary conditions. The quality of the criteria matters. Vague or holistic criteria invite leniency bias and self-serving judgments [~M]; specific, task-relevant criteria (e.g., analytic rubrics, quality checklists, comparison to annotated exemplars) support more accurate self-evaluation [+M]. Accuracy also improves when learners have prior exposure to exemplars of the target quality, and when self assessment is practiced repeatedly rather than used once [+M]. There is a known tension with feedback: if self assessment replaces external feedback entirely, learners may lack the information needed to close the gap between their judgment and the standard — self assessment works best alongside, not instead of, teacher or peer feedback [~M] (see Action-oriented feedback). A further boundary condition is that checklists used as evaluation tools can fail in certain online contexts (see Checklist evaluation ineffective online) [-M], so the format of the criteria is not neutral. Novices and younger learners are also more prone to leniency and miscalibration, so structured criteria and modeling of the self-assessment process matter most where self-regulation is weakest [~M].

Design implications. To realize the self-regulatory benefit, criteria should be introduced early enough to guide forethought — not only consulted after work is complete — and the self-assessment step should ask learners to identify evidence in their own work against each criterion, not merely assign a score. Co-constructing criteria with students is a promising but under-documented variant. Where self assessment is used, pair it with a mechanism for acting on the judgment (revision, goal-setting) so that accurate monitoring translates into regulated effort.

Open questions. Of the works cited in this merged paragraph, only Panadero's group is recorded under Evidence (as the 2017 meta-analyses); Zimmerman (2002), Andrade (2010), Andrade & Valtcheva (2009) and Panadero & Alonso-Tapia (2013) are not, so the bracketed tags above rest on no entry on this page. Key moderators to document include learner age and domain expertise, whether criteria are co-constructed with learners, and whether effects on self-regulation transfer beyond the assessed task.
