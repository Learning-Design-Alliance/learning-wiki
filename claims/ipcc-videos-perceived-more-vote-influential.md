## Evidence

### Carter 2016

Carter, B. E., and Wiles, J. R. (2016). Comparing effects of comedic and authoritative video presentations on student knowledge and attitudes about climate change. Bioscene, 42(1), 16-24. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1103782

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

In-class clicker responses immediately after viewing in the quasi-experiment; the Comedy and IPCC groups differed significantly, with IPCC videos rated more vote-influential (t = 3.975, p < 0.0001). Shown in Figure 1; no Control comparison was possible.

> "There was a significant difference between the Comedy and IPCC groups in how influential students thought the video could be on the way a person votes, with the authoritative videos from the IPCC perceived to be more influential. A Welch two sample t-test indicates a significant difference in the means (t = 3.975, p < 0.0001)."

### Carter 2016 (2)

Carter, B. E., and Wiles, J. R. (2016). Comparing effects of comedic and authoritative video presentations on student knowledge and attitudes about climate change. Bioscene, 42(1), 16-24. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1103782

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

The same in-class perception data showed no perceived difference between the video sets for changing attitudes or voting on GCC-related issues, qualifying the voting-influence difference.

> "Students perceived both video sets to be equally influential in terms of how they may change people’s attitudes or the way people might vote on issues related to GCC."
