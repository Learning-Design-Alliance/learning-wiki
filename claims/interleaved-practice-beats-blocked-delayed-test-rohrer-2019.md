## Subclaims
`q4 i3` One month after an interleaved review, the interleaved group outscored the blocked group 61% vs. 38%, d = 0.83. [→ Doug Rohrer 2019](#doug-rohrer-2019)
`q4 i?` A positive interleaving effect was observed for each of the 15 teachers (ds = 0.23 to 1.48). [→ Doug Rohrer 2019](#doug-rohrer-2019)

`q4 i?` Effects differed by kind of problem (d = 0.86, 0.63, 0.40 and 0.34), but the authors call these sizes misleading: they fell in the order in which the blocked group had practised each kind, so the kind the blocked group practised earliest, and so longest before the test, showed the largest effect. Averaged over the four kinds, the time from last practice to test was nearly equal in the two groups. [→ Rohrer et al. 2020](#rohrer-et-al-2020)

Pre-registered cluster randomized controlled trial: 54 seventh-grade classes nested in five schools, 787 analyzed students, four months of mostly interleaved or mostly blocked worksheets, then a common interleaved review and an unannounced test. The article reports the printed effect size d = 0.83 with 95% CI [0.68, 0.97].

> "On the test, the interleaved group outscored the blocked group by a large margin. Table 6 lists descriptive measures. The effect size was large, Cohen’s d = 0.83, 95% CI = [0.68, 0.97]"

### Rohrer et al. 2020

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, C.-N. (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 112*(1), 40–52. [doi:10.1037/edu0000367](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)

`q4 · pre-registered cluster randomised trial` · `i? · the per-kind effect sizes are printed but the authors say they are confounded with time since practice` · `n=787 students in 54 classes` · `causal · r3`

The trial ran in 2017–2018 in five middle schools of one Florida district, in an honours seventh-grade mathematics course taught by 15 teachers with no prior link to the authors or training in the intervention. Classes were randomised within teacher (28 interleaved, 26 blocked); 69 of 856 consenting students (8%) left the course or missed the test, at similar rates in both groups, and the groups did not differ on the previous year's state mathematics test (345 vs. 346). Each of eight worksheets had eight problems: the four kinds that appeared on the test (graphs of proportional relationships, inequalities, simplifying expressions, circles) were interleaved for one group and blocked for the other, while unrelated filler problems ran the other way, so the comparison is mostly interleaved against mostly blocked. Both groups then had the same interleaved review, and the test, given about a month later (33 days on average), was scored blind to condition by two raters (α = .89). Students saw the solutions and corrected their own errors, so no practice-performance measure exists. The authors list four caveats: interleaved practice probably takes students longer, and time on task was not measured; the benefit may be smaller at short test delays; students likely had some blocked practice when each skill was first taught, so the data do not support avoiding blocked practice entirely; and every study of interleaved mathematics practice gave corrective feedback, which may be necessary. They attribute the large effect partly to the spacing and retrieval practice that interleaving brings with it. Pre-registration, materials and data: https://osf.io/pfeg4/.
