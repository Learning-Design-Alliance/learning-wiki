## Evidence

### Rowland 2019

Rowland, A. A., Dounas-Frazer, D. R., Ríos, L., Lewandowski, H. J., & Corwin, L. A. (2019). Using the Life Grid Interview Technique in Science Education Research. [doi:10.1186/s40594-019-0186-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-019-0186-z)

`q1 · i?` · `qualitative · r1`

The authors' qualitative judgment across both implementations: facilitating "rich descriptions of respondent narratives was perhaps the most important function of the life grid," supported by excerpts where respondents cross-referenced columns and reflected on blank entries. No effect size is reported.

> "Overall, we found that using the life grid to facilitate rich descriptions of respondent narratives was perhaps the most important function of the life grid in both the biology and physics studies."

### Rowland 2019 (2)

Rowland, A. A., Dounas-Frazer, D. R., Ríos, L., Lewandowski, H. J., & Corwin, L. A. (2019). Using the Life Grid Interview Technique in Science Education Research. [doi:10.1186/s40594-019-0186-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-019-0186-z)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

A contrasting result the article reports from Bell (2005), who found interviews that were relatively quick and devoid of detail and recommended the grid only for factual data. The authors attribute the incongruity to differences in grid size, indicator specificity, and facilitation techniques.

> "In using the life grid technique, Bell (2005) felt that the linear and event-centered structure of the grid discouraged respondents from providing rich details about their experiences."
