## Evidence

### Alex Maries and Chandralekha Singh 2016

Alex Maries and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Should Students be Provided Diagrams or Asked to Draw Them While Solving Introductory Physics Problems?. Originally published 2010. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1601.04357](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1601.04357)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Comparison of three recitation-quiz intervention groups (Group 1 told to draw, Group 2 given a diagram, Group 3 comparison) on two electrostatics problems in an algebra-based course of 111 students. Group 2 averages were "lower by roughly 20% compared to the other groups" and ANOVA t-tests were performed. No effect size is printed, so impact is unquantified.

> "It is evident from Table 2 that students who were given the diagram performed significantly worse than all the others; their averages are lower by roughly 20% compared to the other groups."

### Alex Maries and Chandralekha Singh 2016 (2)

Alex Maries and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Should Students be Provided Diagrams or Asked to Draw Them While Solving Introductory Physics Problems?. Originally published 2010. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1601.04357](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1601.04357)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

ANOVA between groups with t-tests printed p-values: for problem 1, p = 0.015 (Groups 1-2), p < 0.001 (Groups 2-3), p = 0.193 (Groups 1-3); for problem 2, p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p = 0.342. The authors note "how small the p values are".

> "Table 3 shows that Group 2 (which was given the diagram) did significantly worse than the other two groups. More noteworthy is how small the p values are (three of them being less than 0.001)."
