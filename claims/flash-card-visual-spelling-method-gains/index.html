Evidence¶

Winch 1913¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(10), 579–592. doi:10.1037/h0076021

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Subsidiary experiment, third method, with Standard I boys: ten-minute daily lessons, each word exposed twice and written from memory twice, harder words given four exposures. The author notes the method was costly in time and complicated, and that boys surreptitiously muttered, adding articulatory and auditory factors.

"On the following Monday morning, December 16th, the whole list of 30 words was dictated. The average number correctly spelt was 20.8 and every boy in the class was able to spell some words correctly."

Winch 1913 (2)¶

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. Journal of Educational Psychology, 4(10), 579–592. doi:10.1037/h0076021

q2 · i? · design · r2

Subsidiary experiment, second method: same flash-card procedure but with twenty skeleton words completed on the blackboard on days four and five. The Head Master concluded "the 'skeleton' words were of little use" and discarded them; some weaker boys spelt better than ever before.

"On the following Mon- day morning the whole list was dictated. There was an aver- age number of correct spellings amounting to 17.7."

Related Claims¶

  • For Standard IV boys (average age 11), a combined articulatory-auditory-visual spelling method with directed attention yields higher dictation scores than a silent visual method without directed attention — related
  • A combined visual-motor-articulatory-auditory spelling method with teacher-directed attention produces better spelling than a predominantly visual silent method in young low-ability boys — related
  • A head-to-head teaching contest between a primarily visual method and a combined method produced nearly identical averages (22.9 vs 23.0) and could not decide between the methods because of uncontrolled confounds — related
  • The two word lists were confirmed to be of equal difficulty before practice, with a correlation of +.93 between performance on the two lists — related
  • The superiority of the combined directed method held across all ability levels, with the advantage present in every section of a grouped correlation table — related
  • Both directed multisensory and silent visual spelling methods produced large improvement from baseline after 40 minutes of practice spread over four days — related
  • Across Standards II and IV (ages 8 to 11) in the same school, the spelling method with pronounced auditory, articulatory, and visual factors plus directed attention proved definitely superior to a more purely visual method with less directed attention — related
  • Reviewer credits the method with directness, concentration from flash-cards, valuable action responses, and stimulating effect — related
