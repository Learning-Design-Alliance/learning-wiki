## Evidence

### Siegler 1991

Robert S. Siegler and Kevin Crowley. (1991). The Microgenetic Method: A Direct Means for Studying Cognitive Development. American Psychologist, Vol. 46, No. 6, 606-620. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606

`q3 · i?` · `review · r2`

Trial-by-trial analysis across all 11 weeks of the microgenetic experiment found zero use of illegitimate strategies such as counting the first addend twice, raising the question of what constrained strategy generation.

> "not one child ever used a strategy that violated the principles underlying addition. Several potential illegitimate strategies were superficially similar to legitimate approaches and therefore could easily have been tried."

### Siegler 1991 (2)

Robert S. Siegler and Kevin Crowley. (1991). The Microgenetic Method: A Direct Means for Studying Cognitive Development. American Psychologist, Vol. 46, No. 6, 606-620. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Theoretical account (type e) offered by the authors: the goal sketch directs searches toward procedures meeting the goals and away from illegitimate ones. Consistent evidence cited: five-year-olds not yet using min nonetheless judged it superior to strategies violating addition goals (Reeve & Humpal, 1989).

> "The strategy generation process was constrained by a goal sketch, which specified the hierarchy of objectives that a satisfactory strategy needed to meet."
