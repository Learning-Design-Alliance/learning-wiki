# Instruction can harm learners in ways its performance data do not show.

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 source · `q1` theoretical argument with illustrative cases · no effect size

The claim is about effects a design produces beyond what it was built to produce, and about the reason those effects go unseen: the evidence designers usually review measures performance, not the learner's experience of the instruction.

## Subclaims

`q1 i?` Design decisions have enduring effects on learners that performance data do not reveal, and without deliberate reflection those unintended effects can harm learners. [→ Osguthorpe et al. 2018](#osguthorpe-et-al-2018)

`q1 · theoretical argument with illustrative cases` · `i? · no effect size` · `n=N/A`

A philosophical argument, first published in *Educational Technology* in 2003, that instructional design is a moral endeavour. Its central case is a superintendent who, thirty years on, still recalled the shame of walking to the front of her fifth-grade class to take the purple, lowest-level reader from a colour-coded reading kit. The authors pair it with a training unit that counted a trainee's wrong answer as a "defect" in the person. They do not think either designer intended harm: "that is precisely the point: Instruction leads to unintended results, and without careful reflection, those results can harm learners." The authors mention ongoing studies (reflections from 86 college students and 27 sixth graders, and interviews with 9 designers) but report them only as quotations, so the claim rests on the argument.

## Discussion

**What the argument establishes and what it does not.** It shows that the harm is possible and that the usual evidence is blind to it. It does not measure how common such effects are, or which design features produce them. The reading-kit case is one person's recollection, offered as an illustration rather than as data.

**The remedy is a practice, not a rule.** The authors propose reflexive judgment, meaning deliberate reflection on what is right and good in a design decision, developed through five designer consciences. See [Moral Dimensions of Instructional Design](../theories/moral-dimensions-of-instructional-design.md). A designer applying this would check a design for visible sorting, labelling or public failure that a learner might carry away, even where the performance data are good.

**A testable edge.** Visible ability grouping and labelling is the most concrete mechanism in the argument. It is also one that empirical work on belonging and identity threat can speak to, which would be the place to look for evidence that tests this claim.
