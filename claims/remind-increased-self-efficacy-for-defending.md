# Playing REMind significantly increased children's perceived self-efficacy for defending peers

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · design `r2` · `q2` · `i2` medium

## Subclaims
`q2 i2` Children's perceived self-efficacy for defending increased significantly from pre-test to post-test after playing REMind, with a medium-to-large effect size. [→ Elaheh Sanoubari 2026](#elaheh-sanoubari-2026)

## Evidence

### Elaheh Sanoubari 2026

Elaheh Sanoubari, Neil Fernandes, Keith Rebello, Alicia Pan, Andrew Houston, and Kerstin Dautenhahn. (2026). Play-Testing REMind: Evaluating an Educational Robot-Mediated Role-Play Game. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00300 [doi:10.48550/arxiv.2604.00300](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2604.00300)

`q2 · i2` · `design · r2`

Pre/post paired-samples t-test in the play-testing study with 18 children aged 9-10. Self-efficacy was measured with one sliding-scale item asking "How easy or hard would it be for you to defend the victim". Means rose from 39.2 to 53.9, with "Cohen's d = 0.74".

> "Children's perceived self-efficacy significantly increased from pre-test (M = 39.2, SD = 20.5) to post-test (M = 53.9, SD = 21.3), t(17) = 3.12, p = .003, following the intervention. Cohen's d = 0.74 indicated a medium-to-large effect size."
