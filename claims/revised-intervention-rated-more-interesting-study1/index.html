Yeager 2016¶

Yeager, D. S., Romero, C., Paunesku, D., Hulleman, C. S., Schneider, B., Hinojosa, C., Lee, H. Y., O'Brien, J., Flint, K., Roberts, A., Trott, J., Greene, D., Walton, G. M., & Dweck, C. S. (2016). Using Design Thinking to Improve Psychological Interventions: The Case of the Growth Mindset During the Transition to High School. Journal of Educational Psychology, 108(3), 374–391. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000098

q3 · i? · design · r3

Fidelity measures in the Study 1 randomized comparison: students in both conditions rated interest and perceived learning on 5-point scales. The revised version was "rated as more interesting, t(6454)=4.44, p<.001" and higher on perceived learning; no effect size printed. Distraction did not differ between conditions.

"Next, the revised intervention was rated as more interesting, t(6454)=4.44, p<.001, and also as more likely to cause participants to feel as though they learned something, t(6454)=6.25, p<.001. This means the design process was successful in making the new intervention engaging."
