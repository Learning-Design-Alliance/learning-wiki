Prior Knowledge Determines New Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · quant-synthesis r3 · q4 · i3 large · n=8,776 effect sizes

New learning is built on what the learner already knows: existing knowledge structures determine what can be understood, how new information is organized, and where misconceptions will interfere. This is a foundational premise of cognitive load theory and schema-based accounts of learning rather than a single experimental finding — its Evidence section therefore awaits a dedicated synthesis of the supporting literature.

Evidence¶

Simonsmeier et al. 2022¶

Simonsmeier, B. A., Flaig, M., Deiglmayr, A., Schalk, L., & Schneider, M. (2022). Domain-specific prior knowledge and learning: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychologist, 57(1), 31–54. doi:10.1080/00461520.2021.1939700

q4 · meta-analysis · i3 · large stability correlation, r=.534; near-zero for normalized gains, r=-.059 · n=8,776 effect sizes · quant-synthesis · r3

A meta-analysis of 8,776 effect sizes examined the relationship between domain-specific prior knowledge (measured at Time 1) and knowledge outcomes measured later, across studies spanning many ages, domains and designs. Two distinct relationships were separated: the stability of individual differences (correlation between pretest and posttest knowledge scores) was high, r+ = .534 — learners who know more at the start tend to know more at the end, supporting the claim that new learning builds on and is strongly predicted by prior knowledge. But the predictive power of prior knowledge for normalized learning gains (how much a learner improves, controlling for starting point) was low and centered near zero, r+ = -.059, with a large 95% prediction interval of [-.688, .621] — meaning the direction and size of prior knowledge's effect on gains varies enormously across contexts, sometimes helping and sometimes hurting. The authors argue this variability falsifies both "knowledge is power" and "the effect of prior knowledge is negligible" as general statements, and calls for research on the conditions (moderators) under which prior knowledge helps, hurts, or does nothing — directly supporting this page's Discussion section on expertise reversal, moderators and boundary conditions rather than a single uniform effect.

Discussion¶

This claim is one of the most robust premises in educational psychology, but its strength is also its boundary condition: because prior knowledge acts as a filter and scaffold, learners with different starting points respond differently to the same instruction. The same instructional treatment is rarely optimal across the prior-knowledge distribution [~S].

Activation matters, not just possession. Having relevant knowledge is not sufficient; it must be retrieved and connected to the new material. Deliberately prompting recall of relevant prior knowledge at the start of instruction improves comprehension and integration [~M] — see Activation improves learning. and the strategy of activating prior knowledge. Advance organizers work by providing a bridge between existing knowledge and new content [~M] — see Advance organizers improve learning.

Misconceptions resist correction. When prior knowledge is inaccurate, it does not simply get overwritten; learners often assimilate new information into flawed schemas or revert to misconceptions after instruction. Productive conceptual change typically requires creating cognitive disequilibrium — surfacing the conflict between the learner's prediction and the evidence — rather than mere re-explanation [~M].

Expertise reversal. The same prior knowledge that enables learning changes what instruction is helpful. High-knowledge learners are burdened by scaffolds designed for novices, and low-knowledge learners are overwhelmed by instruction designed for experts [~S] — see expertise reversal effect. This makes prior-knowledge assessment a prerequisite for sequencing decisions such as fading worked examples.

Working-memory interaction. Prior knowledge reduces the working-memory load of new material by allowing chunking and automatisation; learners without relevant schemas must assemble everything from parts [~S] — see chunking reduces working memory load. and cognitive load theory.

Design implications. Because prior knowledge governs what instruction can accomplish, designers should (a) assess prior knowledge before selecting treatments, (b) activate relevant knowledge at the point of need rather than assuming it will be retrieved spontaneously, and (c) diagnose misconceptions early, since unaddressed inaccuracies distort how new content is encoded. Sequencing decisions — from advance organizers to fading of worked examples — should be driven by measured learner starting points, not by a one-size-fits-all progression.

Open questions. How durable misconception correction is across time, and how precisely prior knowledge must match new content (versus merely being analogically related), remain active areas. Analogies can bridge partial gaps [~M] — see analogical reasoning improves transfer.
