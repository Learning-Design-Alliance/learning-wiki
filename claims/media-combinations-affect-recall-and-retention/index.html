Different media combinations significantly affect the recall and retention of information¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r? · q3 · n=37

How information is distributed across media channels (e.g., text, images, narration) changes how well learners recall and retain it, in line with the predictions of Dual Coding Theory.

In an extended in-class experiment, 37 students studied the same content presented in different media combinations (varying text and image presentations in a computer-based environment). Recall and retention measures showed that outcomes differed across media combinations, and — in the author's words — "in all cases the predictions of Dual Coding theory are borne out": combinations that supported both verbal and imagistic coding produced better recall than those that did not.

Discussion¶

Theoretical framing. The finding is interpreted through Dual Coding Theory: presenting information so that learners can encode it both verbally and visually creates two retrieval routes, improving recall and retention. This aligns with the broader multimedia literature on combining words and pictures, and with chunking and cognitive load management as complementary mechanisms for keeping combined presentations within working-memory limits.

Single-study limitation. The claim currently rests on one small (n=37) classroom experiment reported at a conference, in a single domain, without pre-registration. The broad claim that media combinations "significantly" affect recall should be treated as provisional until replicated with larger samples and controlled designs.

Boundary conditions. Media combinations are not uniformly beneficial: adding a second channel can also add load. Whether a combination helps depends on the material, the learner's experience, and how the channels are integrated — consistent with the expertise-reversal pattern documented for worked examples and other supports.
