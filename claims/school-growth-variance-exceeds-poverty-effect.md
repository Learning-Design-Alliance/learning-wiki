# Growth rates varied tremendously between schools regardless of poverty, with top-quartile schools producing more than double the growth of the worst quartile

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Michael Dahlin and Beth Tarasawa 2013

Michael Dahlin and Beth Tarasawa. (2013). A Level Playing Field? How College Readiness Standards Change the Accountability Game. Northwest Evaluation Association. https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/a-level-playing-field-college-readiness-standards-whitepaper/

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Descriptive and HLM growth analyses of the school-defined cohorts show large between-school spread in growth among the top-10% students. The report concludes that "the quality of the school mattered a great deal more than the poverty rate of the school in determining student growth." No effect sizes are printed.

> "Within high- and low-poverty schools, growth rates varied tremendously; some schools showed extraordinary growth and others showed abysmal growth."

### Michael Dahlin and Beth Tarasawa 2013 (2)

Michael Dahlin and Beth Tarasawa. (2013). A Level Playing Field? How College Readiness Standards Change the Accountability Game. Northwest Evaluation Association. https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/a-level-playing-field-college-readiness-standards-whitepaper/

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Comparison of the highest-growth high-poverty schools with the lowest-growth low-poverty schools shows the top high-poverty schools "entirely erased and surpassed their achievement gap" at both elementary (22 vs 27 schools) and middle school (13 vs 16 schools) levels, per the report's figures and foreword.

> "the 22 high-poverty elementary schools with the best growth rates entirely erased and surpassed their achievement gap relative to the 27 low-poverty schools with the lowest growth rates. And the 13 high-poverty middle schools with the highest rates of growth closed and surpassed their achievement gap relative to the 16 low-poverty schools with the lowest growth rates."
