# SEL Programs Improve Behavior And Achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i0`–`i1`

School-based social-emotional learning (SEL) programs that explicitly teach self-regulation, social skills, and responsible decision-making improve students' behavioral outcomes and academic achievement.

## Subclaims

`q4 i1` Across 213 universal school-based SEL programs (K–12), participants outperformed controls on conduct problems, positive social behaviour and academic performance at post-test, with small effects (ES 0.22–0.27), and academic gains were significant only when school staff delivered the program. [→ Durlak et al. 2011](#durlak-et-al-2011)

`q4 i?` A contemporary meta-analysis of 424 studies (2008–2020, 53 countries) reports significantly improved behaviours, school functioning and academic achievement for students in universal school-based SEL interventions; effect sizes were not read here (abstract only). [→ Cipriano et al. 2023](#cipriano-et-al-2023)

`q3 i0` Restricted to RCTs and rigorous quasi-experiments reporting quantitative achievement outcomes (2011–2021), SEL programs' effect on overall academic achievement is positive but negligible (g = 0.08), and smaller still in randomised studies (g = 0.063), which qualifies the size of the achievement claim. [→ Zhao and Sang 2025](#zhao-and-sang-2025)

## Evidence

### Durlak et al. 2011

Durlak, J. A., Weissberg, R. P., Dymnicki, A. B., Taylor, R. D., & Schellinger, K. B. (2011). The Impact of Enhancing Students' Social and Emotional Learning: A Meta-Analysis of School-Based Universal Interventions. *Child Development, 82*(1), 405–432. [doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i1 · small effect, ES=0.27 academic performance, ES=0.22 conduct problems` · `n=213 studies (270,034 students)`

A meta-analysis of 213 universal, school-based social and emotional learning programs for kindergarten through high school students, compared with control groups at post-test. All six outcome categories improved significantly: SEL skills (ES 0.57), attitudes (0.23), positive social behaviour (0.24), conduct problems (0.22), emotional distress (0.24) and academic performance (0.27, from 35 interventions), which the authors translate into an 11-percentile-point gain in achievement. Academic performance improved significantly only when school staff ran the program (classroom teacher delivery ES 0.34; non-school personnel ES 0.12, not significant). Effects were moderated by use of four recommended skill-development practices (SAFE) and by reported implementation problems; the 33 studies with follow-up at least six months later still showed significant effects, including academic performance (ES 0.32, k = 8).

### Cipriano et al. 2023

Cipriano, C., Strambler, M. J., Naples, L. H., Ha, C., Kirk, M., Wood, M., Sehgal, K., Zieher, A. K., Eveleigh, A., McCarthy, M., Funaro, M., Ponnock, A., Chow, J. C., & Durlak, J. (2023). The state of evidence for social and emotional learning: A contemporary meta-analysis of universal school-based SEL interventions. *Child Development, 94*(5), 1181–1204. [doi:10.1111/cdev.13968](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13968)

`q4 · systematic review and meta-analysis` · `i? · effect sizes not in the abstract read` · `n=424 studies (575,361 students)`

A systematic review and meta-analysis of universal school-based SEL interventions for kindergarten to 12th-grade students, covering studies from 2008 through 2020: 424 studies from 53 countries, 252 distinct interventions and 575,361 students. Compared with control conditions, participating students showed significantly improved skills, attitudes, behaviours, school climate and safety, peer relationships, school functioning and academic achievement. Substantial heterogeneity in program content, features, context and implementation quality moderated outcomes. Only the abstract was read, so no pooled effect size is recorded here.

### Zhao and Sang 2025

Zhao, Y., & Sang, B. (2025). The Effect of Social–Emotional Learning Programs on Elementary and Middle School Students' Academic Achievement: A Meta-Analytic Review. *Behavioral Sciences, 15*(11), 1527. [doi:10.3390/bs15111527](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15111527)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i0 · negligible effect, g=0.08` · `n=22 studies (24,510 students)`

A meta-analysis of 22 studies (17 universal, 5 targeted programs; 2011–2021) of SEL programs for elementary and middle school students, limited to randomised or rigorous quasi-experimental designs with quantitative achievement outcomes. SEL students showed higher overall academic achievement than controls, but the effect was very small (g = 0.08, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.12), similar across English language arts, mathematics and science, and larger for GPA (g = 0.33, from 6 effect sizes). Quasi-experimental studies showed significantly larger effects (g = 0.27, k = 12) than randomised trials (g = 0.063, k = 64), and 20 of the 22 studies were in elementary schools, so middle-school conclusions are preliminary. This does not contradict the claim's direction, but it indicates the academic benefit in rigorous recent trials is much smaller than the Durlak et al. (2011) estimate.

## Discussion

**Scope and mechanism.** The claim covers universal, school-based SEL curricula (e.g., sequenced lessons teaching emotion regulation, empathy, and interpersonal problem-solving), not one-off workshops or purely punitive behavior-management systems. The proposed mechanism is that improved self-regulation and classroom climate free attentional and motivational resources for learning — connecting to [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) and [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md). A second, complementary pathway runs through classroom climate: students who trust peers and teachers participate more, persist longer, and are less absent, which is why SEL is often framed as a precondition for [collaborative learning](../patterns/collaborative-learning.md) rather than a competitor to academic instruction.

**Moderators to watch.** Effects likely depend on implementation fidelity, dosage (sustained multi-year programs vs. brief units), and developmental fit. Programs delivered by classroom teachers with ongoing practice and integration into academic content are generally expected to outperform pull-out, scripted-only delivery. Cultural responsiveness of program materials is an open question for generalization across contexts. Learner age is another plausible moderator: self-regulation instruction may show its largest returns in early grades, when regulatory habits are still forming, while adolescent programs may need to be framed as autonomy-respecting rather than corrective to avoid reactance — consistent with [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md).

**Boundary conditions.** SEL is not a substitute for explicit academic instruction; programs that consume substantial instructional time without integration may show weaker academic effects. Poorly implemented SEL can also be perceived as inauthentic, undermining the classroom climate it aims to build. A related risk is "curricular dilution": if SEL time displaces [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)-informed academic instruction without producing compensating gains in engagement, net achievement effects can approach zero.

**Open questions.** How durable are behavioral gains after programs end? Do academic effects operate directly or indirectly through improved behavior and engagement? These require longitudinal and mediation studies not yet represented in this page's evidence base.

**Evidence status.** No studies are currently catalogued on this page. The claim is widely reported in the SEL literature (notably meta-analytic syntheses of universal school-based programs), but per wiki policy no specific effect sizes or citations are asserted here until verified Evidence entries are added.
