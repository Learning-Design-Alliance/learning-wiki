Interdisciplinary humanities units improve students' interdisciplinary competences¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r2 · q2 · i2 medium · n=586 students (380 intervention, 206 comparison) across 17 classes and 5 schools for the quantitative measure; n=128 students, 4 classes, 2 schools for the qualitative discourse substudy (from a broader project of 142 teachers and 1,109 students across 10 schools)

Middle-school students who completed interdisciplinary humanities units (combining subjects like history, Bible, and literature around a shared theme) showed statistically significant, medium-sized gains in a novel measure of interdisciplinary competence — comprehension, integration, and synthesis across disciplines — compared to a non-randomized comparison group of students in the same schools, with knowledge-building and identity work appearing in classroom discourse in roughly equal measure.

q2 i2 On an Interdisciplinary Competences Assessment (six essay questions scored 0-3, Cronbach's alpha .70-.74), the intervention group's overall score improved significantly from pretest to posttest (median 5→7 on an 18-point scale; Wilcoxon V=3439.5, p<.001), while the non-randomized comparison group's change was not significant (p=.155); a mixed-effects model found a significant time-by-group interaction for overall competence, F(1, 401.5)=7.4, p=.007. → Novis-Deutsch et al. 2024

q2 i1 Of the six specific competence sub-actions measured, divergent creative synthesis showed the largest intervention-group effect (rank-biserial correlation ≈.61, p<.001) and the only other individually significant time-by-group interaction (F(1, 460.7)=5.4, p=.020); other sub-actions (analogies, contrasts, cross-disciplinary application, convergent integration, reflective synthesis) showed smaller, still-significant within-group pretest-posttest gains for the intervention group only. → Novis-Deutsch et al. 2024

q2 i1 In a separate qualitative substudy, coded classroom discourse from 143 episodes across 17 recorded lessons showed knowledge-building talk (perspective-taking, integrative reasoning) and identity-work talk (personal/cultural/values references) occurring in "nearly equal proportions," and two teachers' classrooms illustrated different balances — one more knowledge-building-focused, one more identity-work-focused — depending on how much the teacher allowed organic divergence from the lesson plan. → Novis-Deutsch et al. 2024

Evidence¶

Novis-Deutsch et al. 2024¶

Novis-Deutsch, N., Cohen, E., Alexander, H., Rahamian, L., Gavish, U., Glick, O., Yehi-Shalom, O., Marcus, G., & Mann, A. (2024). Interdisciplinary learning in the humanities: Knowledge building and identity work. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 33(2), 284-322. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2024.2346915

q2 · quasi-experimental pre/post design with a non-randomized comparison group, plus a nested qualitative discourse-analysis substudy · i2 · statistically significant time-by-group interaction with medium effect sizes (rank-biserial ≈.2-.6), though authors themselves describe the absolute magnitude of change as small · n=586 students (380 intervention, 206 comparison) across 17 classes and 5 schools for the quantitative measure; n=128 students, 4 classes, 2 schools for the qualitative discourse substudy (from a broader project of 142 teachers and 1,109 students across 10 schools) · causal · r2

Middle-school teaching teams across 10 Israeli schools designed and taught two-to-four-subject interdisciplinary humanities units (e.g., "Courage," "Religious Leadership," "Heritage and Family History") over a multi-month professional-development and implementation program. A novel Interdisciplinary Competences Assessment, developed and piloted for this study, measured six specific actions across three competences (comprehension, integration, synthesis) via paired-text essay questions scored by trained raters (Krippendorff's alpha .74-.94). Comparing 380 intervention students to 206 comparison students in the same schools (not randomly assigned) across a 4-to-6-month interval, the intervention group improved significantly on the overall measure and on five of six sub-actions, while the comparison group's changes were not statistically significant. A parallel qualitative analysis of classroom video/audio found that knowledge-building and identity-work discourse occurred in comparable proportions, with teacher instructional style (tightly guided inquiry vs. open, personally-responsive discussion) shaping which was more prominent in a given classroom.

Discussion¶

This is the strongest quantitative evidence in this ingest batch — a real pre/post comparison-group design with a validated, reliable measure and reported effect sizes, rather than a purely qualitative case study. However, it is explicitly a quasi-experiment, not a randomized controlled trial: students were not randomly assigned to intervention/comparison conditions, so unmeasured differences between the schools/classes that did and did not implement the units could confound the result. The authors themselves flag several limitations worth preserving here: the ICA is a preliminary measure (a factor analysis explained only 40-43% of variance, suggesting the six items do not fully capture a single unified construct); the "small magnitude of change" on the raw 18-point scale, despite being statistically significant, led the authors to conclude "further pedagogical development is needed"; and the intervention ran during a COVID-shortened period, limiting duration. Evidence tier is set to q2/evidence_strength: moderate rather than q3/strong specifically because of the non-random assignment and the authors' own caveats about the measure's preliminary validity.
