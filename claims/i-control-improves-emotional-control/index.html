Evidence¶

Smith et al. 2017¶

Stephen W. Smith, Ann P. Daunic, James Algina, Donna L. Pitts, Kristen L. Merrill, Michelle M. Cumming, and Courtney Allen. (2017). Self-Regulation for Students With Emotional and Behavioral Disorders: Preliminary Effects of the I Control Curriculum. Journal of Emotional and Behavioral Disorders. https://doi.org/10.1177/1063426616661702

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Student self-report outcome from the Mplus pre–post pilot analysis; the ERICA Emotional Control main effect was significant (z = −2.39, p = .017, ES = −.40 in Table 2). The article attributes the result to the curriculum's emotion regulation unit.

"We found that all treatment students, regardless of pretest score, who were taught I Control reported better emotional control than students in the control condition on the ERICA; thus, they were better able to modulate inappropriate displays of emotion."

Smith et al. 2017 (2)¶

Stephen W. Smith, Ann P. Daunic, James Algina, Donna L. Pitts, Kristen L. Merrill, Michelle M. Cumming, and Courtney Allen. (2017). Self-Regulation for Students With Emotional and Behavioral Disorders: Preliminary Effects of the I Control Curriculum. Journal of Emotional and Behavioral Disorders. https://doi.org/10.1177/1063426616661702

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Teacher-reported BRIEF Emotional Control subscale in the same pilot analysis: the Covariate × Treatment interaction was significant, with the simple effect significant only at high baseline risk (ES = −.58).

"For the Emotional Control subscale, the simple effect test was significant only when the covariate score was high (z = −2.54, p = .006, ES = −.58)."
