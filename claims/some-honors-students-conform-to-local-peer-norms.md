# For students taking some honors classes, public sign-up reduces sign-up by 15–17 percentage points in nonhonors classes but increases it by 7–9 percentage points in honors classes, so public sign-up rates are over 20 percentage points higher with honors peers

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r2` · `q3`
> **Currency** · newest study 2015 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Among some-honors students, the public treatment reduces sign-up by a statistically significant 15–17 percentage points in nonhonors classes and increases it by 7–9 percentage points in honors classes (significant at the 10 percent level in three of four specifications). [→ Bursztyn Leonardo 2015](#bursztyn-leonardo-2015)
`q3 i?` When choices are public, sign-up rates are over 20 percentage points greater for otherwise identical students in their honors classes than in their nonhonors classes. [→ Bursztyn Leonardo 2015](#bursztyn-leonardo-2015)

## Evidence

### Bursztyn Leonardo 2015

Bursztyn Leonardo, Jensen Robert. (2015). How Does Peer Pressure Affect Educational Investments? The Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(3). https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv021

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Regressions on the 343 some-honors students (42 percent of the sample), comparing students taking the same number of honors classes found in honors versus nonhonors classes at the timing of the visit, which the article argues is exogenous. The article reports the public treatment "reduce[s] sign-up rates by a statistically significant 15–17 percentage points" in nonhonors classes and raises them 7–9 points in honors classes.

> "In nonhonors classes, the effect of the public treatment is to reduce sign-up rates by a statistically significant 15–17 percentage points. In honors classes, the public treatment increases sign-up rates by 7–9 percentage points, with statistical significance at the 10 percent level in three of the four specifications."
