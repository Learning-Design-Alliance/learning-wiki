Evidence¶

Haycock 1998¶

Haycock, Kati. (1998). Good Teaching Matters: How Well-Qualified Teachers Can Close the Gap. Thinking K-16, v3 n2. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED457260

q2 · i? · review · r1

Analysis of Tennessee value-added data by Sanders, grouping teachers into effectiveness quintiles based on learning gains. The reported contrast for low-achieving students is "gains of about 14 percentile points" for Q1 versus 53 percentile points for Q5; no standardized effect size is printed.

"On average, the least effective teachers (Q1) produce gains of about 14 percentile points during the school year. By contrast, the most effective teachers (Q5) posted gains among low-achieving students that averaged 53 percentile points."

Haycock 1998 (2)¶

Haycock, Kati. (1998). Good Teaching Matters: How Well-Qualified Teachers Can Close the Gap. Thinking K-16, v3 n2. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED457260

q2 · i? · review · r1

The same Tennessee value-added analysis extended to middle- and high-achieving students, showing the printed gains of 2 versus 25 points for high achievers and 10 versus mid-30s points for middle achievers under Q1 versus Q5 teachers. No standardized effect size is printed.

"high-achieving students gain an average of only 2 points under the direction of Q I (least effec- tive ) teachers but an average of 25 points under the guidance of Q5 (most effective) teachers. Middle achievers gain a mere 10 points with Q I teachers but in the mid-30s with Q5 teachers."
