# Argumentation Improves Reasoning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q2`–`q3` · `i2` medium

Engaging learners in constructing, critiquing, and defending arguments improves their reasoning skills — including the quality of their justifications, their ability to evaluate evidence, and their transfer of reasoning to new problems.

## Subclaims

`q3 i2` Across 46 studies with 5,415 students, online scientific argumentation activities had a medium positive effect both on learners' argumentation skill ("learn to argue", g = 0.603) and on content learning through argument ("argue to learn", g = 0.596), with larger effects in K-12 than in higher education. Scope: online science argumentation only. [→ Zhou 2023](#zhou-2023)

`q2 i?` A multiyear dialogic-argumentation intervention with young adolescents transferred to individual essays on new topics, producing better argument quality and more attention to evidence than a comparison group given extensive essay-writing practice with whole-class discussion; no effect size is given in the abstract. [→ Kuhn & Crowell 2011](#kuhn-crowell-2011)

## Evidence

### Zhou 2023

Zhou, D. (2023). "Learn to Argue" and "Argue to Learn": Meta-analysis of effective instructional design for online scientific argumentation activities. *Interactive Learning Environments, 32*(9), 4857–4880. [doi:10.1080/10494820.2023.2205904](https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2023.2205904)

`q3 · meta-analysis (not pre-registered, per abstract)` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.603 (learn to argue), g=0.596 (argue to learn)` · `n=46 studies (5,415 students)`

This meta-analysis pooled 46 studies of online scientific [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) activities, involving 5,415 students. It split outcomes into two kinds: gains in argumentation skill itself ("learn to argue") and gains in understanding reached by arguing ("argue to learn"). Both showed a medium positive effect. Effects were largest for natural-science topics and larger in K-12 classrooms than in higher education, and game-based and virtual-reality platforms did better than other platforms. Read from the abstract only, so the heterogeneity, the comparison conditions and the study-quality coding could not be checked.

### Kuhn & Crowell 2011

Kuhn, D., & Crowell, A. (2011). Dialogic argumentation as a vehicle for developing young adolescents' thinking. *Psychological Science, 22*(4), 545–552. [doi:10.1177/0956797611402512](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611402512)

`q2 · quasi-experimental multiyear intervention with comparison groups` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract` · `n=two cohorts of young adolescents (count not stated in the abstract)`

Over several years, two cohorts of young adolescents argued about social issues in electronically conducted peer dialogues. Comparison groups instead did extensive essay-writing practice along with whole-class [discussion](../elements/discussion.md). The dialogue groups transferred the skill to individual essays on new topics: their arguments were of higher quality than the comparison groups', and they paid more attention to how evidence bears on an argument. The comparison-group data suggest that these reasoning skills do not develop on their own at this age. This supports the claim's transfer component, with the caveat that the design is not described as randomised.

## Discussion

**Mechanisms.** Argumentation is hypothesized to improve reasoning through several routes: it forces learners to make implicit assumptions explicit, requires coordination of claims with evidence and warrants, and exposes learners to counterarguments that prompt reconsideration of naive conceptions. This aligns with constructivist accounts in which cognitive conflict drives conceptual change — see [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md).

**Scope and moderators.** The claim should be read as conditional on structure. Unstructured or poorly scaffolded argumentation can devolve into opinion exchange with little evidential reasoning; benefits are most consistently reported when [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) is scaffolded (e.g., with argument frames, sentence starters, or structured roles) and when learners have sufficient domain knowledge to generate meaningful claims. Pairing argumentation with [assigned positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) or [debate](../patterns/debate.md) formats can ensure learners engage with both sides of an issue. Argumentation also depends on productive classroom discourse norms — see [Accountable talk](../strategies/accountable_talk.md) for one approach to structuring talk so that reasoning is made public and subject to critique.

**Domain dependence.** Much of the supporting literature comes from science education (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning frameworks) and from dialogue-based approaches such as collaborative reasoning and reciprocal teaching. Transfer of argumentation skill to everyday reasoning and informal contexts is less well established than within-domain gains, and the field lacks a consensus on how best to assess argumentation quality.

**Open questions.** Key unresolved issues include how much prior knowledge is needed before argumentation becomes productive, whether individual written argumentation yields the same benefits as dialogic (peer) argumentation, and how durable the gains are over time. Until controlled studies are added to the Evidence section, the strength of this claim cannot be rated.
