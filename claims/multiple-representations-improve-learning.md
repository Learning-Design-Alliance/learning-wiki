# Multiple representations improve learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q2` quasi-experiment · `i2` medium

Presenting the same content in more than one representational format (e.g., verbal and visual, or concrete and abstract) can support learning by giving learners complementary routes into the material. The benefit depends on the representations being relevant, well-coordinated, and manageable within working memory.

## Subclaims

`q2 i2` In a field study in an undergraduate electrodynamics course, students who worked multi-representational vector-field tasks (sketching plus interactive simulation, alongside the algebraic form) scored significantly higher on a vector-field performance test than students doing traditional calculation-only tasks. [→ Hahn and Klein 2025](#hahn-and-klein-2025)

`q2 i?` A review of STEM learning research concludes that adding a second, different type of visual representation to text can help students build the representational competencies needed to understand content, but only under specific conditions tied to students' existing skill at connecting representations — it is not unconditionally beneficial. [→ Rau 2017](#rau-2017)

## Evidence

### Hahn and Klein 2025

Hahn, L., & Klein, P. (2025). The impact of multiple representations on students' understanding of vector field concepts: Implementation of simulations and sketching activities into lecture-based recitations in undergraduate physics. *Frontiers in Psychology, 16*, 1544764. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1544764](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1544764)

`q2 · quasi-experimental field study (within- and between-subjects, intact recitation groups)` · `i2 · medium effect, d=0.40` · `n=81`

A within- and between-subjects field study (N = 81) embedded multi-representational learning tasks — sketching activities and interactive computer simulations paired with the existing algebraic (formula) representation of vector fields — into lecture-based recitations of a first-year university electrodynamics course, comparing an intervention group against a control group doing traditional calculation-based tasks. The intervention group scored significantly higher on a post-intervention vector field performance test (p = 0.04, d = 0.40) and showed larger normalized learning gains on items addressing conceptual understanding and representational competencies, though it also reported higher perceived cognitive load (extraneous d = 0.75, intrinsic d = 0.47, germane d = 0.48) while working through the tasks.

### Rau 2017

Rau, M. A. (2017). Conditions for the Effectiveness of Multiple Visual Representations in Enhancing STEM Learning. *Educational Psychology Review, 29*(4), 717-761. [doi:10.1007/s10648-016-9365-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9365-3)

`q2 · narrative literature review` · `i? · no single effect size reported` · `n=N/A (narrative synthesis of multiple studies)`

This review addresses the "representation dilemma": students often must learn unfamiliar content from visual representations they do not yet know how to read, so more representations are not automatically better. It compares "text plus a single type of visual representation" against "text plus multiple types of visual representations," derives seven hypotheses about when the multiple-representation condition outperforms the single-representation one, and reviews empirical evidence for each — concluding that benefit depends on students' representational competencies (their skill at connecting different representation types to the underlying content) rather than being a simple function of representation count.

## Discussion

**Mechanisms.** Multiple representations are typically justified through two complementary mechanisms: dual coding, in which verbal and visual channels are processed separately and can reinforce one another (see [Dual coding theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md)) [+M], and cognitive flexibility, in which multiple representations help learners build flexible, restructurable knowledge rather than a single brittle mental model (see [Cognitive flexibility theory](../patterns/cognitive-flexibility-theory.md) and [Multiple cases support cognitive flexibility](cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md)) [+W]. A third mechanism is complementary information: different representations make different aspects of a concept visible (e.g., a graph shows trends, an equation shows exact relations), so no single format carries the full idea.

**Boundary conditions.** The benefit is not automatic. Representations must be coordinated — learners must actively connect them, or they may process each in isolation and gain little [~M]. Adding representations also adds processing demands, so poorly designed combinations can overload working memory (see [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md)) [-M]. Irrelevant or decorative material should be excluded (see [Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning](coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md)) [-S], and the total material should be manageable (see [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)) [+M]. Designers should also sequence or integrate representations so learners are not forced to split attention between them — spatially or temporally integrating formats reduces extraneous load relative to presenting them separately.

**Expertise.** As with other forms of instructional support, redundant representations can lose their value or become counterproductive as expertise grows — an expertise reversal pattern (see [Expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)) [~S]. Novices benefit most from well-integrated multiple representations; advanced learners may need only one efficient format.

**Open questions.** Which representational pairings are most productive for which domains, and how best to scaffold learners in translating between representations, remain active design questions. The recorded evidence is one field study (d = 0.40) and a narrative review that stresses the benefit holds only under specific conditions.
