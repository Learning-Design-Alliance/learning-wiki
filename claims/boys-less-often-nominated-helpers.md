## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Gender negatively predicted giving help: boys were less often reported as helpers, and the cross-gender aversion was stronger for girls than for boys. [→ van Rijsewijk 2016](#van-rijsewijk-2016)

## Evidence

### van Rijsewijk 2016

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

RSiena meta-analysis of prosocial networks: the gender alter effect was b = -0.15 (odds = 0.86), so "boys were less often reported as helpers"; gender did not predict receiving help (b = 0.08, odds = 1.09).

> "Gender was not associated with receiving help over time (b ! .08, SE ! .10, odds ! 1.09), but negatively predicted giving help (b ! #.15, SE ! .03, odds ! .86). Thus, boys were less often reported as helpers."

### van Rijsewijk 2016 (2)

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Same RSiena analysis: gender similarity effect b = 0.50 (odds = 1.65); selection tables showed aversion to cross-gender helping "stronger for girls (#.36) than for boys (#.13)". About 80% of prosocial relations were same-gender.

> "Also, the similarity effect was significant (b ! .50, SE ! .12, odds ! 1.65). The selection table suggests an aversion to (receiving) help (from) cross-gender peers, and this tendency appeared to be stronger for girls (#.36) than for boys (#.13)."
