# Introductory physics students often treat centripetal force as a new physical force of nature rather than a component of the net force, leading them to incorrectly conclude apparent weight increases at the top of a curved path

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` When asked for the normal force on a person at the top of a curved path, many students used the equilibrium form of Newton's Second Law and included centripetal force as a separate physical force, obtaining FN = mg + mv2/r instead of the correct FN = mg − mv2/r. ## Evidence

### Chandralekha Singh 2016

Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Centripetal Acceleration: Often Forgotten or Misinterpreted. arXiv. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.06361](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.06361)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

The article reports, citing its reference [12], that introductory physics students asked to find the normal force (apparent weight) on a person moving along a curved path at the top of the curve incorrectly applied the equilibrium form of Newton's Second Law, concluding apparent weight increases rather than decreases.

> "In their free body diagram, they incorrectly included centripet al force when passing over the highest point on the curve as if it was a separate physical force in ad dition to the normal force and the gravitational force."
