# Teachers actively synthesize professional-development practices with their own context rather than transferring them intact

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

When teachers bring a practice learned in professional development back to their classroom, they actively reshape it by synthesizing it with their own problems of practice, professional identity, implementation constraints, and goals — meaning two teachers from the same PD session can end up with substantially different, but each personally meaningful, versions of the same practice. This means fidelity of implementation (whether a teacher used the practice "as designed") is an incomplete lens for evaluating what teachers actually learned.

## Subclaims
`q2 i2` Two experienced teachers who attended the same professional-development sessions and each selected a different target practice (a questioning strategy; a feedback strategy) recontextualized their practices in markedly different directions over a school year, driven by different local problems of practice, professional identities, and implementation constraints. [→ Marshall & Horn 2025](#marshall-horn-2025)

`q2 i1` One teacher's modifications preserved the original practice's intent (increasing student discourse and independence), while the other's modifications shifted the practice away from its original conceptual intent toward faster, more procedural feedback under time pressure — a divergence that a fidelity-of-implementation measure alone would not have distinguished from the first teacher's faithful-in-spirit adaptation. [→ Marshall & Horn 2025](#marshall-horn-2025)

## Evidence

### Marshall & Horn 2025

Marshall, S. A., & Horn, I. S. (2025). Teachers as agentic synthesizers: Recontextualizing personally meaningful practices from professional development. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 34*(3), 246-284. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2468230](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2468230)

`q2 · longitudinal ethnographic case study with two intensively observed focal teachers (from a larger cohort of 12), classroom observation and interviews across a school year` · `i1-i2 · substantial qualitative differences in adaptation direction; no quantified student-learning outcome` · `n=2 focal cases (from 12 observed teachers)`

Two middle-school mathematics teachers attended the same professional-development sessions and each chose a practice to try: a "notice-and-wonder" questioning strategy, and a rough-draft/final-draft feedback strategy. Researchers tracked both teachers' classroom enactments and reasoning over a school year. One teacher synthesized the questioning practice with a goal of fostering student independence, adding new elements (research prompts, paired whiteboard work) that extended the practice's original intent. The other, facing time pressure across 143 students in multiple sections, synthesized the feedback practice with those constraints by shifting from conceptually oriented written feedback to faster error-pointing feedback — a change the authors characterize as diverging from, rather than extending, the practice's original conceptual purpose — before later adding a "My Favorite Mistake" routine.

## Discussion
This is a two-case ethnographic study (n=2 intensive focal cases) selected from a larger cohort of 12 observed teachers; the two cases were chosen to illustrate contrasting recontextualization directions, not to represent a typical or average pattern across the full cohort. There is no measure of student learning outcomes associated with either teacher's adapted practice, so the claim describes a mechanism of teacher learning and adaptation, not a demonstrated effect on students. The judgment that one adaptation "diverged from" and the other "extended" the original practice's intent rests on the researchers' interpretation of the PD's stated goals, which is a reasonable but not fully independent basis for evaluating fidelity.
