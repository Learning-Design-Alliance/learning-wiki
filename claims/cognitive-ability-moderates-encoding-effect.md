# Cognitive ability moderates the encoding effect: higher-ability students benefit more, and lower-ability students may be harmed by note-taking

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · review `r3` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The magnitude of the encoding effect partly depends on working memory ability, and students with lower memory or math ability may not benefit from note-taking at all. [→ Jansen 2017](#jansen-2017)

## Evidence

### Jansen 2017

Jansen, R. S., Lakens, D., & IJsselsteijn, W. (2017). An integrative review of the cognitive costs and benefits of note-taking. Educational Research Review. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.10.001

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

Narrative review synthesis of studies by Peper and Mayer (1978), Berliner (1971), Kiewra and Benton (1988), Kiewra et al. (1987), and Peverly et al. (2007), which measured math ability, short-term memory, or working memory and related these to note-taking effects on memory tests.

> "Overall it can be concluded that cognitive ability is an important moderator for the relationship between note-taking and memory for lecture content."
