Ngo et al. 2024¶

Ngo, T. T.-N., Chen, H. H.-J., & Lai, K. K.-W. (2024). The effectiveness of automatic speech recognition in ESL/EFL pronunciation: A meta-analysis. ReCALL, 36(1), 4–21. doi:10.1017/S0958344023000113

q3 · meta-analysis (three-level, random effects) · i2 · medium effect, g=0.69 · n=15 studies (38 effect sizes) · quant-synthesis · r2

A meta-analysis of 15 studies (2008–2021, 38 effect sizes) comparing ESL/EFL learners who practised pronunciation with automatic speech recognition (ASR) against a non-ASR condition. The pooled effect was g = 0.69 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.08), with substantial heterogeneity (Q = 227.70; I² 56.56% between studies). The authors did not test for publication bias, and name the small number of primary studies as the main limitation. Read from the publisher's open-access full text; the article was published online in 2023. By learning activity, practising with ASR alone gave g = 0.44 (11 studies, 95% CI −1.13 to 2.01), with a peer g = 0.89 (5 studies, 95% CI −0.69 to 2.48), and with a teacher g = 1.24 (2 studies). The authors recommend peer activities, citing Elimat & AbuSeileek (2014) among others, and advise caution with young children, who may be distracted by group members. This bears against the claim for ESL/EFL learners in general.

"The pooled effect size was medium (g = 0.69), and the confidence interval was not across zero (95% CI = [0.31, 1.08])."

Discussion¶

Ngo et al. (2024) point the other way from this claim: across ASR studies, practising alone produced the smallest pooled effect and practising with a peer or teacher larger ones, although every learning-activity estimate has a confidence interval that crosses zero. Elimat & AbuSeileek (2014), the study this claim reports through Ali (2016), is in Ngo et al.'s sample, and Ngo et al. cite it both for peer groups of about four and for caution with young children. The claim's finding may therefore hold for third-grade learners but is not what the pooled evidence across ESL/EFL learners shows.
