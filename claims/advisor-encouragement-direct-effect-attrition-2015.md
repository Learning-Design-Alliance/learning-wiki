## Evidence

### Rachel Ivie 2018

Rachel Ivie, Susan White, and Raymond Y. Chu. (2018). Three years later: gender differences in the advisor's impact on career choices in astronomy and astrophysics. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1811.11836](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1811.11836)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Magnitude of direct effects presented in Figure 4 of the 2015 LSAGS multivariate analysis. The article reports that a one-step change in perceived advisor encouragement means an individual is "1.6 times less likely to be working in astronomy in 2015"; two steps yield 2.6 times and Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree 4.3 times, after taking into account 2012 status.

> "While working out of field in 2012 has the largest impact, we see that changing the perception of the advisor by just one step (from Disagree to Strongly Disagree, for example) means an individual is 1.6 times less likely to be working in astronomy in 2015"

### Rachel Ivie 2018 (2)

Rachel Ivie, Susan White, and Raymond Y. Chu. (2018). Three years later: gender differences in the advisor's impact on career choices in astronomy and astrophysics. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1811.11836](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1811.11836)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

The multivariate analysis of the third LSAGS survey reports two direct effects on working out of astronomy in 2015. The article states "encouragement from one's advisor to attain goals" was one of these direct effects, alongside prior attrition status.

> "We found two direct effects on working out of astronomy in 2015: working out of astronomy in 2012 and encouragement from one's advisor to attain goals."
