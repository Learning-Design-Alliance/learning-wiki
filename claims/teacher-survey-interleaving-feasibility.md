## Evidence

### Doug Rohrer 2019

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, C.-N. (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 112*(1), 40–52. [doi:10.1037/edu0000367](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r3`

Anonymous 23-item paper-and-pencil survey completed by all 15 teachers several weeks after the test, before they were told the results, preceded by a brief tutorial. "a good way to improve students' scores on unit exams (14)".

> "Teachers agreed (or strongly agreed) that interleaved practice is a good way to improve students' scores on unit exams (14) and final exams (14) and is appropriate for both low-achieving students (13) and high-achieving math students (15)."

### Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015

Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., & Stershic, S. (2015). Interleaved practice improves mathematics learning. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 107*(3), 900–908. [doi:10.1037/edu0000001](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001)

`q2 · anonymous survey of the teachers in a classroom experiment` · `i? · counts of responses only` · `n=3 teachers` · `associational · r1`

The three seventh-grade mathematics teachers in the study described on [Interleaved practice produces higher mathematics test scores than blocked practice in a classroom experiment](interleaved-practice-higher-math-test-scores.md) completed an anonymous 10-item survey near the end of the practice phase, before the test. All three agreed that interleaved assignments improved learning more than blocked ones and that they would recommend it to other teachers; all three said they would use it in future (two agreed, one strongly agreed); none agreed that it interfered with their teaching or that going over interleaved assignments was harder. Their answers were split on whether students found interleaved practice more challenging (one agreed), slower (one agreed) or less likable (one agreed, two neutral), and on using it with lower-achieving students (one agreed). The authors call these data speculative at best, given three teachers whose close involvement may have biased them; students were not surveyed.
