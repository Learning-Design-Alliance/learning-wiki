## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The degree to which a school enrolls high-achieving versus low-achieving students is a stronger determinant of school safety than neighborhood crime or poverty; after controlling for incoming achievement, crime and poverty show no relationship with safety. [→ Matthew P. Steinberg 2011](#matthew-p-steinberg-2011)

## Evidence

### Matthew P. Steinberg 2011

Matthew P. Steinberg, Elaine Allensworth, David W. Johnson. (2011). Student and Teacher Safety in Chicago Public Schools: The Roles of Community Context and School Social Organization. Consortium on Chicago School Research, University of Chicago Urban Education Institute. https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/student-and-teacher-safety-chicago-public-schools-roles-community-context-and-school

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Statistical models of school safety using CPS survey data, census data, and police crime records find that "after accounting for the incoming academic achievement of the school's students," neighborhood crime and poverty no longer predict safety. Achievement composition is the stronger compositional correlate.

> "After accounting for the incoming academic achievement of the school's students, there is effectively no relationship between crime and poverty and school safety. Crime and poverty are related to school safety largely because students living in high-poverty, high-crime neighborhoods are more likely than children from other areas to enter school with low academic achievement."
