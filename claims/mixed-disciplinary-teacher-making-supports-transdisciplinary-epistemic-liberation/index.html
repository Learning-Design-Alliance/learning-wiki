Mixed-disciplinary teacher making supports transdisciplinary epistemic liberation¶

Claim · All claims
Evidence · 1 study · design r2 · q3 · n=6 core K-12 teachers plus 2 engineer co-participants, spanning art, science, and math/computing, one 5-day workshop plus subsequent classroom implementation
Currency · newest study 2021 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

When art, science, and computing teachers jointly built programmable sculptural lanterns representing a shared classroom garden, all participating teachers raised evaluative criteria from disciplines outside their own — evidence that hands-on, mixed-disciplinary making can expand (not just apply) teachers' sense of what makes a representation "good."

q3 i? Across a 5-day mixed-disciplinary teacher workshop, video-coded discourse identified 89 conversational episodes in which teachers raised epistemic criteria from disciplines outside their own "home" discipline (communication, connection, explanation, expression, and interaction criteria were each raised by teachers across all three disciplines represented), with inter-rater agreement of 100% on 86% of codes. → Finch et al. 2021

q3 i? Teachers navigated conflicts between disciplinary criteria in two contrasting ways: some reached an unexamined "compromise" that balanced competing criteria without discussing the trade-off, while others engaged in explicit "critical surrender" — naming the tension aloud and deliberately choosing to leave it unresolved (e.g., adding a supplementary written explanation rather than forcing a science-versus-narrative resolution) — the latter pattern is what the study characterizes as transdisciplinary (not merely interdisciplinary) integration. → Finch et al. 2021

q3 i? Teachers who could not see sufficient coverage of their own discipline's required standards in the project (e.g., a physics teacher finding only 2-3 relevant standards) either substantially modified their implementation plan (moving the project to an after-school club) or extended the project's timeline (from 5 to 9 weeks) to accommodate additional disciplinary goals — showing standards-coverage concerns as a real constraint on transdisciplinary implementation, not just a design nicety. → Finch et al. 2021

q3 i? Same-discipline teacher pairs pursued measurably deeper single-discipline investigation than mixed-discipline pairs (e.g., only the pair of two science teachers researched the mechanistic effect of a specific condition on plant transpiration), indicating a breadth-versus-depth trade-off in how design groups should be composed depending on the goal. → Finch et al. 2021

Evidence¶

Finch et al. 2021¶

Finch, L., Moreno, C., & Shapiro, R. B. (2021). Luminous Science: Teachers Designing For and Developing Transdisciplinary Thinking and Learning. Cognition and Instruction, 39(4), 512-560. https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2021.1945064

q3 · peer-reviewed design-based research with systematic video/audio coding (grounded theory plus deductive coding against the Boix Mansilla disciplinary-integration framework), tracked inter-rater agreement, and triangulated artifacts (teacher storylines, daily reflections); purposive small sample, not a controlled or randomized design i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may n=6 core K-12 teachers plus 2 engineer co-participants, spanning art, science, and math/computing, one 5-day workshop plus subsequent classroom implementation · design · r2

Six K-12 teachers (art, science, computing/math) and two engineers spent a 5-day workshop building Luminous Science Transdisciplinary Curriculum lanterns representing a shared classroom hydroponic garden, with all activity recorded via multiple fixed and roving cameras and audio recorders. Discourse was coded for epistemic criteria (what teachers said made a representation "good") and for how teachers navigated tensions between disciplinary criteria, then teachers' subsequent classroom implementation plans were compared for how fully they achieved transdisciplinary (versus multi- or interdisciplinary) integration.

Discussion¶

This is a small, purposively-sampled design-based research study (6 core teachers) at one workshop, not a generalizable or controlled comparison; the finding that all teachers raised cross-disciplinary criteria is a within-group pattern, not evidence about how common this is across other teacher populations or workshop designs. The "critical surrender" pattern that the authors treat as evidence of the deepest (transdisciplinary) integration is illustrated through a small number of detailed discourse examples rather than a separately quantified count across all groups. Institutional constraints (standards coverage, a school's Problem-Based-Learning framework) are documented as real barriers that prevented full transdisciplinary implementation for at least two of the six teachers, showing this pattern's success is contingent on institutional support, not guaranteed by the workshop design alone.
