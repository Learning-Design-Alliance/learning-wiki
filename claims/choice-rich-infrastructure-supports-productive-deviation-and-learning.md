# Choice-rich infrastructure enables self-sustained, self-extending student projects well beyond a challenge's designed scope

Two sixth-grade students, given sustained access to a choice-rich, ungraded in-school STEAM infrastructure, extended a single game-design challenge into a year-long, self-directed project involving skills and practices the challenge never taught, encountering and independently resolving 23 discrete technical problems along the way.

## Subclaims
`q2 i2` Two students spent approximately 22.5 hours across 34 sessions (over 50% of their scheduled time for the year) extending the "Game Designer" challenge into two original games, using techniques from the challenge (level design, character-behavior modification) alongside entirely new practices not covered by it (building a game from scratch, using an external asset repository, reading and debugging error reports). [→ Hilppö & Stevens 2024](#hilppo-stevens-2024)

`q2 i2` The students encountered and resolved 23 discrete technical problems across their two games (8 in the first, 15 in the more ambitious second), following a recurring four-phase resolution cycle — problem-finding, discussion, implementation, testing — with problem frequency and resolution time both declining markedly after the midpoint of the year, consistent with developing procedural fluency. [→ Hilppö & Stevens 2024](#hilppo-stevens-2024)

`q1 i1` Both games were uploaded to a public game-asset repository (113 and 44 downloads respectively) and the two students became recognized peer experts within their studio, sought out by classmates for help with the same tools; the classroom facilitator independently praised their collaborative problem-solving in an end-of-year interview. [→ Hilppö & Stevens 2024](#hilppo-stevens-2024)

Two sixth-grade students at a FUSE Studio implementation (see [Alternative In-School STEAM Learning Infrastructure](../patterns/alternative-in-school-steam-learning-infrastructure.md)) were followed intensively across a full school year using first-person action-camera video, room video, field notes, and server logs. Deliberately selected as an "extreme case" for the duration and extent of their departure from the original challenge structure, their trajectory — from an initial game-design tutorial to two original, publicly-shared games — was analyzed via interaction analysis to characterize what problem-solving and self-directed learning look like when infrastructure supports genuine deviation from a designed task.

## Discussion
The authors explicitly frame this as an extreme case chosen to illustrate a *possibility*, not a typical outcome — most students in the same studios did not extend a challenge this far. There is no comparison group and no measure of how common such extended engagement is across the broader 7-studio, multi-school implementation this case was drawn from. The claim should be read as evidence of what a well-designed choice-rich infrastructure makes *possible* for at least some students, not as an estimate of how often or how reliably it produces this outcome.
