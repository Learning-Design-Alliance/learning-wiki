# Peer Assessment Improves Performance

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i1` small · n=54 studies (k=141 effect sizes)

When learners evaluate one another's work against explicit criteria, the act of judging quality deepens their own understanding and can improve subsequent performance. The claim covers both directions of the exchange — gains from assessing peers and gains from being assessed by peers — and is currently unverified pending Evidence entries.

## Subclaims

`q4 i1` A meta-analysis of 54 experimental/quasi-experimental control-group studies (k = 141) finds peer assessment produces a small-to-medium improvement in students' academic performance (essay writing, tests, and practical skills) relative to no assessment, and a comparable improvement relative to teacher assessment. [→ Double et al. 2020](#double-et-al-2020)

## Evidence

### Double et al. 2020

Double, K. S., McGrane, J. A., & Hopfenbeck, T. N. (2020). The impact of peer assessment on academic performance: A meta-analysis of control group studies. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(2), 481–509. [doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3)

`q4 · meta-analysis of experimental/quasi-experimental control-group studies` · `i1 · small-to-medium effect, g=0.31` · `n=54 studies (k=141 effect sizes)`

A meta-analysis of 54 control-group studies (141 effect sizes) spanning primary, secondary and tertiary students across subjects, comparing peer-assessment interventions (grading, peer dialogue, written feedback) against no-assessment and teacher-assessment control conditions on academic performance (test scores, essay/writing quality, practical skills). Peer assessment improved performance relative to no assessment (g = 0.31, p = .004) and relative to teacher assessment (g = 0.28, p = .007), and its effect did not differ significantly from self-assessment (g = 0.23, p = .209). Meta-regressions found the effect was robust across moderators such as online/offline delivery, assessment frequency and education level, i.e., no single implementation detail was required for the effect to hold.

## Discussion

**Mechanisms.** Two routes plausibly drive any performance benefit. First, reviewing peers' work forces learners to apply criteria to authentic artifacts, which resembles the [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md) benefit: studying others' solutions with evaluative intent exposes quality differences without the full load of production. Second, receiving structured feedback from peers adds feedback episodes beyond what an instructor alone can provide, aligning with [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) principles. Both routes also position peer assessment as a form of [active learning](../principles/active-learning.md): learners construct evaluative judgments rather than passively receive them.

**Moderators to establish.** The literature suggests the effect depends heavily on conditions: rubric quality and criterion specificity, whether assessors are trained, the domain (peer judgment is more reliable for structured products than for open-ended creative work), and learner expertise — novices may lack the standards knowledge to judge accurately, echoing the expertise-reversal concerns in [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md). Anonymity and formative (vs. summative) use also moderate both accuracy and acceptance of peer judgments. Rubric scaffolding is likely a precondition rather than an optional enhancement — see [Rubrics improve student work quality.](rubrics-improve-student-work.md).

**Open questions.** Whether the benefit comes primarily from *giving* assessment (judging peers) or *receiving* it (being judged) remains contested; several studies suggest the assessor role produces larger learning gains, consistent with the generative-processing rationale behind [annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md). A second open question is whether peer grades are reliable enough for summative use, or whether the claim should be restricted to formative deployments as in [assessment for learning improves achievement.](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md).

*Merged from “Peer Assessment Improves Academic Performance” (peer-assessment-improves-academic-performance):* **Mechanisms.** Peer assessment plausibly improves performance through several routes: acting as an evaluator forces learners to internalize quality criteria, which supports self-regulation of their own work; receiving feedback from peers adds perspectives beyond instructor feedback; and the social accountability of being reviewed can increase engagement. These mechanisms connect to [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) and [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). Notably, the "giving" side may be the more powerful lever: reviewing a peer's work requires comparing it against criteria and exemplars, which is precisely the evaluative judgment learners often fail to apply to their own drafts. This evaluative-judgment route is consistent with the broader formative-assessment evidence summarized in [Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md).

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The benefit likely depends on the quality and specificity of the criteria learners are given — unstructured peer review tends to produce vague, unreliable judgments, while rubrics and exemplars anchor evaluations. Training in how to give feedback, and anonymity where social dynamics would distort judgments, are common design responses. Peer grades should generally be treated as formative rather than summative, since agreement with instructor ratings is imperfect and varies by task complexity; complex or open-ended products (essays, designs) show lower peer–instructor agreement than more objective work. Because peer feedback is one delivery channel for feedback effects generally, gains here should be interpreted against the baseline documented in [Feedback Improves Learning](feedback-improves-learning.md) — poorly specified peer comments may add little over no feedback at all.

**Open questions.** Whether the performance gains come primarily from giving feedback, receiving it, or both remains unresolved, as does the durability of gains across domains and learner ages. Younger learners and novices may lack the domain knowledge to judge quality accurately, which could limit both the reliability of their reviews and the learning benefit of reviewing. Studies still need to be added before this claim can be rated.

*Merged from “Peer Assessment Improves Achievement” (peer-assessment-improves-achievement):* **Two mechanisms, one claim.** Peer assessment plausibly improves achievement through two distinct routes: (1) recipients receive timely, actionable feedback that informs revision, and (2) assessors engage deeply with criteria and exemplars while evaluating others' work, which can clarify quality standards. These routes have different boundary conditions, and evidence for one does not automatically support the other. The second route resembles the benefit of studying worked examples — critiquing others' work exposes learners to models of quality without the full cost of producing work themselves.

**Quality of feedback matters.** Peer feedback is only as good as the criteria and training learners receive. Unstructured peer commenting tends to produce vague praise [-M]; rubric-supported, criterion-referenced peer assessment is more likely to yield feedback that supports improvement [+M]. This connects to [Assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] — peer assessment is one delivery mechanism for formative assessment, and its achievement effects are plausibly mediated by the same feedback-quality conditions. The feedback route also depends on the general conditions under which [Feedback improves learning outcomes](feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S] operates: feedback must be specific, actionable, and acted upon.

**Reliability concerns.** Peer ratings can diverge from instructor ratings, particularly for novices and for complex or subjective work [-M]. Divergence is not necessarily a problem when peer assessment is used formatively (to drive revision) rather than summatively (to assign grades) [~M]. Where stakes are attached, aggregation across multiple assessors and calibration against instructor-anchored exemplars are common mitigations.

**Boundary conditions.** The claim should be read as conditional rather than universal. Achievement gains are most plausible when: assessors are trained on explicit criteria; feedback is actionable and tied to revision; recipients actually act on the feedback; and the task domain has articulable quality criteria rather than purely tacit standards. Peer assessment used summatively with untrained assessors [-M], or on tasks where novices cannot recognize quality [-M], is unlikely to improve achievement and may mislead learners.

**Assessor benefits.** The second mechanism — learning while judging — is conceptually adjacent to [Self-assessment improves self-regulated learning](self-assessment-improves-self-regulated-learning.md) [+W]: applying criteria to someone else's work builds the evaluative knowledge learners then apply to their own. This route may operate even when the assessor's feedback is never used by the recipient, which makes it worth isolating empirically from the feedback-delivery route. It also aligns with the broader pattern that [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M] — acting as an evaluator is a more active engagement with content than passively receiving instructor comments.

**Open questions.** The strength of the achievement effect likely depends on whether feedback is actually acted upon, whether assessors are trained, and whether the assessed work is revised. Studies isolating these moderators are needed before this claim can be rated for evidence strength.
