# Studying one's highest-curiosity items produced better recall than studying lowest-curiosity items

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Participants who studied items they had rated as most curious recalled more on a posttest than participants who studied their lowest-curiosity items. [→ Metcalfe 2023](#metcalfe-2023)

## Evidence

### Metcalfe 2023

Metcalfe, J., Vuorre, M., Towner, E., & Eich, T. S. (2023). Curiosity: The effects of feedback and confidence on the desire to know. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, vol. 152, no. 2, pp. 464-482. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001284

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

A learning-test phase embedded in Experiment 2 used only no-feedback items: one group studied their highest-curiosity items and the other their lowest, then both were tested. "the group that studied and was tested on high-curiosity items recalled more on the posttest" (M = .81 vs. M = .63, t[59] = 4.64, p < .001).
