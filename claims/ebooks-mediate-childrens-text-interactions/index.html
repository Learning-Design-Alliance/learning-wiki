Evidence¶

Broemmel et al. 2010¶

Amy D. Broemmel, Mary Jane Moran, and Deborah A. Wooten. (2010). The Impact of Animated Books on the Vocabulary and Language Development of Preschool-Aged Children in Two School Settings. University of Tennessee. http://www.movingpicturebooks.com/downloads/the-alphabet-songbook.php

q1 · i? · causal · r1

Qualitative analysis (constant comparative method) of researcher field notes from center-time observations in all four classrooms. Children more typically huddled in twos and threes around the computer with e-books than with traditional picture books.

"The traditional picture book facilitated a back-and-forth discussion with the researcher, while the animated stories tended to support children’s group reading, knowledge sharing, and collective anticipation of a story line or a forthcoming image."

Broemmel et al. 2010 (2)¶

Amy D. Broemmel, Mary Jane Moran, and Deborah A. Wooten. (2010). The Impact of Animated Books on the Vocabulary and Language Development of Preschool-Aged Children in Two School Settings. University of Tennessee. http://www.movingpicturebooks.com/downloads/the-alphabet-songbook.php

q1 · i? · causal · r1

Classroom vignettes and a teacher focus group (four teachers, one hour, audio-taped and transcribed). Teachers reported children mimicked e-book voices, shouted what came next, and used e-books and print books interchangeably as referents.

"The e-books created contexts in which children assumed a stance of “self as reader.”"
