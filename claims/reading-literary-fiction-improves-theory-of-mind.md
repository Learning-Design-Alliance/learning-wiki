# Reading Literary Fiction Improves Theory Of Mind

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Reading literary fiction — as distinct from popular fiction or non-fiction — temporarily improves readers' performance on theory of mind (ToM) tasks, the ability to infer others' mental states.

## Discussion

**Replication controversy.** The claim originates from a widely cited pair of experiments (Kidd & Castano, 2013) reporting that brief assignments of literary fiction improved performance on the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test and the Yoni task relative to popular fiction, non-fiction, or nothing [-W]. Subsequent multi-lab and direct replication efforts have produced mixed results, with several failing to find the effect at the original magnitude [~W]. The claim should therefore be treated as emerging rather than established, and any instructional design built on it should be piloted and measured locally.

**Boundary conditions.** Where effects have been found, they are typically short-lived (measured immediately after reading), small in magnitude, and dependent on the text genuinely requiring inference about characters' mental states — literary fiction's defining feature in this literature is its demand that readers construct characters' minds from sparse cues [~W]. Texts that do not impose this inferential demand (formulaic popular fiction, expository non-fiction) are not expected to produce the effect [-W]. This makes the *selection of text* the critical design variable: a "fiction reading" intervention using plot-driven genre fiction is unlikely to engage the mechanism at all.

**Mechanism.** The proposed mechanism is that literary fiction functions as a low-stakes simulation of social cognition: readers practice inferring motives, emotions, and beliefs of characters, which primes the same processes measured by ToM tasks [~W]. This connects to broader claims about narrative and empathy — see [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) — though empathy interventions and ToM training are distinct constructs and should not be conflated. The inferential work readers do — filling gaps about character intention from sparse cues — also parallels the constructive inference described in [Analogical reasoning improves transfer.](analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md).

**Design implications.** If used instructionally, the effect is best treated as a short-term activation or priming phenomenon rather than durable training: pair reading with immediate social-inference discussion or perspective-taking tasks rather than expecting lasting ToM gains [~W]. Discussion formats that require learners to articulate and defend interpretations of characters' mental states align with the mechanism better than silent reading alone — see [Discussion-based learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md) and [Act it out](../elements/act-it-out.md). Role-play and drama-based formats ([Acting-role-play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md)) plausibly engage the same social-inference processes more directly than reading, though head-to-head comparisons are lacking.

**Open questions.** Whether repeated exposure produces durable gains (rather than momentary priming), whether effects transfer beyond laboratory ToM tests to real social behavior, and whether effects differ by reader age or reading skill all remain unresolved.
