Evidence¶

Xin Sun 2023¶

Xin Sun, Owen Norton and Shaylene E. Nancekivell. (2023). Beware the myth: learning styles affect parents', children's, and teachers' thinking about children's academic potential. npj Science of Learning 8:46. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-023-00190-x

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Experiment 2 exploratory open-ended questions asked participants to list three subjects each learner would excel at; word clouds (Fig. 3) visualized frequencies. The quote reports teachers' top subjects for visual learners with printed percentages; parents' top five were math (19.56%), English (9.96%), art (7.75%), history (7.75%), reading (5.54%).

"Teachers viewed visual learners as likely to excel at math/mathematics (22.65%), history (14.89%), English (9.39%), art (8.74%), and reading (5.83%)."

Related Claims¶

  • In forced choice, parents and teachers pick the visual learner as smarter and the hands-on learner as sportier, with teachers showing a larger effect — related
  • Parents and teachers predict visual learners earn higher grades in core subjects and hands-on learners higher grades in non-core subjects (pre-registered) — related
  • Hands-on learners are predicted higher grades in art, gym, and music, and are picked as working harder, replicating the smarter judgment — related
  • Science is an exception: hands-on and visual learners are predicted equal science grades despite visual advantage in math, language arts, and social studies — related
  • Labeling children as visual versus hands-on learners biases adults' and children's judgments of intelligence and subject expectations — a broader claim this one bears on
