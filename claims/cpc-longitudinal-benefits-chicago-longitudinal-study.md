## Subclaims
`q2 i?` CPC preschool participation was associated with higher school readiness, higher reading and math achievement, reduced grade retention, and reduced special education placement, and by age 22 with higher high school completion and lower juvenile arrest rates. ## Evidence

### Reynolds Arthur J. 2017

Reynolds Arthur J., Hayakawa Momoko, Ou Suh-Ruu, Mondi Christina F., Englund Michelle M., Candee Allyson J., Smerillo Nicole E. (2017). Scaling and Sustaining Effective Early Childhood Programs Through School-Family-University Collaboration. Child Development. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12901

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The Chicago Longitudinal Study quasi-experimentally compared 989 CPC participants born 1979-1980 with 550 matched children in usual programs. Regression analyses associated CPC preschool with higher achievement and completion; the article reports a "higher rate of high school completion and a lower rate of juvenile arrest" by age 22.

> "By age 22, the CPC preschool program is found to be associated with a higher rate of high school completion and a lower rate of juvenile arrest"

### Reynolds Arthur J. 2017 (2)

Reynolds Arthur J., Hayakawa Momoko, Ou Suh-Ruu, Mondi Christina F., Englund Michelle M., Candee Allyson J., Smerillo Nicole E. (2017). Scaling and Sustaining Effective Early Childhood Programs Through School-Family-University Collaboration. Child Development. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12901

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

Cost-benefit analyses cited in the Pay for Success planning phase report benefits exceeding costs "by a factor of 7 to 10", largely from special education, juvenile court, and child welfare savings; the payment structure was "based on evidence that CPC improves school achievement and reduces the need for special education by up to 41%".

> "Two cost-benefit analyses documented that at an average cost per child of $9,500 (2015) for preschool, benefits exceed costs by a factor of 7 to 10"
