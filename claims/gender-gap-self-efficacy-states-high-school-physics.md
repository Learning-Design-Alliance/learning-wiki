# Female high school students experience much lower self-efficacy states in physics than male students, with a very large effect size of d = 0.88, and the difference does not occur in other school courses.

## Subclaims
`q2 i3` In a representative sample of high school students, the gender difference in self-efficacy states in physics was very large (d = 0.88, p < 0.001) and more than three times larger than the next largest gender difference across 10 school activities. [→ Jayson M. Nissen 2016](#jayson-m-nissen-2016)
`q2 i?` Male and female students had similar self-efficacy states in the nine other school activities, with effect sizes ranging from -0.26 to 0.10 in the table of results. [→ Jayson M. Nissen 2016](#jayson-m-nissen-2016)

## Evidence

### Jayson M. Nissen 2016

Jayson M. Nissen. (2016). Are inequities in self-efficacy a systemic feature of physics education?. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1612.09188](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1612.09188)

`q2 · i3` · `associational · r2`

Secondary analysis of the SSYSD ESM dataset (35,464 experiences from 4,816 students at 33 schools, 1993–1997). T-tests with Bonferroni correction compared male and female self-efficacy states; "The gender diﬀerence in students’ self-eﬃcacy states in physics was very large" (d = 0.88, p < 0.001), more than three times the next largest difference (Table I).

> "The gender diﬀerence in students’ self-eﬃcacy states in physics was very large with an eﬀect size of d = 0.88 and was a statistically reliable diﬀerence with p<0.001."
