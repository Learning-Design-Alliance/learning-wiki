Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study (2 entries) · causal r? · q3

Emphasis cues such as underlining do not simply boost learning overall — they shift attention toward whatever is emphasized, so test performance follows the relevancy of the marked material rather than the mere presence of marking.

Evidence¶

Primary evidence link: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED265520

Nist 1985¶

Nist, S. L., & Hogrebe, M. C. (1985). The Effects of High and Low Relevant Text Underlining on Test Performance. ERIC ED265520

q3 · peer-reviewed experiment, not pre-registered · i? · F values only, no effect size printed · n=67 · causal · r?

Sixty-seven college freshmen read text passages with underlining applied to either high-relevant or low-relevant content, then completed a test with questions matched to both relevancy levels. A weighted-means ANOVA showed a significant main effect for relevancy on the high-relevant score, F(1, 49) = 13.56, p < .01: learners performed better on high-relevant questions when that material was the emphasized content. The pattern demonstrates that emphasis directs attention to the marked material, and test performance tracks what was marked.

Nist 1985 (2)¶

Nist, S. L., & Hogrebe, M. C. (1985). The Effects of High and Low Relevant Text Underlining on Test Performance. ERIC ED265520

q3 · peer-reviewed experiment, not pre-registered · i? · F values only, no effect size printed · n=67 · causal · r?

In the same study, the main effect for relevancy was also significant for the low-relevant score, F(1, 40) = 6.98, p < .05. Learners scored higher on low-relevant questions when the low-relevant material was the emphasized content. Together with the high-relevant result, emphasis raised scores on whichever material was emphasized; that the effect is symmetric, of equal size regardless of the material's instructional importance, is not shown by the two F tests printed (unsourced on this page).

Discussion¶

Attention follows marking, not importance. The two findings combine into a single mechanism: emphasis acts as an attention cue, and learners allocate study time and processing to cued content. This is double-edged. Marking the right content scaffolds selective attention and improves outcomes; marking the wrong content misdirects attention away from what matters, producing the same measurable shift toward trivial material. This aligns with the broader coherence finding that irrelevant material hurts learning — see Irrelevant material hurts learning (coherence principle). [+M]

Design implication. The relevancy of instructor-provided emphasis is a design decision with direct assessment consequences. Textbook highlighting, slide emphasis, and pre-annotated readings should mark content that maps onto intended learning outcomes, not decorative or incidental detail. For learner-generated marking, the quality of what students choose to underline matters as much as the act of marking — see Annotating improves learning. [+M]

Boundary conditions. Both subclaims rest on one experiment with 67 college freshmen reading expository text with underlining as the emphasis device. Generalization to other marking formats (bolding, color), other populations, and longer or more complex texts is not yet established. No effect size is printed (only F values), and outcomes were measured immediately; durability of the attention-shift effect over delay is untested here.
