Evidence¶

Rowland 2014¶

Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. Psychological Bulletin, 140(6), 1432–1463. doi:10.1037/a0037559

q3 · i? · quant-synthesis · r3

Meta-analytic review of testing versus restudy (159 effect sizes from 61 studies). Its introduction summarises the literature: there is substantial agreement that testing effects emerge after days or weeks, many researchers have seen smaller, null or reversed effects at intervals of minutes, and numerous other studies show reliable effects at those intervals, so the form of the interaction "has not been firmly established". The meta-analysis's own categorical moderator analysis found larger effects at intervals of at least 1 day (g = 0.69, CI [0.56, 0.81]) than under 1 day (g = 0.41, CI [0.31, 0.51]), and the authors conclude that "testing reliably benefits retention compared with restudy even at short intervals." No single magnitude for the moderation itself is printed, so i?.

"many researchers have observed that testing effects are of lesser magnitude, null, or even reverse to restudy advantages, at short retention intervals on the order of minutes"

  • Several candidate moderators show no reliable effects: retention interval, test type, presentation mode, and publication status — related
  • The pretesting effect on text memory grows with retention interval, being largest at one week — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Retrieval practice improves long-term retention — related
  • Retrieval practice effects are larger at retention intervals greater than 1 day (g = 0.69) than at intervals less than 1 day (g = 0.41) in Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis — related
  • The testing effect is largest at a retention interval of 1–6 days (g = 0.82) and robust across all intervals — related
  • The retrieval (TTST) advantage appeared at both short (5 min) and long (24 h) retention delays, with no reliable evidence that it differed between them — reports the opposite
  • Retrieval practice produces reliable positive effects at short delays, although some forgetting must set in so that the final test is not at ceiling — related
  • Learning-technique effects do not shrink across test delays from the same day to six months — reports the opposite
  • Retrieval practice produces better retention than restudying, with meta-analytic reviews reporting people 2.5 times as likely to remember tested information and medium-to-large classroom effects — related
