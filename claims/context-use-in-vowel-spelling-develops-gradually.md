# Use of consonantal context in vowel spelling develops gradually, emerging around age 8 and peaking around age 11

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · `q2`–`q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In a nonword spelling study of U.S. children aged about 6 to 13, influences of the following consonant on vowel spelling appeared from around age 8 and reached a maximum around age 11. [→ Treiman Rebecca 2013](#treiman-rebecca-2013)
`q2 i?` Children younger than about 8 showed no context effects for the examined vowel-consonant pairs, and British 5- and 6-year-olds' choices followed overall frequency only. [→ Treiman Rebecca 2013 (2)](#treiman-rebecca-2013-2)

## Evidence

### Treiman Rebecca 2013

Treiman Rebecca, Kessler Brett. (2013). Learning to Use an Alphabetic Writing System. Language Learning and Development. https://doi.org/10.1080/15475441.2013.812016

`q3 · i?` · `review · r3`

Experiment 1 of Treiman and Kessler (2006) as reviewed here: U.S. children aged about 6 to 13 spelled nonwords like /klεd/ and /klεb/. Older children produced more ‹ea› spellings before /d/ than /b/, contrary to context-free pooled probabilities.

> "For the older children in the study—children of around 8 and older—influences of the following consonant began to be apparent. The effects of consonantal context reached a maximum around the age of 11."

### Treiman Rebecca 2013 (2)

Treiman Rebecca, Kessler Brett. (2013). Learning to Use an Alphabetic Writing System. Language Learning and Development. https://doi.org/10.1080/15475441.2013.812016

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The same study's younger children, and a cited study of British 5- and 6-year-olds, showed vowel choices predicted by overall correspondence frequency with no contextual effect, even though context conditioning exists in children's reading materials.

> "children who were younger than around 8 years of age did not show context effects for the particular pairs of vowels and following consonants that were examined."
