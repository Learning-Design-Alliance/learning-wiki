# Formalisation of theory offers generative models, counterfactual reasoning, and optimal-performance yardsticks unavailable to verbal accounts

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · `q1` argument or single case

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` Bayesian Network models are generative: computed inferences generate data comparable with empirical data and other models, and 'what if' questions support counterfactual reasoning, which many philosophers of science consider pivotal to explanatory power. [→ Baker 2024](#baker-2024)
`q1 i?` Because Bayesian model performance defines rational (mathematically optimal) behaviour for a task, it provides a yardstick for comparing human performance with optimal performance, which relative comparisons alone cannot. [→ Baker 2024 (2)](#baker-2024-2)

## Evidence

### Baker 2024

Baker, M., Reimann, P. (2024). CSCL: a learning and collaboration science?. International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11412-024-09433-4

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Theoretical argument (e) about methodology, not an empirical test. The authors argue formalisation manages information, makes assumptions explicit, and enables model-generated data and what-if exploration.

> "since a Bayesian Network is a specification language and a computational mechanism simultaneously, models formalized in this language are generative."

### Baker 2024 (2)

Baker, M., Reimann, P. (2024). CSCL: a learning and collaboration science?. International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11412-024-09433-4

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Theoretical argument drawn from the Bayesian framework literature: without such an optimal-performance yardstick, the authors claim, performance differences between individuals or groups can only be interpreted relative to each other.

> "Since the performance of a Bayesian model on a task defines rational behaviour for that task — it is the mathematically optimal choice (Jacobs & Kruschke, 2011) — it provides a yardstick for comparing human performance with optimal performance."
