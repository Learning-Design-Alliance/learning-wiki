# In the biology course ARS questions were intended mainly to check in and elicit immediate recall, while the engineering course used ARS questions to prompt conceptual and quantitative reasoning on new scenarios

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` In the biology course, 75% of ARS questions targeted immediate recall with no evidence of intended conceptual reasoning, while the engineering ARS questions were intended to elicit recognition of concepts (73%) and conceptual reasoning (73%) and included quantitative reasoning (60%). [→ Koretsky 2018](#koretsky-2018)
`q2 i?` Reflective interviews corroborate the difference: the biology instructor used ARS to check in with students in large classes, while the engineering instructor used ARS to push students beyond procedural problem solving toward building knowledge structures for transfer. [→ Koretsky 2018 (2)](#koretsky-2018-2)

## Evidence

### Koretsky 2018

Koretsky, M., Keeler, J., Ivanovitch, J., & Cao, Y. (2018). The role of pedagogical tools in active learning: a case for sense-making. International Journal of STEM Education 5:18. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-018-0116-5

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Analysis of think-aloud interviews with the two instructors about their ARS questions, coded with emergent coding (engineering: 15 of 31 questions analyzed; biology: all 31). Percentages of questions coded per thinking process are reported; the engineering questions "also included elements of quantitative reasoning (60%)".

> "While the biology instructor expected students to be able to recognize a concept in 25% of the questions, there was no evidence that the ARS questions were intended to evoke further conceptual reasoning. In contrast, the engineering instructor rarely sought to elicit immediate recall (7%), but rather to provide students experiences where they needed to select information from questions (47%) and recognize concepts (73%) to prompt conceptual reasoning (73%)."

### Koretsky 2018 (2)

Koretsky, M., Keeler, J., Ivanovitch, J., & Cao, Y. (2018). The role of pedagogical tools in active learning: a case for sense-making. International Journal of STEM Education 5:18. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-018-0116-5

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Interpretive analysis from the reflective interview study, triangulated with think-aloud interview coding and student ARS response data. The description quotes the article's own interpretive synthesis; no effect sizes are printed.

> "The biology instructor used the ARS as a periodic check-in with students whereas the engineering instructor used ARS questions more extensively as an opportunity for students to develop their understanding and “create that knowledge structure” they needed for adaptive transfer and problem solving."
