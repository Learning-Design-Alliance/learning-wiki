# Students' algebra mal-rules are used very irregularly, are severely skewed in frequency, and differ in explanatory power from school to school

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

> **Currency** · newest study 1990 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

A mal-rule is a buggy rewriting rule (for example, an incorrect way of moving a term across an equation) used to explain a student's error. This claim is about how stable and how general such rules are.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` Mal-rules are very unstable: students typically use them very irregularly rather than applying a buggy rule every time it could apply. [→ Payne 1990](#payne-1990)

`q2 i?` Mal-rule frequency is severely skewed, with a few frequent mal-rules and many infrequent ones, and different mal-rules have explanatory power in different schools; many of the most powerful mal-rules found had not been reported before. [→ Payne 1990](#payne-1990)

`q2 i?` Mal-rule diagnosis was more successful with more skilled students, and every mal-rule found was associated with high confidence ratings by at least one student, so confidence did not separate slips from mistakes. [→ Payne 1990](#payne-1990)

### Payne 1990

Payne, S. J., & Squibb, H. R. (1990). Algebra mal-rules and cognitive accounts of error. *Cognitive Science, 14*(3), 445–481. [doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1403_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1403_4)

`q2 · observational error-analysis study` · `i? · no effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=students in three schools (number not stated in the abstract)` · `associational · r?`

An empirical study of elementary algebra errors in three separate schools, with errors diagnosed as mal-rules in the sense proposed by Sleeman. The authors report the instability, skew and school-specificity above, and argue that slips and mistakes cannot be cleanly separated: most errors depend on properties of the knowledge base and the cognitive architecture, so errors in a procedural skill cannot be assumed to be purely syntactic in origin. Coded from the published abstract; the full text was not read.

## Discussion

For diagnostic assessment the finding cuts two ways. A catalogue of mal-rules built in one population may not transfer to another, and a single wrong answer attributed to a mal-rule is weak evidence that the student will make the same error next time. Both fit the [knowledge-in-pieces](../theories/knowledge-in-pieces.md) view of errors as context-dependent, and both bear on systems that model or simulate a student by a fixed set of buggy rules. The same pattern, inconsistent rule use below mastery, was reported for signed-number arithmetic ([Tatsuoka et al. 1989](arithmetic-rule-use-inconsistent-below-mastery-stable-at-mastery.md)).
