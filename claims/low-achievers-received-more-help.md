## Subclaims
`q3 i?` The negative ego effect for academic achievement indicates higher achievers received help less often, and low achievers tended to nominate high achievers as helpers. [→ van Rijsewijk 2016](#van-rijsewijk-2016)

## Evidence

### van Rijsewijk 2016

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

RSiena meta-analysis: academic achievement ego effect b = -0.17 (odds = 0.84); no significant alter or similarity effect. The article concludes "low-achieving adolescents generally received help more often, and in particular from high-achieving peers."

> "The negative ego effect for academic achievement suggests that higher achievers received help less often (b ! #.17, SE ! .02, odds ! .84)."

### van Rijsewijk 2016 (2)

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Ego-alter selection table for academic achievement: low achievers' attraction to high achievers was .73, while higher achievers' attraction to low or high achievers was -.71 and -.46. This complementary pattern ran counter to the study's first hypothesis.

> "In Table 6, it can be seen that higher achievers less likely mentioned low- or high-achieving peers as their helpers (#.71 and #.46). Low-achieving students, however, tended to mention high-achieving peers as their helpers (.73)."
