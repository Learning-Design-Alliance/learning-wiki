# Students struggled to match correct physical intuition to equations in the springs-in-series problem despite extreme case reasoning

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Students correctly identified that the effective spring constant should approximate that of the softer spring when one spring is much stiffer, but were unable to match this intuition to a correct equation and reverted to their original incorrect answer. [→ Mark Eichenlaub 2019](#mark-eichenlaub-2019)

## Evidence

### Mark Eichenlaub 2019

Mark Eichenlaub, Edward F. Redish. (2019). Blending physical knowledge with mathematical form in physics problem solving. arXiv preprint. Originally published 2018. [doi:10.1007/978-3-030-04627-9_6](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-04627-9_6)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

In the springs-in-series problem, students Lizzie, Lelia, and Myra correctly concluded from extreme case reasoning that "the effective constant should be (very nearly) the same as that of the softer spring" but could not map this to an equation, reverting to keff = k1 + k2.

> "Their physical intuition is correct, but in the remaining time, they are unable to match their intuition to an equation, and ultimately revert to their original answer of keff = k1 + k2."
