## Evidence

### Chandralekha Singh 2016

Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Assessing Student Expertise in Introductory Physics with Isomorphic Problems, Part II: Effect of Some Potential Factors on Problem Solving and Transfer. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.07678](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.07678)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Experiment 3 across nine introductory physics courses: friction and non-friction isomorphic problems given back to back. Students framed the problems differently, e.g., believing static friction is always at its maximum value or that kinetic friction keeps a car at rest on an incline.

> "Consistent with hypothesis H5, students had difﬁculty in seeing the deep connection between the isomorphic problems not involving friction and those involving friction [21], even though they were given back to back, and in transferring relevant knowledge to the problem involving friction."

### Chandralekha Singh 2016 (2)

Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Assessing Student Expertise in Introductory Physics with Isomorphic Problems, Part II: Effect of Some Potential Factors on Problem Solving and Transfer. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.07678](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.07678)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Experiment 3 car-on-incline pair: the weight and normal force are identical across the paired problems, yet roughly 40% chose the maximum-static-friction response, and pairing with question (18) did not improve performance on question (20).

> "the most common incorrect response to question (20) was μsN = 11,700 N (∼ 40%). Giving both questions (18) and (20) did not improve stu- dent performance on question (20) compared to when it was given alone."
