## Evidence

### Qing X. Ryan 2015

Qing X. Ryan, Steven J. Pollock, and Bethany R. Wilcox. (2015). Student Difficulties with Boundary Conditions in Electrodynamics. arXiv. [doi:10.1119/perc.2015.pr.066](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2015.pr.066)

`q2 · i?`

Coding of exam solutions at ACER element C2 (selecting appropriate BCs and field components) across normal-incidence (128 solutions) and oblique-incidence (36 solutions) questions. The article reports 11% (14/128) wrong BCs equation and 33% (12/36) wrong components; no effect size printed.

> "About a tenth (11%, 14 out of 128) of the solutions chose the wrong BCs equation. In the case of oblique incidence, students need to determine the correct components of E to be used with the corresponding boundary conditions (element C2). Over a third (33%, 12 out of 36) of the solutions had the wrong components (e.g., use sin instead of cos)."

### Qing X. Ryan 2015 (2)

Qing X. Ryan, Steven J. Pollock, and Bethany R. Wilcox. (2015). Student Difficulties with Boundary Conditions in Electrodynamics. arXiv. [doi:10.1119/perc.2015.pr.066](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2015.pr.066)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Coding of exam solutions at ACER element C3 (setting up equations at the boundary). The article reports that in "over a third (36%, 46/128) of the solutions" the exponential terms were lost despite complex forms having been previously obtained; no effect size printed.

> "However, in over a third (36%, 46/128) of the solutions, E ﬁeld in medium 1 was replaced with the sum of only the amplitudes of the incoming and reﬂected E ﬁeld (E0,in +E0,ref l)."
