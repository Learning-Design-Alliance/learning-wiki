# Teacher expectation effects on achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q2` quasi-experiment · n=large (about 11,000 students, followed for 5 years)

Teacher expectations about individual students' ability can influence those students' subsequent achievement, in part through differences in instructional treatment and interaction quality. The claim concerns the self-fulfilling-prophecy pathway (expectation → differential treatment → achievement change), not the accuracy of teacher judgments per se.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` A large-scale Dutch longitudinal multilevel study found that teacher expectation bias — the gap between a teacher's predicted and actual (model-estimated) expectation for a student — significantly predicted student performance up to 5 years later, after controlling for prior achievement, IQ and achievement motivation, with the bias effect partly dissipating in the first two years but then remaining stable through secondary school. ## Evidence

### de Boer et al. 2010

de Boer, H., Bosker, R. J., & Van der Werf, M. P. C. (2010). Sustainability of teacher expectation bias effects on long-term student performance. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 102*(1), 168–179. [doi:10.1037/a0017289](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017289)

`q2 · quasi-experimental/observational study with statistical controls (large-scale multilevel regression, not a manipulated field experiment)` · `i? · no standardized effect size (d/r) reported; raw regression coefficients and a "months of education" metric given instead` · `n=large (about 11,000 students, followed for 5 years)`

A Dutch cohort of roughly 11,000 students entering secondary school in 1999 was followed for 5 years. Teacher expectation bias was operationalized as the residual from a multilevel model predicting teachers' secondary-track recommendations from students' prior achievement, IQ, and achievement motivation — i.e., the part of the expectation not explained by actual student characteristics. In the full model, severe negative expectation bias was associated with a coefficient of −0.479 and severe positive bias with +0.426 on the Year-5 performance ("education ladder") score, controlling for prior achievement, IQ, socioeconomic status, gender, ethnicity, achievement motivation, parents' aspirations and grade repetition; the bias-performance link also mediated much of the effect of parents' aspirations on performance. The bias effect on performance was larger for students with higher prior achievement and higher parental aspirations than for those with lower scores on these variables, and effects were partly dissipated (but not eliminated) in the first two years before stabilizing.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The commonly proposed pathway is differential treatment: teachers with higher expectations tend to give students more wait time, more challenging tasks, richer feedback, and warmer interpersonal climate, while low-expectation students receive more rote, tightly scaffolded interactions. Over time these differences compound into achievement gaps that then confirm the original expectation — a self-fulfilling prophecy loop. Expectancy effects also interact with student [belonging](../elements/belonging.md): students who sense low expectations may disengage, reinforcing the cycle.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Expectation effects are typically small on average and highly variable. They appear strongest for younger students, for students from stigmatized or low-status groups, and when expectations are formed early before much performance data exists. Effects attenuate as students accumulate objective achievement records that anchor teacher judgments. High expectations alone — without corresponding instructional support — show little benefit; the effect runs through changed teaching behavior, not teacher belief per se. This connects to broader findings that [assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) when teachers use evidence rather than impressions to calibrate instruction.

**Constraints on interpretation.** Because expectations are largely correlated with prior achievement, isolating causal effects requires field experiments in which expectations are manipulated independently of actual student performance; most classroom evidence cannot do this, so correlational estimates conflate expectation effects with accurate prediction. Interventions that raise teacher expectations without changing day-to-day instructional behavior have shown little or no achievement impact — belief change is necessary but not sufficient. There is also a risk of overcorrection: uniformly high expressed expectations that ignore current skill levels can produce frustration and disengagement, particularly when task difficulty is not managed through [cognitive load reduction](../principles/cognitive-load-reduction.md) or [adaptive difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md).

**Open questions.** Most evidence comes from classroom-correlational designs and a small number of field experiments; the durability of expectation effects beyond one school year, and whether expectation-focused teacher interventions (e.g., brief messaging about malleable ability) produce lasting achievement gains, remain contested. The one study recorded above is a large Dutch longitudinal study in which teacher expectation bias predicted performance five years later after controls, but it is correlational and reports no standardized effect size.
