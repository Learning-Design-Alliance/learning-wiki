Questioning Strategies Improve Learning¶

Deliberate use of questions — posed before, during, or after instruction — improves learning by prompting retrieval, focusing attention, and stimulating elaboration. The scope of this claim covers teacher-posed and text-embedded questions across age groups and subject domains.

Evidence¶

Redfield & Rousseau 1981¶

Redfield, D. L., & Rousseau, E. W. (1981). A Meta-Analysis of Experimental Research on Teacher Questioning Behavior. Review of Educational Research, 51(2), 237–245. doi:10.3102/00346543051002237

q3 · meta-analysis of 20 experimental studies (abstract only) · i? · no standardized effect size reported in what was read · n=20 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

Synthesized 20 experimental studies on the relationship between teachers' use of "higher" cognitive questions (requiring learners to manipulate information to generate a response) versus "lower" cognitive questions (verbatim recall/recognition) and student achievement. Effect sizes were computed examining program monitoring and experimental validity as moderators. The synthesis concluded that achievement gains can be expected when higher-cognitive questions predominate during instruction. Only the publisher abstract was available (paywalled full text; not found via Unpaywall, Semantic Scholar, or ERIC), so no specific numeric effect size could be verified — the i code is left unestablished rather than guessed.

Rosenshine et al 1996¶

Rosenshine, B., Meister, C., & Chapman, S. (1996). Teaching Students to Generate Questions: A Review of the Intervention Studies. Review of Educational Research, 66(2), 181–221. doi:10.3102/00346543066002181

q3 · review/synthesis of intervention studies (abstract only) · i1 · small–medium effect, median d=0.36 (standardized tests) · n=studies not counted in abstract · quant-synthesis · r?

Reviewed intervention studies in which students were taught the cognitive strategy of generating their own questions about text as a comprehension aid (a self-questioning/text-embedded-questioning strategy, distinct from teacher-posed questions). Comprehension was measured on new material at the end of training. Across studies, question-generation training improved comprehension, with a median effect size of 0.36 (64th percentile) on standardized comprehension tests and 0.86 (81st percentile) on experimenter-developed tests; a traditional skill-based approach and a reciprocal-teaching approach produced similar results. Only the abstract was read (paywalled full text; not found via Unpaywall or Semantic Scholar), so the number of contributing studies is not confirmed and the i code reflects only the standardized-test figure the abstract states as a reported effect size.

Tobin 1987¶

Tobin, K. (1987). The Role of Wait Time in Higher Cognitive Level Learning. Review of Educational Research, 57(1), 69–95. doi:10.3102/00346543057001069

q3 · review/synthesis of classroom studies (abstract only) · i? · no standardized effect size reported in what was read · n=multiple studies across grade levels/subjects, count not stated in abstract · review · r?

Reviewed studies of "wait time" — the duration of pauses separating utterances during teacher–student verbal interaction — across a range of subject areas and grade levels. When average wait time exceeded a threshold of about 3 seconds, both teacher and student discourse changed and higher-cognitive-level achievement gains were observed in elementary, middle, and high school science, and in middle school mathematics. The abstract frames wait time as facilitating higher-cognitive learning by giving teachers and students more time to think. Only the publisher abstract was available (paywalled full text; not found via Unpaywall or Semantic Scholar), so no standardized effect size could be confirmed from what was read.

Discussion¶

Mechanisms. Questions are commonly hypothesized to work through several routes: they trigger retrieval practice (strengthening memory traces), they direct attention to to-be-learned content, and they can induce the cognitive disequilibrium that motivates conceptual change — see Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.. Pre-questions may also function as advance organizers, activating relevant prior knowledge — see Activation improves learning. and Activating prior knowledge.

Moderators to expect. The literature on questioning suggests the effect depends heavily on question type and timing. Higher-order questions (analysis, inference, application) are generally expected to outperform simple factual recall questions for deeper learning, though factual questions may suffice for building foundational knowledge. Open-ended questions impose more generation demands; whether that helps or hurts depends on learner expertise and working-memory capacity, consistent with Cognitive load theory and the expertise reversal effect. Question placement also matters: pre-questions can narrow attention toward questioned content at the expense of adjacent unqueried material.

Timing and format. Post-questions function primarily as retrieval practice and are expected to show benefits most clearly when followed by feedback; pre-questions trade some attention-narrowing cost against preparation and activation benefits. Embedded text questions (adjunct questions) are expected to help most when they target the same material learners will later be assessed on, and to show transfer costs when assessment targets unqueried content. This alignment requirement parallels the broader principle that assessment for learning improves achievement when practice and assessment are congruent.

Wait-time. In classroom dialogue, the interval a teacher allows between posing a question and calling on a learner is a well-known moderator: very short wait-times tend to produce short, low-level student responses, while extending wait-time by a few seconds is associated with longer and more reasoned responses. This interacts with Accountable talk norms that press students to justify and build on one another's answers, and with discussion-based learning formats where question quality largely determines the depth of exchange.

Constraints. Several conditions are known to blunt or reverse the benefit of questioning. Pre-questions reliably reduce attention to and later recall of material adjacent to but not covered by the question, so a question set that samples only part of the material can distort what is learned [-M]. Questions whose difficulty exceeds learners' available capacity — very open prompts for novices, multi-step questions under time pressure — can add extraneous load rather than productive effort [~M]. Rapid-fire low-level questioning with near-zero wait-time tends to elicit short factual answers and can suppress the elaborative reasoning the strategy is meant to induce [-M]. Finally, adjunct questions that target content different from the eventual assessment can produce transfer-appropriate-processing costs, improving performance on questioned material while leaving — or harming — performance on the rest [~M].

Open questions. The three reviews recorded above were read as abstracts; only the student-generated-questions review reports an effect size. Pre-questioning effects are recorded on the pretesting claim pages, and text-processing research on adjunct questions is not yet recorded here.
