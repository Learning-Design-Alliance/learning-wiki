## Evidence

### Owens Melinda T. 2017

Owens Melinda T., Tanner Kimberly D. (2017). Teaching as Brain Changing: Exploring Connections between Neuroscience and Innovative Teaching. CBE—Life Sciences Education. https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.17-01-0005

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The article reports, citing two studies, a physiological contrast between students facing stereotype threat and students who do not: "much larger increases in blood pressure and cortisol levels" during evaluation. No effect sizes are printed.

> "students who face stereotype threat exhibit much larger increases in blood pressure and cortisol levels in situations in which they are being evaluated, like taking a test, than students who do not face that threat (Blascovich et al., 2001; Townsend et al., 2011)."

### Owens Melinda T. 2017 (2)

Owens Melinda T., Tanner Kimberly D. (2017). Teaching as Brain Changing: Exploring Connections between Neuroscience and Innovative Teaching. CBE—Life Sciences Education. https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.17-01-0005

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The article reports, citing two social-psychological intervention studies, that "one-time, 15-minute exercises" countering stereotype threat effects persist "for months or years" when they allow values affirmation or external attribution of belonging worries. No effect sizes are printed.

> "Even one-time, 15-minute exercises have been shown to counteract the negative effects of stereotype threat for months or years if the exercise allows students to affirm their values or attribute worries about belonging to external factors such as the transition to college rather than to their personal or racial identities (Cohen et al., 2006; Yeager and Walton, 2011)."
