# Teaching literature and calculus textbooks perpetuate the misconception that drag is linear in velocity, applicable only at Reynolds numbers around 3

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` A calculus textbook applies linear resistance to a car coasting to a stop, but the car's Reynolds number (~3×10⁶) is far above the transition value (~3) where drag switches from v² to linear dependence. [→ Sanjoy Mahajan and David W. Hogg 2004](#sanjoy-mahajan-and-david-w-hogg-2004)

## Evidence

### Sanjoy Mahajan and David W. Hogg 2004

Sanjoy Mahajan and David W. Hogg. (2004). Introductory physics: The new scholasticism. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.physics/0412107](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.physics/0412107)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

An analytical critique: for a car of length ~2 m at v ∼ 20 m s⁻¹, Re ∼ 3 × 10⁶, so Stokes (linear) drag applies only when the speed has fallen to ~2 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹ — by which point, the authors write, "The car has certainly coasted to a stop!" They also cite a teaching article assuming linear air resistance for simplicity.

> "Drag switches from v2 (turbulent) to v (Stokes) drag at a Reynolds number of say Re ∼ 3 (see Tritton’s excellent text book5 (pp. 32–34)), which occurs when v falls by a factor of 10 6 so when v ∼ 2 × 10−5 m s−1."
