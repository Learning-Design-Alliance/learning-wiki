## Evidence

### Poos 2017

Poos, J., van den Bosch, K. & Janssen, C.P. (2017). Battling bias: Effects of training and training context. Computers & Education, vol. 111, pp. 101-113. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.04.004

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Prerequisite check with the 30-item immersion questionnaire (Jennet et al., 2008), scored as a proportion of the per-condition maximum: game-based (M = 0.60) and text-based (M = 0.59) conditions did not differ significantly; equivalence was not tested.

> "A one-way ANOVA revealed no signi ﬁcant effect of Context on the immersion scores, ( F (1,78) ¼ 0.43, p ¼ 0.51). Participants in game-based conditions ( M ¼ 0.60, SD ¼ 0.04; raw score: 90.18 points out of maximum of 150 points) did not have higher immersion scores than participants in text-based conditions ( M ¼ 0.59, SD ¼ 0.05; raw score: 83.1 out of 140)."

### Poos 2017 (2)

Poos, J., van den Bosch, K. & Janssen, C.P. (2017). Battling bias: Effects of training and training context. Computers & Education, vol. 111, pp. 101-113. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.04.004

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

In the General discussion the authors interpret their pattern of null Context effects and equal immersion as showing a narrative is sufficient to engage learners in immersive learning and that adding interactive game-play elements had no added value in this study.

> "Our ﬁndings show that embedding training in the context of a game provides no additional performance bene ﬁts, when compared to a training using text-based narratives. Groups performed equal with respect to committing or avoiding bias, on both the in-context and the out-context measurements."
