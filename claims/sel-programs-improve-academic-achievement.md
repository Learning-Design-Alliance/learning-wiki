# SEL Programs Improve Academic Achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Social-emotional learning (SEL) programs — structured curricula that teach self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making — produce gains in academic achievement alongside their social-emotional outcomes.

## Discussion

**Proposed mechanism.** The mechanism most commonly proposed is indirect: SEL programs improve classroom behavior, engagement, and student–teacher relationships, which in turn create conditions for more instructional time and better learning. This pathway parallels the belonging-to-engagement-to-achievement chain described in [Belonging interventions improve outcomes](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) and is consistent with the motivational account in [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md). Programs that integrate social-emotional skill practice into academic content (rather than delivering it as a stand-alone add-on) are generally argued to show stronger academic spillover, because the skills are practiced in the context where they must be applied.

**Boundary conditions.** Effects on achievement are typically smaller than effects on social-emotional competencies themselves, and depend on implementation quality — dosage, fidelity, and teacher training are recurring moderators in the broader SEL literature. Programs delivered poorly or as brief one-off interventions should not be expected to move achievement. There is also an open question about developmental timing: whether early-childhood, elementary, or secondary implementation yields the largest academic return remains contested.

**Causal direction.** Because many supporting studies are quasi-experimental or rely on school-level adoption, selection effects (schools that adopt SEL programs differ systematically from those that do not) cannot be fully ruled out. Randomized trials with academic outcome measures are needed before the achievement effect can be treated as firmly established.

**Status.** This page currently has no verified Evidence entries, so no effect size or specific study claim should be relied on yet. Studies (ideally randomized trials or meta-analytic syntheses with academic outcome measures) still need to be added before this claim can be treated as supported.
