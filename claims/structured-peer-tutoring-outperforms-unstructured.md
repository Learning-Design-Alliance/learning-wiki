# Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Peer tutoring produces larger learning gains when the tutoring relationship is structured — with defined roles, training for tutors, scripted or scaffolded interaction protocols, and regular monitoring — than when students are simply paired and told to help each other.

## Discussion

**What "structured" means here.** Structure in peer tutoring typically includes: (a) explicit tutor training before tutoring begins, (b) clear role differentiation (tutor/tutee, sometimes alternating), (c) scripted or semi-scripted interaction routines — e.g., prompting, questioning, and feedback protocols rather than answer-giving, and (d) ongoing teacher monitoring and feedback on tutoring quality. Unstructured arrangements lack these supports and tend to drift toward answer-sharing or off-task talk.

**Why structure matters mechanistically.** Structured protocols push tutors toward explanation, questioning, and elaboration rather than simply supplying answers, which benefits both tutor and tutee — the same elaborative-explanation mechanism that underlies broader [collaborative learning](../patterns/collaborative-learning.md) benefits [+M]. Structure also manages the working-memory and skill demands placed on tutors, who are themselves novices — consistent with [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and the broader claim that [cognitive overload degrades learning](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Without training and scaffolds, tutors often model unproductive help-seeking and help-giving behaviors [-M].

**Boundary conditions and constraints.** The advantage of structure is likely strongest for younger learners and low-ability tutors, who have fewer self-regulation resources to organize an interaction on their own [-M]. Highly able, well-motivated dyads may extract benefit from looser arrangements — a possible manifestation of the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md), in which scaffolds that help novices become redundant or constraining for the more competent [-M]. Overly rigid scripting can suppress the productive negotiation of meaning that makes peer tutoring valuable in the first place, and can reduce tutoring to recitation rather than dialogue [~M]. Unstructured pairings without tutor training frequently devolve into answer-giving or off-task interaction, eroding the gains peer tutoring is meant to deliver [-M]. Role reversal (reciprocal tutoring) may add benefits by giving both partners the chance to explain, but this is an open design question rather than a settled finding [~W].

**Open questions.** The relative contribution of each structural component (training vs. scripting vs. monitoring) is not well separated in the literature; most effective implementations bundle them. Whether reciprocal role reversal adds gains beyond fixed roles, and how much structure to fade as tutors gain experience, remain unsettled design questions.
