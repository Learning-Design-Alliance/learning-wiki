# The abstract advantage stems from the use of multiple representations rather than concreteness itself: single concrete and abstract conditions did not differ, and both outperformed multiple concrete representations

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` In Experiment 3, the multiple-concrete group scored significantly lower than both the abstract and single-concrete groups, which did not differ from each other. [→ Amy Bennett 2018](#amy-bennett-2018)

## Evidence

### Amy Bennett 2018

Amy Bennett, Matthew Inglis and Camilla Gilmore. (2018). The Cost of Multiple Representations: Learning Number Symbols with Abstract and Concrete Representations. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000318

`q3 · i2` · `causal · r3`

Experiment 3 randomly assigned 120 children to abstract, single-concrete or multiple-concrete conditions. The pre-registered ANOVA found "a significant effect of condition on accuracy" with partial η² = .129; the multiple-concrete group (M = .56) was beaten by abstract (M = .70) and single-concrete (M = .67), which did not differ (p = .566).

> "There was a significant effect of condition on accuracy, F(2, 117) = 8.66, p < .001, p2 = .129, shown in Figure 4. Post hoc Tukey HSD tests indicated that the mean score for the multiple-concrete group was significantly lower than for the abstract (p < .001) or single-concrete groups (p = .010)"
