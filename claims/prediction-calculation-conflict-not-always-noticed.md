## Evidence

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2017

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2017). Effect of Scaffolding on Helping Introductory Physics Students Solve Quantitative Problems Involving Strong Alternative Conceptions. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.05697](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.05697)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Examination of intervention 2 students' responses to the steeper-incline prediction questions in both courses; examples include a calculus-based student predicting a larger frictional force on a steeper angle from daily experience.

> "We found that in both courses, most students’ reasoning behind their first predictions about the magnitude of the static frictional force on a steeper incline could be classified into one of three categories: (1) daily experience and correct interpretation/prediction, (2) daily experience and incorrect interpretation/prediction, and (3) answer based on the calculated result."

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2017 (2)

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2017). Effect of Scaffolding on Helping Introductory Physics Students Solve Quantitative Problems Involving Strong Alternative Conceptions. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.05697](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.05697)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Analysis of intervention 2 students' prediction-versus-calculation consistency check: some students gave alternative explanations of their daily experience, some predicted from calculation rather than experience, and some made calculation mistakes, so the intended conflict was not always noticed.

> "However, as indicated by the data, n ot all students were able to discover the inconsistency in their responses."
