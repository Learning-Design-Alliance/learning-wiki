# Explicit Writing Strategy Instruction Improves Writing

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Teaching students explicit, step-by-step strategies for planning, drafting, and revising — rather than relying on immersion in writing alone — improves the quality of their written compositions.

## Discussion

The claim is best understood as a family of effects rather than a single finding: strategy instruction spans planning strategies (goal-setting, outlining, idea generation), drafting strategies, and revising strategies, and effects may differ across these stages. The mechanism is usually framed in self-regulatory terms — strategies externalize and scaffold the executive processes skilled writers perform automatically, freeing attention for content and rhetoric (see [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) and [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md)). Writing is among the most cognitively demanding school tasks, juggling ideation, transcription, and genre conventions under working-memory limits; an explicit strategy routine offloads process decisions the way [worked examples](worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) offload search, substituting a proven procedure for trial-and-error discovery of *how* to manage the task.

Several moderators are plausible and should be checked against the evidence once entries are added. First, an expertise-reversal pattern is likely: highly skilled writers may find explicit strategy routines redundant or constraining, mirroring the [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) documented for worked examples. Second, effects probably depend on the strategies being *generalizable* — taught with modeling, guided practice, and fading toward independent use — rather than delivered as static checklists; instruction that stops at telling students the steps, without [modeled demonstration](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) and supported practice, would be expected to yield weaker results. Checklist-style evaluation without modeling has shown weak or null effects in at least one online-learning context (see [Checklist evaluation is ineffective online](checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md)), reinforcing that the *teaching* of the strategy, not just its provision, carries the effect. Third, genre matters: strategies validated for persuasive or expository writing may transfer poorly to narrative or disciplinary writing without adaptation, so designers should match the taught routine to the target text type.

Open questions for future evidence entries include the durability of effects after instruction ends, the minimum dosage needed, whether effects hold equally for adolescent and adult learners, and how strategy instruction interacts with transcription fluency — for young or struggling writers, limited handwriting and spelling automaticity may consume the capacity the strategy is meant to free.
