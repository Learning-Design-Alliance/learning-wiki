Positioning personal experience as an epistemic resource supports expansion from inward to outward critical orientations¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · design r2 · q2 · n=69 students (2 classes), grades 9-12 charter school, ~78% qualifying for economic assistance

When instruction treats learners' personal, lived experience with a technology or topic as a legitimate starting point for disciplinary analysis — rather than a motivational hook set aside once "real" content begins — learners are more likely to extend individual ("inward") concerns into systemic, collective ("outward") critique.

Higgs, Kaimana & Isero 2026¶

Higgs, J. M., Kaimana, M., & Isero, M. (2026). Leveraging Onto-Epistemic Heterogeneity for "Outward" Exploration: Cultivating Critical AI Awareness in the ELA Classroom. Journal of the Learning Sciences. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2026.2686084

q2 · social design-based experiment (qualitative + pre/post survey), single site · i? · reported as a qualitative/descriptive expansion, not a quantified effect size · n=69 students (2 classes), grades 9-12 charter school, ~78% qualifying for economic assistance · design · r2

A teacher-researcher team designed a three-month English 12 unit (about 50 sessions) at a U.S. charter high school to build "critical AI awareness" — students' capacity to analyze how AI, social hierarchies, and their own ways of being co-shape one another. The design was grounded in onto-epistemic heterogeneity, operationalized as three paired stances: reading as exploration (diverse texts on algorithmic bias/manipulation), talking as exploration (discussion drawing on students' own experience with AI-driven social media), and writing as exploration (reflective and analytic writing). Analyzing student essays, discussions, digital journals, and pre/post surveys, the authors found students developed "nested" orientations: personal (inward) concerns about their own social-media use increasingly anchored broader (outward) critiques of algorithmic bias, corporate incentives, and racialized harm, and students began envisioning collective (rather than only individual) responses.

Discussion¶

This is a single-site design-based study (evidence tier q2) without a comparison condition, so it cannot isolate which specific design element (the OEH framing generally, the reading/talking/writing structure, or the specific texts chosen) drove the reported shift, and the "expansion" finding rests mainly on qualitative discourse analysis and self-report survey data rather than a validated outcome measure. The teacher-researchers were also the unit's designers, which the authors themselves flag as an analytic risk they partially mitigated (inductive coding, a non-designer analyst, cross-checking interpretations). The finding should be read as evidence that this design is plausible for building critical, systemic understanding of a technology often presented as neutral, not as a validated general effect.
