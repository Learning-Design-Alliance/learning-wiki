# Self Regulated Learning Predicts Achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i1`–`i2`

Learners who set goals, monitor their understanding, and adapt their strategies tend to earn higher achievement than learners who do not — but the relationship is correlational and moderated by context. [~M]

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 84 intervention studies in primary and secondary school, training students in self-regulated learning produced an average effect of 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use and motivation combined, with larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training and in mathematics than in reading/writing. [→ Dignath & Büttner 2008](#dignath-buttner-2008)

`q3 i1` In elementary and secondary students, measured self-regulated learning is only weakly correlated with academic achievement (metacognitive processes r = 0.20; cognitive strategies r = 0.11), and the correlation varies by process, subject, grade level and how SRL and achievement are measured. [→ Dent & Koenka 2016](#dent-koenka-2016)

## Evidence

### Dignath & Büttner 2008

Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level. *Metacognition and Learning, 3*(3), 231–264. [doi:10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)

`q4 · meta-analysis of intervention studies` · `i2 · medium-to-large effect, average ES = 0.69` · `n=84 studies (357 effect sizes)`

Two separate meta-analyses pooled self-regulated learning training studies: 49 with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students, 357 effect sizes in all. Outcomes were academic performance, strategy use and motivation, and the 0.69 average covers all three together. The abstract does not give a separate figure for achievement. Meta-regression found larger effects when researchers, not regular teachers, delivered the training, and in mathematics compared with reading/writing or other subjects. The theory behind the programme and the type of strategy taught had different effects at the two school levels. Read as abstract only.

### Dent & Koenka 2016

Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 28*(3), 425–474. [doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8)

`q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies` · `i1 · small correlation, r = 0.20 (metacognitive processes), r = 0.11 (cognitive strategies)` · `n=?`

Two meta-analyses of elementary and secondary school students related achievement to (1) metacognitive processes of self-regulated learning and (2) use of cognitive strategies. Both overall correlations were small: r = 0.20 for metacognitive processes and r = 0.11 for cognitive strategies. The correlations differed significantly by the specific process or strategy, academic subject, grade level, type of SRL measure and type of achievement measure. This is the correlational arm of the claim. It supports a real but modest association, not a causal effect. The number of studies was not in the abstract. Read as abstract only.

## Discussion

**Correlation vs. causation.** Most support for this claim comes from correlational studies relating self-reported SRL (e.g., MSLQ scores) to grades or test scores [~M]. High-achieving learners may simply report better regulation, and self-reports of SRL correlate only modestly with actual regulatory behavior measured online [-M]. Causal evidence comes mainly from SRL training interventions, which show that teaching strategies such as goal-setting, self-monitoring, and strategy adaptation improves outcomes [+M] — see [Self Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

**Moderators.** The SRL–achievement link is stronger when tasks are complex and ill-structured, where learners must manage their own pacing and strategy selection [~M]; in highly scaffolded environments, external structure substitutes for self-regulation and the correlation weakens [-M]. Developmental level matters: younger learners show weaker regulation–outcome links because their monitoring is less accurate [-W]. Domain-specific strategy knowledge is a prerequisite — generic "study skills" instruction without domain strategies shows much smaller effects [~M].

**Measurement caveat.** Findings differ substantially by instrument: self-report questionnaires, trace data from learning platforms, and observational measures of SRL often disagree, so effect sizes should be interpreted relative to how SRL was measured [-W]. Trace-based measures can capture regulatory behavior that learners cannot accurately report, but introduce their own validity questions about what log events actually indicate.

**Design implication.** Because regulation can be scaffolded or offloaded, designers should treat SRL as trainable rather than as a learner prerequisite: prompt planning and self-monitoring at points of decision, fade those prompts as competence grows, and pair strategy instruction with domain content rather than teaching it in isolation. Feedback that learners can act on — see [Assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) — feeds the monitoring phase directly. Where learners lack the domain knowledge to regulate effectively, external structure should carry the load first, consistent with [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md).

**Open questions.** Whether the SRL–achievement link is stronger in online and self-paced environments (where learners bear more regulatory burden) than in face-to-face instruction remains contested; much of the evidence base predates large-scale learning analytics, and the field still lacks consensus on how best to measure regulation in situ.
