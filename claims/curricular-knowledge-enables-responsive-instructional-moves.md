# Developing curricular design knowledge enables small-scale responsive adaptations within a structured curriculum

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Undergraduate physics Learning Assistants who developed a shared model of why a research-based tutorial curriculum's activities and question sequences were designed as they were subsequently made six distinct, responsive instructional moves — such as choosing when to intervene, referring students to other sections, and inventing on-the-fly activities — that they explicitly attributed to that knowledge.

## Subclaims
`q2 i2` Across a year of teaching reflections, 14 undergraduate Learning Assistants documented six recurring instructional moves they attributed to their developing knowledge of the *Tutorials in Introductory Physics* curriculum's design purposes: explaining the instructional strategy behind a stuck section, referring students to other sections, prioritizing time toward higher-leverage exercises, choosing when (and when not) to intervene, anticipating and addressing curriculum-targeted misconceptions, and deviating from the tutorial sequence when student thinking didn't match what the curriculum anticipated. [→ Robertson et al. 2021](#robertson-et-al-2021)

`q2 i2` A before/after contrast within the same course context showed a qualitative shift: in an untrained cohort (2011-2012), Learning Assistants described tutorial exercises only in terms of broad goals (e.g., "enhance conceptual understanding") and often intervened preemptively mid-sequence; after a year of explicit curricular-knowledge development (2012-2013), Learning Assistants identified the specific misconception or design purpose behind individual exercises and adjusted their timing and intervention decisions accordingly. [→ Robertson et al. 2021](#robertson-et-al-2021)

## Evidence

### Robertson et al. 2021

Robertson, A. D., Gray, K. E., Lovegren, C. E., Killough, K. L., & Wenzinger, S. T. (2021). Curricular Knowledge as a Resource for Responsive Instruction: A Case Study. *Cognition and Instruction, 39*(2), 149-180. [https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2020.1832096](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2020.1832096)

`q2 · qualitative case study with triangulated data sources (teaching reflections, researcher field notes, whiteboard photos, an independent-study narrative reconstruction) over 30 weeks; no control group or comparison cohort beyond an informal before/after observation; authors explicitly aim for "plausibility," not reproducibility` `i2 · a clear, consistent qualitative pattern across 14 Learning Assistants, with a well-illustrated real-time example, but no quantified learning outcome and no measure of effects on students' own learning` `n=14 undergraduate Learning Assistants, three 10-week academic quarters, one introductory physics course sequence at a US university`

Fourteen undergraduate Learning Assistants (LAs) supporting an introductory physics course built via the *Tutorials in Introductory Physics* curriculum participated in a three-pronged program (classroom teaching, weekly content preparation, and a weekly pedagogy course) that included explicit work analyzing why the tutorials' activities and question sequences were structured as they were. Over 30 weeks, LAs' teaching reflections, prompted by questions like "describe an interaction where your awareness of the curriculum's implicit structure affected how you responded to a student," were analyzed thematically. Six recurring instructional moves emerged, all tied by the LAs themselves to their developing knowledge of the curriculum's design logic — for example, one LA invented a new elicit-confront-resolve activity in real time (using overlapping transparent pages to test a student's wave-interference prediction) when the printed tutorial didn't address the student's actual misconception. This connects to [Curricular Knowledge as a Resource for Responsiveness](../principles/curricular-knowledge-as-a-resource-for-responsiveness.md).

## Discussion
This is a single-institution, single-curriculum case study without a control group; the authors are explicit that they aim to demonstrate *possibility*, not to establish how common or reliable this kind of knowledge development is across contexts. The claim is about teacher knowledge and instructional moves, not about downstream student learning outcomes — the study includes no measure of whether students taught by LAs with more developed curricular knowledge learned more. The six-moves framework is grounded in the specific design structure of one curriculum (elicit-confront-resolve sequences, "genesis" vs. "tool" exercises) and may not transfer directly to curricula organized differently.
