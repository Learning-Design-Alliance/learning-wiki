## Evidence

### Yusuf Pisan 2026

Yusuf Pisan. (2026). Teaching Intro AI When the Tools Can Do the Homework: A Course Redesign and a Student Bill of Rights. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05175 [doi:10.48550/arxiv.2608.05175](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2608.05175)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Week-2 in-class exercise (The Algorithmic Professor?; 44 of 46 students present, nine group submissions) in which groups deliberated on instructor AI use and produced a Student Bill of AI Rights. The author reports the framework's usability as his own practitioner judgment, with no causal or learning-outcome claim.

> "handing students the task of articulating the rules under which AI is used in their course, even a scaffolded task, produced a concrete accountability framework that I found more usable than most institutional guidance, and the act of submitting myself to rules my students endorsed changed how AI was discussed in the room."

### Yusuf Pisan 2026 (2)

Yusuf Pisan. (2026). Teaching Intro AI When the Tools Can Do the Homework: A Course Redesign and a Student Bill of Rights. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05175

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Provenance analysis of the exercise: the Gemini-generated assignment instructed groups to produce a Student Bill of AI Rights and offered three example rights, which the groups then converged on. The author attributes the Beta-Test Rule name to the model rather than the students and calls this loop the finding.

> "an AI-generated exercise proposed the terms on which AI use should be governed, a cohort of students deliberated and endorsed them, and their human instructor then chose to be bound by them."
