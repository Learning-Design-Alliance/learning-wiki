Evidence¶

Sneck S. 2019¶

Sneck S., Viholainen H., Syväoja H., Kankaapää A., Hakonen H., Poikkeus A.-M., Tammelin T. (2019). Effects of school-based physical activity on mathematics performance in children: a systematic review. The International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-019-0866-6

q3 · i? · quant-synthesis · r3

Meta-regression of study-level variables across the 11 studies in the meta-analysis, conducted because heterogeneity was large. The review reports age (β = −0.051, p = 0.045) and duration (β = −0.003, p = 0.002) as significant moderators.

"In the moderator analysis, the age of participants (β = − 0.051, p = 0.045) and the duration of intervention (β = − 0.003, p = 0.002) were found to explain heterogeneity. The type of intervention was not found to influence ESs."

Discussion¶

Related Claims¶

  • Large-scale Scandinavian multicomponent PA interventions showed no significant positive effects on mathematics performance, raising a possible upper-limit question
  • School-based physical activity interventions show a small positive pooled effect on children's mathematics performance (d = 0.23) across 11 randomised trials
  • Across 29 controlled studies, positive overall effects of school-based PA on mathematics performance appear in 45% of studies and neutral effects in 52%, with only one negative subgroup result
  • Children with lower baseline achievement, lower IQ or lower school engagement may benefit more from school-based PA interventions, though evidence is mixed
  • Acute PA before mathematics testing shows mixed effects: 10- and 20-min exercise breaks raised math scores but 5-min breaks did not, and pre-test PA sessions helped in two of four studies
  • Mathematics is the subject where positive associations with physical activity are found most consistently in systematic reviews, though meta-analytic effects range from null to medium — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Publication type, course type, educational level, and experimental duration did not significantly moderate the effect of context-based learning on academic achievement — related
  • Meta-regression finds intervention methods, duration, and participant type do not explain the heterogeneity in TBL effects — related
  • Meta-regression identifies percentage of female participants, sample size, and interdisciplinary participation as moderators of effect size — related
  • Meta-regression finds discipline, country, year, and learner identity do not significantly explain heterogeneity in TBL-versus-PBL effects — related
  • Knowledge gains from e‐learning are not significantly moderated by study design, student level, delivery synchronicity, intervention duration, or repeat testing — related
  • Intervention duration does not significantly predict GenAI writing effect sizes, which the authors attribute to limited temporal diversity in the evidence base — related
