# Fifth and sixth graders showed higher delayed retention and lower cognitive load when learning science from videos than from illustrated texts

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Haavisto 2025

Haavisto, M., Lepola, J., & Jaakkola, T. (2025). The “simple” view of learning from illustrated texts and videos. Learning and Instruction, 100, 102200. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102200

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Within-subjects classroom experiment in which 109 fifth and sixth graders studied two science topics in both modalities; mixed-effects models on standardized delayed-test scores showed "higher delayed retention, with a small effect size (R2mar = .016)" (delayed retention means .598 video vs .549 illustrated text).

> "Videos significantly outperformed illustrated texts in two outcome measures (Table 2): lower cognitive load, with a small effect size (R2mar = .046), and higher delayed retention, with a small effect size (R2mar = .016)."

### Haavisto 2025 (2)

Haavisto, M., Lepola, J., & Jaakkola, T. (2025). The “simple” view of learning from illustrated texts and videos. Learning and Instruction, 100, 102200. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102200

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Same within-subjects experiment: cognitive load self-ratings on Paas's nine-point scale after initial study were lower in the video condition (M = 3.74) than the illustrated text condition (M = 4.36), "with a small effect size (R2mar = .046)".

> "Videos significantly outperformed illustrated texts in two outcome measures (Table 2): lower cognitive load, with a small effect size (R2mar = .046), and higher delayed retention, with a small effect size (R2mar = .016)."
