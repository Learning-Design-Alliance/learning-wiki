# Descriptive passing rates were higher for advised at-risk students (57.7%) than non-advised students (33.3%), while the withdrawal difference was not statistically significant

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Among 128 at-risk students, the passing rate was 57.7% for advised students versus 33.3% for non-advised students. [→ Zhang et al. 2014](#zhang-et-al-2014)
`q2 i?` The withdrawal rate difference between advised (3.8%) and non-advised (10.8%) students was not statistically significant. [→ Zhang et al. 2014 (2)](#zhang-et-al-2014-2)

## Evidence

### Zhang et al. 2014

Yi Zhang, Qiang Fei, Munir Quddus, Carolyn Davis. (2014). An examination of the impact of early intervention on learning outcomes of at-risk students. Research in Higher Education Journal, Volume 26. http://www.aabri.com/copyright.html

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Summary statistics (Table 1) from the matched-sample study of 128 at-risk students, of whom 26 (20.3%) sought advising; these are descriptive means, and the t-test confirmed a higher passing rate for advising students.

> "The passing rate is much higher (57.7%) for students who received additional advising compared to students without the benefit of advising (33.3%)."

### Zhang et al. 2014 (2)

Yi Zhang, Qiang Fei, Munir Quddus, Carolyn Davis. (2014). An examination of the impact of early intervention on learning outcomes of at-risk students. Research in Higher Education Journal, Volume 26. http://www.aabri.com/copyright.html

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

T-test of mean differences (Table 2) between advising and non-advising groups found no statistically significant difference in withdrawal; the authors attribute this to the very small number of withdrawals. Equivalence was not tested.

> "But the student withdrawal is not significantly different between the two groups. This may be due to the very small sample size of withdrawal in our dataset."
