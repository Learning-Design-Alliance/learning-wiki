## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Emotions vary across simulation phases: anxiety, insecurity and joking to mask fear predominate at the beginning; stress, embarrassment, confusion, enjoyment and pride during the scenario; and a wide range from fear to enjoyment and pride after the end. [→ Madsgaard 2022](#madsgaard-2022)

## Evidence

### Madsgaard 2022

Madsgaard, A., Smith‐Strøm, H., Hunskår, I., & Røykenes, K. (2022). A rollercoaster of emotions: An integrative review of emotions and its impact on health professional students' learning in simulation‐based education. Nursing Open, 9, 108–121. https://doi.org/10.1002/nop2.1100

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review's thematic analysis of the 16 included studies found emotions "vary before, during and after the simulation", with post-simulation emotions ranging "from fear, anxiety, shame, frustration, confidence, calm" to enjoyment and pride.

> "The various reported emotional outcomes after the end of the simulation range from fear, anxiety, shame, frustration, confidence, calm, nervousness, disappointment, surprise and worry, to enjoyment, excitement, happiness, pride and feeling challenged"

### Madsgaard 2022 (2)

Madsgaard, A., Smith‐Strøm, H., Hunskår, I., & Røykenes, K. (2022). A rollercoaster of emotions: An integrative review of emotions and its impact on health professional students' learning in simulation‐based education. Nursing Open, 9, 108–121. https://doi.org/10.1002/nop2.1100

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports, citing Fraser and McLaughlin (2018), a survey study of medical students, that "tranquillity seems to increase during the debriefing". The review attributes this finding to that single included study.

> "Fraser and McLaughlin (2018) found that tranquillity seems to increase during the debriefing, which suggest that students feel comfortable and relaxed during the debriefing."
