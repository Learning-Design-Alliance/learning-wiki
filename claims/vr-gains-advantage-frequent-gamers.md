# Among students reporting regular video game play, VR produced significantly higher gains on dipole question pairs than video (d = 0.30) and static images (d = 0.42)

## Subclaims
`q2 i1` For seven repeated pre-post question pairs on dipole fields, students reporting regular video game play gained significantly more in the VR treatment than in the video treatment (t = 1.7, p = 0.04, d = 0.30) and the image treatment (t = 2.4, p < 0.01, d = 0.42). ## Evidence

### J. R. Smith 2018

J. R. Smith, A. Byrum, T. M. McCormick, N. Young, C. Orban, and C. D. Porter. (2018). A controlled study of stereoscopic virtual reality in freshman electrostatics. Originally published 2017. [doi:10.1119/perc.2017.pr.089](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2017.pr.089)

`q2 · i1` · `causal · r2`

Secondary analysis of the randomized electric field study separating students by self-reported video game play frequency (often = more than once per week). Among regular players, VR gains exceeded video (d = 0.30) and image (d = 0.42) treatments on seven repeated dipole question pairs.

> "those who received the VR treatment had signiﬁcantly higher gains on these seven paired questions than did those who received the video treatment (t-test,t = 1.7,p = 0.04,d = 0.30), and those who received the image treatment (t-test,t = 2.4,p< 0.01,d = 0.42)."
