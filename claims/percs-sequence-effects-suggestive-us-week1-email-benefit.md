# In PERCS, differences in effects of email sequences on week-4 return were mostly not significant, though sequences starting with an email tended to be slightly more beneficial for US learners but not Indian learners

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Differences in effects of various email sequences on week-4 course return were not significant but only suggestive. [→ Timothy NeCamp 2019](#timothy-necamp-2019)

## Evidence

### Timothy NeCamp 2019

Timothy NeCamp, Josh Gardner, Christopher Brooks. (2019). Beyond A/B Testing: Sequential Randomization for Developing Interventions in Scaled Digital Learning Environments. arXiv. [doi:10.1145/3303772.3303812](https://doi.org/10.1145/3303772.3303812)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

PERCS SRT results (Section 5.1.4) comparing 95% confidence intervals for the proportion of students returning in week 4 after each three-week email sequence. The authors state differences were "not signiﬁcant but only suggestive"; no effect size is printed.

> "Overall, for both countries, the conﬁdence intervals across sequences are mostly overlapping, indicating that differences in effects of various email sequences are not signiﬁcant but only suggestive."

### Timothy NeCamp 2019 (2)

Timothy NeCamp, Josh Gardner, Christopher Brooks. (2019). Beyond A/B Testing: Sequential Randomization for Developing Interventions in Scaled Digital Learning Environments. arXiv. [doi:10.1145/3303772.3303812](https://doi.org/10.1145/3303772.3303812)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

PERCS sequence comparison (Figure 3, Section 5.1.4) of week-4 return proportions by country. The finding is hedged ("tend to be slightly more beneﬁcial") and holds for US learners only; no effect size is printed.

> "For US learners, sequences with emails sent in the ﬁrst week (i.e., those se- quences which start withE1, the last four columns in Figure 3), tend to be slightly more beneﬁcial than sequences without. For Indian learners this is not true."
