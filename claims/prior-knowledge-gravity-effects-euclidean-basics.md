## Subclaims
`q2 i?` In the pre-questionnaire, 63.5% described gravity by its everyday effects, 26.5% gave misconceptions, 5% described gravity as interaction, and 5% did not answer. [→ Matteo Luca Ruggiero 2021](#matteo-luca-ruggiero-2021)
`q2 i?` Pupils were quite familiar with the fact that parallel lines never meet, though the triangle-angle rule had been studied by only one class in the sample. [→ Matteo Luca Ruggiero 2021 (2)](#matteo-luca-ruggiero-2021-2)

## Evidence

### Matteo Luca Ruggiero 2021

Matteo Luca Ruggiero, Sara Mattiello, Matteo Leone. (2021). Physics for the masses: teaching Einsteinian gravity in primary school. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.1088/1361-6552/ac1ca3](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6552/ac1ca3)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Pre-intervention questionnaire on the meaning of gravity, administered two weeks before activities to the 58 pupils; answers were categorised into awareness levels shown in Figure 3, with no reference to the modern geometric view of gravity.

> "eﬀects of gravity (63.5%): many students describe gravity in conn ection with its eﬀects on everyday life, as they can learn from books"

### Matteo Luca Ruggiero 2021 (2)

Matteo Luca Ruggiero, Sara Mattiello, Matteo Leone. (2021). Physics for the masses: teaching Einsteinian gravity in primary school. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.1088/1361-6552/ac1ca3](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6552/ac1ca3)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Pre-intervention Euclidean-geometry items on triangle angle sums and parallel lines. The article notes the 180-degree rule had been studied by only one class, and that wrong answers likely reflect not remembering the rule rather than knowledge of non-Euclidean geometries.

> "this result is very well known also from everyday life ( "Parallel lines cannot meet because they are as railways") even though students do not know the formulation of Euclid's ﬁfth postulate"
