Executive functions and working memory correlate moderately to strongly with mathematics achievement (r = .28 to .40), from preschool through elementary school¶

Executive-function composites correlate r = .40 (preschool) and .36 (elementary school) with mathematics achievement, composite working memory .38 and working memory .35; cognitive flexibility (.35) is more closely related in younger children. These are correlations compiled across meta-analyses: they describe how strongly the characteristic goes with mathematics achievement, not what causes it.

Evidence¶

Breit et al. 2025¶

Breit, M., Schneider, M., & Preckel, F. (2025). Mathematics achievement and learner characteristics: A systematic review of meta-analyses. Learning and Individual Differences, 118, 102621. doi:10.1016/j.lindif.2024.102621

q3 · systematic review of meta-analyses · i2 · medium effect, r = .28 to .40 · n=30 meta-analyses, 66 correlations from 13,853 effect sizes (~4.66 million participants) · review · r3

The review compiles zero-order correlations from published meta-analyses (each with at least ten effect sizes, the largest one kept where several covered a variable), found by a systematic PsycINFO search in July 2024. It pools nothing further and controls for nothing: many of these characteristics correlate with one another, so a correlation here says how strongly the characteristic goes with mathematics achievement, not that it causes it. Preschool executive-function composite r = .40, updating .36, shifting .32, inhibition .30 (Emslander & Scherer 2022, up to k = 363); elementary-school executive functions .36, inhibitory control .29, shifting .28 (Spiegel et al. 2021, k = 1,034); cognitive flexibility .35 (de Santana et al. 2022, k = 30, n = 35,355 children); composite working memory .38, working memory .35 (k = 829, n = 27,860), numerical .34, visual-spatial .31 and verbal working memory .30 (Peng et al. 2016). The preschool and elementary estimates differ by about .04 with overlapping intervals; the cognitive-flexibility correlation was larger in younger children.

Discussion¶

The authors relate this to the load of mathematical problem solving, which requires holding and manipulating several pieces of information at once. The review tests no executive-function training.
