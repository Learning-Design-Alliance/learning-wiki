## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Unlike arithmetic and spelling tests, pupils' history answers could not be graded on an absolute right-or-wrong standard, so two grading directors defined answers worth 10 per cent, 5 per cent, and intermediate points, and it was impracticable for one person to make eighty judgments each on 933 pupils. ## Evidence

### Sackett 1922

Sackett, L. W. (1922). A Scale in Ancient History. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://psycnet.apa.org/journals/edu

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Procedural account from the article's grading section: because answers showed "varying degrees of Tightness or wrongness," two directors set weighted values and graded many papers themselves while supervising assistants. The author admits the weighting sometimes varied far enough from right-and-wrong to be almost arbitrary but more equitable.

> "It was found to be impracticable to ask one person to make eighty judgments each on 933 pupils. Two directors of the grading were chosen and they worked over the correct and adequate answers which would be counted as 10 per cent, those that would be considered 5 per cent., and those for certain intermediate points."
