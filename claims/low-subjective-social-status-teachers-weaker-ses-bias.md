## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Teachers with low subjective social status praised low- and high-SES students equally often and attributed their successes equally often to effort, whereas teachers with high subjective social status showed SES biases in inflated praise, modest praise, and effort attributions. [→ Schoneveld 2023](#schoneveld-2023)

## Evidence

### Schoneveld 2023

Schoneveld, E., & Brummelman, E. (2023). "You did incredibly well!": teachers' inflated praise can make children from low-SES backgrounds seem less smart (but more hardworking). npj Science of Learning, 8:31. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-023-00183-w

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Exploratory moderator analysis in Study 1 (N = 106 teachers), examining subjective social status measured with the MacArthur ladder scale. "Teachers with low subjective social status praised low- and high-SES students equally often" across all praise types and effort attributions.

> "Teachers with low subjective social status praised low- and high-SES students equally often; this was the case for inflated praise, modest praise, and all praise combined. They also attributed the success of low-SES students equally often to effort as they did the success of high-SES students."
