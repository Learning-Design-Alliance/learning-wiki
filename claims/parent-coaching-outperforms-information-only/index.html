Parent Coaching Outperforms Information Only¶

Coaching parents through active practice with feedback produces larger gains in parent skills and child outcomes than providing information alone (e.g., written materials, lectures, or psychoeducation without guided practice).

Evidence¶

Kaminski et al. 2008¶

Kaminski, J. W., Valle, L. A., Filene, J. H., & Boyle, C. L. (2008). A Meta-analytic Review of Components Associated with Parent Training Program Effectiveness. Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology, 36(4), 567–589. doi:10.1007/s10802-007-9201-9

q4 · meta-analysis · i? · no pooled effect size reported for this specific component · n=77 published evaluations · quant-synthesis · r?

This component analysis pooled 77 published evaluations of parent training programs for children aged 0–7, using program content and delivery-method characteristics as moderators of effect sizes on parenting behavior and child externalizing behavior. After controlling for research-design differences, requiring parents to practice new skills with their own children during sessions — the active, coached component, as opposed to programs that only convey content — was one of a small set of components consistently associated with larger effects; components limited to conveying information (e.g., teaching problem-solving or promoting cognitive/academic skills) were among those associated with smaller effects. The abstract reports which components predict larger vs. smaller effects but does not give a single pooled effect size for "practice" alone, so no d/g/r/OR value is coded here.

Sanders et al. 2000¶

Sanders, M. R., Markie-Dadds, C., Tully, L. A., & Bor, W. (2000). The triple P-positive parenting program: A comparison of enhanced, standard, and self-directed behavioral family intervention for parents of children with early onset conduct problems. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 68(4), 624–640. doi:10.1037/0022-006X.68.4.624

q3 · randomized controlled trial · i? · no standardized effect size reported in what was read · n=305 preschoolers · causal · r?

305 preschoolers at high risk of developing conduct problems were randomly assigned to enhanced behavioral family intervention (EBFI, practitioner-coached plus extra support), standard BFI (SBFI, practitioner-coached), self-directed BFI (SDBFI, workbook/written materials with minimal practitioner contact), or a wait-list control. At postintervention, the two practitioner-assisted (coached) conditions outperformed the self-directed and wait-list conditions on parent-reported disruptive child behavior, dysfunctional parenting, parental competence, and consumer satisfaction, and EBFI showed the greatest reliable improvement in children's behavior overall. By one-year follow-up, all three active conditions had converged on similarly improved observed disruptive behavior, but the two coached conditions still showed greater improvement than self-directed on parent-reported disruptive behavior — a partial qualification of the claim's durability that the page's Discussion should note.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The proposed advantage of coaching over information-only delivery aligns with broader learning-design principles: skill acquisition requires practice with feedback, not merely exposure to declarative content. Information-only formats place the full burden of translating knowledge into behavior on the parent, whereas coaching embeds modeling, guided rehearsal, and corrective feedback — consistent with cognitive apprenticeship and demonstration as an instructional element. Coaching also leverages feedback at the point of performance, which information-only formats structurally cannot provide. In-the-moment skills such as responsive caregiving are executed under time pressure with a child present, so the translation from "knowing that" to "doing now" is exactly where information-only formats fail and coaching succeeds.

Boundary conditions. Coaching is more resource-intensive, so the claim is most relevant where behavior change — not awareness — is the goal. For low-complexity knowledge or when parents are highly motivated and skilled, information-only formats may suffice; the advantage should be largest for complex interaction skills (e.g., responsive caregiving, language stimulation, behavior management) that require in-the-moment execution. Dose and fidelity of coaching are likely moderators: brief or low-fidelity coaching may not outperform well-designed self-directed materials. This mirrors the broader pattern in which instructional support that helps novices can become inefficient when learners already possess the component skills — see the expertise reversal effect.

Delivery format. Whether coaching must occur in person, or whether telehealth and video-based coaching preserve the advantage, is an open empirical question. The mechanism argument suggests the active ingredients are modeling, rehearsal, and feedback rather than physical co-presence, which would predict that well-designed remote coaching retains most of the benefit — but the evidence recorded above does not test remote coaching.

Open questions. Comparative effect sizes across delivery formats (in-person vs. telehealth coaching), child age bands, and maintenance of gains after coaching ends remain to be documented on this page.
