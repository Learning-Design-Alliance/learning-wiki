# Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement

Learners who tutor peers — or who are tutored by them — tend to show higher achievement than learners receiving equivalent instruction without tutoring, with the strongest benefits typically accruing to the tutor.

## Discussion

**Tutor gains.** A recurring finding in the peer tutoring literature is that the *tutor* often benefits as much as or more than the tutee. Preparing to explain, generating explanations, and detecting and repairing one's own knowledge gaps during tutoring are forms of elaborative retrieval and self-explanation, which plausibly explains why tutoring functions as a powerful learning activity for the tutor rather than merely a delivery mechanism. This aligns with the broader "protégé effect" documented for teaching-related activities — see [Teaching as learning](../strategies/teaching-as-learning.md).

**Moderators to establish.** Open questions that the evidence base must settle before this claim can be graded include: structured versus unstructured tutoring formats, same-ability versus cross-ability pairing, age and domain of learners, and whether effects persist on delayed or transfer measures rather than only immediate post-tests. Reciprocal tutoring designs, where learners alternate roles, are one common attempt to capture both tutor and tutee benefits.

**Boundary conditions.** Peer tutoring is not a substitute for expertise: tutors with inaccurate understanding can propagate errors, and benefits depend on tutors having sufficient mastery of the material they explain. Implementation quality — training tutors, providing structured materials, and monitoring sessions — is likely to moderate outcomes substantially. From a [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) perspective, explaining to a peer can benefit the tutor through germane elaboration, but an overloaded or under-prepared tutor may fail to generate useful explanations at all.

**Interpreting against the right baseline.** Because tutoring is an active, interactive format, any comparison against passive instruction conflates tutoring-specific effects with the general advantage of [active learning](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md). Well-designed studies compare tutoring against other active formats, not only lecture.

**Role assignment and structure.** Because tutor and tutee roles engage different mechanisms — elaborative explanation for the tutor, guided practice with feedback for the tutee — designs that fix one learner in each role may systematically favor one party. Reciprocal or role-swapping designs, and structured protocols that script the explanation and questioning moves, are the main implementation responses; both remain open moderators pending evidence.
