# Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r?` · `q3`–`q4` · `i1` small

Learners who take part in school peer-tutoring programs, as tutees or as tutors, tend to show higher achievement than comparable learners without tutoring. One meta-analysis (65 evaluations) found gains for tutees and the same pattern of gains for tutors, without a pooled effect size in the abstract read; another (50 mathematics studies) found a small average effect for participants (g = 0.333). Neither recorded entry shows that tutors gain more than tutees.

## Evidence

### Cohen et al. 1982

Cohen, P. A., Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1982). Educational Outcomes of Tutoring: A Meta-analysis of Findings. *American Educational Research Journal, 19*(2), 237–248. [doi:10.3102/00028312019002237](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=65 independent evaluations` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A meta-analysis of 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programs, spanning tutee and tutor outcomes. Tutored students outperformed control students on examinations and developed more positive attitudes toward the subject matter; students who served as tutors showed the same pattern of academic and attitudinal gains. Participation had little or no effect on the self-esteem of either tutors or tutees. Only the abstract was read (the full article is paywalled and no open-access copy was found), so no pooled effect-size statistic could be confirmed from the text — the abstract itself states the finding only in directional terms.

### Alegre-Ansuategui et al. 2017

Alegre-Ansuategui, F. J., Moliner, L., Lorenzo, G., & Maroto, A. (2017). Peer Tutoring and Academic Achievement in Mathematics: A Meta-Analysis. *EURASIA Journal of Mathematics, Science and Technology Education, 14*(1). [doi:10.12973/ejmste/79805](https://doi.org/10.12973/ejmste/79805)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i1 · small effect, Hedge's g = 0.333` · `n=50 studies` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A meta-analysis of 50 independent studies of peer-tutoring programs in mathematics across multiple educational stages found that 88% of the programs had a positive effect on participants' academic performance, with a pooled Hedge's g of 0.333. Moderator analyses found tutee/tutor age, assigned roles, tutee ability level (disabled/at-risk vs. not), session length and frequency were not significant moderators, while educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day (in-school vs. out-of-school) and sample size were significant moderators of the effect. Crossref carries no page range for this record; the article's own header gives pp. 337–354 (2018;14(1)), which is noted here but not asserted as the Crossref-verified value.

## Discussion

**Tutor gains.** Cohen et al. (1982) report that tutors showed the same pattern of academic and attitudinal gains as tutees; the recorded entries do not compare the size of the two, so the claim often made in the peer-tutoring literature that the *tutor* benefits as much as or more than the tutee is not established here. <!-- deprecated 2026-10-05 (not in the entries): **Tutor gains.** A recurring finding in the peer tutoring literature is that the *tutor* often benefits as much as or more than the tutee. --> Preparing to explain, generating explanations, and detecting and repairing one's own knowledge gaps during tutoring are forms of elaborative retrieval and self-explanation, which plausibly explains why tutoring functions as a powerful learning activity for the tutor rather than merely a delivery mechanism. This aligns with the broader "protégé effect" documented for teaching-related activities — see [Teaching as learning](../strategies/teaching-as-learning.md).

**Moderators to establish.** Two meta-analyses are recorded, one read only as an abstract; Alegre-Ansuategui et al. (2017) found educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size moderated the effect in mathematics. Open questions the recorded evidence does not settle include: <!-- deprecated 2026-10-05 (stale): Open questions that the evidence base must settle before this claim can be graded include: --> structured versus unstructured tutoring formats, same-ability versus cross-ability pairing, age and domain of learners, and whether effects persist on delayed or transfer measures rather than only immediate post-tests. Reciprocal tutoring designs, where learners alternate roles, are one common attempt to capture both tutor and tutee benefits.

**Boundary conditions.** Peer tutoring is not a substitute for expertise: tutors with inaccurate understanding can propagate errors, and benefits depend on tutors having sufficient mastery of the material they explain. Implementation quality — training tutors, providing structured materials, and monitoring sessions — is likely to moderate outcomes substantially. From a [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) perspective, explaining to a peer can benefit the tutor through germane elaboration, but an overloaded or under-prepared tutor may fail to generate useful explanations at all.

**Interpreting against the right baseline.** Because tutoring is an active, interactive format, any comparison against passive instruction conflates tutoring-specific effects with the general advantage of [active learning](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md). Well-designed studies compare tutoring against other active formats, not only lecture.

**Role assignment and structure.** Because tutor and tutee roles engage different mechanisms — elaborative explanation for the tutor, guided practice with feedback for the tutee — designs that fix one learner in each role may systematically favor one party. Reciprocal or role-swapping designs, and structured protocols that script the explanation and questioning moves, are the main implementation responses; neither is tested by the evidence recorded here.

*Merged from “Tutoring benefits both tutors and tutees” (tutoring-benefits-tutors-and-tutees):* **Why tutors benefit.** The dominant explanation is that preparing to teach forces the tutor to organize, elaborate, and verbalize their knowledge, exposing gaps that passive review leaves hidden. This aligns with the generative-processing logic behind [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) and with [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md): knowledge is consolidated through use rather than reception. Explaining to another person also adds social accountability, which [Social learning theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) treats as a powerful motivator for effortful engagement.

**Why tutees benefit.** Tutees receive individualized pacing, immediate feedback, and opportunities to ask questions that whole-class settings rarely permit. One-to-one and small-group interaction also supports the social presence conditions described in [Community of inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md).

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Tutor benefits are largest when tutors are adequately prepared and the material is within reach of their current competence; asking a near-peer to tutor content they have not yet mastered can entrench errors for both parties. Structured protocols (prompt cards, scripted question sequences) tend to outperform unstructured "just help each other" arrangements, consistent with the scaffolding logic of [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — unstructured tutoring can impose extraneous load on both roles. Reciprocal designs, where partners alternate roles, distribute these benefits and avoid fixed status hierarchies.

**Design implications.** Effective programs typically (a) train tutors in specific questioning and explanation routines before sessions begin, (b) provide materials that structure the interaction rather than leaving roles open-ended, and (c) build in role rotation so neither partner is permanently cast as the weaker learner. These design features echo the scaffolding-and-fading approach described in [Cognitive apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md), where responsibility is gradually transferred to the learner.

**Open questions.** The relative magnitude of tutor versus tutee gains across age bands and domains, and the durability of tutor-side gains over time, still need systematic synthesis on this page. The recorded meta-analysis of tutoring programmes reports gains for tutors but no effect size for them.
