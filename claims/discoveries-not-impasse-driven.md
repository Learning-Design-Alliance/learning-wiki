# Min-strategy discoveries were not impasse-driven: they occurred on representative, mostly previously solved problems

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Discoveries occurred on problems representative of the whole set, most of which the child had earlier solved correctly, contradicting impasse-driven learning accounts. [→ Siegler 1991](#siegler-1991)

## Evidence

### Siegler 1991

Robert S. Siegler and Kevin Crowley. (1991). The Microgenetic Method: A Direct Means for Studying Cognitive Development. American Psychologist, Vol. 46, No. 6, 606-620. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606

`q3 · i?` · `review · r2`

Trial-by-trial analysis of the discovery problems in the Siegler and Jenkins microgenetic experiment. The article reports this was "directly at odds" with impasse-driven models such as SOAR and RT2. Preceding problems in the discovery session were not unusually difficult (88% small addend; 75% correct).

> "The problems on which discoveries were made were quite representative of the total set of problems that children encountered: 2 + 5, 5 + 2, 4+1, 3+ 9, 1+ 24, and 4+ 3 (twice). Apparently, discoveries can occur in the course of solving easy problems, difficult problems, or problems of middling difficulty."

### Siegler 1991 (2)

Robert S. Siegler and Kevin Crowley. (1991). The Microgenetic Method: A Direct Means for Studying Cognitive Development. American Psychologist, Vol. 46, No. 6, 606-620. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.46.6.606

`q3 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Analysis of performance immediately preceding discovery within the same session: solution times were doubled (medians of 18 vs. 9 seconds), often with partial strategy execution and pauses, suggesting heightened cognitive activity before the discovery.

> "Children took twice as long to solve problems in the same session but before the discovery as on problems in general (medians of 18 vs. 9 seconds)."
