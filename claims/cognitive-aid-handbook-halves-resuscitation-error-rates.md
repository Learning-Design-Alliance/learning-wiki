# Using the Emergency Protocols Handbook during simulated resuscitation crises significantly reduced team error rates, with an estimated relative reduction of 54.0%

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In a randomised controlled simulation trial, error rates were significantly lower when teams used the handbook (17.9%, 95% CI 14.4–22.0) than when they did not (38.9%, 95% CI 34.2–43.9), a relative reduction of 54.0%. [→ Hall Charlotte 2020](#hall-charlotte-2020)

## Evidence

### Hall Charlotte 2020

Hall Charlotte, Robertson Dean, Rolfe Margaret, Pascoe Sharene, Passey Megan E., Pit Sabrina Winona. (2020). Do cognitive aids reduce error rates in resuscitation team performance? Trial of emergency medicine protocols in simulation training (TEMPIST) in Australia. Human Resources for Health. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12960-019-0441-x

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Randomised controlled trial in a high-fidelity simulation laboratory: 21 groups each managed four scenarios, two with and two without the handbook. The generalised mixed model showed a significant main effect of handbook use (F1, 64 = 42.8, p < 0.001), with a "relative reduction in error of 54.0%".

> "Overall, scenarios using the handbook exhibited significantly lower estimated error rates: 17.9% (95% CI 14.4–22.0) versus 38.9% (95% CI 34.2–43.9), for a relative reduction in error of 54.0% (95% CI 49.9–57.9)"

### Hall Charlotte 2020 (2)

Hall Charlotte, Robertson Dean, Rolfe Margaret, Pascoe Sharene, Passey Megan E., Pit Sabrina Winona. (2020). Do cognitive aids reduce error rates in resuscitation team performance? Trial of emergency medicine protocols in simulation training (TEMPIST) in Australia. Human Resources for Health. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12960-019-0441-x

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Descriptive results from the same trial: 84 simulations, 1260 key tasks scored out of 15 key tasks per scenario. The article reports the "unadjusted error rate in the handbook group was 18.8% (121/645)" versus 38.9% without.

> "The unadjusted error rate in the handbook group was 18.8% (121/645) versus 38.9% (239/615) in the non-handbook group."
