# Parent Coaching Outperforms Information Only

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Coaching parents through active practice with feedback produces larger gains in parent skills and child outcomes than providing information alone (e.g., written materials, lectures, or psychoeducation without guided practice).

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The proposed advantage of coaching over information-only delivery aligns with broader learning-design principles: skill acquisition requires [practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback, not merely exposure to declarative content. Information-only formats place the full burden of translating knowledge into behavior on the parent, whereas coaching embeds modeling, guided rehearsal, and corrective feedback — consistent with [cognitive apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) and [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) as an instructional element. Coaching also leverages [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the point of performance, which information-only formats structurally cannot provide. In-the-moment skills such as responsive caregiving are executed under time pressure with a child present, so the translation from "knowing that" to "doing now" is exactly where information-only formats fail and coaching succeeds.

**Boundary conditions.** Coaching is more resource-intensive, so the claim is most relevant where behavior change — not awareness — is the goal. For low-complexity knowledge or when parents are highly motivated and skilled, information-only formats may suffice; the advantage should be largest for complex interaction skills (e.g., responsive caregiving, language stimulation, behavior management) that require in-the-moment execution. Dose and fidelity of coaching are likely moderators: brief or low-fidelity coaching may not outperform well-designed self-directed materials. This mirrors the broader pattern in which instructional support that helps novices can become inefficient when learners already possess the component skills — see the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md).

**Delivery format.** Whether coaching must occur in person, or whether telehealth and video-based coaching preserve the advantage, is an open empirical question. The mechanism argument suggests the active ingredients are modeling, rehearsal, and feedback rather than physical co-presence, which would predict that well-designed remote coaching retains most of the benefit — but this page currently has no evidence entries to support or refute that prediction.

**Open questions.** Comparative effect sizes across delivery formats (in-person vs. telehealth coaching), child age bands, and maintenance of gains after coaching ends remain to be documented on this page. Studies are needed before this claim can be rated for evidence strength.
