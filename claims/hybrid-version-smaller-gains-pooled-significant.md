## Evidence

### Masel J. 2015

Masel J., Humphrey P. T., Blackburn B., Levine J. A. (2015). Evidence-Based Medicine as a Tool for Undergraduate Probability and Statistics Education. CBE—Life Sciences Education 14(4). https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.15-04-0079

`q3 · i?` · `design · r2`

Pre/post QRQ and ATS comparison in the first hybrid offering (Spring 2015, 29 students). The article reports improvements of "0.28 pretest SDs" with QRQ p = 0.046 and ATS p = 0.077, smaller than the face-to-face class; no d, g, r or η² is printed, so impact is uncoded.

> "QRQ and ATS scores each showed improvements of 0.28 pretest SDs (p = 0.046 for QRQ; p = 0.077 for ATS; Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). These effect sizes are on the whole (nonstatistically significantly) smaller, around half the size of the fully face-to-face class discussed at length above."

### Masel J. 2015 (2)

Masel J., Humphrey P. T., Blackburn B., Levine J. A. (2015). Evidence-Based Medicine as a Tool for Undergraduate Probability and Statistics Education. CBE—Life Sciences Education 14(4). https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.15-04-0079

`q3 · i?` · `design · r2`

Pooled analysis across the Spring 2014 and Spring 2015 offerings combining both cohorts' pre/post data; the article reports pooled effect sizes of 0.46 (QRQ) and 0.42 (ATS) pretest SDs, both p < 0.001; no d, g, r or η² is printed, so impact is uncoded.

> "When data from both years were combined, effect sizes for both QRQ and ATS overall scores were intermediate and remained statistically significant (0.46 and 0.42 pretest SDs for QRQ and ATS, respectively; both p < 0.001; Supplemental Tables 3 and 4)."
