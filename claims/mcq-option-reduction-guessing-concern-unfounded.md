# Fear that reducing MCQ options increases correct answers by guessing is unfounded, because examinees engage in educated rather than blind guessing

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Reducing the number of options does not lead to increased correct answers by guessing, since examinees eliminate the least plausible distractors rather than guessing blindly. [→ Fozzard Nicolette 2018](#fozzard-nicolette-2018)

## Evidence

### Fozzard Nicolette 2018

Fozzard Nicolette, Pearson Andrew, du Toit Eugene, Naug Helen, Wen William, Peak Ian R. (2018). Analysis of MCQ and distractor use in a large first year Health Faculty Foundation Program: assessing the effects of changing from five to four options. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-018-1346-4

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

The article's discussion, citing a prior meta-analysis, addresses staff and student concerns that fewer options make exams easier through guessing. It reports that "reducing options does not lead to increased correct answers by guessing" because examinees guess in an educated way, eliminating least plausible distractors.

> "one author in a meta-analysis suggests that reducing options does not lead to increased correct answers by guessing; even in three-option questions guessing is unlikely because "Examinees are unlikely to engage in blind guessing, but rather educated guessing where the least plausible distractors are eliminated, essentially reducing the 4- or 5-option item to a 3- or 2-option item""
