# Experimenter-generated underlining is as effective as student-generated underlining for test performance

The act of generating one's own underlining does not, by itself, improve test performance over simply studying text that someone else has underlined — what matters is which content gets marked, not who marks it.

## Subclaims

`q3 i0` Students who generate their own underlining do not perform significantly better on tests than students given experimenter-generated underlining. [→ Nist 1985](#nist-1985)

## Evidence

Primary evidence link: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED265520

### Nist 1985

Nist, S. L., & Hogrebe, M. C. (1985). The Effects of High and Low Relevant Text Underlining on Test Performance. [ERIC ED265520](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED265520)

`q3 · peer-reviewed experiment, not pre-registered` · `i0 · null effect` · `n not reported in stub`

In a controlled experiment comparing student-generated underlining with experimenter-generated underlining, the authors found that "subjects who generated their own underlining did not perform significantly better" than those given experimenter-generated underlining. The study also manipulated the relevance of the underlined content (high vs. low relevant text), suggesting that the selection of what to mark — rather than the generative act of marking — drives any effect on test performance.

## Discussion

**Generation is not the active ingredient.** A common intuition holds that [annotating](../principles/annotating.md) text oneself is a generative activity that deepens processing relative to passively reviewing someone else's marks. This finding contradicts that intuition for underlining specifically: the generative act of drawing the line appears to add little beyond attending to already-marked content. This aligns with the broader literature on text marking, where underlining is among the weaker learning strategies and its value depends heavily on the quality of selection.

**Selection quality is the moderator.** Because the study manipulated the relevance of underlined content, the practical implication is that poorly chosen marks — whether made by students or experimenters — can be useless or harmful, while well-chosen marks help regardless of who makes them. Novices often select poorly when marking on their own, which may explain why self-generated underlining frequently fails to beat provided marks.

**Scope limits.** The claim concerns underlining specifically and immediate test performance; it does not establish that all forms of [annotating](../principles/annotating.md) are equivalent to provided marks. Richer annotation (marginal notes, elaborative comments, self-questioning) involves different cognitive processes than drawing lines and may show genuine generation benefits. Single-study support in one text domain also warrants replication before strong design prescriptions are drawn.
