# Punitive responses to absence do not improve attendance and can worsen it

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · review `r1` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Punitive actions have not been shown to improve attendance; a South Carolina study found absences got worse when probation was used to respond to truancy. [→ EdTrust 2023](#edtrust-2023)
`q2 i?` School and district punishments such as refusing make-up work, barring extracurricular activities, or suspending students for absences exacerbate the problem. [→ EdTrust 2023 (2)](#edtrust-2023-2)

## Evidence

### EdTrust 2023

EdTrust. (2023). 5 Things for Advocates to Know About Chronic Absenteeism. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED627994

`q2 · i?` · `review · r1`

The brief reports that "Punitive actions have not been shown to improve attendance", citing the South Carolina probation study; civil penalties like tickets and fines and criminal penalties like jailing parents have not deterred absences.

> "A study in South Carolina shows, for example, that absences got worse when using probation to respond to truancy."

### EdTrust 2023 (2)

EdTrust. (2023). 5 Things for Advocates to Know About Chronic Absenteeism. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED627994

`q2 · i?` · `review · r1`

The brief adds that punitive actions do not lead to a real understanding of why kids miss school and that a criminal record has long-lasting negative impacts for students and their families.

> "Additionally, school and district-level actions that aim to punish students by refusing to accept make-up work, barring students from extracurricular activities, or suspending them for absences, only exacerbate the problem at hand."
