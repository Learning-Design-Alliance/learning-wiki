# Ranking-based evaluation is more reliable and less manipulable than numeric rating, whose scores are noisy, inflated, and inconsistent

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Paul 2025

Paul, U., Mantravadi, A., Shah, J., Shah, S., Mylavarapu, S. V., Rashid, M. P., & Gehringer, E. (2025). Scaling Success: A Systematic Review of Peer Grading Strategies for Accuracy, Efficiency, and Learning in Contemporary Education. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11677

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports, citing Jimenez-Romero et al. and Formanek et al.'s astronomy MOOC analysis, an "average error of 42%" for peer scores versus teacher scores and inter-rater agreement of "just 0.32". Agreement coefficients are printed; no standardized effect size.

> "Jimenez-Romero et al. [65] evaluated the accuracy of peer-assigned scores compared to teacher-assigned scores, revealing an average error of 42%. Formanek et al. [2] found rating to be highly unreliable and inconsistent, with an inter-rater agreement of just 0.32."

### Paul 2025 (2)

Paul, U., Mantravadi, A., Shah, J., Shah, S., Mylavarapu, S. V., Rashid, M. P., & Gehringer, E. (2025). Scaling Success: A Systematic Review of Peer Grading Strategies for Accuracy, Efficiency, and Learning in Contemporary Education. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11677

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review attributes to Jingjing et al. the finding that "the relative order of submissions remained consistent" despite fluctuating absolute scores, supporting ranking's retained accuracy. Qualitative consistency claim; no effect size.

> "Jingjing et al. [71] demonstrated that while numeric scores varied considerably, the relative order of submissions remained consistent—suggesting that ranking retains the underlying accuracy of peer evaluations even when absolute scores fluctuate."

### Paul 2025 (3)

Paul, U., Mantravadi, A., Shah, J., Shah, S., Mylavarapu, S. V., Rashid, M. P., & Gehringer, E. (2025). Scaling Success: A Systematic Review of Peer Grading Strategies for Accuracy, Efficiency, and Learning in Contemporary Education. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11677

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review notes, citing Caragiannis et al., that ranking systems are "less susceptible to strategic manipulation", reducing incentives to assign low scores to elevate one's own standing. No effect size is printed.

> "Caragiannis et al. [72] noted that ranking systems are also less susceptible to strategic manipulation."
