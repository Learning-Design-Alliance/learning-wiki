Surface Formatting Changes Shift LLM Grades Without Penalising Them¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r? · q2 Evidence quality · This claim rests on one research release that has not been peer-reviewed. Its research and writing were carried out by an AI system (Claude Code) under one human author, and its scope audit and reproduction were made by AI agents with no part in the work; no research team independent of the author has reviewed or replicated it. Treat it as a lead to check, not an established finding.

Reformatting an answer without changing its words moves the grade a level in about two cases in five on matched answers (39%, against 25% for re-grading identical text; 17–37% across seven variants at n = 30 each), with no consistent direction across variants and no intervals — so it reads as a shift in where the grader settles rather than a penalty for any particular style, and it is larger than the noise of simply grading the same text twice. One model, single runs; no penalty was detected, which is not the same as showing there is none.

  • The movement is not merely re-sampling. On matched cases, reformatting moves the grade more than twice as far as re-grading byte-identical text does (mean absolute change 0.46 against 0.21).
  • Because it is a shift rather than noise around a stable centre, averaging repeated grades does not remove it.
  • Grading byte-identical text twice already lands on a different integer level about a quarter of the time, which is the floor any account of this has to start from.

Discussion¶

Scope of the claim. The perturbations tested preserve the words and their order exactly; only layout, capitalisation and punctuation vary. Variants that alter characters as well as layout behave about the same, so the sensitivity is not specific to whitespace. Nothing here speaks to substantive edits, where a real grade change and a noise flip are inseparable by construction.

Why "not a penalty" matters as much as "it moves". The complaint that reaches a product team is almost always directional — a learner reformatted and the mark went down. That happens, and it also goes up, with no consistent direction detected across variants, which means the fix is not a formatting-invariance instruction or a style rule for learners. Treating it as a penalty leads to the wrong intervention.

The fix that follows is architectural rather than prompt-level. Key the grade on a normalised content signature — words in order, case and punctuation stripped — and return the stored grade when a learner resubmits the same words. In the measurement every layout-only variant hit such a signature, and the typo variant, which changes a word, correctly did not. It also removes the cost of the re-grade.

One non-obvious constraint on that fix. The cache must be keyed per learner and never shared on content alone. In the corpus measured, 2% of answers were identical text from different students, and the human graded 15 of those 18 groups differently — mostly crediting one and zeroing the rest. An integrity judgement is about provenance, not content, so a content-only cache would hand the credited grade to the student who was zeroed.

What is left over. Residual movement on genuinely edited resubmissions stays, and nothing cheap addresses it. The honest options there are a better-calibrated model and showing bands rather than false-precision points.
