# Robot adaptiveness effects on human autonomy are mixed: autonomy-supportive behaviours and as-needed assistance help, but mere choice or high interactivity may not

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Glawe 2025

Glawe, F., Schmeckel, T., Brauner, P., & Ziefle, M. (2025). Human Autonomy and Sense of Agency in Human-Robot Interaction: A Systematic Literature Review. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22271

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports Henkemans et al.'s experiment with a personal robot in a diabetes educational game; the robot's autonomy-, competence-, and relatedness-satisfying behaviours "significantly increased human autonomy". A separate work-settings survey found as-needed assistance associated with higher autonomy.

> "A personal robot that exhibited human-autonomy- satisfying behaviours (alongside competence- and relatedness-satisfying behaviours), such as offer- ing choices to continue or pause, acknowledging mood, and integrating personally relevant scenarios, significantly increased human autonomy in a diabetes-related educational game [45]."

### Glawe 2025 (2)

Glawe, F., Schmeckel, T., Brauner, P., & Ziefle, M. (2025). Human Autonomy and Sense of Agency in Human-Robot Interaction: A Systematic Literature Review. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22271

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review reports that a social robot deployed as an online learning companion with basic psychological need-supportive behaviours did not affect human autonomy, and that highly interactive support in adult education did not increase autonomy; an adaptive tutoring robot yielded only descriptively higher autonomy.

> "Offering choice alone also had no discernible impact on perceptions of a learning companion robot [54]."
