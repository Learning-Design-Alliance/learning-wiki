## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Candidates with religious beliefs were significantly less likely to pass written examinations than those with no religion, while for clinical examinations Buddhist and Christian candidates were less likely but Hindu and Sikh candidates more likely to pass. ## Evidence

### Ellis Ricky 2025

Ellis Ricky, Knapton Andy, Cannon Jane, Lee Amanda J., Cleland Jennifer. (2025). Differential attainment in UK postgraduate medical examinations: examining the relationship between sociodemographic differences and examination performance. BMC Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04034-w

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r3`

Multivariate logistic regression on all candidates' first attempts, adjusted for ethnicity, prior attainment and other factors; Hindu (OR 1.12) and Sikh (OR 1.28) candidates were significantly more likely to pass clinical examinations.

> "Candidates identifying as Buddhist and Christian were significantly less likely to pass clinical examinations at the first attempt (OR 0.68 (95% CI 0.57 to 0.80) and OR 0.93 (95% CI 0.87 to 0.99), respectively)."

### Ellis Ricky 2025 (2)

Ellis Ricky, Knapton Andy, Cannon Jane, Lee Amanda J., Cleland Jennifer. (2025). Differential attainment in UK postgraduate medical examinations: examining the relationship between sociodemographic differences and examination performance. BMC Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04034-w

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r3`

UKG-only multivariate logistic regression (48,430 written; 27,380 clinical first attempts); religion remained significant for written outcomes but not for clinical outcomes in this subgroup.

> "In contrast to the high-level all-candidates analysis, religion was not a statistically significant predictor of clinical examination outcomes for UKGs after adjusting for other sociodemographic factors and prior academic attainment."
