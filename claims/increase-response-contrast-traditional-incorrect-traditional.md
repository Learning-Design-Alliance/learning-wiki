## Evidence

### Hawkins 2011

Hawkins, J. M., Frank, B. W., Thompson, J. R., Wittmann, M. C., & Wemyss, T. M. (2011). Probing Student Understanding With Alternative Questioning Strategies. Originally published 2006. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1107.1825](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1107.1825)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Response-level analysis of the trial pretest shows increase and decrease differ: as printed, "increase is actively viewed as incorrect where decrease is not," despite similar percentages choosing each as correct.

> "On the traditional question, 23% of the students selected increase as the correct response. On the incorrect traditional question, 44% of students eliminated increase."

### Hawkins 2011 (2)

Hawkins, J. M., Frank, B. W., Thompson, J. R., Wittmann, M. C., & Wemyss, T. M. (2011). Probing Student Understanding With Alternative Questioning Strategies. Originally published 2006. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1107.1825](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1107.1825)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Given increase question type results: only 8% of students said the bulb's brightness increases, which the authors note is inconsistent with the 23% choosing increase on the traditional question; they attribute this possibly to students choosing the best option without endorsing it.

> "Looking at the results of the given increase question type we see only 8% of students said that the brightness of the bulb increases."
