## Evidence

### Trevor I. Smith 2024

Trevor I. Smith, Mohammad A. Kazmi, Richard R. Sarles, III, Joshua A. Sbrana, Cody W. Soper, and Nasrine Bendjilali. (2024). Comparing item response theory models for ranking incorrect response options. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Authors' interpretation of the mechanism behind cross-data-set ranking differences, supported by response-frequency patterns in Fig. 1 showing DS2 students more likely to choose less-common response options than DS1 students.

> "the level of understanding of an individual student will have a larger effect on the estimated parameter of a response chosen by very few students than it would on the parameter of a response chosen by many stu-dents."

### Trevor I. Smith 2024 (2)

Trevor I. Smith, Mohammad A. Kazmi, Richard R. Sarles, III, Joshua A. Sbrana, Cody W. Soper, and Nasrine Bendjilali. (2024). Comparing item response theory models for ranking incorrect response options. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Item 19 case study: response frequencies in Fig. 1(b) show responses E and F chosen by less than 1% of DS1 students each, and Table I shows removing them would make DS1 and DS2 rankings consistent. The authors suggest DS2 rankings may be more representative of the overall population.

> "Fig. 1(b) shows that responses E and F are very rare for DS1 (less than 1% each), and Table I shows that, if responses E and F were removed, the rankings for DS1 and DS2 would be consistent with each other."
