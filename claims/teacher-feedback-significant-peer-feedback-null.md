# Teacher-to-student feedback shows a significant positive effect (g = .41) while student-to-student feedback is non-significant (g = .32)

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q3` peer-reviewed experiment · `i2` medium

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` Teacher-provided feedback (n = 24) yielded a significant summary effect of Hedges' g = .41 (p = .004); peer-provided feedback (n = 2) yielded g = .32 that was statistically non-significant (p = .395). [→ Nalline S. Baliram & Jeffrey J. Youde 2018](#nalline-s-baliram-jeffrey-j-youde-2018)

## Evidence

### Nalline S. Baliram & Jeffrey J. Youde 2018

Nalline S. Baliram & Jeffrey J. Youde. (2018). A Meta-analytic Synthesis: Examining the academic impacts of feedback on student achievement. International Dialogues on Education, 5(2), 76-90. https://portal.issn.org/resource/ISSN/2198-5944

`q3 · i2`

Moderator analysis splitting the 26 data sets by feedback provider. The teacher-to-student subgroup was significant with "Hedges’ g = .41"; the peer-to-student subgroup, based on only two data sets, was not, and the authors treat it as inconclusive.

> "The meta-analysis for teacher-provided feedback ( n = 24) indicated a summary overall effect of Hedges’ g = .41. Tests of statistical significance indicated support for the rejection of the null hypothesis ( p = .004). The meta-analysis for peer -provided feedback ( n = 2) indicated a s ummary overall effect of Hedges’ g = .32 with statistical non-significance indicated (p = .395)."
