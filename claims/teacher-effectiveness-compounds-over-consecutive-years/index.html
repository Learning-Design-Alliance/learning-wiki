Effective teachers compound their advantage over consecutive years, and low-achieving students are disproportionately assigned to ineffective ones¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · associational r? · q2 · i3 large · n=low-achieving 4th-grade cohort, Dallas Independent School District

Teacher effectiveness, tracked as a growth or value-added measure rather than a single-year snapshot, produces large downstream differences in student outcomes — and the students most likely to benefit from an effective teacher are, on average, less likely to actually get one across consecutive years.

Dallas study cited in Bracey 2004¶

Low-achieving fourth-grade students in Dallas were followed for three years and classified by whether their teachers over that span were effective or ineffective; 90 percent of students with effective teachers passed the seventh-grade math test, compared with only 42 percent of students with ineffective teachers. The same study found that low-achieving students were more likely than high-achieving students to be assigned an ineffective teacher for all three years running — meaning the students who would benefit most from strong instruction were, if anything, less likely to consistently receive it. This finding is cited secondhand via Bracey (2004) rather than traced to the original Dallas research report, so the original study's design details (exact effectiveness measure, sample size, statistical controls) could not be verified against primary source text.

Discussion¶

This finding is the empirical backbone of the argument for growth (or "value-added") models in teacher and school accountability, discussed in High-Stakes Testing and Accountability Effects: if teacher effectiveness compounds this strongly over just three years, then accountability systems measuring only absolute proficiency at one point in time — rather than growth attributable to a specific teacher or school — miss exactly the information that matters most for closing achievement gaps. It also raises an uncomfortable distributional question: if assignment patterns systematically route low-achieving students toward less effective teachers (whether through scheduling convenience, seniority-based placement preferences, or other structural causes not examined in this citation), then teacher-assignment policy itself becomes a lever either for narrowing or widening achievement gaps, independent of any single teacher's individual skill. A limitation worth flagging is that this claim rests on a single secondarily-cited study; broader research on teacher effects and value-added measurement (a substantial literature not reviewed here) would be needed to establish how typical this specific compounding pattern is across districts and contexts.
