## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Postal workers learning typewriter keystrokes acquired the skill faster under massed practice but retained it better under distributed practice. [→ Soderstrom 2015](#soderstrom-2015)
`q2 i?` In Shea and Morgan's experiment, blocked practice produced better acquisition performance, but random (interleaved) practice produced better retention at 10 min and 10 days. [→ Soderstrom 2015 (2)](#soderstrom-2015-2)

## Evidence

### Soderstrom 2015

Nicholas C. Soderstrom and Robert A. Bjork. (2015). Learning Versus Performance: An Integrative Review. Perspectives on Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

Narrative review of the Baddeley and Longman (1978) study commissioned by the British Postal Service, varying practice per day and number of days. The review reports the distributed group required more days to reach criterion yet showed better long-term retention.

> "In sum, massed practice supported quicker acquisition of the keystrokes, but distributed practice led to better long-term retention of the skill (see also Simon & Bjork, 2001)."

### Soderstrom 2015 (2)

Nicholas C. Soderstrom and Robert A. Bjork. (2015). Learning Versus Performance: An Integrative Review. Perspectives on Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

Narrative review of J. B. Shea and Morgan (1979), in which participants learned three movement patterns under blocked versus random schedules with practice time equated, then were tested in B-B, B-R, R-B, and R-R subgroups after 10 min and 10 days.

> "the advantage of blocked practice during acquisition was no longer evident after a delay. In fact, the pattern reversed when learning was assessed after 10 min and 10 days—that is, overall, those who initially practiced the skills in a random order exhibited the most learning."
