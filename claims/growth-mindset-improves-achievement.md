# Growth mindset improves achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Teaching learners that abilities are malleable (a "growth mindset") can raise academic achievement, but effects are small on average and depend heavily on who is targeted and how the intervention is delivered.

## Discussion

**Small average effects, meaningful for at-risk students.** The best-known national study of mindset interventions in U.S. high schools (Yeager et al., 2019, *Nature*) found overall effects on GPA were small (roughly 0.1 GPA points) [~M], but effects were meaningfully larger for lower-achieving and economically disadvantaged students [+M]. This pattern — near-null averages masking heterogeneous effects — is the central interpretive challenge for this claim. A widely cited meta-analysis (Sisk et al., 2018, *Psychological Science*) reported weak overall correlations between mindset and achievement and small intervention effects, concluding that mindset is not a universal lever [~M]. Until formal Evidence entries are added for these studies, the page's `weak` strength rating reflects this mixed meta-analytic picture rather than absence of research.

**Delivery conditions matter.** Effects appear strongest when interventions are (a) delivered as integrated, sequenced sessions rather than one-off assemblies, (b) embedded in academic content rather than generic motivation, and (c) accompanied by structural changes in teaching practice. A standalone "you can do anything" session without supporting instruction tends to produce no durable change [-M]. Mindset interventions also interact with classroom context: in classrooms where teachers hold fixed-mindset beliefs or use practices that signal ability is fixed (e.g., public ability ranking), student mindset gains are unlikely to translate into achievement [~M]. This contextual dependence parallels the heterogeneity seen in other brief social-psychological interventions such as [belonging interventions](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md).

**Not a substitute for instruction.** Mindset manipulations address beliefs about learning, not the quality of learning itself. They should be treated as a complement to — never a replacement for — sound instructional design such as [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md). Claims that mindset alone can close achievement gaps are not supported [-S].

**Open questions.** Durability beyond one academic year, effects outside Western schooling contexts, and the mechanisms linking mindset change to behavior change (persistence, strategy use, help-seeking) remain contested in the literature. The strategy-use pathway is plausibly mediated by [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md), but direct mediation evidence is thin.
