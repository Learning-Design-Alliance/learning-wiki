# Growth mindset improves achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i0` negligible

Teaching learners that abilities are malleable (a "growth mindset") can raise academic achievement, but effects are small on average and depend heavily on who is targeted and how the intervention is delivered.

## Subclaims

`q4 i0` A short (under one hour), online growth-mindset intervention modestly raised core-course GPA among lower-achieving ninth-graders in a nationally representative, pre-registered US field experiment. [→ Yeager et al. 2019](#yeager-et-al-2019)

`q4 i?` Across two large meta-analyses of the mindset literature, both the mindset–achievement correlation and the effect of mindset interventions on achievement were weak overall, with somewhat more support among low-SES or academically at-risk students. [→ Sisk et al. 2018](#sisk-et-al-2018)

## Evidence

### Yeager et al. 2019

L., & Dweck, C. S. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. *Nature, 573*(7774), 364–369. [doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y)

`q4 · pre-registered, individually randomized field experiment` · `i0 · negligible effect, standardized mean difference = 0.11` · `n=6,320 (target subgroup); N=12,490 overall`

A stratified random sample of 65 US public high schools (12,490 ninth-graders, individually randomized) received either a brief, self-administered online growth-mindset intervention or a control activity. Among the pre-registered subgroup of lower-achieving students (n=6,320, k=65 schools), the intervention raised core-course (math, science, English, social studies) GPA at the end of ninth grade by B=0.10 grade points (95% CI 0.04–0.16, t=3.51, P=0.001), a standardized mean difference effect size of 0.11. The same subgroup also showed reduced fixed-mindset beliefs (n=5,650, standardized effect 0.33). Effects on GPA were larger in schools whose peer norms already supported the growth-mindset message.

### Sisk et al. 2018

Two meta-analyses. *Psychological Science, 29*(4), 549–571. [doi:10.1177/0956797617739704](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704)

`q4 · meta-analysis (two, k=273 and k=43)` · `i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=365,915 (correlational meta-analysis); n=57,155 (intervention meta-analysis)`

Two meta-analyses: the first (k=273 studies, N=365,915) examined the correlational strength of the mindset–achievement relationship and its moderators; the second (k=43 studies, N=57,155) examined the effect of mindset *interventions* on achievement and its moderators. The abstract reports overall effects as weak for both meta-analyses, with some support for the theory's prediction that students of low socioeconomic status or academically at-risk students benefit more from mindset interventions than the general population. ## Discussion

**Small average effects, meaningful for at-risk students.** The best-known national study of mindset interventions in U.S. high schools (Yeager et al., 2019, *Nature*) found overall effects on GPA were small (roughly 0.1 GPA points) [~M], but effects were meaningfully larger for lower-achieving and economically disadvantaged students [+M]. This pattern — near-null averages masking heterogeneous effects — is the central interpretive challenge for this claim. A widely cited meta-analysis (Sisk et al., 2018, *Psychological Science*) reported weak overall correlations between mindset and achievement and small intervention effects, concluding that mindset is not a universal lever [~M]. Until formal Evidence entries are added for these studies, the page's `weak` strength rating reflects this mixed meta-analytic picture rather than absence of research.

**Delivery conditions matter.** Effects appear strongest when interventions are (a) delivered as integrated, sequenced sessions rather than one-off assemblies, (b) embedded in academic content rather than generic motivation, and (c) accompanied by structural changes in teaching practice. A standalone "you can do anything" session without supporting instruction tends to produce no durable change [-M]. Mindset interventions also interact with classroom context: in classrooms where teachers hold fixed-mindset beliefs or use practices that signal ability is fixed (e.g., public ability ranking), student mindset gains are unlikely to translate into achievement [~M]. This contextual dependence parallels the heterogeneity seen in other brief social-psychological interventions such as [belonging interventions](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md).

**Not a substitute for instruction.** Mindset manipulations address beliefs about learning, not the quality of learning itself. They should be treated as a complement to — never a replacement for — sound instructional design such as [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md). Claims that mindset alone can close achievement gaps are not supported [-S].

**Open questions.** Durability beyond one academic year, effects outside Western schooling contexts, and the mechanisms linking mindset change to behavior change (persistence, strategy use, help-seeking) remain contested in the literature. The strategy-use pathway is plausibly mediated by [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md), but direct mediation evidence is thin.

*Merged from “Growth Mindset Interventions Improve Achievement” (growth-mindset-interventions-improve-achievement):* **Effect size and heterogeneity.** The overall effect of mindset interventions on achievement is small on average, and the literature is marked by substantial heterogeneity and an ongoing replication debate. Effects appear larger for at-risk or academically vulnerable students, for interventions delivered through structured multi-session curricula rather than single sessions, and when interventions are embedded in school contexts that reinforce the growth message. Short, standalone assemblies or one-off lessons often produce negligible achievement gains. Until studies are added to the Evidence section above, no effect-size estimate can be stated with confidence on this page.

**Mechanism.** Mindset interventions are theorized to work by changing how students interpret difficulty — from evidence of fixed inability to a signal that effort and strategy change are worthwhile. This places mindset work close to related motivational levers: see [Belonging interventions improve outcomes](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) and [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md), which target adjacent social-motivational pathways. The "growth" framing is most credible to students when it is paired with concrete strategy instruction; effort praise without strategy support can even backfire, since students who try hard and fail anyway may conclude ability really is fixed.

**Boundary conditions.** A growth message delivered in a classroom that still rewards only performance, ranks students publicly, or offers no strategy support is unlikely to change behavior. Mindset interventions pair most naturally with [Assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) practices that make improvement visible and actionable. Open questions include how long effects persist, whether effects compound across repeated doses, and how much of the measured effect reflects expectancy or demand characteristics rather than genuine belief change.

**Design implications.** Practitioners should treat mindset interventions as a complement to, not a substitute for, instructional quality. The most defensible implementations (a) target students at risk of disengagement, (b) use multi-session curricula with spaced follow-ups, and (c) align grading, feedback, and classroom norms with the malleability message. Measuring belief change — not just achievement — helps distinguish genuine mechanism from placebo effects.

*Merged from “Growth mindset interventions produce small positive effects on academic outcomes” (growth-mindset-interventions-small-effects):* **Small average effects, heterogeneous conditions.** The claim is deliberately about magnitude: growth mindset interventions are not discredited, but their average effect on grades or test scores is small (typically around d ≈ 0.08–0.10 in large meta-analyses) [-W], with wide heterogeneity across studies. Effects tend to be larger for at-risk or low-achieving students [+M], in studies with higher design quality [+M], and when interventions change actual teaching practices rather than delivering a one-off message [+M].

**Moderators matter more than the mean.** A one-size-fits-all reading of the literature is misleading. The intervention appears to work by redirecting effort and persistence after setbacks, so it should benefit learners most when the learning environment actually rewards renewed effort — i.e., when tasks are challenging but attainable and feedback is actionable [~M]. In environments where effort cannot change outcomes, a mindset message alone is unlikely to help [-M]. This aligns with broader findings that [assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md): mindset messages presuppose an environment in which effort is informative and rewarded.

**Distal outcome, brief treatment.** Most interventions are short (a few sessions or less) targeting a distal outcome (semester grades), which caps the plausible effect size. Designers should treat mindset interventions as cheap, low-risk complements to — not substitutes for — instructional quality, feedback, and [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).

**Replication controversy.** Large-scale preregistered replications and national studies have found effects near zero on average [-W], sharpening the debate over publication bias in the original small-sample literature. The most defensible reading is that effects are real but rare and narrow: concentrated in struggling students in supportive environments, not general-purpose boosts.

**Open questions.** Long-term durability of effects, dose–response relationships, and whether effects compound when combined with [belonging interventions](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) remain actively debated. It is also unresolved whether mindset gains persist once the intervention context ends, or whether they require ongoing environmental support to maintain.
