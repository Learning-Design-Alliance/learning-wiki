## Evidence

### Sayer 2015

Sayer, R., Maries, A., & Singh, C. (2015). Developing and evaluating a tutorial on the double-slit experiment. [doi:10.1119/perc.2015.pr.070](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2015.pr.070)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r3`

Pre/post categorization of responses to Q3-5 (lamp intensity halved so only half of Na atoms scatter photons, wavelength significantly larger than slit separation), summarized in Table VII. Categories (A) and (B) indicate recognition that intensity does not matter in this situation.

> "While about 94% of undergraduates recognized this fact on the post -test, only about 51% of the graduate students did so."

### Sayer 2015 (2)

Sayer, R., Maries, A., & Singh, C. (2015). Developing and evaluating a tutorial on the double-slit experiment. [doi:10.1119/perc.2015.pr.070](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2015.pr.070)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Authors' interpretation: the difficulty reflects the "phenomenological primitive" that changing the input should change the output. "changing the intensity of the lamp has no effect on the pattern" in this situation. More graduates used this primitive on the post-test (40%) than the pre-test (34%).

> "The common difficulty with question pair 3 -5 illustrates a powerful phenomenological primitive, i.e., if you change something in the input, it should change something in the output [10]."
