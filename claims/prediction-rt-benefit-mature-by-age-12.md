# Reaction-time benefit from predictive context is similar across ages 12–24, suggesting mature prediction abilities by age 12

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r2` · `q3` · `i3` large

## Subclaims
`q3 i3` Reaction times to predicted targets were significantly shorter than to random targets, with no effect of age group and no predictability × age interaction. [→ Thillay A 2015](#thillay-a-2015)

## Evidence

### Thillay A 2015

Thillay A, Roux S, Gissot V, Carteau-Martin I, Knight RT, Bonnet-Brilhault F and Bidet-Caulet A (2015) Sustained attention and prediction: distinct brain maturation trajectories during adolescence. Front. Hum. Neurosci. 9:519. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00519

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r2`

Repeated-measures ANOVA on reaction times in the 36-participant detection task compared predicted versus random targets across the three age groups. The large predictability effect ("F(1,33) = 91.23, p< 0.001, ηp = 0.734") did not interact with age, indicating adolescents extracted and used predictive information as well as adults.

> "The RT to predTs were significantly shorter than those to randTs ( F(1,33) = 91.23, p< 0.001, ηp = 0.734, power = 1.000). No effect of age group ( F(2,33) = 1.24, p = 0.302, ηp = 0.070), nor predictability × age interaction ( F(2,33) = 0.44, p = 0.645, ηp = 0.026) was found significant."
