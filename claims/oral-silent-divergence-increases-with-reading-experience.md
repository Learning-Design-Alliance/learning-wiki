# The oral–silent reading difference increases with reading experience, plausibly because a less extensive orthographic lexicon forces dyslexic readers to rely on phonological processing during silent reading

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Madelon van den Boer 2022

Madelon van den Boer, Loes Bazen, Elise de Bree. (2022). The Same yet Different: Oral and Silent Reading in Children and Adolescents with Dyslexia. Journal of Psycholinguistic Research. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-022-09856-w

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Authors' interpretation in the General Discussion, combining their findings with Gagliano et al. (2015): students with dyslexia read less, yielding a less extensive orthographic lexicon, "thus hampering reading directly through the orthographic lexicon during silent reading" and increasing reliance on phonological processing.

> "With increasing reading experience, oral and silent reading seem to diverge more. For individuals with dyslexia, who lag behind in reading experience, this difference appears smaller than for their peers with average to good reading abilities."
