## Evidence

### Scott Williams and Michael Courtney 2010

Scott Williams and Michael Courtney. (2010). Why Cheating is Wrong. U.S. Air Force Academy. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1102.1506](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1102.1506)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Theoretical argument from the commentary's second section: credentials certify competence, so dishonestly earned credentials create an "unfounded illusion of professional competence." The authors illustrate with physicians and lab technicians who cheated in required courses.

> "since academic credentials are criteria for professional certifications, academic dishonesty carries the risk of the unfounded illusion of professional competence."

### Scott Williams and Michael Courtney 2010 (2)

Scott Williams and Michael Courtney. (2010). Why Cheating is Wrong. U.S. Air Force Academy. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1102.1506](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1102.1506)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

The authors' normative argument that the public expects doctors, pilots, engineers, and military officers to have genuinely earned their credentials, and that cheating in professional training damages both expertise expectations and public trust.

> "Cheating is wrong because academic dishonesty in the training of these professions undermines both the expected level of expertise and the expected level of trust."
