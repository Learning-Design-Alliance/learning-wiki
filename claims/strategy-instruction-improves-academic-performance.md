# Strategy Instruction Improves Academic Performance

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i1`–`i2`

Explicitly teaching learners cognitive and metacognitive strategies — how to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own thinking — raises academic performance across domains and age groups.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 84 intervention studies in primary and secondary school, training students in self-regulated learning produced an average effect of 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use and motivation combined, with larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training and in mathematics than in reading/writing. [→ Dignath & Büttner 2008](#dignath-buttner-2008)

`q3 i1` In elementary and secondary students, measured self-regulated learning is only weakly correlated with academic achievement (metacognitive processes r = 0.20; cognitive strategies r = 0.11), and the correlation varies by process, subject, grade level and how SRL and achievement are measured. [→ Dent & Koenka 2016](#dent-koenka-2016)

## Evidence

### Dignath & Büttner 2008

Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level. *Metacognition and Learning, 3*(3), 231–264. [doi:10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)

`q4 · meta-analysis of intervention studies` · `i2 · medium-to-large effect, average ES = 0.69` · `n=84 studies (357 effect sizes)`

Two separate meta-analyses pooled self-regulated learning training studies: 49 with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students, 357 effect sizes in all. Outcomes were academic performance, strategy use and motivation, and the 0.69 average covers all three together. The abstract does not give a separate figure for achievement. Meta-regression found larger effects when researchers, not regular teachers, delivered the training, and in mathematics compared with reading/writing or other subjects. The theory behind the programme and the type of strategy taught had different effects at the two school levels. Read as abstract only.

### Dent & Koenka 2016

Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 28*(3), 425–474. [doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8)

`q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies` · `i1 · small correlation, r = 0.20 (metacognitive processes), r = 0.11 (cognitive strategies)` · `n=?`

Two meta-analyses of elementary and secondary school students related achievement to (1) metacognitive processes of self-regulated learning and (2) use of cognitive strategies. Both overall correlations were small: r = 0.20 for metacognitive processes and r = 0.11 for cognitive strategies. The correlations differed significantly by the specific process or strategy, academic subject, grade level, type of SRL measure and type of achievement measure. This is the correlational arm of the claim. It supports a real but modest association, not a causal effect. The number of studies was not in the abstract. Read as abstract only.

## Discussion

**Scope of the claim.** "Strategy instruction" here covers explicit teaching of domain strategies (e.g., summarization, self-questioning, graphic organization) and metacognitive routines (planning, monitoring, evaluating). The claim is deliberately broad; the strength of the effect almost certainly varies by strategy type, domain, and learner age; the training meta-analysis recorded above reports larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training.

**Likely moderators.** Strategy instruction is generally expected to work best when strategies are taught explicitly with modeling and guided practice rather than merely recommended, when learners are novices who lack effective default approaches, and when instruction includes why-and-when conditions for strategy use so learners transfer the strategy to new tasks. Instruction that stops at naming a strategy, without practice and feedback, should be expected to produce much weaker effects. Strategy instruction also interacts with load: a well-chosen strategy can free working-memory resources (see [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)), but a poorly matched strategy can add extraneous load.

**Boundary conditions to test.** Three conditions in which strategy instruction should be expected to underperform: (1) when the strategy is taught as a generic recommendation without modeling or guided practice; (2) when the strategy is mismatched to the task or the learner's expertise level — for advanced learners, a taught strategy can be redundant or even interfere with an already-efficient approach, echoing the expertise-reversal pattern described in [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md); (3) when the strategy itself imposes more processing than it saves, degrading rather than supporting performance under [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md).

**Relation to self-regulation.** Strategy instruction is one of the most trainable components of self-regulated learning; teaching learners *how* to study is a lever on the broader construct described in [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). Open questions for this page: how durable strategy effects are after instruction ends, and whether effects on performance are mediated by strategy use itself or by increased time on task.

**What evidence is needed.** To calibrate this claim, the page requires at minimum: (a) a meta-analysis quantifying the effect of strategy instruction on academic performance overall; (b) evidence separating cognitive from metacognitive strategy training; and (c) evidence on durability — whether strategy use persists after instruction ends. Until such entries are added, the claim should be treated as a broad framing statement rather than a calibrated effect.
