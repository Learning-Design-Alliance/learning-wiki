Training parents to be involved in homework raises homework completion (significant under fixed effects only) and cuts homework problems but barely moves achievement, and the correlation between parent homework involvement and achievement depends on grade, type of involvement and subject¶

Parent involvement in homework covers monitoring that homework is done, setting rules about when and where it is done, and helping directly with its content. Its overall relation to achievement is small and inconsistent; the experiments show clearer effects on the proximal outcomes (whether homework gets done, and how much trouble it causes) than on achievement.

Evidence¶

Patall, Cooper & Robinson 2008¶

Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). Parent involvement in homework: A research synthesis. Review of Educational Research, 78(4), 1039–1101. doi:10.3102/0034654308325185

q3 · meta-analysis of randomized and quasi-experimental parent-training studies plus correlational studies, not pre-registered · i1 · small effects on achievement and homework completion (d ≈ .1 to .28), large on homework problems (d = −.84) · n=14 parent-training studies; 20 correlational studies (22 samples); 23 studies with statistical controls · quant-synthesis · r?

A synthesis of studies from 1987–2003 of students in kindergarten to grade 12 in the United States and Canada, analysed by design. Parent-training experiments (14 studies, 11 unpublished): in six with families randomly assigned, training for homework involvement raised homework completion (d = .28, 95% CI .00 to .55 fixed; .28 random, p < .06) and reduced homework problems (d = −.84 fixed, −1.20 random), but its effect on achievement was not significant (d = .11 fixed, .09 random, five studies); one study found poorer attitudes after training (d = −.49). Within these experiments, grade moderated the achievement effect under fixed effects (grades 2–5 d = .22, 95% CI .02 to .42; grades 6–8 d = −.18), though not significantly under random effects; no experiment included high school students. Three experiments that assigned classrooms found no achievement effect (d = .00), and five quasi-experiments found a positive one (d = .23 fixed, .22 random) that the authors trust less because of how groups were equated and analysed. Studies with statistical controls were split: every coefficient from three national-survey (NELS) reports was negative, and of 20 other samples 10 were positive, 8 negative and 2 mixed. Simple correlations (20 studies, 22 samples, median n about 180): overall r = .04 (fixed; significant) and .07 (random; not significant), with large heterogeneity. Robust moderators were grade (grades 1–6 r = .06, grades 7–8 r = −.17, grades 9–12 r = .17), type of involvement (rule setting r = .54, direct aid .10, monitoring −.09; the monitoring entry in Table 12 is misprinted as −.90, and the text gives −.09) and subject (reading .20, language arts .12, mathematics −.19). Correlations were positive in four low-income samples (r = .36) and negative in three middle-class samples (r = −.19), a difference significant only under fixed effects. The authors note that the correlations likely mix a helpful effect of involvement with parents getting more involved when children are struggling, especially after the move to middle school.

Discussion¶

The experiments and the correlations agree on three points: involvement does more for homework behaviour than for achievement, it looks helpful in elementary school but not in middle school, and its form matters. Rules about when and where homework happens and direct help with content relate positively to achievement, while monitoring alone relates negatively. In the experiments, the one training programme centred on monitoring completion was also the only one with a negative effect on completion. This fits the finding on parental compliance versus process support. The rule-setting and subject findings rest on correlations from a handful of samples, and most differences held only under fixed-effect assumptions. Later and larger syntheses that pool home and school involvement are on Parent involvement is associated with higher academic achievement.
