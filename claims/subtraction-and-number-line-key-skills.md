## Evidence

### Käser 2013

Käser, T., Busetto, A.G., Solenthaler, B., Baschera, G.-M., Kohn, J., Kucian, K., von Aster, M., Gross, M. (2013). Modelling and Optimizing Mathematics Learning in Children. International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-013-0003-7

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Key-skill analysis of input logs from 63 participants: mean normalized key-skill numbers were 0.26 (SD 0.19) in number representation, 0.17 (SD 0.2) in addition and 0.37 (SD 0.15) in subtraction; the subtraction difference was significant at p<.001. No effect size printed.

> "the normalized number of key skills in subtraction is significantly higher than in the two other categories (indicated by a two-sided t-test: p<. 001 for both categories)."

### Käser 2013 (2)

Käser, T., Busetto, A.G., Solenthaler, B., Baschera, G.-M., Kohn, J., Kucian, K., von Aster, M., Gross, M. (2013). Modelling and Optimizing Mathematics Learning in Children. International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-013-0003-7

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Descriptive key-skill analysis: indicating a number's position on a 0–100 number line was difficult for 52% of children, and more than 50% had problems with Subtraction 2,1 TC and Subtraction 2,2 TC, which the article links to spatial number representation.

> "More than 50 % of the children also had problems in subtraction in the number range from 0–100, when a ten crossing was involved (Subtraction 2,1 TC and Subtraction 2,2 TC in Fig.2)."
