Evidence¶

Sunae Kim 2025¶

Sunae Kim, Paul L Harris, Benjamin Néher. (2025). Curiosity can influence metacognitive processes. Metacognition and Learning (2025) 20:39. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-025-09444-y

q3 · i3 · causal · r2

Experiment 1 mixed-effects regression (participants as random effect) found curiosity lower on correct trials, "F(1, 2598) = 179, p <.001" with β = −0.57; mean curiosity was 49.6 on correct vs 65.9 on incorrect trials (0–100 scale).

"Participants' curiosity level was lower for correct than for incorrect answers F(1, 2598) = 179, p <.001 (b = −15.86, SE = 1.19, β = − 0.57)."

Related Claims¶

  • The opt-out and curiosity–accuracy findings replicate in a larger UK sample (Experiment 3A)
  • Exposure to others' discrepant curiosity ratings reduces the curiosity gap between opted-out and non-opted-out trials and between incorrect and correct trials, altering metacognitive control — related
  • Without feedback, people are more curious about high-confidence errors than about equally high-confidence correct answers, replicated across experiments — related
  • Adults opt out of answering more on trials they later answer incorrectly, and curiosity is higher on opted-out trials (metacognitive control) — related
  • Making high-confidence errors about educational misconceptions intensifies preservice teachers' surprise, curiosity, confusion, and achievement-related anger — related
  • Students' confidence distinguishes accurate from inaccurate model-evaluation trials, with a large printed effect size (d = 1.09), though students remain often confident on inaccurate trials — related
