# Leveraging students' outside disciplinary expertise can productively change their affective stance toward physics (Violet case study)

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` A longitudinal case study of one student found that creating opportunities to draw on her biology expertise shifted her from expecting to hate physics to a more positive orientation in which physics, biology, and chemistry were linked. [→ Redish 2014](#redish-2014)

## Evidence

### Redish 2014

Redish, E. F., Sawtelle, V., & Turpen, C. (2014). The role physics can play in a multi-disciplinary curriculum for non-physics scientists and engineers. Proceedings of the Frontiers in Mathematics and Science Education Research Conference, Famagusta, North Cyprus. [doi:10.30935/scimath/9621](https://doi.org/10.30935/scimath/9621)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Longitudinal case study of one student, Violet, in the second iteration of NEXUS/Physics, based on classroom observation and interviews. Violet entered publicly expecting physics to make her life a living hell, but after a group task modeling DNA as a spring, in which she explained DNA structure to the TA at the chalkboard, she reported that seeing the relationship among the three subjects made her like physics more.

> "By creating opportunities to leverage her outside expertise she left the course with a more positive orientation toward physics, and describing the subject as productively linked to biology and chemistry."
