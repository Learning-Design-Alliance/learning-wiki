# Highlighting shows low utility for improving learning outcomes

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Highlighting and underlining text is one of the most widely used study strategies, yet it shows little benefit for learning outcomes relative to simply reading.

## Discussion

**Why highlighting underperforms.** Highlighting is a passive, low-effort strategy: it can be performed fluently without the deep processing that produces durable memory [-M]. It may even create an *illusion of competence* — learners mistake the fluency of re-reading highlighted text for genuine understanding [-M], and highlighted fragments can disrupt the integration of ideas across a passage by isolating individual sentences from their context [-W]. This places highlighting in tension with [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md): rather than imposing the desirable difficulties that drive encoding, it minimizes effort at exactly the moment effort is needed.

**Learner skill matters.** The utility of highlighting depends heavily on how it is done [~M]. Learners who highlight indiscriminately — marking large proportions of the text — gain little, because over-highlighting eliminates the selectivity that might otherwise direct attention to key ideas [-M]. Even skilled highlighting mostly helps on questions that directly target the highlighted content, not on inference or integration questions [~M]. This mirrors the [coherence principle](coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md): extraneous emphasis can divert attention from essential content rather than concentrating it [-M].

**Better alternatives.** Strategies that require generative processing — [retrieval practice](../strategies/retrieval-practice.md), self-explanation, and [annotating](../principles/annotating.md) with written notes — consistently outperform highlighting because they force learners to reconstruct and elaborate the material rather than merely select it [+M]. Highlighting may retain a modest role as a first-pass selection step that feeds a more demanding second pass (e.g., converting highlights into questions or summaries) [~W], but as a standalone strategy its expected payoff is low.

**Open questions.** Most evidence comes from short laboratory texts with immediate or short-delay tests; effects on complex, multi-session learning of authentic materials are less well established. Digital highlighting tools add features (colors, aggregation across documents) whose learning benefits remain largely untested.
