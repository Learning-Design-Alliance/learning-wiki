# Peer Feedback Improves Writing

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Structured peer feedback on writing improves the quality of subsequent drafts and revisions, and engaging students as reviewers of others' writing can also strengthen their own writing. The claim covers both directions of the exchange — receiving feedback and giving it — and applies to structured peer review arrangements rather than informal comment exchange.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Peer feedback is thought to work through two routes: receiving feedback gives writers actionable, audience-based input for revision, and giving feedback requires evaluating text against criteria, which deepens the reviewer's own understanding of quality writing. Both routes align with [Assessment for Learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) — feedback used by learners to close gaps, rather than merely delivered to them. The reviewer route also draws on [Annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md)-style engagement: critiquing a peer's text forces close reading against explicit criteria.

**Conditions for effectiveness.** The literature consistently suggests peer feedback helps most when reviewers are trained, when feedback is criterion-referenced (rubrics, exemplars), and when writers are required to act on it through structured revision. Unstructured or purely evaluative comments ("good job") tend not to improve drafts. Peer feedback also complements rather than replaces instructor feedback; its distinctive value is volume, timeliness, and exposure to authentic audience variation — see [Authentic audiences improve student work](authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md).

**Constraints.** Peer feedback fails or backfires under identifiable conditions. Untrained reviewers given no criteria produce vague or purely evaluative comments that do not improve drafts [-M]. Novice reviewers may lack the domain expertise to diagnose substantive problems, so feedback skews toward surface errors [~M]. Social dynamics — friendship bias, fear of offense, status differences — can distort or suppress critical comments, particularly in small face-to-face classes; anonymized or rubric-scaffolded exchange mitigates this [~M]. Where revision is not required or supported, feedback that is received but not acted on produces little gain, consistent with [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [-M]. Peer feedback also cannot substitute for expert feedback when learners need authoritative correction of misconceptions about the discipline's conventions [~M].

**Effects on revision vs. quality.** Effects on writing quality should be distinguished from effects on revision behavior — students often revise in response to peer comments even when the comments themselves are of mixed quality. Revision activity alone is therefore an unreliable proxy for learning gains.

**Open questions.** Whether giving feedback benefits the giver as much as receiving benefits the receiver, and how feedback quality mediates writing gains, remain active areas of study. This page needs primary evidence entries before its strength can be rated.
