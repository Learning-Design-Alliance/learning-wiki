# Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

When learners encounter evidence or ideas that conflict with their existing conceptions, the resulting cognitive disequilibrium can motivate them to reorganize their knowledge — the mechanism by which conceptual change occurs.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The claim rests on a Piagetian account of learning: existing schemas are stable until perturbed, and it is the discomfort of disequilibrium — not the conflicting information alone — that drives accommodation. This places the claim within [constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) and connects it to the [cognitive disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) principle as a design lever: instruction must first surface the learner's prior conception, then present a conflict it cannot explain. Classic conceptual-change designs operationalize this as a "predict–observe–explain" sequence, where learners commit to a prediction before seeing disconfirming evidence.

**Boundary conditions.** Disequilibrium is motivating only within a window. If the conflict is too weak, learners assimilate the anomaly to their existing conception and change nothing [~M]; if it is too strong, or if the conflicting material imposes excessive processing demands, learners may disengage or experience the overload that [degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M] rather than productive restructuring. Learners also need sufficient prior knowledge to notice the conflict at all — without [activation](activation-improves-learning.md) of the existing conception, there is nothing to bring into disequilibrium [~M]. Learners with low epistemic tolerance or low self-efficacy in the domain may respond to conflict defensively rather than with restructuring [~W].

**Design implications.** Effective disequilibrium designs share three features: (1) elicit and externalize the prior conception (a committed prediction, an initial explanation) so the conflict has something to attach to; (2) present the disconfirming evidence immediately after the commitment, while the expectation is still active; (3) support resolution — through [analogies](../elements/analogies.md), contrasting cases, or guided discussion — rather than leaving learners to resolve the conflict unaided. Sequences such as the [5E learning cycle](../patterns/5e-learning-cycle.md) build this elicit–conflict–resolve structure into their engage and explain phases.

**Open questions.** The strength of this claim depends on how "conceptual change" is measured: self-reported satisfaction of curiosity, post-test performance, and durable restructuring of misconceptions are different outcomes, and evidence supporting the claim in one does not automatically support it in others. Studies still need to be added to establish the evidence base for this page.
