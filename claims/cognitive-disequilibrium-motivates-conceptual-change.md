## Evidence

### Pacaci et al. 2024

Pacaci, C., Ustun, U., & Ozdemir, O. F. (2024). Effectiveness of conceptual change strategies in science education: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 61*(6), 1263–1325. [doi:10.1002/tea.21887](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.21887)

`q4 · random-effects meta-analysis with robust Bayesian bias adjustment` · `i3 · large effect, g=1.10 (adjusted g=0.93)` · `n=218 studies, 18,051 students` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A random-effects meta-analysis of 218 primary studies testing three families of conceptual change strategy in science learning: cognitive conflict, cognitive bridging and ontological category shift. The overall effect was large (g = 1.10), and still large after a robust Bayesian adjustment (g = 0.93). Cognitive-conflict strategies, the family that most directly works through [cognitive disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md), showed the same effect (g = 1.10, k = 150). Effect sizes varied widely, with a prediction interval of 0.19 to 2.38. The studies measure learning outcomes, not whether disequilibrium was felt, so they support conflict-based *instruction* rather than isolating disequilibrium as the mechanism. The meta-analysis was read as an abstract only.

This meta-analysis combined experimental and quasi-experimental studies from reading education and science education that tested instructional interventions aimed at students' misconceptions. It identified which strategies produced conceptual change and reported that the effective ones shared an element of producing conceptual conflict. Only the brief ERIC abstract was read, so its effect sizes and study counts are not recorded here.

### Lehman et al. 2013

Lehman, B., D'Mello, S., Strain, A., Mills, C., Gross, M., Dobbins, A., Wallace, P., Millis, K., & Graesser, A. (2013). Inducing and Tracking Confusion with Contradictions during Complex Learning. *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education, 22*(1–2), 85–105. [doi:10.3233/JAI-130025](https://doi.org/10.3233/JAI-130025)

`q3 · peer-reviewed within-subjects laboratory experiment` · `i? · no standardized effect size reported; interaction F(3, 240) = 2.56, p = .028` · `n=32` · `causal · r1`

Thirty-two undergraduates took part in eight computer-based "trialogues" about research-methods case studies with an animated tutor agent and an animated student agent. The researchers induced disequilibrium by having the agents disagree or state false information, then asking the learner to decide between them. The contradictions increased self-reported confusion, but on their own they did not improve posttest scores (p = 1.00). In a follow-up analysis that split cases by how confused learners reported being (a median split), learners scored higher after contradictions only in the high-confusion cases. Disequilibrium helped only when it was actually experienced, and the study gave no scaffolding to help learners resolve it.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The claim rests on a Piagetian account of learning: existing schemas are stable until perturbed, and it is the discomfort of disequilibrium — not the conflicting information alone — that drives accommodation. This places the claim within [constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) and connects it to the [cognitive disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) principle as a design lever: instruction must first surface the learner's prior conception, then present a conflict it cannot explain. Classic conceptual-change designs operationalize this as a "predict–observe–explain" sequence, where learners commit to a prediction before seeing disconfirming evidence.

**Boundary conditions.** Disequilibrium is motivating only within a window. If the conflict is too weak, learners assimilate the anomaly to their existing conception and change nothing [~M]; if it is too strong, or if the conflicting material imposes excessive processing demands, learners may disengage or experience the overload that [degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M] rather than productive restructuring. Learners also need sufficient prior knowledge to notice the conflict at all — without [activation](activation-improves-learning.md) of the existing conception, there is nothing to bring into disequilibrium [~M]. Learners with low epistemic tolerance or low self-efficacy in the domain may respond to conflict defensively rather than with restructuring [~W].

**Design implications.** Effective disequilibrium designs share three features: (1) elicit and externalize the prior conception (a committed prediction, an initial explanation) so the conflict has something to attach to; (2) present the disconfirming evidence immediately after the commitment, while the expectation is still active; (3) support resolution — through [analogies](../elements/analogies.md), contrasting cases, or guided discussion — rather than leaving learners to resolve the conflict unaided. Sequences such as the [5E learning cycle](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md) build this elicit–conflict–resolve structure into their engage and explain phases.

**Open questions.** The strength of this claim depends on how "conceptual change" is measured: self-reported satisfaction of curiosity, post-test performance, and durable restructuring of misconceptions are different outcomes, and evidence supporting the claim in one does not automatically support it in others. <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale): Studies still need to be added to establish the evidence base for this page. --> Three studies are recorded above: two meta-analyses of conflict-based instruction (Pacaci et al. 2024 and Guzzetti et al. 1993, both read from abstracts), which measure learning outcomes rather than disequilibrium itself, and one small within-subjects experiment (Lehman et al. 2013) in which staged contradictions helped only when learners reported being confused. None measures durable restructuring of misconceptions, and none isolates disequilibrium as the mechanism.
