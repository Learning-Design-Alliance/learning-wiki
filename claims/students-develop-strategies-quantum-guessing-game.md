## Evidence

### Theodore A. Corcovilos 2018

Theodore A. Corcovilos. (2018). A Simple game simulating quantum measurements of qubits. [doi:10.1119/1.5036620](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.5036620)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Informal instructor observations of seventeen students playing the game (six sophomores in a Modern Physics survey course and eleven junior/senior students in a spins-first Introductory Quantum Mechanics course). The author reports students were "actively engaged" and that after a couple of rounds they developed more sophisticated strategies, "as evidenced by more accurate and more aggressive guessing, resulting in better scores."

> "Informal observations of the students 37 while playing the game show the students were actively engaged. The students were talkative and bought into the competitive nature of the game."

### Theodore A. Corcovilos 2018 (2)

Theodore A. Corcovilos. (2018). A Simple game simulating quantum measurements of qubits. [doi:10.1119/1.5036620](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.5036620)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

The same informal observations describe the strategy progression: students began with elimination of states based on single results "as if every state were a measurement eigenstate," then became more skeptical of any single result because of the chance of unlikely measurements.

> "Early on they used a simple strategy of eliminating states based on single measurement results, as if every state were a measurement eigenstate. Then they became more skeptical of any single result because of the chance of “bad” (unlikely) measurements."
