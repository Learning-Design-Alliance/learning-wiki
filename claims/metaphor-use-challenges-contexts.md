## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Dismissive or stigmatizing prefaces, forcing a metaphor into conversation, phrasing that implied symptoms were not real, and metaphors patients could not relate to (including culturally insensitive ones) reduced metaphor effectiveness. [→ Ha Monica MD 2023](#ha-monica-md-2023)

## Evidence

### Ha Monica MD 2023

Ha Monica MD, Rowe Allyson MD, Hendrix Katlynn, Anwar Summer BS, Mang Khup BS, Wen Frances PhD, Coon Kim EdD, Bragg Jedediah MSW, PhD, Foulks Kristin MPH, Miller-Cribbs Julie MSW, PhD, Jelley Martina MD, MSPH. (2023). Use of Metaphor as an Explanation Tool in Adverse Childhood Experiences Simulation Training. The Permanente Journal. https://doi.org/10.7812/TPP/22.159

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Qualitative content analysis integrating transcribed segments with simulated patient and faculty feedback. The article also reports that phrasing such as "makes the pain worse than it actually is" triggered defensive reactions, and that the sunburn metaphor "may not be culturally sensitive to those with a darker complexion."

> "First, if the clinician prefaced the ACEs discussion using dismissive or stigmatizing language, such as “this may sound weird” or “this is strange, but...” then the metaphor encounter was less likely to receive an effective rating."
