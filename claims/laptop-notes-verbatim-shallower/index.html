Claims

Laptop note-takers transcribed more verbatim and did worse on conceptual questions than longhand note-takers in one set of experiments, but a direct replication found no consistent difference in test performance¶

Claim · All claims
Evidence · 2 studies · 2 causal r? · q3

The proposed mechanism is that laptops, being faster than handwriting, invite learners to transcribe lectures word-for-word rather than summarizing, paraphrasing, and selecting — encoding processes that support comprehension. The claim concerns the style of note-taking that each medium tends to induce, not an inherent property of the devices themselves. Of the two studies recorded, the original experiments support the performance cost and the direct replication does not, so the learning cost is fragile rather than established.

Evidence¶

Mueller Oppenheimer 2014¶

Mueller, P. A., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2014). The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard: Advantages of Longhand Over Laptop Note Taking. Psychological Science, 25(6), 1159–1168. doi:10.1177/0956797614524581

q3 · peer-reviewed experiment (three studies, not pre-registered) · i? · no effect size reported in what was read · n=three experiments, per-study n not stated in the abstract · causal · r?

Three experiments comparing college students who took lecture notes on laptops versus longhand. Laptop note-takers took more notes overall, but tended to transcribe lecture content verbatim rather than summarizing or paraphrasing it. Across the studies, laptop note-takers performed worse than longhand note-takers on conceptual/application test questions, though not consistently on factual-recall questions, which the authors attribute to shallower processing during verbatim transcription. Only the abstract was read (no open-access full text or effect-size figures found); the specific per-study n and effect-size statistics were not available in that text.

Morehead Dunlosky Rawson 2019¶

Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K. A. (2019). How Much Mightier Is the Pen than the Keyboard for Note-Taking? A Replication and Extension of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014). Educational Psychology Review, 31(3), 753–780. doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2

q3 · peer-reviewed direct replication with two experiments, plus an internal meta-analysis · i? · described as small and non-significant; no effect size in what was read · n=unreported in what was read (two experiments, plus added eWriter and no-notes conditions) · causal · r?

A direct replication of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) that added an eWriter group and a no-notes group across two experiments. Test performance did not consistently differ between longhand, laptop, eWriter, or even the no-notes group; group differences shrank further once students were allowed to study their own notes before testing. A meta-analysis combining the direct replications found only small, non-significant effects favoring longhand. The authors conclude that, given this and other available evidence, it is premature to say one note-taking method is superior for learning.

Discussion¶

Mechanism, not hardware. The claim is about how notes are taken, not the device itself. Verbatim transcription is the hypothesized mediator: when note-taking becomes capture rather than synthesis, the generative processing that benefits memory and comprehension is lost. This aligns with the broader finding that annotating improves learning when annotation requires transformation of material rather than passive marking. Handwriting's slower pace acts as a natural constraint that forces selection and paraphrase, consistent with chunking reduces working memory load — summarizing notes forces chunking, while verbatim transcription does not.

Boundary conditions to test. The effect should be weakest when learners are explicitly instructed to summarize rather than transcribe, when the lecture pace is too fast for synthesis — a condition under which cognitive overload degrades learning and learners may fall back on transcription as a coping strategy — or when content is dense with technical detail where verbatim capture may genuinely aid later review. Learners with motor or writing difficulties may also face a trade-off not captured by the general claim; for these learners, laptop capture may be the more accessible option, and the appropriate intervention is training in generative note-taking strategies rather than device restriction.

Open questions. Whether longhand advantages persist when laptop users receive summarization training, and whether the effect holds for conceptual (as opposed to factual) test questions, remain active areas of replication. Confidence in this claim should stay provisional: a direct replication (Morehead et al. 2019, above) found no consistent difference between longhand and laptop groups, so the performance cost of laptop notes is fragile. Whether the replication also found more verbatim overlap in laptop notes is not stated in its abstract.
