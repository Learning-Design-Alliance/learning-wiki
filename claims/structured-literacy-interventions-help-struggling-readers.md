# Structured Literacy Interventions Help Struggling Readers

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i0` negligible

Structured literacy — explicit, systematic, cumulative instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, and decoding — improves word reading for students who struggle, relative to less explicit or incidental approaches.

## Subclaims

`q4 i0` A meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (49 comparisons) of reading-disabled children and adolescents finds phonics instruction — the core explicit, systematic decoding component of structured literacy — is the only treatment approach whose effect on reading and spelling performance is statistically confirmed, with a small-to-moderate publication-bias-adjusted effect. [→ Galuschka et al. 2014](#galuschka-et-al-2014)

`q3 i0` A meta-analysis of Orton-Gillingham (a branded structured-literacy approach) interventions for students with or at risk for word-level reading disabilities found positive but statistically non-significant mean effects on foundational skills and on vocabulary/comprehension, qualifying how strong current evidence is for one widely-mandated structured-literacy program specifically. [→ Stevens et al. 2021](#stevens-et-al-2021)

## Evidence

### Galuschka et al. 2014

Galuschka, K., Ise, E., Krick, K., & Schulte-Körne, G. (2014). Effectiveness of Treatment Approaches for Children and Adolescents with Reading Disabilities: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials. *PLoS ONE, 9*(2), e89900. [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0089900](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0089900)

`q4 · meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials` · `i0 · negligible effect, g'=0.198 (95% CI 0.039–0.357)` · `n=22 RCTs (49 comparisons)`

Systematic literature search (ERIC, PsycINFO, PubMed, Cochrane, plus unpublished-trial searches) identified 22 RCTs comparing treatments for children/adolescents below the 25th percentile in reading. Across seven treatment categories (phonics, phonemic awareness, reading fluency, reading comprehension, auditory training, medical treatment, coloured overlays/lenses), phonics instruction — 29 of the 49 comparisons — was "the only approach whose efficacy on reading and spelling performance ... is statistically confirmed," with a publication-bias-adjusted effect of g' = 0.198 (95% CI [0.039, 0.357]); the mean effect sizes of every other treatment category did not reach statistical significance. The authors conclude that "systematic instruction of letter-sound-correspondences and decoding strategies, and the application of these skills in reading and writing activities, is the most effective method for improving literacy skills of children and adolescents with reading disabilities."

### Stevens et al. 2021

Stevens, E. A., Austin, C., Moore, C., Scammacca, N., Boucher, A. N., & Vaughn, S. (2021). Current State of the Evidence: Examining the Effects of Orton-Gillingham Reading Interventions for Students With or at Risk for Word-Level Reading Disabilities. *Exceptional Children, 87*(4), 397–417. [doi:10.1177/0014402921993406](https://doi.org/10.1177/0014402921993406)

`q3 · meta-analysis (mixed RCT/quasi-experimental)` · `i0 · non-significant effects, ES=0.22 (foundational skills) and 0.14 (vocabulary/comprehension)` · `n=16 studies in meta-analysis (24 identified)`

A meta-analysis of 24 identified studies (16 with sufficient data for pooling; 15 studies of quasi-experimental or randomized design) of Orton-Gillingham, described in the paper as "a direct, explicit, multisensory, structured, sequential, diagnostic, and prescriptive approach to reading," for students with or at risk for word-level reading disabilities. The weighted mean effect on foundational-skill outcomes (phonological awareness, phonics, fluency, spelling) was ES = 0.22 (95% CI [−0.33, 0.77]), not statistically significant (p = .40); vocabulary/comprehension outcomes showed ES = 0.14, also non-significant (p = .59). The authors state this "yielded findings lacking support for the effectiveness of Orton-Gillingham interventions" comparable to a prior synthesis and What Works Clearinghouse reports, while noting the mean effect was positive and that more high-quality, adequately powered research is needed.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The proposed mechanism is that explicit, sequenced decoding instruction builds automatic word recognition, which in turn frees attentional resources for comprehension — see [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md). Structured literacy's emphasis on small, cumulative steps also aligns with working-memory limits: instruction that chunks new grapheme–phoneme correspondences rather than presenting them all at once reduces load — see [Chunking reduces working memory load.](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md). Unstructured or incidental approaches, by contrast, can overwhelm struggling readers whose decoding is not yet automatic — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md).

**Boundary conditions.** The claim is scoped to *struggling* readers. For students who acquire decoding incidentally or arrive already fluent, heavy explicit phonics instruction may add little and could displace time on vocabulary, syntax, and comprehension work — a plausible expertise-reversal-style moderator, consistent with the general pattern that highly scaffolded instruction loses its advantage as expertise grows — see [Expertise reversal effect.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md). Effects should also be expected to be strongest on word-level outcomes (decoding, word reading, spelling) and weaker or more indirect on comprehension, which depends on language knowledge the intervention does not directly teach.

**Design implications.** Practitioners implementing structured literacy should treat it as one component of a comprehensive reading program: once decoding is secured, instructional time is better shifted toward vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and background knowledge. Cumulative review — revisiting previously taught correspondences rather than only adding new ones — is a defining feature of the approach and aligns with spacing and retrieval practice principles. Intervention delivery should be explicit and teacher-directed rather than discovery-oriented; the broader pattern favoring explicit guidance for novices — see [Direct instruction.](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) and [Cognitive load theory.](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — is the theoretical backbone of the structured literacy movement.

**Open questions.** Optimal dosage and intensity, the durability of gains after intervention ends, and how much comprehension benefit follows once decoding is secured all require stronger evidence than this page currently carries. The evidence recorded above finds small effects: phonics instruction was the only approach with a confirmed effect for reading disabilities (g′ = 0.198), and Orton-Gillingham programs showed non-significant effects.
