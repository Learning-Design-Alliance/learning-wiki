Evidence¶

Triona 2003¶

Triona, L. M., & Klahr, D. (2003). Point and Click or Grab and Heft: Comparing the Influence of Physical and Virtual Instructional Materials on Elementary School Students' Ability to Design Experiments. Cognition and Instruction, 21(2), 149–173. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2102_02

q3 · i? · causal · r2

A 2 (condition: physical vs. virtual) × 3 (phase) MANOVA with phase within-participant on 92 children's experiment designs found "no main effect of condition, F(1, 90) = .01, ns". Proportions of unconfounded experiments rose from pretest M = .20 (SD = .26) to posttest M = .65 (SD = .41).

"showed a main effect for phase, F(2, 89) = 65.93, p < .001, with no main effect of condition, F(1, 90) = .01, ns, and no interaction between phase and condition, F(2, 89) = 0.73, ns."

Triona 2003 (2)¶

Triona, L. M., & Klahr, D. (2003). Point and Click or Grab and Heft: Comparing the Influence of Physical and Virtual Instructional Materials on Elementary School Students' Ability to Design Experiments. Cognition and Instruction, 21(2), 149–173. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2102_02

q3 · i? · causal · r2

In the transfer phase one week after training, all children worked with physical ramps. The article reports that virtual-trained children "designed as many unconfounded experiments with the physical materials during the transfer phase" as physical-trained children, despite the extra medium change.

"Moreover, children trained with virtual materials designed as many unconfounded experiments with the physical materials during the transfer phase as children who used physical materials for all phases."
