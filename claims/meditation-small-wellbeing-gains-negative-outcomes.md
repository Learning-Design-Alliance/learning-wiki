# School meditation programmes foster small improvements in well-being, more consistently reducing negative outcomes than promoting positive ones

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Meditation programmes show small improvements in student well-being, with benefits more consistent for reducing negative states (anxiety, stress, negative affect) than for promoting positive states. [→ Lea Waters 2015](#lea-waters-2015)
`q3 i3` In Mendelson et al.'s RCT, mood was unaffected but meditating students scored significantly lower on involuntary stress responses (e.g. involuntary engagement d=.83, rumination d=.70). [→ Lea Waters 2015 (2)](#lea-waters-2015-2)

## Evidence

### Lea Waters 2015

Lea Waters, Adam Barsky, Amanda Ridd, Kelly Allen. (2015). Contemplative Education: A Systematic, Evidence-Based Review of the effect of Meditation Interventions in Schools. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9258-2

`q3 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review's synthesis of nine studies (n=1,483) on well-being, providing 17 results of which 59% were significant, with Cohen's d ranging from 0.02 to 0.62. The review concludes "The benefits are more consistent for reducing negative rather than promoting positive outcomes."

> "The overall profile suggests that meditation fosters small improvements in well-being. The benefits are more consistent for reducing negative rather than promoting positive outcomes."

### Lea Waters 2015 (2)

Lea Waters, Adam Barsky, Amanda Ridd, Kelly Allen. (2015). Contemplative Education: A Systematic, Evidence-Based Review of the effect of Meditation Interventions in Schools. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9258-2

`q3 · i3` · `review · r3`

Randomised controlled trial of 12 weeks of mindfulness meditation in fourth and fifth graders (intervention=51, control=46). The review reports no differences in positive or negative affect, but significant reductions in stress responses with printed effect sizes, e.g. "involuntary engagement (d=.83)" and rumination d=.70.

> "at post-test, students who underwent the intervention scored significantly lower on emotional arousal ( d=.64), intrusive thoughts (d=.51), involuntary engagement (d=.83) and rumination ( d=.70), suggesting that while mood was unaffected, general stress and coping responses were improved."
