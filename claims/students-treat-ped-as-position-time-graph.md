## Evidence

### Stephanik 2011

Stephanik, B. M., and Shaffer, P. S. (2011). Examining student ability to interpret and use potential energy diagrams for classical systems. [doi:10.1063/1.3680071](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3680071)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Analysis of written and interview responses to the diagram questions: "Between 10% and 30% of the students" read kinematic quantities directly off the diagram, consistent with interpreting it as a position vs. time graph. Students also described local minima as turn-around points.

> "Between 10% and 30% of the students found the ve- locity of the particle from the slope of the potential en- ergy diagram or the acceleration from the second deriva- tive."

### Stephanik 2011 (2)

Stephanik, B. M., and Shaffer, P. S. (2011). Examining student ability to interpret and use potential energy diagrams for classical systems. [doi:10.1063/1.3680071](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3680071)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

In a sophomore-level quantum mechanics class, students sketched a potential energy diagram for an Earth-ball system near the surface of Earth; "Roughly 20% of the students drew curved graphs," appearing to convey the increasing rate of change with time "through the slope of the graph."

> "Roughly 20% of the students drew curved graphs as shown in Fig. 2. (The ﬂat line represents total energy.) A common justiﬁcation was “[t]he ball starts with a po- tential energy of mgh."
