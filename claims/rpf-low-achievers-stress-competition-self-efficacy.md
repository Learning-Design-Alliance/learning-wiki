# Low-achieving children receiving improvement-based relative performance feedback report higher perceived stress and higher liking of competition, with suggestive evidence for higher self-efficacy

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` Low-achieving children in the treatment group perceived more stress (0.36 SD, p = .006) than low-achievers in the control group, but did not report more somatic problems. [→ Hermes et al. 2021](#hermes-et-al-2021)
`q3 i1` Low-achievers in the treatment group agreed much more strongly that they liked to compete (heterogeneous effect 0.52 SD, p = .004; treatment-control difference 0.27 SD, p = .085). [→ Hermes et al. 2021](#hermes-et-al-2021)
`q3 i?` Self-efficacy effects for low-achievers were substantial but only close to statistical significance (0.45 SD, p = .109; difference 0.34 SD, p = .154). [→ Hermes et al. 2021](#hermes-et-al-2021)

## Evidence

### Hermes et al. 2021

Henning Hermes, Martin Huschens, Franz Rothlauf, Daniel Schunk. (2021). Motivating low-achievers—Relative performance feedback in primary schools. Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2021.04.004

`q3 · i2` · `causal · r2`

Child self-report questionnaires administered pre- and post-treatment; children were blind to treatment condition. The printed perceived-stress effect size for low-achievers was an effect size of 0.36 SD (p = .006), with no increase in reported somatic problems.

> "children in the treatment group perceived more stress (0.36 SD, p = .006); at the same time, they did not report more somatic problems."
