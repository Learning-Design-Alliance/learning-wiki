The contrast being perceived is the same; the task is not. A learner whose first language already encodes the distinction hears it, and one whose first language does not may not hear it at any level.

Evidence¶

Pešková 2026¶

Pešková, A. (2026). Identification of consonant length in L2 Italian: the role of cross-linguistic influence. Frontiers in Language Sciences, 5. doi:10.3389/flang.2026.1911194

q2 · cross-sectional group comparison with a native-speaker control group · i? · accuracy percentages by group (89.8% vs 64% on the same contrast); no standardized effect size printed · n=104 · associational · r?

104 learners of Italian identified singleton against geminate consonants in trisyllabic pseudowords (sàpolo vs sàppolo), grouped by first language. Accuracy tracked whether the learner's L1 encodes phonological quantity in consonants or vowels, L1 background had the stronger and more systematic effect; accuracy tended to rise with proficiency, but that effect varied across L1 groups and was not significant. The experiment was an identification task (with orthographic support); compared with a previous discrimination study with the same participants, identification was more accurate in every group except the Finnish learners.

Discussion¶

A difficulty ordering derived from proficiency alone will be wrong for a mixed-L1 cohort. The same item is a different task for a Finnish learner and a German one, and adaptive sequencing that models only level cannot see the difference. This is the perception-side counterpart of a more general point: Prior Knowledge Determines New Learning [+S], where the relevant prior knowledge is a phonological inventory rather than domain content.

It separates two failures that look identical in the data. A learner who does not know a contrast and a learner who cannot hear it both score low. The second will show as weak performance in listening and normal performance in writing, and treating them as the same evidence understates what the learner knows.

Scope. One contrast in one language, and a perception task rather than production. Whether the effect size holds for grammatical rather than phonological contrasts is untested here — though Italian grammatical gender stays incomplete for L2 and heritage speakers alike [~M] finds a related conditionality in morphology, where a typologically closer L1 helps only in some configurations.
