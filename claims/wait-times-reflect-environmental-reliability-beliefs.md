# Children's wait-times on sustained delay-of-gratification tasks reflect beliefs about environmental reliability in addition to self-control capacity

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · `q2`–`q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Because manipulating experimenter reliability changed wait-times, wait-times cannot be attributed solely to self-control capacity differences. [→ Kidd 2013](#kidd-2013)
`q2 i?` The authors conclude that an unreliable worldview, in addition to self-control, may be causally related to later life outcomes. [→ Kidd 2013 (2)](#kidd-2013-2)

## Evidence

### Kidd 2013

Kidd, C., Palmeri, H., & Aslin, R. N. (2013). Rational snacking: Young children's decision-making on the marshmallow task is moderated by beliefs about environmental reliability. Cognition, 126(1), 109-114. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2012.08.004

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Authors' interpretation of their randomized experiment: the condition effect (reliable vs unreliable) is inconsistent with self-control deficiency as the sole cause, since such deficiencies should appear in both conditions. This is the authors' reasoning, not an additional measurement.

> "If self-control capacity differences were the primary causal mechanism implicated in children's wait-times, then information about the reliability of the environment should not have affected them. If deficiencies in self-control caused children to eat treats early, then one would expect such deficiencies to be present in the reliable condition as well as in the unreliable condition."

### Kidd 2013 (2)

Kidd, C., Palmeri, H., & Aslin, R. N. (2013). Rational snacking: Young children's decision-making on the marshmallow task is moderated by beliefs about environmental reliability. Cognition, 126(1), 109-114. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2012.08.004

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

The authors' own hedge in the Discussion: their data do not show self-control is irrelevant, only that attributing most variance and the longitudinal correlations to self-control alone is premature. No effect size is printed for this interpretive claim.

> "our data do not demonstrate that self-control is irrelevant in explaining the variance in children's wait-times on the original marshmallow task studies. They do, however, strongly indicate that it is premature to conclude that most of the observed variance—and the longitudinal correlation between wait-times and later life outcomes—is due to differences in individuals' self-control capacities."
