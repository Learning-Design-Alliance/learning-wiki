# Peer Assessment Structured Criteria Improve Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i0` negligible · n=54 studies, k=141 effect sizes

Peer assessment produces more reliable judgments and better learning outcomes when reviewers are given explicit, structured criteria (rubrics, checklists, or sentence stems) rather than asked to evaluate open-endedly. The mechanism is attentional: criteria direct reviewers to specific dimensions of quality rather than leaving them to rely on general impressions.

## Subclaims

`q4 i0` A meta-analysis of 54 studies (k=141) of peer assessment and academic performance found no significant difference between studies giving reviewers a specific script/rubric (g=0.29) and freeform, unstructured peer assessment (g=0.42) — the moderator effect for structure was small and non-significant (b=-0.13, 95% CI -0.51 to 0.25, p=.455), so this well-powered synthesis does not support that structured criteria improve outcomes over open-ended review, though the freeform subgroup was small (13 studies, k=51). ## Evidence

### Double et al. 2020

Double, K. S., McGrane, J. A., & Hopfenbeck, T. N. (2020). The impact of peer assessment on academic performance: A meta-analysis of control group studies. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(2), 481–509. [doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3)

`q4 · meta-analysis of experimental/quasi-experimental control-group studies` · `i0 · moderator effect below d=0.2, non-significant` · `n=54 studies, k=141 effect sizes`

Meta-analysis of 54 experimental/quasi-experimental studies (k=141 effect sizes) on peer assessment's effect on academic performance in primary, secondary and tertiary students. Overall peer assessment showed a small-to-medium benefit versus no assessment (g=0.31, p<.001). Among several moderators tested via meta-regression, one directly addresses this claim: whether a specific script or rubric was provided to reviewers versus freeform, unstructured assessment. Structured-criteria studies showed g=0.29 (p<.001) and freeform studies showed a numerically larger g=0.42 (p=.030), but the difference between them was small and not statistically significant (b=-0.13, 95% CI -0.51 to 0.25, p=.455), and the authors note the freeform subgroup was small (13 of 54 studies, k=51 of 141). This is the only outcome-level moderator test of criteria structure in the corpus and it does not support the claim as stated — see Discussion.

**Note for whoever merges this:** this evidence qualifies rather than confirms the page's claim — the meta-analysis's own moderator test runs numerically opposite to "structured beats unstructured" (freeform g=0.42 > structured g=0.29), though non-significant and based on a small freeform subgroup (13 of 54 studies). It also tests structure's effect on the *reviewee's* academic performance from receiving feedback, not on reviewer learning or inter-rater reliability — a claim about rubric effects on scoring reliability is a different construct and is not addressed here.

## Discussion

**The recorded evidence runs against the title.** In the one meta-analysis recorded above, studies using structured criteria (rubrics or scripts) showed g = 0.29 and freeform studies g = 0.42; the difference was not significant, and the freeform subgroup is small (13 of 54 studies). So structured criteria are not shown to improve the learning effect of peer assessment. Their benefit for the reliability of peer judgments is a separate question this evidence does not test. Renaming is the maintainer's call.

**Why structure matters.** Unstructured peer review invites halo effects, leniency bias, and attention to surface features (length, neatness) rather than quality of reasoning. Structured criteria direct reviewers' attention to specific dimensions of quality, which benefits reviewers as much as reviewees: applying criteria to a peer's work is itself an act of evaluation and self-monitoring that can transfer to revising one's own work. This aligns with the broader logic of [Assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) — assessment tasks that make quality criteria visible support learning, not just grading.

**Boundary conditions.** Structured criteria can backfire. A checklist-only focus can produce mechanical, criterion-ticking reviews with little substantive feedback — see [Checklist evaluation is ineffective online](checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md). Criteria also cannot compensate for reviewers who lack the domain knowledge to recognize quality; novices may apply criteria literally without understanding the underlying standard. Training reviewers on worked examples of graded work before assessing, and combining criteria with open comment fields, are common mitigations.

**Moderators to watch.** The value of structure likely depends on (a) reviewer expertise — highly detailed rubrics may lose value or even hinder learners with more developed evaluative skill, consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md); (b) stakes — criteria may improve reliability most when reviews carry grading consequences; and (c) reviewer training — criteria without calibration practice may still yield inconsistent judgments.

**Open questions.** How much structure is optimal — detailed rubrics versus a few holistic criteria — and how criteria interact with anonymity, grading stakes, and reviewer expertise remain under-specified.
