# Irrelevant changing-state sounds impair children's serial recall of visually presented items, with developmental evidence pointing to two mechanisms

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 2 review `r2`–`r3` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Irrelevant sounds with a changing-state characteristic (distinct varying auditory objects) impair serial recall, whereas steady-state sounds such as continuous broadband noise have minor or no effect. [→ Klatte M 2013](#klatte-m-2013)
`q2 i?` The magnitude of the irrelevant sound effect increases with decreasing age in some studies (39% performance drop in second-graders vs 11% in adults), but other studies found no age difference in the effect of background speech. [→ Klatte M 2013 (2)](#klatte-m-2013-2)

## Evidence

### Klatte M 2013

Klatte M, Bergström K and Lachmann T (2013) Does noise affect learning? A short review on noise effects on cognitive performance in children. Front. Psychol. 4:578. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00578

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review's synthesis of the irrelevant sound effect literature: changing-state sounds evoke the ISE while "steady state sounds... have a minor or no effect"; the ISE occurs even at low intensity and with meaningless speech.

> "irrelevant sounds consisting of different syllables or tones evoke an ISE, whereas steady state sounds, e.g., continuous broadband noise or repetitions of single syllables or tones, have a minor or no effect."

### Klatte M 2013 (2)

Klatte M, Bergström K and Lachmann T (2013) Does noise affect learning? A short review on noise effects on cognitive performance in children. Front. Psychol. 4:578. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00578

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review reports Elliott's developmental study found a dramatic ISE increase with decreasing age, interpreted as evidence for attentional control's role; Klatte et al. (2010b) found background-speech effects that "did not differ in magnitude between ﬁrst-grade children and adults".

> "Performance drop relative to quiet was 39% in the second-graders, as opposed to 11% in the adults."
