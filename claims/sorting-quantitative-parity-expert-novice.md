# Expert and novice sorting shows quantitative parity in number of categories, but experts take longer on the first sorting trial

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Experts and novices produced similar numbers of sorting categories and achieved stable sorts, with no gross quantitative differences. [→ Chi et al. 1981](#chi-et-al-1981)
`q3 i?` Experts took longer than novices on the first sorting trial (18 minutes versus 12 minutes on average), though both groups sorted quickly on the second trial. [→ Chi et al. 1981 (2)](#chi-et-al-1981-2)

## Evidence

### Chi et al. 1981

Michelene Chi, Paul Feltovich, Robert Glaser. (1981). Categorization and Representation of Physics Problems by Experts and Novices. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Quantitative results of the Study One sorting task (8 experts, 8 novices, 24 problems): category counts "8.4 for the experts and 8.6 for the novices" and four largest categories covering 80 versus 74 percent of problems; both groups re-sorted consistently.

> "There were no differences in the number of categories produced by each group (8.4 for the experts and 8.6 for the novices), and the four largest categories produced by each subject captured the majority of the problems (80 percent for the experts and 74 percent for the novices)."

### Chi et al. 1981 (2)

Michelene Chi, Paul Feltovich, Robert Glaser. (1981). Categorization and Representation of Physics Problems by Experts and Novices. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Timing data from the first Study One sorting trial: experts averaged "18 minutes or 45 seconds per problem" versus novices' 12 minutes (30 seconds per problem); both groups were fast on the second trial (4.6 vs 5.5 minutes).

> "In fact, experts took locger (18 minutes or 45 seconds per problem, on the average) to sort the problems in the f m t trial than novices (12 minutes or 30 seconds)."
