## Evidence

### S. B. McKagan 2008

S. B. McKagan, K. K. Perkins, and C. E. Wieman. (2008). A Deeper Look at Student Learning of Quantum Mechanics: the Case of Tunneling. https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.3194 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0802.3194](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0802.3194)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Observations, online student questions, and tutorial responses in the transformed courses: many students drew a well instead of a barrier for a wire with an air gap, explaining the air gap was a "hole". The article reports no effect size for these qualitative findings.

> "In the transformed courses, we observed that students still struggled with the basic meaning of potential energy graphs, but as we reﬁned our curriculum, their questions about these graphs became more sophisticated, illustrating a struggle to relate the graphs to physical reality in a deep and meaningful way."

### S. B. McKagan 2008 (2)

S. B. McKagan, K. K. Perkins, and C. E. Wieman. (2008). A Deeper Look at Student Learning of Quantum Mechanics: the Case of Tunneling. https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.3194 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0802.3194](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0802.3194)

`q1 · i?` · `causal · r1`

After adding instruction (Figure 8) on building a square well by superimposing Coulomb potentials of atoms in a wire, "anecdotal observations indicated that fewer students drew a well instead of a barrier". On an end-of-course homework question, nearly all students gave clear and correct explanations relating the graph to a real physical context.

> "After this instruction, anecdotal observations indicated that fewer students drew a well instead of a barrier in the Tutorial."
