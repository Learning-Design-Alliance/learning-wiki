# Hands-on learning improves achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Physically manipulating materials or enacting concepts during instruction can improve achievement relative to passive reception, but the effect depends heavily on how the activity is structured and what learners already know.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Plausible routes from hands-on activity to achievement include grounding abstract concepts in perceptual-motor experience, generating [cognitive disequilibrium](cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) that motivates conceptual change, and increasing engagement — see [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. However, physical activity alone does not guarantee cognitive engagement; the benefit depends on the activity prompting learners to process the target concept, not merely manipulate materials. This is consistent with the broader finding that [active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) only when activities require meaningful cognitive processing [~M].

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Hands-on approaches impose extraneous load when materials, goals, or procedures are poorly scaffolded [-M] — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) and [Cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). Guidance matters: minimally guided discovery versions of hands-on learning underperform guided versions for novices [-S], consistent with [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md). The benefit also plausibly reverses for advanced learners, mirroring the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Hands-on formats are also harder to scale and assess than [worked-example](../elements/demonstration.md)-based or [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) formats [-W].

**Enactment vs. sense-making.** A recurring confound in this literature is that "hands-on" conditions typically bundle physical manipulation with prediction, discussion, and reflection. Until studies isolate enactment from guided sense-making, it remains unclear whether the physical component contributes anything beyond the accompanying cognitive activity [~W]. Designers should therefore treat the manipulative as a vehicle for structured sense-making rather than as the active ingredient itself.

**Design implications.** Where hands-on formats are used, pair manipulation with prediction prompts, structured discussion, and reflection so that the physical experience is explicitly connected to the target concept. Scaffold procedures and materials to limit extraneous load, and fade that scaffolding as learners gain expertise — the same fading logic applied in [worked example](../elements/demonstration.md) sequences.

**Open questions.** Whether the advantage stems from the physical manipulation itself or from the accompanying discussion, prediction, and reflection remains unresolved. Studies isolating enactment from guided sense-making are needed before the claim can be stated with confidence.
