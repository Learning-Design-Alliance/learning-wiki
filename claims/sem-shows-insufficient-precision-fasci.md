# Standard errors of measurement show precision insufficient for most decisions: SEM Δ for SCI is about 0.35 (95% CI ±0.70), making criterion-referenced SCI decisions very difficult to defend

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` SEM Δ for SCI with five items and three raters is about 0.35, giving a 95% confidence interval of roughly +/- 0.70 against an observed score range of 0 to 1.67. [→ Talbot 2017](#talbot-2017)
`q2 i?` SEM δ is about 0.23 for both dimensions (95% CI +/- 0.46), so top-quartile respondents overlap with middle and bottom quartiles, precluding most relative claims. [→ Talbot 2017 (2)](#talbot-2017-2)

## Evidence

### Talbot 2017

Talbot, R. M., III. (2017). A Multi-Faceted Approach to Scrutinizing the Reliability of a Measure of STEM Teacher Strategic Knowledge. University of Colorado Denver. Originally published 2014. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1702.06592](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1702.06592)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

SEM analysis plotted as a function of number of items (Figures 11–13) for 60 respondents. The article concludes that with a CI spanning 0.30 to 1.70 around a mean SCI score of 1, "one could not make such a claim" that the respondent is emblematic of that construct level.

> "the SEM Δ for five items and three raters on the SCI dimension is about 0.35. This means that a 95% confidence interval around a respondent's SCI score would be roughly +/- 0.70. This is a very large range with respect to the observed range of SCI scores, which is from 0 to 1.67"

### Talbot 2017 (2)

Talbot, R. M., III. (2017). A Multi-Faceted Approach to Scrutinizing the Reliability of a Measure of STEM Teacher Strategic Knowledge. University of Colorado Denver. Originally published 2014. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1702.06592](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1702.06592)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Plot of all 60 mean SCI scores with 95% confidence intervals based on SEM δ = 0.23 (Figure 14; Figure 15 for FA). The article reports one can distinguish only about two groups on SCI, and even with ten items not three.

> "Many respondents in the top quartile of SCI scores (which ranges from 1.11 to 1.67) overlap with many respondents in the middle and bottom quartiles. In other words, there is not enough precision in measurement to distinguish each of these quartiles"
