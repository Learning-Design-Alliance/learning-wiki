Prior Knowledge Needed For Accurate Self Assessment¶

Evidence¶

Zell & Krizan 2014¶

Zell, E., & Krizan, Z. (2014). Do people have insight into their abilities? A metasynthesis. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 9(2), 111–125. doi:10.1177/1745691613518075

q4 · meta-analysis of 22 meta-analyses · i2 · medium, mean r=.29 (SD=.11), individual meta-analytic effects .09–.63 · n=22 meta-analyses · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-synthesis pooling 22 published meta-analyses that each compared people's self-evaluations of ability (academic ability, intelligence, language competence, medical skills, sports ability, vocational skills) against objective performance measures (test scores, grades, supervisor ratings). The overall correlation between self-evaluation and performance was moderate rather than strong. Accuracy was significantly higher when the self-evaluation was specific to a defined domain rather than broad, and when the performance task was objective, familiar, or low in complexity The synthesis reports these moderators of a correlation; it does not measure prior knowledge or show that knowledge or experience is necessary for, or causes, accurate self-assessment. Reading familiarity as a stand-in for domain knowledge is this page's interpretation, not the authors' finding.

Hacker et al. 2000¶

Hacker, D. J., Bol, L., Horgan, D. D., & Rakow, E. A. (2000). Test prediction and performance in a classroom context. Journal of Educational Psychology, 92(1), 160–170. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.160

q2 · classroom observational study, undergraduates (n=96), repeated over a semester · i? · no effect size reported in what could be read · n=96 · associational · r?

Undergraduates (N=96) in a semester-length course predicted their score before each of several exams and postdicted it immediately after, across the term. Performance-quartile analysis showed that the highest-performing students' predictions and postdictions were accurate and grew more accurate across successive exams; middling performers predicted only moderately well but postdicted well; the lowest-performing students showed gross overconfidence in both prediction and postdiction, i.e. their self-assessments were least accurate exactly where their command of the tested material was weakest. Only the author-published abstract/description was available to this session, not the full results tables, so no quantitative effect size could be verified.

Discussion¶

The core mechanism is that self-assessment requires monitoring one's own comprehension, and monitoring depends on the same knowledge structures that support performance. Novices lack the schemas needed to distinguish what they know from what they merely feel familiar — the fluency of reading or hearing material is easily mistaken for understanding [-M]. This is closely tied to the expertise reversal effect: the cues learners use to judge their competence change in meaning as expertise grows [~M].

A practical consequence is that self-assessment tasks given before learners have adequate domain experience may produce confident errors rather than useful reflection [-M]. Designers should pair self-assessment with external reference points — worked examples, rubrics, or exemplar comparisons — so that judgments are anchored to criteria rather than to subjective fluency [+M]. Calibration typically improves with practice and feedback [+M], so early self-ratings should be treated as formative signals, not accurate measures. This places the claim within self-regulated learning, where accurate monitoring is the hinge on which effective study decisions depend.

Boundary conditions to watch: self-assessment is least reliable when learners are judging material they have only passively encountered [-M], when criteria are vague [-M], and when the domain is one where misconceptions feel coherent (learners can fluently reconstruct an incorrect model and rate it highly) [-M]. Conversely, self-assessment becomes more trustworthy as schemas consolidate — meaning it is best positioned late in a learning sequence rather than early [~M], and best used alongside assessment for learning rather than in place of it [+M].

Open questions: how much prior knowledge is "enough" for calibration in a given domain, and whether self-assessment accuracy transfers across domains or must be trained domain by domain. Studies establishing these boundary conditions still need to be added to this page.
