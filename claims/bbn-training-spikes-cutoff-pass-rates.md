# Post-training, more students pass the SPIKES cut-off in the training group than in the control group, but the mBAS cut-off difference is not statistically significant

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` At post-test, 73.0% of the training group passed the SPIKES cut-off versus 45.2% of controls (p = 0.02); the mBAS pass-rate difference (62.2% vs 35.5%) was not statistically significant (p = 0.07). [→ Servotte J-C 2019](#servotte-j-c-2019)

## Evidence

### Servotte J-C 2019

Servotte J-C, Bragard I, Szyld D, Van Ngoc P, Scholtes B, Van Cauwenberge I, Donneau A-F, Dardenne N, Goosse M, Pilote B, Guillaume M, Ghuysen A. (2019). Efficacy of a Short Role-Play Training on Breaking Bad News in the Emergency Department. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine. [doi:10.5811//westjem.2019.8.43441](https://doi.org/10.5811//westjem.2019.8.43441)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

McNemar and χ2 comparisons of cut-off pass rates (SPIKES passing ≥ 11; mBAS passing ≤ 14) in the randomized trial. Within the training group, pre-to-post improvement was significant for both tools (p<0.001); in controls it was not.

> "At post-test, we found a statistically significant (p = 0.02) difference for the SPIKES cut-off score: the TG had a higher number of participants passing the cut-off score (27 students passed; 73.0%) than the CG (14 passed; 45.2%)."
