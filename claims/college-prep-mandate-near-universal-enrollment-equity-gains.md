# A universal college-prep mandate shifted nearly all ninth graders into Algebra I and English I and largely closed enrollment gaps by race, ability, and special education status

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · associational `r?` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` By 2000 virtually all Chicago ninth graders were enrolled in English I and Algebra I, with the largest enrollment shifts among low-ability and special education students. [→ Elaine Allensworth 2008](#elaine-allensworth-2008)
`q2 i?` Enrollment differences by race largely disappeared and gaps by incoming ability and special education eligibility declined dramatically after the policy. [→ Elaine Allensworth 2008 (2)](#elaine-allensworth-2008-2)

## Evidence

### Elaine Allensworth 2008

Elaine Allensworth, Takako Nomi, Nicholas Montgomery, & Valerie E. Lee. (2008). College Preparatory Curriculum for All: Consequences of Ninth-Grade Course Taking in Algebra and English on Academic Outcomes in Chicago. Consortium on Chicago School Research at the University of Chicago. https://ccsr.uchicago.edu

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r?`

Descriptive results from an interrupted time-series cohort design covering all first-time ninth graders in 59 Chicago high schools, 1994-2004. Post-policy enrollment reached near-universal levels: "very close to 100% of students not eligible for special education services were enrolled in Algebra I, regardless of ability".

> "During the post-policy years, very close to 100% of students not eligible for special education services were enrolled in Algebra I, regardless of ability; 96 percent enrolled in English I."
