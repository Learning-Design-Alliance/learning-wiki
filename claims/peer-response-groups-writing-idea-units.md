# Peer response groups increase the number of idea units in English learners' writing but show no significant effect on composition quality or sentences written (Prater & Bermudez, 1993)

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · causal `r?` · `q3` · `i2` medium

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` The peer response group condition produced statistically significantly more idea units (effect size 0.73) than individual writing. [→ What Works Clearinghouse 2007](#what-works-clearinghouse-2007)
`q3 i?` No statistically significant differences favored the peer response group on composition quality (0.15), total sentences (0.33), or total words (0.62 after WWC multiple-comparison correction). [→ What Works Clearinghouse 2007 (2)](#what-works-clearinghouse-2007-2)

## Evidence

### What Works Clearinghouse 2007

What Works Clearinghouse, U.S. Department of Education. (2007). WWC Intervention Report: Peer Tutoring and Response Groups. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc

`q3 · i2` · `quant-synthesis · r1`

Randomized controlled trial of 46 fourth-grade English language learners in two Houston-area elementary schools, assigned to mixed-ability peer response groups or individual writing for four weeks. Appendix A3 reports an idea-units effect size of 0.73, statistically significant, improvement index +27; the impact was at least 0.25, substantively important by WWC standards.

> "Prater and Bermudez (1993) reported statistically significant differences favoring the peer response group on the number of words written and number of ideas presented in student compositions but no statistically significant differences in overall composition quality and number of sentences written."

### What Works Clearinghouse 2007 (2)

What Works Clearinghouse, U.S. Department of Education. (2007). WWC Intervention Report: Peer Tutoring and Response Groups. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r?`

In the same RCT, Appendix A3 reports effect sizes of 0.15 for composition quality, 0.33 for total sentences, and 0.62 for total words written, none statistically significant after WWC corrections. Equivalence was not tested.

> "Corrections for multiple comparisons did change Prater & Bermudez (1993) outcomes for total words written from statistically significant to non-significant."
