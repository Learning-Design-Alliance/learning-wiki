## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Participants in life science courses selected higher proportions of women than physical science participants for study partners (60.4% vs. 31.9%), help (55.6% vs. 31.1%), knowledgeable (56.3% vs. 27.2%), and best students (54.7% vs. 27.3%); in physical sciences both genders disproportionately selected men. ## Evidence

### Bloodhart 2020

Bloodhart, B., Balgopal, M. M., Casper, A. M. A., Sample McMeeking, L. B., & Fischer, E. V. (2020). Outperforming yet undervalued: Undergraduate women in STEM. PLoS ONE 15(6): e0234685. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0234685

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Course-type main effects from the same four multilevel peer-nomination models of survey participants, with classroom nesting and controls for the proportion of women enrolled. The same section reports parallel contrasts for knowledgeable (F(1, 553) = 61.88, p < .001) and best-student (F(1, 1.59) = 45.83, p < .05) nominations. No standardized effect sizes are printed.

> "Four significant main effects of course type revealed that participants in life science courses (M = 60.4%) were more likely than participants in physical science courses (M = 31.9%) to select a higher proportion of women students with whom they study, F (1, 541) = 71.00, p < .001, and participants in life science courses (M = 55.6%) were also more likely than participants in physical science courses (M = 31.1%) to select a higher proportion of women students from whom they sought help, F (1, 3.88) = 12.85, p < .05."
