## Evidence

### Cacey S. Stevens 2015

Cacey S. Stevens, Michael Marder, and Sidney R. Nagel. (2015). Patterns in Illinois Educational School Data. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Analysis of average Math ACT scores for each Illinois high school as a function of poverty concentration (Fig. 5a). The authors report that "ACT Math per- formance declines with increasing concentration of low- income students", noting most schools below 20% poverty average above 20 while few schools above 80% poverty do.

> "As with other state standardized exams, ACT Math per- formance declines with increasing concentration of low- income students."

### Cacey S. Stevens 2015 (2)

Cacey S. Stevens, Michael Marder, and Sidney R. Nagel. (2015). Patterns in Illinois Educational School Data. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Comparison of Chicago charter and neighborhood schools' average Math ACT scores by poverty level (Fig. 5b). The authors note charter scores "cluster around similar scores to neighbor- hood schools at the same poverty level", with Noble Street at 21.2 the exception; campus-level scores ranged from 19.2 to 23.7.

> "However, our present study indicates that scores of most charter schools cluster around similar scores to neighbor- hood schools at the same poverty level, as was also noted in a recent report [19]. There is an exception: the No- ble Street Charter System with an average score of 21.2."
