# Female residents use the don't know option more than male residents, but the net effect on formula scores shows no gender advantage or disadvantage

## Subclaims
`q3 i0` Gender significantly contributed to DKO use, with higher use for female participants, though the effect is small (β = 0.08). [→ Ravesloot C. J. 2015](#ravesloot-c-j-2015)

## Evidence

### Ravesloot C. J. 2015

Ravesloot C. J., Van der Schaaf M. F., Muijtjens A. M. M., Haaring C., Kruitwagen C. L. J. J., Beek F. J. A., Bakker J., Van Schaik J. P. J., Cate Th. J. Ten. (2015). The don’t know option in progress testing. Advances in Health Sciences Education. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10459-015-9604-2

`q3 · i0` · `causal · r2`

Simultaneous multiple regression of DKO score on gender, controlling for group, training length and NR score, in the 337-resident study. The article reports "a higher use for female versus male participants, although the effect is small (β = 0.08)" (p = 0.045, b = 3.05).

> "Gender was found to significantly contribute to the variance of DKO option use (t test, p = 0.045, b = 3.05), showing a higher use for female versus male participants, although the effect is small (β = 0.08)"

### Ravesloot C. J. 2015 (2)

Ravesloot C. J., Van der Schaaf M. F., Muijtjens A. M. M., Haaring C., Kruitwagen C. L. J. J., Beek F. J. A., Bakker J., Van Schaik J. P. J., Cate Th. J. Ten. (2015). The don’t know option in progress testing. Advances in Health Sciences Education. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10459-015-9604-2

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

From the sequential regression of FS score in the same study: the article concludes "there is no significant effect of gender" on FS scores, interpreting that women's better use of the don't know option is compensated by their higher tendency to use it. Equivalence was not formally tested.

> "The answer can be found in the Model 2 analysis in Table 2, and the outcome is: there is no significant effect of gender"
