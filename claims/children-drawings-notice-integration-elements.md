## Evidence

### Tozadore 2025

Tozadore, D., Ertug, N., Chaker, Y., & Abderrahim, M. (2025). RoboBuddy in the Classroom: Exploring LLM-Powered Social Robots for Storytelling in Learning and Integration Activities. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16706 [doi:10.48550/arxiv.2508.16706](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2508.16706)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

In the final feedback workshop, students could draw liked or disliked aspects. The article prints the participation rates: 7 of 13 (53.8%) Year 2-1 students versus 4 of 14 (28.5%) Year 2-2 students; these are descriptive percentages with no test.

> "Seven out of 13 (53.8%) students from Year 2-1 provided a drawing, against only 4 out of 14 (28.5%) from Year 2-2."

### Tozadore 2025 (2)

Tozadore, D., Ertug, N., Chaker, Y., & Abderrahim, M. (2025). RoboBuddy in the Classroom: Exploring LLM-Powered Social Robots for Storytelling in Learning and Integration Activities. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16706. [doi:10.48550/arxiv.2508.16706](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2508.16706)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Qualitative analysis of the drawings (Fig. 3) in the exploratory classroom study. The authors interpret drawings of students communicating in each other's languages and of the sad robot as recognition of the embedded integration and emotional elements; this is the authors' interpretation, not a tested result.

> "the robot was not the most notable element of the interaction. After “connecting” the children, the robot disappeared from the spot light, enabling the students to feel more excited and comfortable communicating with their peers, even attempting to speak each other’s languages"
