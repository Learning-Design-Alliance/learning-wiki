## Evidence

### Cárdenas 2022

Cárdenas, D., Lattimore, F., Steinberg, D., & Reynolds, K. J. (2022). Youth well-being predicts later academic success. Scientific Reports. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-05780-0

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Numerical results shown in Figs. 1 and 2 as partial dependence plots from kernelized Bayesian ridge and gradient boosted tree models; the article concludes there are "no critical levels of depression and well-being more broadly where academic performance will suffer or benefit most".

> "Bootstrap average effect estimates in these plots (red dashed line) are relatively linear for both the kernel and tree models, which suggests that the linear-in-treatment models may not suffer from model mis-specification bias."

### Cárdenas 2022 (2)

Cárdenas, D., Lattimore, F., Steinberg, D., & Reynolds, K. J. (2022). Youth well-being predicts later academic success. Scientific Reports. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-05780-0

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Cross-validation RMSE comparison across the model set; the article interprets this as evidence that nonlinear associations exist between controls and well-being and/or NAPLAN scores, so completely linear models may be misspecified.

> "the completely nonlinear and nonlinear-in-controls models slightly, but consistently, outperform the linear Bayesian ridge model—which is completely linear—in terms of cross validation root mean squared error (RMSE)."
