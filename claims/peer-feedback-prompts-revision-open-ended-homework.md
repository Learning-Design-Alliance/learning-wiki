## Evidence

### Daniel L. Reinholz 2016

Daniel L. Reinholz, Dimitri R. Dounas-Frazer. (2016). Using Peer Feedback to Promote Reflection on Open-Ended Problems. [doi:10.1119/1.4961181](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.4961181)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Case illustration from the physics course: Alex's initial CV-problem solution did not explain how he computed a 17 m head start; his peer reviewer asked him to show his work, and his revised solution elaborated the reasoning, "given feedback from a peer reviewer that his reasoning was not clear".

> "This is an example of a PAR revision cycle: given feedback from a peer reviewer that his reasoning was not clear, Alex provided further elaboration in his solution."

### Daniel L. Reinholz 2016 (2)

Daniel L. Reinholz, Dimitri R. Dounas-Frazer. (2016). Using Peer Feedback to Promote Reflection on Open-Ended Problems. [doi:10.1119/1.4961181](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.4961181)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r2`

Case illustration from the same course: Bobbie's initial UA-problem velocity and acceleration graphs were qualitatively inconsistent with her position graph; after her peer reviewer marked the graphs and commented on the inconsistency, she revised them, "generating position, velocity, and acceleration graphs that were consistent with one another".

> "In response to this feedback, Bobbie revised her work accordingly, generating position, velocity, and acceleration graphs that were consistent with one another (Fig. 2)."
