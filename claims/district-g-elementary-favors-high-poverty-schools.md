# District G shows the opposite pattern: highest-poverty elementary schools were most likely to have highest-performing teachers

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r2` · `q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In District G elementary schools, students in the highest-poverty quintile were the most likely to have a highest-performing teacher, a statistically significant divergence from equal access in the direction favoring high-poverty schools. [→ Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max 2011](#steven-glazerman-and-jeffrey-max-2011)

## Evidence

### Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max 2011

Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max. (2011). Do Low-Income Students Have Equal Access to the Highest-Performing Teachers? NCEE Evaluation Brief, NCEE 2011-4016. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20114016/pdf/20114016_app.pdf

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Figure 3 analysis for District G elementary teachers (N=407 teachers as printed); the chi-square test of no relationship between quintile and percent highest-performing was rejected at the 0.05 level.

> "Figure 3 shows the most extreme example of a relationship that goes in the opposite direction, where the students most likely to have a highest -performing teacher were those in the highest poverty schools."
