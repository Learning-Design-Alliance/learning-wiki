Thematic analysis of optional free-response survey answers (87% of participants answered at least one) found ai plan participants "often request ideas and then not use or incorporate them", citing redundancy or preference for their own ideas.

"In some cases this was because the AI-generated ideas were redundant with participants’ own ideas, e.g., "the AI did come up with outline ideas, but they matched/confirmed what I had already thought"."

Related Claims¶

  • AI support shifts perceived attribution of ideas and text toward the AI, most strongly for AI-generated drafts, and an AI draft based on the writer's own outline contributed more ideas than expected (27%). — related
  • Any AI writing support decreases feelings of ownership compared to no AI support, with ownership following a gradient by stage (highest with no AI, then planning, revision, and AI-generated draft lowest). — related
  • AI support during drafting lowers ownership substantially more than support during planning or revision, and planning support preserves more ownership than revision support. — related
  • Experimental-group students framed AI as a flexible writing scaffold supporting their own composing rather than a substitute for authorship — related
  • Synchronous AI suggestions shape subsequent writing, offering new ideas but risking influence over the writer's voice and argument — related
  • Students reported text supports structured, iterative planning while some valued voice for getting initial ideas out — related
  • Evaluators attribute substantially less ownership to writers who used GenAI ideas, but impose only a marginal profit-sharing penalty — related
  • Synchronous collaboration with an artificial agent amplifies contextual misalignment because the agent only sees ongoing output, not the writer's planning process — related
  • AI ghostwriter effect: users do not claim ownership of AI-generated texts yet fail to publicly acknowledge the AI's contribution — related
