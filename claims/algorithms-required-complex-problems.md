## Evidence

### Hartman 2021

Hartman, J. R.; Nelson, E. A. (2021). A Paradigm Shift: The Implications of Working Memory Limits for Physics and Chemistry Instruction. Originally published 2020. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2102.00454](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2102.00454)

`q2 · i?` · `review · r1`

The review reports, citing cognitive studies, that reasoning without algorithms fails for assigned physical science problems because non-recallable relationships overload working memory. No effect size is printed.

> "cognitive studies show that for the not‐simple but well‐structured problems that are the focus of assignments in pre‐graduate‐school physical science courses, student reasoning without applying algorithms is not likely to reliably lead to correct answers."

### Hartman 2021 (2)

Hartman, J. R.; Nelson, E. A. (2021). A Paradigm Shift: The Implications of Working Memory Limits for Physics and Chemistry Instruction. Originally published 2020. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2102.00454](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2102.00454)

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review quotes Willingham's argument that the assumption students can be taught to think like experts is flawed, and adds Kirschner and Hendrick's advice that beginners are not little experts; constructing an expert framework generally requires about 10 years of problem solving.

> "[A] flawed assumption underlies the logic, namely that students are capable of doing what scientists or historians do…. No one thinks like a scientist … without a great deal of training…."
