# An instructional video as a stand-alone first exposure yields higher practical skill scores than unsupervised self-study in last-year medical students

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Last-year medical students who watched a ten-minute instructional video scored significantly higher on a humeral intraosseous access test than students who did ten minutes of unsupervised self-study (mean 14.8 vs. 7.7, p < 0.001). [→ Ott Thomas 2024](#ott-thomas-2024)

## Evidence

### Ott Thomas 2024

Ott Thomas, Demare Tim, Möhrke Julia, Silber Saskia, Schwab Johannes, Reuter Lukas, Westhphal Ruben, Schmidtmann Irene, Dietz Sven-Oliver, Pirlich Nina, Ziebart Alexander, Engelhard Kristin. (2024). Does an instructional video as a stand-alone tool promote the acquisition of practical clinical skills? A randomised simulation research trial of skills acquisition and short-term retention. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-024-05714-6

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Randomised controlled two-armed simulation trial (Study 1, primary endpoint) with 78 last-year medical students randomised to video-first or self-study-first sequences; blinded videotape scoring on a 0–20 checklist. The video group "scored significantly greater than the group that did self-study" (14.8 vs. 7.7, p < 0.001).

> "the group that watched the instructional video at that point in time scored significantly greater than the group that did self-study (Self-Vid at T1) (at T1: Vid-Self: mean 14.8, SD 3.5 vs. Self-Vid: mean 7.7, SD 2.6, p < 0.001)"
