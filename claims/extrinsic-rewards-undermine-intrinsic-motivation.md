# Extrinsic Rewards Undermine Intrinsic Motivation

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Offering external rewards (prizes, grades, points, payment) for an already-interesting activity can reduce learners' later voluntary engagement with that activity. The claim concerns free-choice persistence and interest after rewards are removed, not task performance while rewards are still available.

## Discussion

**Scope and mechanism.** The claim is usually explained by the overjustification account: when an external reward supplies a sufficient justification for performing an already-interesting task, learners reattribute their engagement to the reward rather than to their own interest, and voluntary engagement drops once the reward is removed. This sits in tension with the reinforcement tradition of [behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), which predicts rewards should increase the rewarded behavior — the contradiction is sharpest for measures of free-choice persistence and self-reported interest rather than for task performance while rewards are still available.

**Likely moderators.** The effect is generally understood to depend on how the reward is framed and delivered: tangible versus verbal rewards, rewards promised in advance versus given unexpectedly, and rewards contingent merely on task engagement versus on performance quality. Rewards interpreted as informational — signaling competence or recognition — are less likely to undermine interest than rewards experienced as controlling [~M]. These moderators are plausible from the general literature but are not yet documented in this page's Evidence section, so they should be treated as open questions pending sourced entries.

**Boundary conditions.** For tasks learners find uninteresting to begin with, there is little intrinsic motivation to undermine, and rewards may operate as straightforward incentives [~M]. This connects to the positive counterpart of the claim: supporting learners' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness sustains interest — see [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) and [Self-determination theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md).

**Design implications.** Pending sourced evidence, the practical guidance is conditional: avoid promising tangible rewards for activities learners already find interesting; if rewards are used, prefer unexpected, performance- or progress-contingent, informational forms (feedback that signals competence) over expected, engagement-contingent, controlling ones [~M]. Gamification systems built on points and badges should be evaluated for whether they displace the interest they are meant to support — see [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md). Where rewards are unavoidable (grading systems, institutional incentives), framing them as feedback on competence rather than as payment for compliance is the safer design choice [~M].

**Open questions.** The Evidence section currently needs: meta-analytic estimates of the undermining effect, studies distinguishing reward types (tangible/verbal, expected/unexpected, engagement-/performance-contingent), and studies in authentic educational settings rather than laboratory tasks.
