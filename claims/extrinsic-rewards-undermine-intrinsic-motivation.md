# Extrinsic Rewards Undermine Intrinsic Motivation

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i1` small

Offering external rewards (prizes, grades, points, payment) for an already-interesting activity can reduce learners' later voluntary engagement with that activity. The claim concerns free-choice persistence and interest after rewards are removed, not task performance while rewards are still available.

## Subclaims

`q4 i1` A 128-study meta-analysis found that engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent tangible rewards significantly reduced learners' later free-choice engagement with the rewarded activity, though the pattern was contested by an earlier meta-analysis reaching the opposite overall conclusion. [→ Deci Koestner Ryan 1999](#deci-koestner-ryan-1999)

`q4 i?` A competing 96-study meta-analysis found no overall undermining effect of reward on free-choice intrinsic motivation, with a significant negative effect confined to expected tangible rewards given merely for doing a task. [→ Cameron Pierce 1994](#cameron-pierce-1994)

## Evidence

### Deci Koestner Ryan 1999

Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Psychological Bulletin, 125*(6), 627–668. [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i1 · small to medium effects, d=-0.28 (performance-contingent) to -0.40 (engagement-contingent)` · `n=128 studies`

A meta-analysis of 128 experiments compared free-choice persistence and self-reported interest in a rewarded activity, after the reward was withdrawn, between reward and no-reward groups. Engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent tangible rewards each significantly undermined free-choice behavior, and engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also undermined self-reported interest; all tangible rewards and all expected rewards undermined free-choice behavior overall. By contrast, positive (verbal) feedback significantly *enhanced* both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31), and tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than for college students. The authors present this as a defense and extension of the overjustification/self-determination account against contemporaneous critiques, including Cameron & Pierce's own reanalyses.

### Cameron Pierce 1994

Cameron, J., & Pierce, W. D. (1994). Reinforcement, reward, and intrinsic motivation: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 64*(3), 363–423. [doi:10.3102/00346543064003363](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003363)

`q4 · meta-analysis of between-groups experiments (plus a secondary within-subject analysis)` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract; described as no overall effect and a minimal negative effect for expected tangible rewards` · `n=96 studies (main analysis), plus 5 studies in a secondary within-subject analysis`

A hierarchical meta-analysis of 96 between-groups experiments compared rewarded and non-rewarded participants on four measures of intrinsic motivation, with a secondary analysis of 5 within-subject studies. Contrary to the overjustification hypothesis, the authors report that reward does not decrease intrinsic motivation overall, and that verbal praise increases it; a negative effect appears only for expected tangible rewards given simply for performing a task, and only on the free-choice behavior measure (not on self-reported attitudes), with the effect described as minimal. (Read as an abstract; the abstract gives no effect sizes.)

## Discussion

**Scope and mechanism.** The claim is usually explained by the overjustification account: when an external reward supplies a sufficient justification for performing an already-interesting task, learners reattribute their engagement to the reward rather than to their own interest, and voluntary engagement drops once the reward is removed. This sits in tension with the reinforcement tradition of [behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), which predicts rewards should increase the rewarded behavior — the contradiction is sharpest for measures of free-choice persistence and self-reported interest rather than for task performance while rewards are still available.

**Likely moderators.** The effect is generally understood to depend on how the reward is framed and delivered: tangible versus verbal rewards, rewards promised in advance versus given unexpectedly, and rewards contingent merely on task engagement versus on performance quality. Rewards interpreted as informational — signaling competence or recognition — are less likely to undermine interest than rewards experienced as controlling [~M]. The two meta-analyses recorded above document several of these moderators, and they disagree about how much they matter: Deci et al. (1999) find undermining for engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards, while Cameron and Pierce (1994) find no overall effect and a minimal one only for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Both find that verbal praise does not undermine intrinsic motivation.

**Boundary conditions.** For tasks learners find uninteresting to begin with, there is little intrinsic motivation to undermine, and rewards may operate as straightforward incentives [~M]. This connects to the positive counterpart of the claim: supporting learners' needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness sustains interest — see [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) and [Self-determination theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md).

**Design implications.** Pending sourced evidence, the practical guidance is conditional: avoid promising tangible rewards for activities learners already find interesting; if rewards are used, prefer unexpected, performance- or progress-contingent, informational forms (feedback that signals competence) over expected, engagement-contingent, controlling ones [~M]. Gamification systems built on points and badges should be evaluated for whether they displace the interest they are meant to support — see [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md). Where rewards are unavoidable (grading systems, institutional incentives), framing them as feedback on competence rather than as payment for compliance is the safer design choice [~M].

**Open questions.** Studies in authentic educational settings, rather than laboratory tasks, are still not recorded here.
