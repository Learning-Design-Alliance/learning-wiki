# In DiffGame the middle tier of students, not the lowest tier, shows the largest learning gain (0.83 vs 0.35 and 0.57)

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` When students are tiered by overall percentage of correct answers, the middle tier shows the largest gain (0.83), exceeding both the weakest (0.35) and strongest (0.57) tiers, contradicting prior game studies where the weakest students gained most. [→ Pedersen 2016](#pedersen-2016)

## Evidence

### Pedersen 2016

Pedersen, M. K., Svenningsen, A., Dohn, N. B., Lieberoth, A., & Sherson, J. (2016). DiffGame: Game-based mathematics learning for physics. [doi:10.1016/j.sbspro.2016.07.047](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sbspro.2016.07.047)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

In the second test with 117 students, gains within 20% percentiles based on overall percentage of correct answers (Figure 2.a) are compared; the middle tier gained 0.83 versus 0.35 for the weakest and 0.57 for the strongest. Only tier gains, no test statistics, are printed.

> "The second test demonstrated a gain from both the weakest (0.35) and the strongest (0.57) students with the middle tier displaying the largest gain (0.83)"
