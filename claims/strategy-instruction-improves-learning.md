# Strategy Instruction Improves Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Explicitly teaching learners cognitive and metacognitive strategies — how to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own thinking — improves learning outcomes across domains. The claim covers both domain strategies (e.g., reading comprehension strategies, problem-solving heuristics) and self-regulation strategies (goal-setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation).

## Discussion

**Scope.** "Strategy instruction" is a family of practices, not a single technique. It includes domain-specific strategies (e.g., summarization, self-questioning, graphic organizers for reading comprehension; worked-example study and heuristic prompts for problem solving) and general self-regulation strategies (goal-setting, planning, self-monitoring, self-evaluation) — see [Self-regulated learning improves academic achievement.](self-regulated-learning-improves-achievement.md). Effects are strongest when strategies are taught explicitly, modeled, and practiced with feedback rather than merely described [~M].

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Benefits depend on sustained, embedded practice: one-off strategy talks produce little transfer, while extended programs with modeling, guided practice, and fading show durable gains [~M]. Strategy instruction also interacts with learner characteristics — novices benefit most from highly structured, step-by-step strategy training, while more advanced learners may find heavy scaffolding redundant (an expertise-reversal pattern consistent with [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md)) [~M]. Transfer is stronger when learners are prompted to apply strategies in varied tasks and when instruction includes conditional knowledge (when and why to use a strategy), not just procedural steps [~M].

**Constraints.** Strategy instruction fails or backfires under identifiable conditions. When strategies are taught as procedures without conditional knowledge, learners apply them indiscriminately or abandon them once support is withdrawn [~M]. For learners who already use effective strategies spontaneously, additional strategy training adds load without benefit and can depress performance — the same expertise-reversal pattern seen with [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md) [~M]. Short, decontextualized interventions (a single lesson on "study skills") show negligible transfer to real coursework [-M]. And when strategy training consumes time that would go to domain content without being integrated into that content, gains on strategy measures do not translate into domain achievement [-W].

**Open questions.** The evidence base for this page has not yet been assembled; the specific effect sizes, domains, and study designs supporting the claim still need to be documented before the strength rating can be set.
