# Misconceptions Interfere With New Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Pre-existing misconceptions — coherent but incorrect mental models — can interfere with the acquisition of accurate new knowledge, particularly when learners fail to recognize the conflict between their prior beliefs and the to-be-learned content.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Misconceptions are not random errors; they are often well-organized, self-consistent frameworks built from everyday experience (e.g., naive physics intuitions like "heavier objects fall faster" or "seasons are caused by distance from the sun"). Because they are coherent and frequently successful in daily life, learners retrieve them readily, and they can compete with — or be assimilated into — newly taught concepts, distorting both encoding and retrieval [~M]. This is the core rationale for conceptual-change instruction: learning requires not just adding knowledge but restructuring or replacing existing schemas, consistent with the view of learners as active constructors of knowledge rather than blank slates (see [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md)).

**Conditions that moderate interference.** Interference is strongest when learners are unaware of their misconception and instruction never surfaces it; simply presenting the correct explanation does not guarantee that the incorrect model is discarded [-M]. Activating prior knowledge so the misconception becomes explicit [~S], creating cognitive disequilibrium by confronting learners with anomalous events or discrepant data [~M], and explicitly refuting the misconception (refutation-style texts and lectures) [~M] are the standard instructional responses. See [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) and [Activation improves learning.](activation-improves-learning.md).

**Instructional implications.** Effective designs diagnose misconceptions before instruction (e.g., via pre-tests or predict–observe–explain tasks), surface them publicly so learners experience the failure of their intuitive model, and then provide explicit refutation plus multiple opportunities to apply the corrected model. Refutation texts that name the misconception directly outperform texts that present only the correct explanation [~M]. Because misconceptions are entrenched, single exposures rarely suffice; spaced re-testing and application in varied contexts are needed to prevent reversion. Designers should also anticipate *re-emergent* misconceptions in assessment: a learner who answers correctly immediately after instruction may revert to the intuitive model on a delayed or transfer item, so assessments should sample unfamiliar contexts and delayed retention, not just near-post-test performance.

**Open questions.** The literature distinguishes between misconceptions that are fully replaced and those that are suppressed but retained — learners often revert to intuitive models under time pressure or in unfamiliar contexts [~M]. How durable conceptual change is across contexts, and how best to assess whether a misconception has truly been displaced rather than merely inhibited, remain active questions.
