Emotion dynamics during problem-solving predict learning outcomes in a manner that depends on scaffolding design¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r? · q3 · i1 small · n=132 university students (59% male), novices to data science, 43-45 per condition

Automated frame-by-frame facial-emotion coding of 132 university students across three randomized problem-solving-before-instruction scaffolding conditions found that failure-driven scaffolding produces more shame and more diverse emotion sequences than success-driven scaffolding, and that the same emotions (e.g., disgust, contempt) correlate with opposite learning-outcome directions depending on which scaffolding condition a student was in.

Evidence¶

Sinha 2022¶

Sinha, T. (2022). Enriching problem-solving followed by instruction with explanatory accounts of emotions. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 31(2), 151-198. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2021.1964506

q3 · randomized three-condition experiment (n=132) with automated multimodal emotion detection (14.8 million video frames, three independent facial-coding schemes) and PANAS/curiosity self-report validation; single site, single domain, no replication · i1-i2 · emotion-incidence differences between randomized conditions are modest-to-medium (d~0.13-0.43) and directly attributable to the manipulation; emotion-outcome correlations (ρ~0.35-0.47) are observational within each condition, not causally isolated · n=132 university students (59% male), novices to data science, 43-45 per condition · causal · r?

132 university students were randomly assigned to failure-driven scaffolding, success-driven scaffolding, or unscaffolded Problem-Solving Followed by Instruction using Failure-Driven Scaffolding on a bivariate data-ranking task. Automated facial action coding across the full session (14.8 million frames) inferred 28 emotion categories per frame; sequential pattern mining identified which emotions co-occurred and in what order. Emotion-incidence differences between the randomized conditions (surprise, confusion, shame) are causally attributable to the scaffolding manipulation. The correlations between emotion incidence and posttest performance, however, are observational associations computed within each condition after random assignment — they show that the same emotion predicts learning in opposite directions depending on instructional context, but they do not establish that any specific emotion causally drives learning.

Discussion¶

The paper's most important methodological caution is built into its own framing: correlations between emotion incidence and posttest scores are not causal evidence that manipulating emotion would change learning, even though the scaffolding conditions themselves were randomized. The context-dependent reversal of the disgust/contempt-outcome relationship across conditions is the paper's most novel and best-supported finding, and argues against treating any emotion as uniformly facilitative or debilitating for learning. Self-report data (PANAS, state curiosity, cognitive dissonance) converges with the automated coding in ordering the three conditions, which increases confidence that the facial-coding measure is capturing something real rather than an artifact of the classifier.
