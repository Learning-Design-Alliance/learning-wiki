# Exposing undergraduate medical students to the unexpected death of a simulation manikin does not negatively affect their perceived self-efficacy for managing sudden-death situations

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` After a simulation in which the manikin unexpectedly died, medical students' perceived self-efficacy regarding managing sudden-death situations was not negatively impacted, whether or not they were warned of the possible death. [→ Weiss Anne 2017](#weiss-anne-2017)

## Evidence

### Weiss Anne 2017

Weiss Anne, Jaffrelot Morgan, Bartier Jean-Claude, Pottecher Thierry, Borraccia Isabelle, Mahoudeau Gilles, Noll Eric, Brunstein Véronique, Delacour Chloé, Pelaccia Thierry. (2017). Does the unexpected death of the manikin in a simulation maintain the participants’ perceived self-efficacy? An observational prospective study with medical students. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-017-0944-x

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Single-centre observational prospective study of 56 undergraduate medical students across eight simulation sessions with a high-fidelity manikin programmed to lose vital signs. The article concludes the "simulated death of the manikin did not have a negative impact on the students’ PSE" in either prebriefing condition.

> "The simulated death of the manikin did not have a negative impact on the students’ PSE, whether or not they had been warned of the possible occurrence of such an event."
