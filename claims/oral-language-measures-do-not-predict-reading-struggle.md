# Oral language measures of syntax, listening comprehension, and oral vocabulary do not predict which English learners will struggle with learning to read

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · review `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Oral proficiency measures do not predict who is likely to struggle with learning to read, whereas early reading measures administered in English screen effectively even for children who know little English. [→ Gersten 2007](#gersten-2007)

## Evidence

### Gersten 2007

Gersten, R., Baker, S.K., Shanahan, T., Linan-Thompson, S., Collins, P., & Scarcella, R. (2007). Effective Literacy and English Language Instruction for English Learners in the Elementary Grades: A Practice Guide (NCEE 2007-4011). Washington, DC: National Center for Education Evaluation and Regional Assistance, Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/6

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The guide's summary, in Recommendation 1's carry-out section, of studies contrasting oral language measures with early reading measures administered in English. It reports that "oral language measures of syntax, listening comprehension, and oral vocabulary do not predict" later reading difficulty; no effect size is printed.

> "In fact, oral language measures of syntax, listening comprehension, and oral vocabulary do not predict  who is likely to struggle with learning to read."
