## Evidence

### Andrew Mason and Chandralekha Singh 2016

Andrew Mason and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Surveying Graduate Students' Attitudes and Approaches to Problem Solving. [doi:10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704](https://doi.org/10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Survey comparison (Figure 4): while more than 30% of graduate students agreed (unfavorable) that problem solving means matching problems to equations for introductory problems, discussions showed graduate students' reasoning differed — basic principles felt automatic to them — whereas introductory students often used a plug-and-chug approach.

> "the average responses of introductory physics students to question (5) were indistinguishable from those of the graduate students for introductory physics problem solv- ing."

### Andrew Mason and Chandralekha Singh 2016 (2)

Andrew Mason and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Surveying Graduate Students' Attitudes and Approaches to Problem Solving. [doi:10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704](https://doi.org/10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Survey item (Figure 5) on explicitly thinking about underlying concepts. Faculty discussions indicated concepts had become obvious through vast experience, invoked automatically; more than 90% of faculty explicitly thought about concepts for graduate-level problems.

> "close to 90% of the graduate students agreed (favorable) that they do so both in the context of introductory and graduate level problem solving. However, only approximately 65% and 55% of the physics faculty and introductory physics students agreed, respectively."
