# Overall reading time did not differ between consistent and inconsistent texts, but participants judged consistent texts more accurately (d = 0.84), indicating inconsistencies were more often overlooked than falsely perceived

## Subclaims
`q3 i3` Average overall reading time did not differ between consistent and inconsistent versions (d < 0.01), while consistency judgement accuracy was significantly higher for consistent texts (Cohen's d = 0.84). [→ Catharina Tibken 2025](#catharina-tibken-2025)

## Evidence

### Catharina Tibken 2025

Catharina Tibken, Simon P. Tiffin-Richards. (2025). Reading behavior as an indicator of comprehension monitoring when reading expository texts. Metacognition and Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-025-09440-2

`q3 · i3`

Descriptive statistics and paired t-tests over 1870 analyzed trials from the eye-tracking experiment. Table 1 prints consistency judgement accuracy of 85% (SD 36) for consistent versus 48% (SD 50) for inconsistent texts, t = 18.2, Cohen's d = 0.84 [0.75, 0.94]; reading time showed d < 0.01.

> "Average overall read- ing time did not differ between consistent and inconsistent versions of the expository texts. Participants were, however, significantly more accurate at judging the consistency of consis- tent, compared to inconsistent versions of the texts. This suggests that participants were more likely to overlook an inconsistency, than to perceive an inconsistency where there was none."
