## Evidence

### Evrard 2013

Evrard, A. E., Mills, M., Winn, D., Jones, K., Tritz, J., and McKay, T. A. (2013). Problem Roulette: Studying Introductory Physics in the Cloud. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1309.7678](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1309.7678)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

The article's framing, citing a review of ten learning techniques from the educational psychology literature, claims PR supports both high-utility techniques: students can effectively take a practice exam within a time limit and can distribute study across days and weeks.

> "practice testing and distributed practice (learning partitioned into multiple sessions) are the only two techniques rated as having high utility"

### Evrard 2013 (2)

Evrard, A. E., Mills, M., Winn, D., Jones, K., Tritz, J., and McKay, T. A. (2013). Problem Roulette: Studying Introductory Physics in the Cloud. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1309.7678](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1309.7678)

`q1 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Qualitative self-report: illustrative student comments describe moving away from rote memorization, and testimonies favor the reported peer-answer histograms and comparison of solution times. No quantitative measure of synthesis is provided.

> "Students attest that working a set of randomly-chosen problems helps them learn and synthesize concepts, and they realize that this is critical for their success in physics."
