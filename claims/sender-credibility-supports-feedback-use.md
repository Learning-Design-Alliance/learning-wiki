# Learners are more willing to act on feedback when they perceive the sender as credible and trustworthy

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · review `r3` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Medical residents and university students reported being more likely to use feedback from apparently credible sources and unlikely to engage with feedback from senders lacking credibility signals. [→ Winstone 2014](#winstone-2014)

## Evidence

### Winstone 2014

Winstone, N. E., Nash, R. A., Parker, M., & Rowntree, J. (2014). Supporting Learners' Agentic Engagement with Feedback: A Systematic Review and a Taxonomy of Recipience Processes. Educational Psychologist (in press).

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports, citing Eva et al. (2012), a focus group study in which students judged feedback from credible sources as more accurate and more usable. Bing-You et al. (1997) similarly found medical residents unlikely to engage with senders lacking credibility signals such as knowledge, experience, and interpersonal skills.

> "undergraduate and postgraduate students judged feedback as more accurate, and claimed they were more likely to use it, if it originated from an apparently credible source"
