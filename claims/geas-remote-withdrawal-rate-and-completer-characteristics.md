# The remote GEAS pilot cohort withdrew at four times the on-campus rate, mostly for non-course reasons, while completers were older and more likely to have studied math beyond algebra

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The distance learning course had a large fractional withdrawal rate, four times that of the on-campus course, with most withdrawing students citing difficulty securing financial aid rather than course-related issues. [→ Nicole P. Vogt 2012](#nicole-p-vogt-2012)
`q2 i?` Students who completed the distance course were older (25-30 years old rather than 20 or younger), more than twice as likely to have children, and had studied math beyond algebra, though their self-assessed math skills were not significantly higher. [→ Nicole P. Vogt 2012 (2)](#nicole-p-vogt-2012-2)

## Evidence

### Nicole P. Vogt 2012

Nicole P. Vogt, Stephen P. Cook, and Amy Smith Muise. (2012). A New Resource for College Distance Education Astronomy Laboratory Exercises. [doi:10.1080/08923647.2013.795365](https://doi.org/10.1080/08923647.2013.795365)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Pilot comparison section reporting cohort characteristics for the small Fall 2011 distance course versus the Fall 2010 classroom course; the article prints the withdrawal-rate ratio but no effect size or test statistic for this contrast.

> "We emphasize that the distance learning class was a small pilot cohort and had a large fractional withdrawal rate (four times that of the on-campus course)."

### Nicole P. Vogt 2012 (2)

Nicole P. Vogt, Stephen P. Cook, and Amy Smith Muise. (2012). A New Resource for College Distance Education Astronomy Laboratory Exercises. [doi:10.1080/08923647.2013.795365](https://doi.org/10.1080/08923647.2013.795365)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Descriptive comparison of distance-course completers against withdrawn and on-campus students in the pilot study; the article reports these demographic and background differences descriptively, with no effect sizes, and notes pre-class survey scores did not differ significantly.

> "Those who completed the course via distance learning were older (25-30 years old, rather than 20 or younger) and more than twice as likely to have children (as expected, cf. Radford 2012), and had studied math beyond algebra"
