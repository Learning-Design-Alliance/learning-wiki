Evidence¶

Means 2021¶

Means, B., Peters, V., Neisler, J., Wiley, K., & Grifths, R. (2021). Lessons from remote learning during COVID-19. Digital Promise. https://doi.org/10.51388/20.500.12265/116

q2 · i? · associational · r2

National probability-based KnowledgePanel survey of undergraduates whose in-person courses went fully remote in spring 2020; final sample of 1,008. The survey found "44% of students" faced connectivity issues interfering at least occasionally, and separately almost a quarter (23%) reported hardware or software problems at least occasionally.

"Internet connectivity issues were serious enough to interfere with students’ ability to attend or participate in their courses at least occasionally for 44% of students, with 16% of students reporting that they experienced such problems often or very often."

Means 2020¶

Means, B., and Neisler, J., with Langer Research Associates. (2020). Suddenly Online: A National Survey of Undergraduates During the COVID-19 Pandemic. San Mateo, CA: Digital Promise. https://digitalpromise.dspacedirect.org/items/32aee7c4-5363-4d50-bc9c-a338e7dfac7e. doi:10.51388/20.500.12265/98

q3 · i? · associational · r2

Structured-response survey items asked students how often connectivity problems interfered with course participation. Ninety-five percent accessed courses via home internet; videoconferencing's high bandwidth demands are given as context.

"Internet connectivity issues were serious enough to interfere with students’ ability to attend or participate in their course at least occasionally for 44 percent of students, with 16 percent of students experiencing such problems often or very often."

Means 2020 (2)¶

Means, B., and Neisler, J., with Langer Research Associates. (2020). Suddenly Online: A National Survey of Undergraduates During the COVID-19 Pandemic. San Mateo, CA: Digital Promise. https://digitalpromise.dspacedirect.org/items/32aee7c4-5363-4d50-bc9c-a338e7dfac7e. doi:10.51388/20.500.12265/98

q3 · i? · associational · r2

Structured-response survey item on hardware/software problem frequency; ten percent of students shared their access device and three percent used a college-provided device.

"Almost a quarter of students (23 percent) experienced hardware or software problems serious enough to impact their ability to attend or participate in their course at least occasionally."

Related Claims¶

  • Higher education digital divides are acute at MSIs, HBCUs, and TCUs, which have stark capability deficits in hardware, software, and broadband — related

  • Technology access problems during the COVID shift were more prevalent among minoritized students and students from lower-income households — related
  • Hispanic and low-income students reported more challenges and technology problems after the shift online than non-Hispanic White and higher-income peers; rural students did not differ — related
  • Immediately after closure, New York charter students lacked adequate devices (44.4% on average) more often than adequate internet access (27.0%), while teachers were almost universally equipped — related
  • Motivation, internet connectivity, and hardware/software problems were widespread challenges in remote STEM courses — possibly the same claim (merge candidate)
  • An estimated one-third of students fail to learn because of psychosocial problems that interfere with engagement in instruction — related
  • Teachers' technology use increased during COVID-19 remote learning, but infrastructure and device barriers hindered effective integration — related

  • In informal virtual programs, technology access was the first and most significant barrier to participating in Hero Elementary — related
  • AI in adult education risks reinforcing existing inequalities via the digital divide, particularly for rural, low-income, and marginalized learners — related
