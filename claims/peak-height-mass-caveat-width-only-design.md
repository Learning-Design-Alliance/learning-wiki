# Peak height also increases with lens mass, but only when the separation-to-Einstein-angle ratio u is held equal, so the curriculum uses width alone to convey mass

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The height of the microlensing peak increases with lens mass only assuming equal u, a caveat the authors judged too burdensome for Astro 101 students. [→ Colin S. Wallace 2016](#colin-s-wallace-2016)

## Evidence

### Colin S. Wallace 2016

Colin S. Wallace, Timothy Chambers, Edward E. Prather, and Gina Brissenden. (2016). Using graphical and pictorial representations to teach introductory astronomy students about the detection of extrasolar planets via gravitational microlensing. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1603.06866](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1603.06866)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r1`

Analytical derivation of maximum brightness at closest alignment; the authors show the brightness fraction grows monotonically as u decreases, so "more massive lenses should produce greater brightening effects" all else being equal.

> "The height of the peak – that is, the ratio of the maximum brightness to the unlensed brightness – also increases as the mass of the lensing object increases."

### Colin S. Wallace 2016 (2)

Colin S. Wallace, Timothy Chambers, Edward E. Prather, and Gina Brissenden. (2016). Using graphical and pictorial representations to teach introductory astronomy students about the detection of extrasolar planets via gravitational microlensing. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1603.06866](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1603.06866)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r?`

Design decision justified by the derivation: comparing perturbation heights across light curves cannot assume equal u, and the caveat would overburden students with novel variables while misrepresenting research scenarios.

> "we purposefully chose to use only the width of a planetary perturbation, and not its height, to convey information about the extrasolar planet's mass in the Lecture-Tutorial."
