# PAIR-C scaffolding shows mixed evidence for deep understanding and reduced misconceptions in emergent-phenomena instruction

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r2` · `q3` · n=50 (24 PAIR-C, 26 control)

Explicitly scaffolding the contrast between emergent (decentralized, collective) and sequential (individualistic, cumulative) causal structure — the [PAIR-C framework](../patterns/pair-c-framework.md) — produced a statistically significant advantage on near-transfer tasks, a non-significant trend on deep-understanding questions, and no far transfer, in one study of 50 undergraduates randomly assigned to two conditions. The authors' abstract states the stronger reading: that PAIR-C "significantly improves students' deep understanding of natural selection, more effectively reducing misconceptions compared to the traditional method."

## Evidence

### Su, Chi & Nagashima 2026

Su, M., Chi, M. T. H., & Nagashima, T. (2026). Applying the PAIR-C Framework to foster deep understanding and address misconceptions in science education. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 35*(1), 130-175. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2546471](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2546471)

`q3 · randomized experiment with a control condition, pre/post design ("randomly assigned to two groups", per the abstract)` · `i? · no d-family effect size is recorded here; the entry reports F and p values only` · `n=50 (24 PAIR-C, 26 control)` · `causal · r2`

Fifty undergraduate students, participating online, learned natural selection using NetLogo agent-based simulations (Peppered Moths, Rock Pocket Mice), either with explicit PAIR-C-dimension scaffolding (annotations, prompts, and feedback framed around the five dimensions) or with traditional instruction focused on differential survival and reproduction using the same simulations. Both groups were assessed pre/post on shallow questions, deep (inter-level causal) questions, near-transfer questions (same concept, different context), far-transfer questions (an unrelated emergent phenomenon), and open-ended misconception coding (nine misconception categories, two raters, Kappa=0.843). The PAIR-C group significantly outperformed control on near-transfer gains. On deep-understanding questions and misconception reduction, PAIR-C showed a larger improvement than control, but the group-by-time interaction in each case did not clear the conventional p<.05 threshold (p=.059, p=.060, p=.095 respectively). Neither group achieved far transfer to an unrelated emergent phenomenon (geese flight formation). The abstract, by contrast, reports that PAIR-C "significantly improves students' deep understanding of natural selection, more effectively reducing misconceptions compared to the traditional method", and that epistemic network analysis showed a more focused misconception pattern in the PAIR-C group; the p-values above have not been re-checked against the full text, which was not available when this entry was corrected (2026-10-15).

## Discussion
This is a single-domain (natural selection), single-population (undergraduates, not K-12 students) randomized study with a modest sample (n=50). On this entry's reading, only the near-transfer result cleared conventional statistical significance; on that reading the deep-understanding and misconception-reduction results are trends. The authors' abstract reports them as significant, so the two readings disagree, and the full text has to settle which holds before either is relied on. The complete absence of far transfer in both groups suggests that naming and contrasting the five dimensions within one domain is not, by itself, sufficient for learners to recognize the same causal structure in an unfamiliar emergent phenomenon — the authors suggest explicit cross-domain analogical comparison (per Jacobson et al., 2020) may be needed for that. Persistence of gains beyond the immediate posttest was not measured.
