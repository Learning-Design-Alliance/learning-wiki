# Implementing AI methodologies introduces a new layer of epistemic dependence and diffused accountability in science education research

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` Researchers increasingly employ pre-trained large language models whose internal workings remain only partially accessible, creating epistemic dependence on instruments that cannot be fully inspected or understood. [→ Paul P. Martin 2026](#paul-p-martin-2026)

Conceptual argument in Phase 6 (implementation and its consequences), paralleling non-experts' use of thermometers. The article notes researchers may have limited knowledge of training data, fine-tuning procedures, or optimization objectives of pre-trained models. Analytical, not empirical.

> "the use of AI methodologies introduces a new layer of epistemic dependence in which researchers must rely on instruments that cannot be fully inspected or understood"

### Paul P. Martin 2026 (2)

Paul P. Martin, Marvin Rost, Jenna Koenen, Nicole Graulich. (2026). Amid an Epistemic Iteration: How AI Methodologies May Transform the Nature of Science Education Research. Science & Education. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11191-026-00789-7

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

The article argues AI systems involve model developers, data curators, software engineers, researchers, and interacting algorithms, so if accountability becomes diffused, "the pathways through which knowledge claims can be challenged or revised become less transparent." Conceptual argument citing Yeung (2018).

> "This creates what Yeung (2018) describes as a “many ‑hands problem,” in which responsibility for errors or harmful consequences becomes difficult to allocate"
