# Age differences in strategy use limit the general-factor account: when youths and adults use different strategies, RTs are not a simple multiple

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` The analogical reasoning task violated the model because solution procedures differed by age, so youth RTs were smaller than expected relative to adult RTs. [→ Kail 1991](#kail-1991)
`q1 i?` Expertise can reduce or eliminate the usual age pattern, as when child chess experts scan chessboards as fast as adult novices. [→ Kail 1991 (2)](#kail-1991-2)

## Evidence

### Kail 1991

Robert Kail. (1991). Developmental Change in Speed of Processing During Childhood and Adolescence. Psychological Bulletin, 109(3), 490-501. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.109.3.490

`q1 · i?` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

Authors' interpretation of the Kail (1988, Exp. 2) discrepancy: "the procedures that subjects used to solve the analogical reasoning task apparently differed by age," removing the basis for predicting youth RTs from adult RTs. Age differences on the reasoning task were smaller than expected and RTs on it typically exceeded 5 s.

> "youths' RTs are expected to be a multiple of adults' RTs only if the solution strategies represented in Equations 1 and 2 are the same for youths and adults."

### Kail 1991 (2)

Robert Kail. (1991). Developmental Change in Speed of Processing During Childhood and Adolescence. Psychological Bulletin, 109(3), 490-501. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.109.3.490

`q1 · i?` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

The article cites Roth (1983) as an example in which greater task knowledge eliminates the usual age differences; the article presents this as an interpretive illustration, not as a re-analysis performed here.

> "children who are chess experts scan chessboards at a rate that does not differ significantly from that of adult novices (Roth, 1983)."
