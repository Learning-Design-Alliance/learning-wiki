# Premodern cultures' narratives assimilate language to the world rather than vice versa, offering environmental educators place-bound narrative strategies

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q1` argument or single case

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` The languages, myths and rituals of cultures such as Australian Aborigines and Native Americans articulate culturally and ecologically located conceptions of self, and their stories assimilate language to the world in contrast to the totalising discourses of modern science. [→ Gough 1992](#gough-1992)

## Evidence

### Gough 1992

Gough, Noel; Kesson, Kathleen. (1992). Body and Narrative as Cultural Text: Toward a Curriculum of Continuity and Connection. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED347544

`q1 · i?`

Theoretical argument in the 'Assimilating body and narrative to place' section. The authors report examples such as Jay's salmon stories and the body paintings of women of the Alyawarre and Mmatyerre communities, who "painted their bodies with designs tka told their personal stones and danced and sang to explain their Dreaming", arguing their mythologies "are closely tied to place" and not exportable like Christianity or Islam.

> "Environmental educators can learn much from the narrative strategies of premodern (or 'not modern') cultures like those of Australian Aborigines and Native Americans."
