Pre-tests reduced adult learners' persistence in a MOOC, though they improved post-test scores among those who completed it¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · causal r? · q3 · n=399

Pretesting — attempting to answer questions about not-yet-learned material before instruction — reduced persistence in one randomized MOOC experiment. The study measured persistence and post-test learning, not motivation itself; that pre-test failure discourages learners is the proposed explanation (see Discussion), not a recorded finding.

Evidence¶

Janelli & Lipnevich 2021¶

Janelli, M., & Lipnevich, A. A. (2021). Effects of pre-tests and feedback on performance outcomes and persistence in Massive Open Online Courses. Computers & Education, 161, 104076. doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2020.104076

q3 · randomized experiment (not pre-registered) · i? · no standardized effect size reported · n=399 · causal · r?

399 adults who enrolled in the American Museum of Natural History's climate-change MOOC were randomly assigned to pre-tests without feedback, pre-tests with basic (correct/incorrect) feedback, pre-tests with elaborate feedback, or a no-pretest control. Pre-tests reduced persistence in the course. Across all students, pre-tests and feedback did not affect post-test learning; among those who completed the course, pre-tests improved it. Read as the article's abstract; completion rates by condition are reported in the authors' companion book chapter and are not recorded here.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The proposed harm (the recorded study measured persistence, not motivation) runs through learners' affective response to error. When a pretest consists of questions learners cannot answer, repeated failure before any instruction can lower self-efficacy and perceived competence, undermining willingness to engage with the subsequent lesson. This is consistent with motivation theories that treat perceived competence as a driver of engagement — see Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation — and it marks a boundary condition on the otherwise well-supported pretesting effect (see Pretesting enhances learning).

Moderators. The motivational cost plausibly depends on how failure is framed and experienced: whether the pretest is low-stakes, whether learners are told in advance that errors are expected and useful, whether feedback follows quickly, and how difficult the pretest items are relative to learners' background knowledge. Learners with low prior knowledge or low academic confidence are the most plausible at-risk group, since they have the least basis for interpreting pretest failure as informative rather than as evidence of inability. Framing matters in both directions: Conflict-based instruction improves science conceptual learning, though staged contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused, and no study isolates disequilibrium as the mechanism captures the case where early failure provokes productive curiosity, while Belonging interventions improve outcomes suggests that normalizing early error as shared and temporary can buffer the harm.

Tension with the pretesting effect. The literature on pretesting largely documents cognitive benefits — enhanced attention to relevant information and better retention of answers that were initially guessed wrong. The motivational claim does not contradict those findings; it identifies a cost that may co-occur with them. A designer's question is therefore not "pretest or not?" but "how do we capture the error-related learning benefit without the motivational cost?" — for example, by framing pretests as brain-teasers rather than assessments, keeping them short, and avoiding grades or public comparison.

Open questions. The size, durability, and generality of the motivational harm remain unresolved. It is not established whether motivational effects translate into measurable differences in subsequent learning outcomes, or whether they dissipate once instruction begins. The one recorded study (Janelli & Lipnevich 2021) measured persistence, not motivation, and was read from its abstract; studies directly measuring motivation alongside retention in pretesting designs are still needed.
