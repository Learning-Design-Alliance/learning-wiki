# Slight control over an algorithm's output reduces algorithm aversion, with process control producing the strongest calibration effects

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 2 theoretical `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The article reports, citing Dietvorst et al., that giving users even a slight degree of control over an algorithm's output was sufficient to reduce algorithm aversion substantially. [→ Pierre Haritz 2026](#pierre-haritz-2026)

## Evidence

### Pierre Haritz 2026

Pierre Haritz, Hendrik Krone, and Thomas Liebig. (2026). Addressing Trust in AI Systems through Education: A Didactic Perspective. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02453 [doi:10.48550/arxiv.2609.02453](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2609.02453)

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Narrative review attributing this experimental finding to Dietvorst et al.'s subsequent study on overcoming algorithm aversion. The article reports slight modification ability "was sufficient to reduce algorithm aversion substantially"; no effect sizes are printed.

> "giving users even a slight degree of control over an algorithm's output, namely the ability to modify its predictions by a small amount, was sufficient to reduce algorithm aversion substantially"

### Pierre Haritz 2026 (2)

Pierre Haritz, Hendrik Krone, and Thomas Liebig. (2026). Addressing Trust in AI Systems through Education: A Didactic Perspective. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02453

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Narrative review attributing this experimental finding to Cheng and Chouldechova's CHI 2023 comparison of outcome and process control. The article reports process control produced "the strongest calibration effects"; no effect sizes are printed.

> "Cheng and Chouldechova extended this finding by distinguishing between outcome control (the ability to adjust outputs) and process control (the ability to influence how the algorithm works), and showed that both forms of control can mitigate aversion, with process control producing the strongest calibration effects"
