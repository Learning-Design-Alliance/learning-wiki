# Students who say being popular is important move toward the locally prevailing norm when sign-up is public, while students who say it is not important are unaffected

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In nonhonors classes, students who say it is important to be popular sign up 20 percentage points less when public than private (p = .002); those who care less show a small, non-significant 4 percentage point effect. [→ Bursztyn Leonardo 2015](#bursztyn-leonardo-2015)
`q3 i?` In honors classes the pattern reverses on a smaller scale: a positive effect of public sign-up for popularity-concerned students and no difference for others. [→ Bursztyn Leonardo 2015 (2)](#bursztyn-leonardo-2015-2)

## Evidence

### Bursztyn Leonardo 2015

Bursztyn Leonardo, Jensen Robert. (2015). How Does Peer Pressure Affect Educational Investments? The Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(3). https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv021

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Heterogeneity analysis splitting the field experiment sample by self-reported importance of being popular (1–2 versus 3–5 on a 1–5 scale), with raw sign-up rates in Figures V and VI confirmed in regressions (Table V). The article reports the "sign-up rate is 20 percentage points lower in the public condition" for popularity-important nonhonors students (p = .002).

> "Figure V shows that for students in nonhonors classes who say that it is important to be popular, the sign-up rate is 20 percentage points lower in the public condition than in the private condition (p = .002). For those who care less about popularity, the effect of a public decision is small (4 percentage points) and not statistically significant."

### Bursztyn Leonardo 2015 (2)

Bursztyn Leonardo, Jensen Robert. (2015). How Does Peer Pressure Affect Educational Investments? The Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(3). https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv021

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r3`

The same popularity heterogeneity analysis in honors classes, where private take-up was already near 100 percent. The article reports "the opposite pattern for honors classes": a positive public effect for popularity-concerned students and none for others.

> "In Figure VI, we observe the opposite pattern for honors classes, although on a smaller scale (since the private take-up rates were already very close to 100 percent): a positive effect of public sign-up for those who care more about popularity, and no difference for those who care less."
