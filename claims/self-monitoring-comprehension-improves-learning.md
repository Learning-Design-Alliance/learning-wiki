# Metacognitive strategy instruction, which includes comprehension monitoring, improves academic performance by a medium amount, and in one non-randomised study comprehension-monitoring training raised low achievers' reading scores

Learners who actively monitor their own comprehension — checking whether they understand, detecting gaps, and repairing them — learn more than learners who read or study passively.

### de Boer et al. 2018

de Boer, H., Donker, A. S., Kostons, D. D. N. M., & van der Werf, G. P. C. (2018). Long-term effects of metacognitive strategy instruction on student academic performance: A meta-analysis. *Educational Research Review, 24*, 98–115. [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002)

`q4 · meta-analysis (48 interventions)` · `i2 · medium effect, Hedges' g=0.50 (posttest), g=0.63 (follow-up)` · `n=48 interventions` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

This meta-analysis pooled 48 studies of metacognitive strategy instruction — a category that includes teaching students to monitor and check their own comprehension — and tracked effects on academic performance from posttest to a later follow-up test. The instruction effect was Hedges' g = 0.50 at posttest and grew slightly to g = 0.63 at follow-up, meaning gains were retained rather than fading. Moderator analyses found low-SES students benefited the most long-term, and that specific component strategies (metacognitive, cognitive, management, or motivational) did not differentially moderate the overall effect, except that including "rehearsal" as a component was associated with a smaller long-term effect. ### Adimora et al. 2017

Adimora, D. E., Nwokenna, E. N., & Omeje, M. O. (2017). Application of comprehension monitoring strategy for achievement and interest of low-achievers in reading comprehension. *New Trends and Issues Proceedings on Humanities and Social Sciences, 3*(3), 347–362. [doi:10.18844/prosoc.v3i3.1582](https://doi.org/10.18844/prosoc.v3i3.1582)

`q2 · quasi-experimental, non-equivalent pretest-posttest control group` · `i? · no standardized effect size reported` · `n=127 (62 experimental, 65 control)` · `causal · r1`

Low-achieving Nigerian senior-secondary students (n=127, four intact classes, two in each of two schools) were assigned to a comprehension-monitoring-strategy (CMS) treatment group or a conventional-instruction control group in a non-randomized pretest-posttest design. The CMS group's mean reading-comprehension score rose from 18.26 to 37.17 (gain of 18.92), versus the control group's rise from 17.75 to 18.68 (gain of 0.93); ANCOVA found a significant effect of treatment on posttest achievement (F = 828.05, p < .001, adjusted R² = .872) after controlling for pretest scores. The same pattern held for a reading-interest measure. Only raw means, an F-statistic and adjusted R² were reported — no Cohen's d, Hedges' g or comparable standardized effect size appears in the text, so impact is coded `i?` rather than computed from the raw scores. ## Discussion

Self-monitoring of comprehension is a core component of [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): learners must notice when text or explanations have not "landed" before they can take corrective action such as rereading, [chunking](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) difficult passages, or seeking help. Without monitoring, learners often experience illusions of competence — fluency of reading is mistaken for understanding — and so fail to repair gaps that later surface on tests.

Monitoring is not the same as judging. A learner can rate confidence high while comprehension is poor; effective monitoring requires standards against which understanding is checked (e.g., "Can I explain this in my own words?"). Designers can support monitoring with structured checkpoints — self-explanation prompts, [3-2-1 reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md) summaries, or low-stakes retrieval questions — that externalize the monitoring process rather than leaving it to the learner's unaided judgment. This connects to broader claims that [annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md) and that [assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md): both work partly by forcing learners to make their current understanding visible and checkable.

Boundary conditions to establish with evidence: monitoring without a repair strategy may only produce awareness of failure; excessive self-monitoring during reading may itself consume working-memory capacity and contribute to [cognitive overload](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md); and novices may lack the domain knowledge to judge their comprehension accurately, so monitoring accuracy likely improves with expertise. Judgments made at the moment of study are also vulnerable to fluency misinterpretation — material that *feels* easy to process is judged as learned even when it is not, which is why [retrieval practice improves retention](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) often reveals gaps that passive self-ratings miss.

Open questions for future evidence passes: whether the benefit of monitoring lies primarily in detection (noticing gaps), in the repair behaviors it triggers, or in both; and whether externally prompted monitoring (checkpoints, prompts) produces the same gains as learner-initiated monitoring.
