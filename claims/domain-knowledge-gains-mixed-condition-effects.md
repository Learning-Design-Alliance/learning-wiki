# Children gained domain knowledge of variable effects indirectly from running experiments, with mixed condition differences on individual variables

> **Evidence** · 1 study (3 entries) · causal `r2` · `q3`
> **Currency** · newest study 2003 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Domain knowledge about variable effects improved from before to after experimentation in both the spring and ramp domains, with no overall condition main effect. [→ Triona 2003](#triona-2003)
`q3 i?` For spring width, children trained with physical materials showed greater incorrect-to-correct change than virtual-trained children. [→ Triona 2003 (2)](#triona-2003-2)
`q3 i?` For ramp ball type, virtual-trained children were more likely to change from incorrect to correct than physical-trained children. [→ Triona 2003 (3)](#triona-2003-3)

## Evidence

### Triona 2003

Triona, L. M., & Klahr, D. (2003). Point and Click or Grab and Heft: Comparing the Influence of Physical and Virtual Instructional Materials on Elementary School Students' Ability to Design Experiments. Cognition and Instruction, 21(2), 149–173. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2102_02

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Separate 2 (condition) × 2 (time of assessment) MANOVAs on proportion-correct descriptions of variable effects found significant main effects of time in both domains, with no condition main effect for springs or ramps.

> "Children knew more about the effects of the variables after designing and running experiments (Springs: M = .80, SD = .22; Ramps: M = .85, SD = .15) than before (Springs: M = .71, SD = .17, 95% CI of diff: .05, .13; Ramps: M = .75, SD = .18, 95% CI of diff: .06, .14)."

### Triona 2003 (2)

Triona, L. M., & Klahr, D. (2003). Point and Click or Grab and Heft: Comparing the Influence of Physical and Virtual Instructional Materials on Elementary School Students' Ability to Design Experiments. Cognition and Instruction, 21(2), 149–173. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2102_02

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Condition-separated McNemar tests on spring width showed "70% becoming correct compared to 7% becoming incorrect" for physical training versus "48% improving compared to 7% deteriorating" for virtual training, both significant.

> "children trained using physical materials had a greater change, χ2(1, N = 46) = 24.03, p < .001, with 70% becoming correct compared to 7% becoming incorrect, in contrast to children trained using virtual materials, with 48% improving compared to 7% deteriorating"

### Triona 2003 (3)

Triona, L. M., & Klahr, D. (2003). Point and Click or Grab and Heft: Comparing the Influence of Physical and Virtual Instructional Materials on Elementary School Students' Ability to Design Experiments. Cognition and Instruction, 21(2), 149–173. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2102_02

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

For ramp ball type, a variable never tested as a target and always physical, "Children trained with virtual materials were more likely to change from incorrect to correct (33%)"; physical-trained children changed in both directions at similar rates.

> "Children trained with virtual materials were more likely to change from incorrect to correct (33%) than from correct to incorrect (7%), χ2(1, N = 92) = 8.00, p < .005, whereas children trained with physical materials were just as likely to change from incorrect to correct (28%) as from correct to incorrect (22%)."
