# Items are more likely to be learned in shorter lists: 75% correct in a five-item list versus 59% in a ten-item list

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` The same five items were recalled more often when presented as a five-item list than as the first five items of a ten-item list, undermining the Estes assumption that list length is irrelevant. [→ Underwood 1962](#underwood-1962)

## Evidence

### Underwood 1962

Underwood, B. J., & Keppel, G. (1962). One-Trial Learning? Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1, 1-13. https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/journal-of-verbal-learning-and-verbal-behavior

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r?`

Data from two conditions of a larger experiment by the senior author's students (Brown and Avard), using bigram stimuli and three-letter word responses with a single study and test trial; the authors cite it as evidence that the shorter the list, the greater the likelihood of correct recall.

> "What is the proportion correct for the five items common to both lists? The values were 75% for the five-item list and 59% for the ten-item list."
