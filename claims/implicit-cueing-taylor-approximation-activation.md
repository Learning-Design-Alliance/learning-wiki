# Upper-division physics students often fail to activate approximation methods under implicit cueing, while explicit cueing reliably triggers them

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` In context-laden problems with implicit cueing, 2 of 4 participants initially plugged in the given numeric value instead of approximating, and both later returned to add approximation methods after seeing formal math problems. [→ Caballero 2012](#caballero-2012)

## Evidence

### Caballero 2012

Caballero, M. D., Wilcox, B. R., Pollock, S. J., & Pepper, R. E. (2012). ACER: A Framework on the Use of Mathematics in Upper-division Physics. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.1063/1.4789659](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4789659)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Think-aloud interview study of eight physics, engineering physics, and astrophysics majors from first- and second-semester classical mechanics at CU Boulder. Under implicit cueing, "2 of 4 participants initially plugged in the given numeric value"; after formal math problems, both asked to return and include approximation methods.

> "However, when solving problems with implicit cueing, 2 of 4 participants initially plugged in the given numeric value (e.g.,φ = 0 in Fig. 3) to determine the approximate expression (e.g., U(φ) ≈ 0)."

### Caballero 2012 (2)

Caballero, M. D., Wilcox, B. R., Pollock, S. J., & Pepper, R. E. (2012). ACER: A Framework on the Use of Mathematics in Upper-division Physics. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.1063/1.4789659](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4789659)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

In the same think-aloud interview series (first study), formal math questions came first and context-laden physics questions with explicit cueing later; no participant failed to begin with a Taylor approximation when cues were explicit.

> "No participant in the explicit cueing study failed to start a problem with a Taylor approximation."
