## Evidence

### Paternotte E. 2016

Paternotte E., Scheele F., van Rossum T. R., Seeleman M. C. M., Scherpbier A. J. J. A., van Dulmen A. M. (2016). How do medical specialists value their own intercultural communication behaviour? A reflective practice study. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-016-0727-9

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r3`

Thematic network analysis of reflective practice interviews with 17 specialists; all participants named language differences as the main cause of problems, and a participant quote notes "when you communicate more simply, it is more difficult to express empathy".

> "All the participants mentioned language differences as the main cause of problems in an intercultural conversation. They experienced that the patient’s level of Dutch language proficiency determined the degree to which language was a barrier."

### Paternotte E. 2016 (2)

Paternotte E., Scheele F., van Rossum T. R., Seeleman M. C. M., Scherpbier A. J. J. A., van Dulmen A. M. (2016). How do medical specialists value their own intercultural communication behaviour? A reflective practice study. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-016-0727-9

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r3`

Same interview study; participants reported adapting pronunciation, articulation, speaking volume and pace, and using gestures such as "I also start to speak in broken Dutch" when consulting with non-native patients.

> "Participants explained that a language barrier made them adapt their communication style, for example the way they pronounced words, that they articulated more clearly, spoke more loudly or more slowly and used more non-verbal ways of communication, such as gestures."
