## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Descriptively, students answered CMC questions correctly 8.0 percentage points less often than non-CMC questions. [→ Nicholas T. Young 2023](#nicholas-t-young-2023)

## Evidence

### Nicholas T. Young 2023

Nicholas T. Young, Mark Mills, Rebecca L. Matz, Eric F. Bell, and Caitlin Hayward. (2023). Complex multiple-choice questions are inequitable for low-income and domestic students (and difficult for everyone). [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2306.11068](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2306.11068)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Descriptive analysis of Problem Roulette responses to Physics II questions. CMC accuracy was 42.0% versus 49.9% for non-CMC; the article reports "8.0 (6.2, 9.7) percentage points lower" for CMC questions. No standardized effect size is printed.

> "Students submitted 3,143 responses to CMC questions with an accuracy rate of 42.0% (95% confidence interval: 40.3%, 43.7%) compared to 72,401 responses to non-CMC questions with an accuracy rate of 49.9% (49.6%, 50.3%)."

### Nicholas T. Young 2023 (2)

Nicholas T. Young, Mark Mills, Rebecca L. Matz, Eric F. Bell, and Caitlin Hayward. (2023). Complex multiple-choice questions are inequitable for low-income and domestic students (and difficult for everyone). [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2306.11068](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2306.11068)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Series of seven simple mixed effects logistic regression models with term, question, and student random intercepts. Question type was significant in every model, with "an odds ratio ranging between 0.70 and 0.75" favoring non-CMC questions. No standardized effect size is printed.

> "In models 1-7, we see that question type is always significant with an odds ratio ranging between 0.70 and 0.75, meaning that the odds of a student answering a question correctly decrease by between 0.691 and 0.753 when the question is a CMC question instead of a non-CMC question."
