# Typewriting skill showed an average daily gain of eighty-five strokes on memory tests taken after a year and a half without practice, attributed to the disappearance of interfering associations rather than neural growth

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r1` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` After a rest interval of a year and a half with no typewriter seen, memory tests showed an average daily gain of eighty-five strokes over the last regular-practice tests. [→ Book 1908](#book-1908)
`q2 i?` The gain was determined to be due to the disappearance with time of interfering associations, leaving the established typewriting associations free to act, not to neural growth. [→ Book 1908](#book-1908)

## Evidence

### Book 1908

Book, William Frederick. (1908). The Role of the Teacher in the Most Expeditious and Economic Learning. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=The+Role+of+the+Teacher+in+the+Most+Expeditious+and+Economic+Learning

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Laboratory memory tests of one regular typewriting subject, ten tests four and a half months and ten more a year and a half after regular practice ceased, under uniform conditions. The tests "showed an average daily gain (deducting all errors) of eighty-five strokes."

> "But the second memory tests, taken after a rest interval of a year and a half, during which time a typewriter was not even seen, showed an average daily gain (deducting all errors) of eighty-five strokes."
