# Emotions aroused by pictures and literature are aesthetic 'pseudo-emotions' that differ from real emotions and are less fatiguing

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` An emotion aroused by a presented picture or literary situation is an aesthetic emotion, which Thorndike calls a pseudo-emotion, and does not cause the same strain as an actual emotion. [→ Henry 1916](#henry-1916)

## Evidence

### Henry 1916

Henry, T. S. (1916). The Education and Control of the Emotions. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://psycnet.apa.org/journals/edu/

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Theoretical argument, attributed to Thorndike, about what happens when art or literature arouses feeling in a child. The article stresses that the presented situation differs from the actual event, so the aroused state is an aesthetic emotion rather than the real one.

> "What we actually have in such a case is an aesthetic emotion—what Thorndike calls a "pseudo-emotion"—which does not cause the same strain as an actual emotion."

### Henry 1916 (2)

Henry, T. S. (1916). The Education and Control of the Emotions. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://psycnet.apa.org/journals/edu/

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r2`

Theoretical argument continuing the pseudo-emotion point: aesthetic emotions enrich life while costing less strain than real emotions. The article draws the practical corollary that real emotions should generally be aroused only for the sake of action.

> "But these emotions contribute much to the richness of life, and their reactions are not nearly so fatiguing as are those of the real emotions, which, in general, should be aroused only for the sake of action."
