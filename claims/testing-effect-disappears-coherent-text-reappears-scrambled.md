# Testing effect disappears for a coherent complex text but reappears, as reduced forgetting, when the same sentences are scrambled

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` With a coherent, high element interactivity science text, testing showed no benefit over restudy on the final test. [→ van Gog 2015](#van-gog-2015)
`q2 i?` When the same sentences were presented in scrambled order, reducing element interactivity, testing slowed the rate of forgetting compared to restudy. [→ van Gog 2015 (2)](#van-gog-2015-2)

## Evidence

### van Gog 2015

van Gog, T., & Sweller, J. (2015). Not New, but Nearly Forgotten: the Testing Effect Decreases or even Disappears as the Complexity of Learning Materials Increases. Educational Psychology Review, 27, 247–264. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review's account of De Jonge et al.'s experiment 1: university students studied a coherent 1070-word science text and then restudied or took a fill-in-the-gap test, with the final test administered immediately or "after 1 week. The results of experiment 1 showed no testing effect."

> "the other half completed the final test after 1 week. The results of experiment 1 showed no testing effect."

### van Gog 2015 (2)

van Gog, T., & Sweller, J. (2015). Not New, but Nearly Forgotten: the Testing Effect Decreases or even Disappears as the Complexity of Learning Materials Increases. Educational Psychology Review, 27, 247–264. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review's account of De Jonge et al.'s experiment 2, which used the same material with sentences in scrambled order: "a benefit of testing was found in experiment 2", but only as "testing slowed down the rate of forgetting compared to restudy".

> "with the sentences being learned in isolation, without reference to other elements of the text, a benefit of testing was found in experiment 2, albeit only in the sense that testing slowed down the rate of forgetting compared to restudy"
