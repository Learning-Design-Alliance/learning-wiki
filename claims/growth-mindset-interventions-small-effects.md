# Growth mindset interventions produce small positive effects on academic outcomes

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Brief interventions teaching learners that abilities are malleable can improve motivation and achievement, but meta-analytic evidence indicates the average effects on academic performance are small and highly conditional on context.

## Discussion

**Small average effects, heterogeneous conditions.** The claim is deliberately about magnitude: growth mindset interventions are not discredited, but their average effect on grades or test scores is small (typically around d ≈ 0.08–0.10 in large meta-analyses) [-W], with wide heterogeneity across studies. Effects tend to be larger for at-risk or low-achieving students [+M], in studies with higher design quality [+M], and when interventions change actual teaching practices rather than delivering a one-off message [+M].

**Moderators matter more than the mean.** A one-size-fits-all reading of the literature is misleading. The intervention appears to work by redirecting effort and persistence after setbacks, so it should benefit learners most when the learning environment actually rewards renewed effort — i.e., when tasks are challenging but attainable and feedback is actionable [~M]. In environments where effort cannot change outcomes, a mindset message alone is unlikely to help [-M]. This aligns with broader findings that [assessment for learning improves achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md): mindset messages presuppose an environment in which effort is informative and rewarded.

**Distal outcome, brief treatment.** Most interventions are short (a few sessions or less) targeting a distal outcome (semester grades), which caps the plausible effect size. Designers should treat mindset interventions as cheap, low-risk complements to — not substitutes for — instructional quality, feedback, and [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).

**Replication controversy.** Large-scale preregistered replications and national studies have found effects near zero on average [-W], sharpening the debate over publication bias in the original small-sample literature. The most defensible reading is that effects are real but rare and narrow: concentrated in struggling students in supportive environments, not general-purpose boosts.

**Open questions.** Long-term durability of effects, dose–response relationships, and whether effects compound when combined with [belonging interventions](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) remain actively debated. It is also unresolved whether mindset gains persist once the intervention context ends, or whether they require ongoing environmental support to maintain.
