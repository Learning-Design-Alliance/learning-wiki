# In a matched observational study within the CTA1 trial, being a high hint user is associated with posttest scores around 0.15 standard deviations lower

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Among CTA1 treatment-group students, high hint users (H=1) scored roughly 0.15 standard deviations lower on the algebra posttest than matched low hint users under three weighting schemes; the article prints weighted estimates but no standardized effect-size statistic, so no effect size is coded. [→ Adam C Sales and John F Pane 2018](#adam-c-sales-and-john-f-pane-2018)

## Evidence

### Adam C Sales and John F Pane 2018

Adam C Sales and John F Pane. (2018). Student Log-Data from a Randomized Evaluation of Educational Technology: A Causal Case Study. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1808.02528](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1808.02528)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Observational study (propensity score optimal full matching within schools) of 2,108 CTA1 treatment students; OLS, ATE, and TOT weighted estimates were -0.18, -0.12, and -0.14, and all three "agree that being a high hint user decreases posttest scores by around 0.15 standard deviations." No standardized effect-size statistic (d, g, r) is printed for this finding.

> "The three estimates all roughly agree that being a high hint user decreases posttest scores by around 0.15 standard deviations. That is, they suggest that requesting hints hurts posttest scores."
