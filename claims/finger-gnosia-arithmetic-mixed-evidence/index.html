q2 · i? · review · r3

Non-randomized controlled trial in which TD participants followed finger gnosia training based on fine motor activities; the review reports "a concurrent improvement in both finger gnosia and arithmetic skills specific to the training group." No effect size printed.

"In their results, Gracia-Bafalluy and Noël (2008) showed a concurrent improvement in both finger gnosia and arithmetic skills specific to the training group."

q2 · i? · review · r3

Two longitudinal studies of TD first-graders yielded contrasting results on the predictive value of finger gnosia for arithmetic one year later; the review describes Noël (2005) as "a medium effect size" without a numeric value, and Malone et al. (2020) as null for finger gnosia.

"The first showed that finger gnosia were a significant predictor of computation skills, with a medium effect size (Noël, 2005), while the second concluded that only number knowledge and numerosity discrimination were significant predictors of arithmetic development, but finger gnosia was not (Malone et al., 2020)."

q2 · i? · review · r3

Among nine cross-sectional studies, three found significant small-to-medium associations, Long et al. (2016) found "no evidence concerning the relation between finger gnosia and arithmetic skills after the effect of age was controlled," and Newman (2016) found the association only in older children.

"Long et al. (2016) concluded that there was no evidence concerning the relation between finger gnosia and arithmetic skills after the effect of age was controlled."
