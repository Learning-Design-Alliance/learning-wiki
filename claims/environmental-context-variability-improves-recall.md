# Studying a word list in two different rooms yields about 40% more recall than studying it twice in the same room when tested in a new room

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Subjects who studied the same list in two different rooms recalled an average of 40% more words than subjects who studied it twice in the same context, illustrating differential storage of environmental contextual components. [→ Glenberg 1979](#glenberg-1979)

## Evidence

### Glenberg 1979

Glenberg, A. M. (1979). Component-level theory of the effects of spacing of repetitions on recall and recognition. Memory & Cognition, 7(2), 95-112. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03197590

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

The review reports S. M. Smith et al. (1978): the same list was studied in two sessions 3 h apart, in the same room or two different rooms, with recall tested 3 h later in a room new to all subjects. The printed comparison is the 40% recall advantage; no standardized effect size is printed.

> "Those subjects who studied the list in two different rooms recalled an average of 40% more words than the subjects who studied the list of words twice in the same context."
