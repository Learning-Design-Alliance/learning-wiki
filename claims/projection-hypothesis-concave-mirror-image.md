# Students locate concave-mirror images on the mirror surface and hold a projection hypothesis that prevents distinguishing real from virtual images

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Even honour students had difficulties correctly describing image production on a concave mirror, often positioning the image on the mirror surface in drawings. [→ Martin E. Horn 2003](#martin-e-horn-2003)

## Evidence

### Martin E. Horn 2003

Martin E. Horn, Helmut F. Mikelskis. (2003). Student Conceptions of Holography. German Physical Society Spring Conference Bremen 2001. Originally published 2001. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.physics/0311108](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.physics/0311108)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r?`

Evaluation of test questions from the holography lesson series, including student sketches of image formation, found that "even honour students have difficulties"; drawings often positioned the image on the mirror surface.

> "An evaluation of the test questions indicated that even honour students have difficulties in correctly describing the production of an image on a concave mirror."

### Martin E. Horn 2003 (2)

Martin E. Horn, Helmut F. Mikelskis. (2003). Student Conceptions of Holography. German Physical Society Spring Conference Bremen 2001. Originally published 2001. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.physics/0311108](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.physics/0311108)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r?`

Analysis of student conceptions of space attributes to numerous students a projection hypothesis, reinforced by intuitively assumed absorption of light at the mirror surface, which "can prevent them from being able to distin- guish between real and virtual images".

> "This hypothesis of projection that numerous students have can prevent them from being able to distin- guish between real and virtual images as the func- tion of the surface of the concave mirror is associ- ated with that of a screen."
