# Dual construction: two simultaneous readouts of one situation — masses-and-motion versus forces-and-distance — yield conflicting answers about kinetic energy

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Students reasoning about identical constant forces on different masses used two arguments about kinetic energy, one from a masses-and-motion readout and one from a forces-and-distance readout. [→ Wittmann 2006](#wittmann-2006)
`q2 i?` The same compensation argument applied to both kinetic energy and momentum yields conclusions that cannot both be true, producing cognitive conflict between the dual constructions. [→ Wittmann 2006 (2)](#wittmann-2006-2)

## Evidence

### Wittmann 2006

Wittmann, M. C. (2006). Using resource graphs to represent conceptual change. Originally published 2005. [doi:10.1103/physrevstper.2.020105](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevstper.2.020105)

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

The article reports, citing University of Washington research on the work-energy and impulse-momentum theorems, that in interviews and written responses "students can use two arguments to consider changes in kinetic energy," based on different readouts of identical-force situations.

> "What we see is that students can use two arguments to consider changes in kinetic energy, one based on a readout of masses and motion, one based on a readout of forces and distance."

### Wittmann 2006 (2)

Wittmann, M. C. (2006). Using resource graphs to represent conceptual change. Originally published 2005. [doi:10.1103/physrevstper.2.020105](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevstper.2.020105)

`q2 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

In the same UW situations, compensation arguments applied to both kinetic energy and momentum yield inconsistent conclusions: "Both answers cannot be true," which the researchers used to create cognitive conflict between the dual constructions.

> "The same compensation argument might lead to the conclusion that the final momenta are also the same. Both answers cannot be true."
