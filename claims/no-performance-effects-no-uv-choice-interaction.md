# Neither choice nor utility value manipulations improve end-of-session regression test performance, and the two manipulations do not interact

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` There was no significant effect of the utility value manipulation on performance (d = 0.03, p = .463) nor of the choice manipulation (d = 0.04, p = .458). [→ Asher 2025](#asher-2025)

## Evidence

### Asher 2025

Asher, M. W., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2025). Using Choice and Utility Value to Promote Interest: Stimulating Situational Interest in a Lesson and Fostering the Development of Interest in Statistics. Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000921

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r?`

Meta-regression across Studies 1–7 on the twelve-minute timed regression test found no significant effect of either manipulation on performance; neither manipulation interacted with baseline confidence or interest (ps ≥ .523).

> "Relative to control, there was no significant effect of the directly-communicated utility value manipulation on performance, d = 0.03, p = .463, nor was there an effect of the choice manipulation, d = 0.04, p = .458."

### Asher 2025 (2)

Asher, M. W., & Harackiewicz, J. M. (2025). Using Choice and Utility Value to Promote Interest: Stimulating Situational Interest in a Lesson and Fostering the Development of Interest in Statistics. Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000921

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r?`

Meta-analyzed D-UV × Choice interaction coefficients from Studies 3 and 4 (combined N = 773) showed no significant interactions for any outcome, consistent with additive rather than redundant combined effects.

> "For all outcomes, there were no significant interactions between the two manipulations, ps ≥ .382."
