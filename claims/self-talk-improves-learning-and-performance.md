# Self Talk Improves Learning And Performance

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Instructional or motivational self-talk — verbalizing task-relevant cues or encouragement to oneself — can improve attention, technique, and performance on learning and motor tasks.

## Discussion

Self-talk research distinguishes two broad forms with different mechanisms. **Instructional self-talk** (cue words or phrases describing the action, e.g., "elbow up" in basketball shooting) is thought to direct attention to task-relevant features and support skill acquisition, and is generally most useful during early learning when learners must consciously control movement or procedure. **Motivational self-talk** ("I can do this") is thought to sustain effort, confidence, and persistence, and tends to matter more in endurance, anxiety-provoking, or high-pressure situations.

Boundary conditions follow from this distinction. Overly verbose self-talk can itself consume working-memory resources, so cue words should be short and task-relevant — consistent with [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and the broader finding that [cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md). As skill develops, instructional self-talk may become redundant or even disruptive (an expertise-reversal-like pattern, cf. [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)), because it forces conscious control over actions that are becoming automatic. Negative or self-critical self-talk is associated with worse performance and higher anxiety, so the valence of the internal dialogue matters as much as its presence.

Open questions include how durable the effects are beyond immediate performance, how self-talk interacts with [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) strategies more broadly, and whether effects transfer from motor tasks (where most evidence originates) to complex academic learning. Until controlled studies are added to the Evidence section, this claim should be treated as a plausible but weakly documented design heuristic rather than an established effect.
