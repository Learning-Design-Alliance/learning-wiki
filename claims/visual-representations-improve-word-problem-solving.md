# Students using visual representations (cards or computer) solved a logic word problem at higher rates than students using the traditional paper-and-pencil method.

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Group II (cards) achieved a 100% solution rate and Group III (computer) 64%, versus 62.5% for the traditional paper-and-pencil group. [→ Debrenti 2015](#debrenti-2015)
`q2 i?` Students using physical cards were more persevering and motivated, with no one giving up, unlike the traditional and computer groups. [→ Debrenti 2015 (2)](#debrenti-2015-2)

## Evidence

### Debrenti 2015

Debrenti, E. (2015). Visual Representations in Mathematics Teaching: An Experiment with Students. Acta Didactica Napocensia, 8(1). https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1064387

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Quasi-experiment with 57 economics freshmen randomly assigned to three groups solving the Einstein puzzle. The card group solved at 100%, computer group at 64%, paper group at 62.5%. No inferential statistics reported; only descriptive proportions.

> "Students who worked with representations (group II and III) achieved better results. They solved the problem in a proportion of 100% and 64% respectively. Students who used the traditional method, working on paper managed to solve the problem in a proportion of only 62,5%."

### Debrenti 2015 (2)

Debrenti, E. (2015). Visual Representations in Mathematics Teaching: An Experiment with Students. Acta Didactica Napocensia, 8(1). https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1064387

`q1 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Author's observational interpretation from post-task interviews and observation of the card group; no systematic measure of perseverance was reported.

> "What we found surprising was that students from group II, who used the cards, were more persevering, their motivation lasted longer, they didn’t give up. They often restarted the exercise, nobody felt frustrated"
