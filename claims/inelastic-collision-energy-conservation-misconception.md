## Evidence

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2010

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2010). Using Analogy to Solve a Three-Step Physics Problem. PERC Proceedings. [doi:10.1063/1.3515228](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3515228)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Error analysis of comparison-group work on the putty problem identified omitting CM as the most common unscaffolded mistake; students related the initial potential energy of putty A directly to the final potential energy of both putties without considering the intermediate collision process.

> "We found that forgetting to invoke the principle of CM is students’ most common mistake on the putty problem when no scaffolding was provided."

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2010 (2)

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2010). Using Analogy to Solve a Three-Step Physics Problem. PERC Proceedings. [doi:10.1063/1.3515228](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3515228)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Table 4 catalogs common mistakes, including answers like mAgho=(mA+mB)ghf that skip the collision. Such mixing-of-processes mistakes were, per the article, more commonly found in the algebra-based course than in the calculus-based course.

> "They didn’t notice that there was an inelastic collision process involved and only the momentum, not the mechanical energy, is conserved during an inelastic collision. Moreover, students incorrectly combined the various sub-problems into one without systematically approaching different parts."
