## Evidence

### Andreas Lieberoth 2014

Andreas Lieberoth, Mads Kock Pedersen, Andreea Catalina Marin, Tilo Planke, Jacob Friis Sherson. (2014). Getting Humans to do Quantum Optimization - User Acquisition, Engagement and Early Results from the Citizen Cyberscience Game Quantum Moves. Human Computation. https://doi.org/10.15346/hc.v1i2.11

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

One-way ANOVAs on play counts per active day by physics interest, gender, and physics education beyond 9th grade in the beta cohort. Post-hoc comparisons showed "high interest in physics leads to significantly more play that low and middle interest (both p<0.001**)", and the 0-3 years group played much more than the 4-6 and 7-10 year groups.

> "The one -way ANOVA shows significant differences for both physics interest (F(2,873)=11.45, p<0.001**), gender (F(1,582)=40.37, p<0.001**) and physics education (F(2,873)=10.87, p<0.001**)."

### Andreas Lieberoth 2014 (2)

Andreas Lieberoth, Mads Kock Pedersen, Andreea Catalina Marin, Tilo Planke, Jacob Friis Sherson. (2014). Getting Humans to do Quantum Optimization - User Acquisition, Engagement and Early Results from the Citizen Cyberscience Game Quantum Moves. Human Computation. https://doi.org/10.15346/hc.v1i2.11

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

One-way ANOVA on average tutorial-level scores found no significant effect of physics interest (F(2,671)=0.76, p=0.469) or education (F(2,671)=0.99, p=0.370). The article concludes the game framing speaks to people with a notable casual interest in physics, but not a too professional background.

> "No significant effect on tutorial scores was foun d for interest (F(2,671)=0.76, p=0.469), nor education F(2,671)=0.99, p=0.370), but there was a significant difference between the genders"
