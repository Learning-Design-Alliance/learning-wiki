## Evidence

### Masel J. 2015

Masel J., Humphrey P. T., Blackburn B., Levine J. A. (2015). Evidence-Based Medicine as a Tool for Undergraduate Probability and Statistics Education. CBE—Life Sciences Education 14(4). https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.15-04-0079

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r1`

Correlational analysis of posttest QRQ against course assessments in the Spring 2014 course; the article reports Pearson's r = 0.5 with course grades and the final exam and r = 0.38 with homework problem sets, which the authors read as alignment with QRQ objectives.

> "posttest QRQ correlations (Pearson’s r) with both course grades as a whole and with our final closed-book exam (included in the Supplemental Materials) were high at 0.5, and even correlations on more effort-based items such as homework problem sets (as found in the Supplemental Materials) were 0.38."

### Masel J. 2015 (2)

Masel J., Humphrey P. T., Blackburn B., Levine J. A. (2015). Evidence-Based Medicine as a Tool for Undergraduate Probability and Statistics Education. CBE—Life Sciences Education 14(4). https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.15-04-0079

`q3 · i?` · `design · r2`

Correlational analysis of gain scores against pretest scores; the article reports Pearson's r = –0.18 (ATS) and –0.19 (QRQ), both p > 0.35, indicating initially stronger students were not systematically more or less likely to benefit.

> "Changes in attitudes and quantitative reasoning reflected in the ATS and QRQ were not significantly correlated with pretest scores (Pearson’s r = –0.18 for ATS; –0.19 for QRQ; both p > 0.35)."
