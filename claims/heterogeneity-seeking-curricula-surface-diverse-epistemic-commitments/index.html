Heterogeneity-seeking modeling curricula surface epistemic commitments in most students, including care-based commitments absent from prior frameworks¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · design r3 · q2 · n=105 sixth graders across 6 implementations (33 focal students interviewed), one US public suburban middle school, 2018-2020

Interviews with 33 focal sixth-graders in a heterogeneity-seeking ecosystems modeling unit found that most students expressed one or more of four epistemic commitments (real-world connection, seeing/showing, controlling variables, caring for), including a "caring for" commitment with no direct analog in prior epistemic-cognition research, and that these commitments shaped model design decisions in ways the curriculum had not anticipated.

A student's caring commitment toward computational-model organisms directly altered a modeling decision: after running a simulation with 31 guppies, she said "that's how it's gonna look—it's gonna be so lonely" and changed the model to 1 guppy, generating a modeling question (guppy social behavior) that was not part of the curriculum's intended learning objective (energy flow). → Pierson et al. 2023

Students sometimes held commitments in unresolved tension without explicitly reconciling them: one student who framed a physical-model manipulation in terms of experimental control ("see how much oxygen the guppy needs before it gets sick") also removed a guppy from a compromised biosphere to protect it, saying euthanizing it for the sake of the experiment "would not really" be okay — illustrating commitments as context-activated resources rather than a fixed hierarchy. → Pierson et al. 2023

Pierson et al. 2023¶

Pierson, A. E., Brady, C. E., Clark, D. B., & Sengupta, P. (2023). Students' Epistemic Commitments in a Heterogeneity-Seeking Modeling Curriculum. Cognition and Instruction, 41(2), 125-157. https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2022.2111431

Thirty-three focal sixth-grade students, drawn from six iterations of a 9-week Heterogeneity-Seeking Modeling Curriculum unit on ecosystem energy flow, were interviewed for 25 minutes each in the unit's final week about how they valued and related different model types (physical biosphere jars, computational simulations, food-web diagrams, embodied role-play, plant observations). Inductive coding of interviews, triangulated with classroom video, identified four recurring epistemic commitments — extending Epistemic Cognition and the AIR model of epistemic cognition (Chinn et al.) by centering student-originated values, including a care-based commitment with no prior analog in the epistemic-cognition literature.

Discussion¶

This is a qualitative, non-experimental design study without a comparison condition (e.g., a matched convergence-seeking curriculum), so the claim that heterogeneity-seeking design specifically causes commitments to surface (rather than merely making them visible) cannot be isolated here. Commitment frequencies are based on self-report interviews conducted at the end of the unit, which may not fully capture commitments that shaped earlier decisions but were not salient enough to mention retrospectively. The claim is about which values students expressed and how they used them, not about a measured content-learning outcome from the curriculum.
