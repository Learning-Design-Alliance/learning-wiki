# Interesting but irrelevant details can impair comprehension and transfer, with effects that vary with the material and the learner

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 studies · 5 causal `r2`, 1 quant-synthesis `r?` · `q2`–`q3` · `i1` small
<!-- deprecated title (2026-09-30, overstated its evidence): Interesting but irrelevant details impair learning -->

Interesting but irrelevant text, pictures, or audio added to instructional materials can impair learning, usually explained as diverting attention and working-memory resources away from the essential content. This is the "seductive details effect" — the general learning-science formulation of what multimedia researchers call the coherence principle. Six studies are recorded: a meta-analysis that finds the effect depends on design features; three earlier experiments (Garner et al. 1992, Harp & Mayer 1998, Sanchez & Wiley 2006) that found poorer recall of main ideas or poorer transfer, the last mainly in readers with lower working-memory capacity; and two decorative-picture studies, one finding no overall harm (Lenzner et al. 2013) and one a small harm (η² = .04) that, on harder texts, appeared only in students with low attentional inhibition (González et al. 2019). Only that one study prints an effect size.

## Evidence

### Sundararajan & Adesope 2020

Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020). Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(3), 707–734. [doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i? · no pooled effect size in the abstract` · `n=not stated in abstract` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

This is a meta-analysis of experiments comparing learning materials with and without seductive details, meaning interesting but irrelevant text or images. It concludes that adding such details can hinder learning. The size of the effect depended on how the seductive details were presented, the type of image used for comparison, the delivery format, language, subject, learner pacing, the type of recall question and how the manipulation was checked. It covers seductive details in general, not only decorative illustrations. Only the abstract was read, so no pooled estimate or study count is recorded here.

### Lenzner et al. 2013

Lenzner, A., Schnotz, W., & Müller, A. (2013). The role of decorative pictures in learning. *Instructional Science, 41*(5), 811–831. [doi:10.1007/s11251-012-9256-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9256-z)

`q3 · three peer-reviewed experiments (incl. eye tracking)` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract` · `n=7th and 8th graders, three experiments (sample sizes not in abstract)` · `causal · r?`

Three experiments with 7th and 8th grade students compared decorative pictures (chosen mainly to look appealing) with [instructional pictures](../elements/demonstration.md) (chosen to inform). Eye tracking showed that learners looked at decorative pictures briefly at first and then mostly ignored them. The pictures improved mood, alertness and calmness and made the material feel less difficult, but they did not raise situational interest. For learning, decorative pictures were neither harmful nor helpful overall. They did, however, reduce the benefit of instructional pictures (the multimedia effect), most strongly for learners with lower prior knowledge. This supports the "does not improve" claim and argues against a blanket "harms" version.

### González et al. 2019

González, F. M., Saux, G., & Burin, D. (2019). The decorative images' seductive effect in e-learning depends on attentional inhibition. *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology, 35*(3). [doi:10.14742/ajet.4577](https://doi.org/10.14742/ajet.4577)

`q3 · within-subjects counterbalanced experiments` · `i1 · small effect, η²=.04 (Study 1)` · `n=126 (Study 1); 65 (Study 2)` · `causal · r2`

In Study 1, 126 first-year undergraduates in a Moodle course read texts with and without decorative, irrelevant images. Each student saw both conditions, in counterbalanced order. Comprehension was a little lower with the images (M = 9.24 vs 9.54; F(1,123) = 4.25, p = .041, η² = .04), and the authors call the effect "very small in magnitude". In Study 2, 65 undergraduates read harder texts spread across several screens. The images had no overall effect (F(1,53) = 0.02), but they interacted with attentional inhibitory control (F(1,53) = 9.18, η² = .15): students less able to ignore irrelevant visual input understood less when the images were present.

### Garner et al. 1992

Garner, R., Gillingham, M. G., & White, C. S. (1992). Effects of "seductive details" on macroprocessing and microprocessing in adults and children. *Cognition and Instruction, 9*(4), 359-373.

`q2 · experimental study with children and adult readers · i? · no source text available to check; the entry prints no effect size · n=moderate (multiple age-group samples)` · `causal · r?`

Readers given passages with vivid but tangential added details recalled those seductive details disproportionately well, but recalled and understood the passage's actual main ideas worse than readers given the same passage without the added details — an early demonstration that interest-arousing content can compete with, rather than support, comprehension of the material it was meant to enliven.

### Harp and Mayer 1998

Harp, S. F., & Mayer, R. E. (1998). How seductive details do their damage: A theory of cognitive interest in science learning. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 90*(3), 414-434. [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414)

`q3 · peer-reviewed experimental study (multiple experiments) · i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may · n=moderate (college student samples across experiments)` · `causal · r?`

Across several experiments on science-text passages (e.g., explaining lightning formation), adding interesting-but-irrelevant details reduced participants' performance on later problem-solving and transfer tests of the passage's causal content, compared to a version without the added details — evidence the effect isn't just weaker recall of trivia-adjacent facts, but genuinely worse understanding of underlying mechanisms.

### Sanchez and Wiley 2006

Sanchez, C. A., & Wiley, J. (2006). An examination of the seductive details effect in terms of working memory capacity. *Memory & Cognition, 34*(2), 344-355.

`q3 · peer-reviewed experimental study measuring individual differences · i? · no source text available to check; the entry prints no effect size · n=moderate (college student sample)` · `causal · r?`

This study found that the seductive details effect was not uniform: readers with lower working memory capacity were substantially more susceptible to distraction and misunderstanding from added irrelevant-but-interesting content than higher-capacity readers, suggesting the effect is at least partly a working-memory-capacity phenomenon rather than a universal property of all learners.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The effect is usually explained through [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md): seductive details consume limited working-memory capacity and induce attentional split between essential and incidental material, producing the same overload documented in [cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]. They are closely related to the [coherence principle](coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+M], which generalizes the finding to irrelevant words, sounds, and graphics in multimedia design.

**Moderators.** The harm is largest for novices and for learners with lower working-memory capacity, because experts can more easily filter irrelevant material — consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Highly interesting details that are *integrated* with the to-be-learned content (rather than merely entertaining) are less damaging [~W], and some learners may benefit emotionally from engaging materials when the details support, rather than compete with, the learning goal [~W].

**Design implications.** Practitioners should audit materials for anecdotes, decorative images, and tangential facts that do not serve the stated learning objective, and cut or relocate them — the same discipline that [chunking reduces working-memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M] applies to managing capacity. Where engagement is the goal, prefer details that elaborate the core content (analogies, applications, worked contexts) over details that merely entertain.

**Constraints.** Removing all engaging material is not automatically beneficial. Materials stripped to bare essentials can reduce motivation and engagement, particularly for younger or less self-regulated learners [~W]. The effect also weakens when learners are given advance organizers or signaling that distinguishes essential from incidental content [~W], and it may reverse for experts, for whom the essential content is already well represented and added elaboration can support schema building [~M].

**Open questions.** The boundary between "irrelevant but motivating" and "irrelevant and distracting" remains contested, and effect sizes vary with how interest is measured. Six studies are recorded, but only González et al. (2019) prints an effect size, and the meta-analysis was read as an abstract with no pooled estimate, so how large the effect is, and for whom, is still not settled here. <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale beside six recorded entries): Replication evidence is still needed before strong design prescriptions can be made with confidence. -->

*Merged from “Seductive Details Harm Learning” (seductive-details-harm-learning):* **Mechanism.** The standard explanation is attentional and cognitive: seductive details are designed to be vivid, emotional, or novel, so they capture attention and consume limited working-memory capacity that would otherwise be spent on essential processing. This places the claim squarely within [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and connects it to the broader finding that [cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md). The effect is conceptually distinct from the [Von Restorff effect on text marking](../theories/von-restorff-effect-text-marking.md): salience helps when it isolates *relevant* content, but harms learning when it highlights *irrelevant* content.

**Relationship to the coherence principle.** This claim is the concrete instance of the more general [coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning](coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md). Designers should treat the two pages together — seductive details are the most common and most tempting violation of coherence, precisely because the added material feels motivating and enriching.

**Why designers add them anyway.** Seductive details are usually added with good intentions: to spark interest, motivate reluctant learners, or make material feel relevant. The claim does not deny that interest and enjoyment may rise; it asserts that these gains do not translate into better learning of the target content, and may come at its expense. Whether well-designed *relevant* elaborations can deliver motivational benefits without the learning cost is an open question — see [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and [Advance organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) for relevant-but-structured alternatives.

**Boundary conditions to establish.** Moderators such as learner expertise, prior knowledge, the emotional vs. merely decorative character of the details, and their placement (adjacent to vs. distant from core content) are only partly documented by the evidence recorded above.

**Constraints on generalization.** The following conditions under which the effect may weaken or reverse are hypotheses, not findings of the evidence recorded above:

- Learners with high prior knowledge may be better able to ignore or quickly triage irrelevant material, reducing the load cost [~W] — consistent with the expertise-reversal pattern described in [Expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md).
- Details that are *relevant* elaborations of core content (analogies, worked examples, advance organizers) fall outside the definition of seductive details and may support learning; the harm applies only to material irrelevant to the learning goal [-W].
- Measures of enjoyment or situational interest may improve even when retention and transfer do not [~W] — so designers must be explicit about which outcome they are optimizing.
- Materials that are already high in intrinsic load leave less spare capacity for irrelevant processing, so the harm should be largest for complex, novice-facing content and smallest for simple or well-practiced material [~W] — see [Cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md).
- When the "irrelevant" material is processed before instruction as advance activation rather than interleaved with it, it may function as prior-knowledge activation rather than as a seductive detail [~W] — see [Activation improves learning](activation-improves-learning.md).

**Design guidance.** The practical rule is conservative: audit every text passage, image, animation, and audio track for relevance to the stated learning goal, and cut or relocate anything whose primary function is entertainment. If motivational content is required, place it before or after the core instruction rather than interleaved with it, so it cannot compete for processing capacity during essential explanation. This pairs naturally with [cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md) and the broader family of load-reduction interventions: the goal is to spend capacity on essential processing, not to decorate it. Where capacity cannot be freed by cutting material, [chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) offers the complementary strategy of structuring what remains.

**Open questions.** Key issues the recorded entries leave unresolved: (1) the size of the effect across retention vs. transfer measures; (2) whether emotional/narrative details harm more than merely decorative ones; (3) whether seductive details placed at the *start* of a lesson (as a hook) differ from those interleaved with core explanation; and (4) how the effect interacts with learner self-regulation — see [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — since learners who can strategically ignore irrelevant material may be partially protected.

*Merged from “Interesting but irrelevant details added to a lesson can distract from learning the material they were meant to enliven” (seductive-details-distract-from-learning):* The practical tension this claim describes sits directly alongside [interest-arousing instructional tactics](../theories/four-phase-interest-development.md) — the same category of move (a surprising fact, an eye-catching image, an anecdote) that triggers situational interest can backfire into seductive-detail interference if it doesn't genuinely connect to the target content. The individual-differences finding (Sanchez & Wiley, 2006) sharpens the practical guidance: struggling or lower-working-memory-capacity students are the ones most at risk from poorly-integrated "interesting" additions, which argues against treating decorative engagement tactics as a uniformly safe default, especially for students already struggling. The most defensible practical stance is to treat any interest-arousing addition as an empirical bet, not a guaranteed improvement — checking whether students appear to be learning *because of* an added feature, or *in spite of* it.
