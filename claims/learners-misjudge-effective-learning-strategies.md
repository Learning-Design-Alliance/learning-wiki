# Learners misjudge which learning strategies are effective

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q2` quasi-experiment

Learners systematically overestimate the effectiveness of low-effort, fluency-based strategies (rereading, highlighting) and underestimate high-effort, generative strategies (retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving). This claim concerns metacognitive monitoring and strategy selection, not the effectiveness of the strategies themselves — those are covered in their own claim pages.

## Evidence

### Karpicke Butler Roediger 2009

Karpicke, J. D., Butler, A. C., & Roediger, H. L. (2009). Metacognitive strategies in student learning: Do students practise retrieval when they study on their own? *Memory, 17*(4), 471–479. [doi:10.1080/09658210802647009](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210802647009)

`q2 · survey study (open-ended and forced-choice questionnaires)` · `i? · no effect size reported (percentages of strategy endorsement, not a standardized effect)` · `n=177 college students (Question 1); 101 and 76 students respectively on the two versions of Question 2`

177 Washington University undergraduates were surveyed about their real-world study habits. On an open-ended free-report question, 83.6% listed rereading notes or a textbook as a strategy they use and 54.8% ranked it their #1 strategy, while only 10.7% (19 of 177) reported practising retrieval (self-testing) at all, and just 1.1% ranked it #1. On a forced-choice question asking whether they would reread, self-test, or do something else after reading a textbook chapter once, 57% chose to restudy and only 18% chose to self-test; of those who did choose self-testing, most explained they did it to check what they had learned rather than because testing itself produces better learning. The authors conclude students "lack metacognitive awareness of the testing effect."

### Bjork Dunlosky Kornell 2013

Bjork, R. A., Dunlosky, J., & Kornell, N. (2013). Self-regulated learning: Beliefs, techniques, and illusions. *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*(1), 417–444. [doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823)

`q2 · narrative review of survey and laboratory studies` · `i? · no single effect size reported (synthesizes multiple studies' correlational and survey findings)` · `n=large (aggregated across the reviewed surveys and experiments, e.g. Kornell & Bjork 2007's 472 students and Karpicke et al. 2009's 177 students)`

This Annual Review of Psychology article synthesizes survey and experimental work on what students believe about how to study and how those beliefs relate to actual learning. It reports that when Kornell & Bjork (2007) asked 472 UCLA students why they used self-testing, only 18% said it was because they learn more from testing than rereading, versus about 70% who said they use it to check what they already know. The review states directly that "most students believe that rereading is a more effective strategy than is self-testing (McCabe 2011)," and frames this fluency-driven misjudgment, together with the dissociation between subjective ease and later retention, as a central reason self-regulated study choices go wrong.

## Discussion

**The fluency illusion.** Learners judge learning by how easy and familiar material feels during study, not by how well it will be retrieved later. Rereading and highlighting make text feel fluent, producing high confidence and poor retention; retrieval practice feels effortful and error-prone, producing low confidence and strong retention. This dissociation between perceived and actual learning is the core reason learners misjudge strategy effectiveness.

**Consequences for self-regulation.** Because strategy selection typically rests on these faulty judgments, learners who believe they are studying effectively persist with ineffective methods. This undermines [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): monitoring is inaccurate, so regulation decisions built on it are also inaccurate. Teaching learners to base judgments on retrieval performance rather than fluency is a promising corrective, but the evidence for specific calibration interventions still needs to be added to this page.

**Boundary conditions.** Misjudgment is strongest for novices, who lack the knowledge to evaluate their comprehension accurately; experts' judgments are better calibrated in their domain. Judgments made after a delay are more accurate than immediate judgments, which partly explains why cramming feels effective — the fluency is real at the moment of study but does not survive the delay to the test.

**Design implications.** If learners cannot be trusted to select strategies on their own, designers and instructors should default to effective structures — [retrieval practice](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md), [spaced practice](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md), and [interleaving](interleaving-improves-transfer.md) — rather than offering open study-strategy choice. Where choice is offered, it should be paired with strategy instruction that explicitly contrasts the felt ease of a strategy with its measured payoff, and with [assessment for learning](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) structures that give learners accurate feedback on what they actually know.

**Open questions.** The evidence recorded above shows what students prefer (rereading over self-testing) rather than measuring the judgment–performance gap directly; judgments-of-learning experiments that do measure it are recorded on the spacing and fluency claim pages.
