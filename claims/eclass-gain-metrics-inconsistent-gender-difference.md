## Evidence

### Bethany R. Wilcox and H. J. Lewandowski 2016

Bethany R. Wilcox and H. J. Lewandowski. (2016). Research-based assessment of students’ beliefs about experimental physics: When is gender a factor?. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.12.020130](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.12.020130)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Comparison of four gain metrics (normalized change, Hake gain, average absolute gain, percent increase over pretest) on the aggregate matched data found gain was "small, but statistically signiﬁcant" in all cases, with magnitude and sign depending on the metric.

> "In all cases, the magnitude of the gain was small, but statistically signiﬁcant; however, both the magnitude and sign of the gain depended on the metric being used."

### Bethany R. Wilcox and H. J. Lewandowski 2016 (2)

Bethany R. Wilcox and H. J. Lewandowski. (2016). Research-based assessment of students’ beliefs about experimental physics: When is gender a factor?. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.12.020130](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.12.020130)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r3`

Gender comparison of gains across the four metrics showed "Average normalized change was also the only metric to result in a statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerence between the gains of men and women (independent sample t-test, p ≪ 0.01)".

> "Average normalized change was also the only metric to result in a statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerence between the gains of men and women (independent sample t-test, p ≪ 0.01)."
