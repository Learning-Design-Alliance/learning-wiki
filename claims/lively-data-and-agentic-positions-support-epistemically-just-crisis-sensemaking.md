# Agentic engagement with lively data supports epistemically just crisis sensemaking

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q2` quasi-experiment · `i1` small · n=4 focal case studies (Wael, Rama, Qassem, Dima), drawn from a larger pool of 19 Palestinian-Arab minority youth in Israel within a broader 51-youth/45-adult multi-country project

When official/institutional data about a crisis is incomplete, delayed, or inaccessible to a minoritized community, youth who take up active roles with data — researching, analyzing, authoring, and communicating it, rather than only receiving it — can use their own embodied and community experience as a legitimate epistemic resource, and can surface gaps and contradictions in dominant narratives that comparing across local, national, and global scales makes visible.

## Subclaims
`q2 i1` Minoritized youth took up four [agentic data-engagement positions](../elements/agentic-data-engagement-positions.md) — active researcher/consumer, analyst, author, and socio-scientific communicator — to fill knowledge gaps left by official sources that were delayed, incomplete, or not translated into their first language. [→ Sedawi & Calabrese Barton 2024](#sedawi-calabrese-barton-2024)

`q2 i1` Youths' sensory and embodied first-hand experience (e.g., a family member's symptoms that official sources had not yet described) functioned as a legitimate epistemic resource that let them critique and supplement, not just emotionally react to, official/scientific narratives. [→ Sedawi & Calabrese Barton 2024](#sedawi-calabrese-barton-2024)

`q2 i1` Explicitly comparing and negotiating between local, national, and global data ("multi-scalar dialectics") let youth notice contradictions dominant narratives missed — e.g., rising local infection rates among vaccinated community members despite national vaccine-effectiveness claims — producing counter-narratives grounded in but not limited to official data. [→ Sedawi & Calabrese Barton 2024](#sedawi-calabrese-barton-2024)

## Evidence

### Sedawi & Calabrese Barton 2024

Sedawi, W., & Calabrese Barton, A. (2024). Toward epistemic justice in socio-scientific decision-making: How youth make sense of lively COVID-19 and vaccines data. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 33*(4-5), 667-718. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2024.2381205](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2024.2381205)

`q2 · critical grounded theory / relational ethnography, longitudinal case studies with counter-narrative analysis, no comparison group` · `i1 · qualitative thematic findings describing shifts in sensemaking and decision-making, not a quantified outcome measure` · `n=4 focal case studies (Wael, Rama, Qassem, Dima), drawn from a larger pool of 19 Palestinian-Arab minority youth in Israel within a broader 51-youth/45-adult multi-country project`

Over three to four interview time points between April 2020 and November 2021 (semi-structured interviews plus bi-weekly experience-sampling), the researchers traced how four Palestinian-Arab minority youth in Israel made sense of and made decisions about COVID-19 and vaccination as the pandemic unfolded. Israeli Ministry of Health guidance was published almost exclusively in Hebrew rather than Arabic, and the state did not publish disaggregated COVID-19 data for this minority, leaving youth to reason from a mix of official sources, social media, and their own and their community's direct experience (e.g., family members' infections, vaccine side effects, local case counts). Youth used this direct experience not merely as emotional reaction but as evidence — for instance, one participant who developed depressive symptoms after infection explicitly noted that "[symptoms of depression] were not mentioned by the experts," using her own case to supplement the official clinical picture. Across cases, youth also drew on data at multiple scales (family/community, national, global) simultaneously, which surfaced tensions — such as rising infections among vaccinated community members even as national data reported high vaccine effectiveness — that a single-scale reading of the data would have missed.

## Discussion
This is a qualitative, single-study, small-sample case analysis (4 focal cases, though drawn from a larger 19-youth dataset) with no comparison group and no quantified outcome measure, so the claim should be read as descriptive theory-building — showing that this kind of epistemically-just sensemaking is possible and coherent — rather than as evidence that scaffolding these agentic positions causes better decisions or outcomes. The youth were also not in a designed instructional intervention; the study documents naturally occurring sensemaking during a real crisis, so it is a proof-of-concept for the underlying [epistemic injustice](../theories/epistemic-injustice.md) framework rather than a test of a specific instructional design. Whether deliberately designing instruction to scaffold these four positions (rather than observing them emerge organically) would reliably produce the same benefits is untested.
