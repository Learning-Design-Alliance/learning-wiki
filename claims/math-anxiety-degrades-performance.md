# Math Anxiety Degrades Performance

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i2` medium · n=332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) from 223 studies

Math anxiety — feelings of tension, apprehension, or dread when manipulating numbers or solving mathematical problems — impairs mathematical performance, primarily by consuming working memory resources needed for computation and reasoning. The impairment is situational rather than uniform: it emerges most strongly when evaluative pressure or high working memory demands collide with anxiety-generated load [~M].

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` A meta-analysis of 223 studies (747 effect sizes, 332 independent samples, ~385,441 participants, 1992–2018) finds a significant small-to-moderate negative correlation between math anxiety and math achievement (r = −.28), significant across nearly every moderator subgroup examined. ## Evidence

### Barroso et al. 2021

Barroso, C., Ganley, C. M., McGraw, A. L., Geer, E. A., Hart, S. A., & Daucourt, M. C. (2021). A meta-analysis of the relation between math anxiety and math achievement. *Psychological Bulletin, 147*(2), 134–168. [doi:10.1037/bul0000307](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000307)

`q4 · well-powered meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, r=-.28` · `n=332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) from 223 studies`

Multilevel meta-analysis of 223 studies published 1992–2018, modeling 747 correlation coefficients from 332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) to account for non-independence among effect sizes from the same sample. The pooled correlation between math anxiety and math achievement was r = −.28 (small-to-moderate, negative, statistically significant), and the relation held for nearly every moderator subgroup tested (grade level, math-ability level, adolescent/adult anxiety scales, math topic of the anxiety scale, and assessment type), with the sole exception of anxiety and approximate-number-system tasks. Grade level, ability level, and scale/assessment type were significant moderators of the size of the relation, and effect sizes were somewhat weaker in grades 3–5 and postsecondary samples than at other ages.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The dominant account is processing-efficiency: anxious thoughts (worry, rumination) act as a secondary task that occupies the same working memory resources mathematics demands, so performance drops most on problems with high working memory demands [~S]. This places math anxiety in the same family as other load-inducing impairments — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) and [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md). Within [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md), anxiety functions as an additional source of extraneous load rather than a separate affective phenomenon.

**Boundary conditions.** The degradation is typically strongest under timed, high-stakes, or evaluative conditions, and weakest when pressure is removed or when learners can offload computation (e.g., scratch work, calculators for routine steps) [~M]. Highly anxious individuals are often not low-ability: performance gaps between high- and low-anxious learners widen with pressure, consistent with anxiety suppressing rather than reflecting competence [~M]. This makes the effect context-dependent rather than uniform — a [~]-direction pattern in which the impairment emerges only when situational demands collide with anxiety-generated load.

**Directionality.** The relationship is likely reciprocal — poor performance breeds anxiety, and anxiety degrades subsequent performance — so this claim should be read as one loop in a cycle, not a simple one-way cause [~W]. This also means interventions can target either side of the loop: reducing evaluative pressure or building fluency to remove sources of failure.

**Design implications.** Because the impairment is load-driven rather than ability-driven, instructional designs that reduce working memory demands — worked examples, externalized computation, untimed practice — should disproportionately benefit highly anxious learners [+W]. Timed testing and public performance of mathematics are the conditions most likely to widen anxiety-based gaps [-M]; designers using [Test-enhanced learning](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) should weigh retrieval-practice benefits against the evaluative pressure that can trigger impairment in anxious learners [~M]. Designers working within [Cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md) should treat anxiety as an additional source of extraneous load, not merely an affective side issue, and appraisal-focused approaches such as [Belonging interventions improve outcomes](belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) address the evaluative concerns that drive the anxiety in the first place [+W].

**Open questions.** Effect sizes vary widely across age groups and task types; whether anxiety specifically harms arithmetic retrieval, procedural execution, or novel problem-solving to different degrees remains contested. The field also lacks strong causal identification for the working-memory account in classroom settings, where most evidence is correlational [-W].
