# Gender-interest stereotypes create a gender gap in interest in a computer science activity: girls, but not boys, show lower interest when the stereotype is present

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · causal `r2` · `q3` · `i2` medium

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` A 2 (stereotype presence) × 2 (gender) ANOVA on interest in a computer science activity showed a significant interaction, F(1,120) = 7.22, P = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.06. [→ Master 2021](#master-2021)
`q3 i2` Girls were less interested in the stereotyped than nonstereotyped activity (d = 0.69) and less interested than boys in the stereotyped activity (d = 0.57); boys' interest did not differ between activities (d = 0.002). [→ Master 2021 (2)](#master-2021-2)

## Evidence

### Master 2021

Master, A., Meltzoff, A. N., & Cheryan, S. (2021). Gender stereotypes about interests start early and cause gender disparities in computer science and engineering. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 118(48), e2100030118. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2100030118

`q3 · i2` · `causal · r2`

Preregistered experiment with 122 children aged 8–9 (49% girls), conducted over Zoom or Google Meet with two computer science activities. The omnibus stereotype × gender interaction was significant, with printed effect size ηp2 = 0.06 (F(1,120) = 7.22, P = 0.008).

> "a 2 (presence of stereotype: stereotyped, nonstereotyped) × 2 (gender: girls, boys) ANOVA on interest revealed a significant interaction, F(1,120) = 7.22, P = 0.008, and ηp2 = 0.06"

### Master 2021 (2)

Master, A., Meltzoff, A. N., & Cheryan, S. (2021). Gender stereotypes about interests start early and cause gender disparities in computer science and engineering. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 118(48), e2100030118. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2100030118

`q3 · i2` · `causal · r2`

Simple effects within the same experiment, with printed effect sizes d = 0.69 (girls, stereotyped vs. nonstereotyped) and d = 0.002 (boys). Girls chose the stereotyped activity less often (35% vs. 65%, P = 0.03); boys chose at chance (P = 1.00); the gender difference in choice was not significant, χ2(1, n = 122) = 2.81, P = 0.09.

> "girls were significantly less interested in the stereotyped than the nonstereotyped activity, P < 0.001 and d = 0.69, and there was no statistically significant difference between boys' interest in the stereotyped and the nonstereotyped activities, P = 0.99 and d = 0.002"
