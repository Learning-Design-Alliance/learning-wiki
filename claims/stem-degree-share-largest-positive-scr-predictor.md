# Community share of post-secondary STEM degrees is the largest positive predictor of physics continuation, while non-STEM post-secondary degrees predict a decline in grade 12 continuation

## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Eamonn Corrigan 2024

Eamonn Corrigan, Martin Williams, and Mary A. Wells. (2024). Beyond gender: The intersectional impact of community demographics on the continuation rates of male and female students into high school physics. Originally published 2023. [doi:10.1139/cjp-2023-0319](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjp-2023-0319)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

RQ1 mixed-effects regression on Ontario school-level data. STEM-degree proportion was the largest average effect ("β11 = 2.30 ± 0.27; β12 = 1.50 ± 0.27"), controlling for STEM employment and any post-secondary degree; no standardised effect size is printed.

> "Among the predictor variables considered, the largest average effect on SCR is found for the proportion of individuals with a post-secondary degree in STEM ( β11 = 2.30 ± 0.27; β12 = 1.50 ± 0.27)."

### Eamonn Corrigan 2024 (2)

Eamonn Corrigan, Martin Williams, and Mary A. Wells. (2024). Beyond gender: The intersectional impact of community demographics on the continuation rates of male and female students into high school physics. Originally published 2023. [doi:10.1139/cjp-2023-0319](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjp-2023-0319)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Same RQ1 models: any-field degree proportion was non-significant for grade 11 and negative for grade 12 ("β12 = −0.59 ± 0.29"). The authors interpret this as a potential role model effect specific to STEM-educated residents.

> "An increased proportion of the community having a post-secondary degree in any field was a statistically significant predictor only for grade 12 physics, with a negative effect ( β12 = −0.59 ± 0.29)."
