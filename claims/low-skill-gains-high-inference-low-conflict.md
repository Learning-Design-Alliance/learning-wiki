## Evidence

### Crosnoe 2010

Crosnoe, R., Morrison, F., Burchinal, M., Pianta, R., Keating, D., Friedman, S. L., Clarke-Stewart, K. A., & the NICHD Early Child Care Research Network. (2010). Instruction, Teacher–Student Relations, and Math Achievement Trajectories in Elementary School. Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017762

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r3`

Predicted trajectories graphed from HLM parameters (Figure 2) for low early skill children by instruction and conflict levels. The high-inference/low-conflict group outperformed both comparison groups; no effect size printed.

> "children in classrooms characterized by high levels of inference skills instruction who had low levels of conflict with teachers in these classrooms demonstrated higher absolute levels of math achievement in elementary school and posted higher rates of increase in math achievement over time than their low early skill group peers in classrooms with low levels of inference skills instruction who had low conflict with teachers."

### Crosnoe 2010 (2)

Crosnoe, R., Morrison, F., Burchinal, M., Pianta, R., Keating, D., Friedman, S. L., Clarke-Stewart, K. A., & the NICHD Early Child Care Research Network. (2010). Instruction, Teacher–Student Relations, and Math Achievement Trajectories in Elementary School. Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017762

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

The same HLM-derived comparison extended to the average and high skill groups: the focal low-skill group's slope was "more pronounced upward" than both, indicating catch-up. No effect size printed.

> "This focal group—low early skill children in classrooms with higher order instruction who had better teacher–student relations—had trajectories of math achievement during elementary school with a more pronounced upward slope than children in the average and high early skill groups (not shown in Figure 2)."
