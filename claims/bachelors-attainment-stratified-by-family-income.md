# Bachelor's degree attainment by age 24 is starkly stratified by family income, with 8 in 10 top-quartile youths earning a degree versus 1 in 9 from families earning under $33,000

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` Young people from families earning $99,000 or more are far more likely to earn a bachelor's degree by age 24 than those from families earning under $33,000. [→ Michael Dannenberg and Mamie Voight 2013](#michael-dannenberg-and-mamie-voight-2013)

`q2 i?` 82 percent of young people from the highest income quartile have a bachelor's degree by age 24, compared with only 8 percent of those from the lowest income quartile. [→ Mamie Lynch 2011](#mamie-lynch-2011)

## Evidence

### Michael Dannenberg and Mamie Voight 2013

Michael Dannenberg and Mamie Voight. (2013). Doing Away With Debt: Using Existing Resources to Ensure College Affordability for Low and Middle-Income Families. The Education Trust. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED543214

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

The report presents national degree-attainment data by family income (Figure 1, sourced to Mortenson), stating "roughly 8 out of 10 young people in families earning $99,000 per year or more earn a bachelor's degree by age 24" versus 1 in 9 for lower-income families. Descriptive statistics only; no effect size printed.

> "roughly 8 out of 10 young people in families earning $99,000 per year or more earn a bachelor's degree by age 24. Among families earning less than $33,000 per year, however, the attainment rate drops to 1 in 9"

### Mamie Lynch 2011

Mamie Lynch, Jennifer Engle, and José L. Cruz. (2011). Priced Out: How the Wrong Financial-Aid Policies Hurt Low-Income Students. The Education Trust. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED520199

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

The report cites this attainment statistic in its conclusion, attributing it via its endnotes, to argue that existing affordability efforts are "not nearly enough to make college a realistic option for the lowest income Americans." The article offers the figure as context rather than as a finding of its own analysis.

> "Is it any surprise then that 82 percent of young people from the highest income quartile in America have a bachelor’s degree by age 24, but that only 8 percent of those from the lowest income quartile do so?"
