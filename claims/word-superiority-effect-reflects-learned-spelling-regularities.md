## Evidence

### Kellman 2009

Kellman, P. J., & Garrigan, P. (2009). Perceptual Learning and Human Expertise. Physics of Life Reviews, 6(2), 53-84. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2008.12.001

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

The review describes the word superiority effect (first studied by Reicher and Wheeler): the exposure time allowing 80% accuracy is substantially shorter for words than for individual letters, even though the word frame provides no information about the correct choice.

> "The fact that most of the effect is preserved with pronounceable nonsense suggests that learning has led to extraction and rapid processing of structural regularities characteristic of English spelling patterns."

### Kellman 2009 (2)

Kellman, P. J., & Garrigan, P. (2009). Perceptual Learning and Human Expertise. Physics of Life Reviews, 6(2), 53-84. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2008.12.001

`q2 · i?` · `review · r3`

The review reports, citing Feitelson & Razel (1984), that beginning readers lack the word superiority effect, consistent with the higher-order-regularities account; it also rejects the overall-word-shape explanation via the nonsense-word finding.

> "The idea that the word superiority effect results from perceptual learning of higher-order regularities would predict that beginning readers would not show the word superiority effect. This proves to be the case (Feitelson & Razel, 1984)."
