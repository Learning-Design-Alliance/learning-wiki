## Evidence

### Andrew J. Mason and Chandralekha Singh 2016

Andrew J. Mason and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Surveying College Introductory Physics Students' Attitudes and Approaches to Problem Solving. [doi:10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704](https://doi.org/10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Survey result for Question 14 shown in Figure 9, with the non-monotonic faculty dip interpreted via individual discussions: faculty invoke principles automatically, whereas introductory students often treat physics as disconnected facts and formulas and look for formulas without conceptual analysis.

> "close to 90% of the Ph.D. students agreed (favorable) that they do so but only approximately 65% and 55% of the physics faculty and introductory students agreed, respectively."

### Andrew J. Mason and Chandralekha Singh 2016 (2)

Andrew J. Mason and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Surveying College Introductory Physics Students' Attitudes and Approaches to Problem Solving. [doi:10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704](https://doi.org/10.1088/0143-0807/37/5/055704)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Survey result for Question 16 with follow-up discussions. The article attributes faculty gut-feeling responses to good intuition developed through vast experience, and notes students may hold gut-feeling and taught views simultaneously that are difficult to merge.

> "Moreover, only 75% of faculty (and an even smaller percentage for Ph.D. students) noted that, while answering conceptual physics questions, they use the physics principles they usually think about when solving quantitative problems rather than mostly using their “gut” feeling."
