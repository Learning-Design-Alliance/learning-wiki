# Tutoring benefits both tutors and tutees

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i1` small

Tutoring is typically framed as a benefit to the tutee, but a substantial body of theory and practice holds that the tutor also gains — through explaining, monitoring, and restructuring their own understanding. This page covers reciprocal and cross-age tutoring arrangements in which learners alternate or take on the tutor role.

## Subclaims

`q3 i?` A meta-analysis of 65 independent tutoring-program evaluations found tutoring raised tutees' academic performance and attitudes relative to controls, with parallel gains for the students who served as tutors. [→ Cohen et al. 1982](#cohen-et-al-1982)

`q4 i1` A meta-analysis of 50 peer-tutoring studies in mathematics found a small-to-moderate average positive effect on academic achievement, moderated by educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size. ## Evidence

### Cohen et al. 1982

Cohen, P. A., Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1982). Educational Outcomes of Tutoring: A Meta-analysis of Findings. *American Educational Research Journal, 19*(2), 237–248. [doi:10.3102/00028312019002237](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=65 independent evaluations`

A meta-analysis of 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programs, spanning tutee and tutor outcomes. Tutored students outperformed control students on examinations and developed more positive attitudes toward the subject matter; students who served as tutors showed the same pattern of academic and attitudinal gains. Participation had little or no effect on the self-esteem of either tutors or tutees. Only the abstract was read (the full article is paywalled and no open-access copy was found), so no pooled effect-size statistic could be confirmed from the text — the abstract itself states the finding only in directional terms.

### Alegre-Ansuategui et al. 2017

Alegre-Ansuategui, F. J., Moliner, L., Lorenzo, G., & Maroto, A. (2017). Peer Tutoring and Academic Achievement in Mathematics: A Meta-Analysis. *EURASIA Journal of Mathematics, Science and Technology Education, 14*(1). [doi:10.12973/ejmste/79805](https://doi.org/10.12973/ejmste/79805)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i1 · small effect, Hedge's g = 0.333` · `n=50 studies`

A meta-analysis of 50 independent studies of peer-tutoring programs in mathematics across multiple educational stages found that 88% of the programs had a positive effect on participants' academic performance, with a pooled Hedge's g of 0.333. Moderator analyses found tutee/tutor age, assigned roles, tutee ability level (disabled/at-risk vs. not), session length and frequency were not significant moderators, while educational stage, study design, program duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day (in-school vs. out-of-school) and sample size were significant moderators of the effect. Crossref carries no page range for this record; the article's own header gives pp. 337–354 (2018;14(1)), which is noted here but not asserted as the Crossref-verified value.

## Discussion

**Why tutors benefit.** The dominant explanation is that preparing to teach forces the tutor to organize, elaborate, and verbalize their knowledge, exposing gaps that passive review leaves hidden. This aligns with the generative-processing logic behind [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) and with [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md): knowledge is consolidated through use rather than reception. Explaining to another person also adds social accountability, which [Social learning theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) treats as a powerful motivator for effortful engagement.

**Why tutees benefit.** Tutees receive individualized pacing, immediate feedback, and opportunities to ask questions that whole-class settings rarely permit. One-to-one and small-group interaction also supports the social presence conditions described in [Community of inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md).

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Tutor benefits are largest when tutors are adequately prepared and the material is within reach of their current competence; asking a near-peer to tutor content they have not yet mastered can entrench errors for both parties. Structured protocols (prompt cards, scripted question sequences) tend to outperform unstructured "just help each other" arrangements, consistent with the scaffolding logic of [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — unstructured tutoring can impose extraneous load on both roles. Reciprocal designs, where partners alternate roles, distribute these benefits and avoid fixed status hierarchies.

**Design implications.** Effective programs typically (a) train tutors in specific questioning and explanation routines before sessions begin, (b) provide materials that structure the interaction rather than leaving roles open-ended, and (c) build in role rotation so neither partner is permanently cast as the weaker learner. These design features echo the scaffolding-and-fading approach described in [Cognitive apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md), where responsibility is gradually transferred to the learner.

**Open questions.** The relative magnitude of tutor versus tutee gains across age bands and domains, and the durability of tutor-side gains over time, still need systematic synthesis on this page. The recorded meta-analysis of tutoring programmes reports gains for tutors but no effect size for them.
