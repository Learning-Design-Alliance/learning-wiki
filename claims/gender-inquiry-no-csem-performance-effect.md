# Asking test takers to indicate their gender before the CSEM does not deteriorate physics conceptual test performance compared with not asking

## Subclaims

`q3 i?` Students randomly assigned to a gender salient condition performed no worse on the CSEM pre-test and post-test than students in the condition without a gender question. [→ Alexandru Maries and Chandralekha Singh 2016](#alexandru-maries-and-chandralekha-singh-2016)

`q2 i?` Female and male students randomly assigned to indicate gender before vs. after the CSEM showed no statistically significant performance differences on pretest or post-test. [→ Alexandru Maries 2018](#alexandru-maries-2018)

`q3 i?` There were no statistically significant differences between male or female students asked versus not asked to indicate gender before the CSEM, on both pre-test and post-test. [→ Alexandru Maries 2017](#alexandru-maries-2017)

## Evidence

### Alexandru Maries and Chandralekha Singh 2016

Alexandru Maries and Chandralekha Singh. (2016). Stereotype Threat?: Effects of Inquiring About Test Takers' Gender on Conceptual Test Performance in Physics. University of Pittsburgh. Originally published 2010. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.07648](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.07648)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Randomized experiment in second semester algebra-based introductory physics at the University of Pittsburgh; students were randomly assigned to gender salient or not salient CSEM conditions. The article reports performance "is not affected by the condition students were assigned to" on both pre- and post-test; no effect size is printed.

> "Figure 1a shows that the performance of males and females on the CSEM is not affected by the condition students were assigned to (gender salient, not salient) both in the pre-test and in the post-test."

### Alexandru Maries 2018

Alexandru Maries, Nafis I. Karim, and Chandralekha Singh. (2018). Is agreeing with a gender stereotype correlated with the performance of female students in introductory physics? Physical Review Physics Education Research 14, 020119. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.14.020119

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Randomized experiment with 170 algebra-based physics II students (female N=99, male N=71) taking the CSEM as pretest and post-test in gender-salient vs. not-salient conditions; "there were no statistically significant differences between the performance of male or female students in the two conditions". Mann-Whitney U post-test p values were 0.320 (female) and 0.975 (male).

> "Figure 1 shows that there were no statistically significant differences between the performance of male or female students in the two conditions (e.g., female students who wrote their gender before taking the CSEM did not perform worse than female students who wrote their gender after taking the CSEM) in the pretest or the post-test."

### Alexandru Maries 2017

Alexandru Maries, Nafis I. Karim, and Chandralekha Singh. (2017). The impact of stereotype threat on gender gap in introductory physics. 2017 PERC Proceedings. https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2017.pr.059

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Randomized experiment with 170 algebra-based introductory physics II students who took the CSEM as pre- and post-test, randomly assigned to a gender-salient or not-salient condition. The article reports "no statistically significant differences" between conditions for either gender at either time point; no effect size is printed.

> "Figure 1 shows that there were no statistically significant differences between male or female students in the two conditions for the pre-test and post-test."
