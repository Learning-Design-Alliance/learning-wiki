Evidence¶

Murat Oztok 2012¶

Murat Oztok, Daniel Zingaro, Clare Brett, Jim Hewitt. (2012). Exploring Asynchronous and Synchronous Tool use in Online Courses. Preprint submitted to Computers & Education. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.08.007

q2 · i? · associational · r1

Quantitative comparison of log data from nine fully online graduate courses, using the 190 students who wrote at least one synchronous message. A paired t-test confirmed the word-count difference between modes; the quote reports "31.8 words on average" versus 160.91.

"In terms of word count, we found that synchronous messages were signiﬁcantly shorter (31.8 words on average) than asynchronous notes (160 .91 words on average)."

Murat Oztok 2012 (2)¶

Murat Oztok, Daniel Zingaro, Clare Brett, Jim Hewitt. (2012). Exploring Asynchronous and Synchronous Tool use in Online Courses. Preprint submitted to Computers & Education. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.08.007

q2 · i? · associational · r2

Paired t-test on each student's average Flesch reading ease in each mode, over the 190 message-writing students, in the same nine-course log dataset. The quote reports reading ease "79 .39 compared to 55 .69" with t(189) = 27.30.

"We found that messages were signiﬁcantly easier to read than asynchronous notes (a reading ease score of 79 .39 compared to 55 .69; t(189) = 27.30, p = 0)."

Murat Oztok 2012 (3)¶

Murat Oztok, Daniel Zingaro, Clare Brett, Jim Hewitt. (2012). Exploring Asynchronous and Synchronous Tool use in Online Courses. Preprint submitted to Computers & Education. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.08.007

q2 · i? · associational · r1

Paired t-test comparing each student's average percentage of Academic Word List words in notes versus messages, same nine-course log dataset, 190 message-writing students. The quote prints "7 .5 percent compared to 3 .9 percent" with t(189) = 19.99.

"We found that asynchronous notes contained much more use of AWL words (7 .5 percent compared to 3 .9 percent; t(189) = 19 .99, p = 0)."
