# Computer-based training reliably improves children's working memory and reasoning, but computerized training has not succeeded in improving 4- to 6-year-olds' inhibitory control

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Cogmed computerized working-memory training improves working memory even on untrained tasks, with gains persisting 6 months and delayed math gains appearing 6 months after training. [→ Diamond Adele 2012](#diamond-adele-2012)
`q2 i0` Attempts to improve 4- to 6-year-olds' inhibitory control with computerized inhibitory-control games or training have not been successful, whereas 9-year-olds improved in task switching and inhibition after computer-based task-switching training. [→ Diamond Adele 2012 (2)](#diamond-adele-2012-2)

## Evidence

### Diamond Adele 2012

Diamond Adele. (2012). Activities and Programs That Improve Children's Executive Functions. Current Directions in Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721412453722

`q2 · i?` · `review · r2`

Narrative review of computerized-training studies; the review reports Cogmed as the most researched approach, repeatedly successful, with two studies (Holmes et al., 2009, 2010) finding working-memory gains remained 6 months after training and math gains evident 6 months later despite no immediate gains.

> "When Cogmed training is on working memory, working memory improves even on untrained tasks (e.g., Klingberg et al., 2005; Thorell et al., 2009)."

### Diamond Adele 2012 (2)

Diamond Adele. (2012). Activities and Programs That Improve Children's Executive Functions. Current Directions in Psychological Science. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721412453722

`q2 · i0` · `review · r2`

Narrative review reporting failed computerized inhibitory-control training in 4- to 6-year-olds (Rueda et al., 2005 found no EF improvements), while 9-year-olds receiving task-switching training improved in both task switching and inhibition (Karbach & Kray, 2009).

> "attempts thus far to improve 4- to 6-year-olds' inhibitory control using computerized inhibitory-control games or training have not been successful (Rueda, Rothbart, McCandliss, Saccomanno, & Posner, 2005; Thorell et al., 2009)."
