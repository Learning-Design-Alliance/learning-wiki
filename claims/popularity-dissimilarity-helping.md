## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Popularity did not predict overall receiving or giving help, but the significant similarity effect showed low-popular adolescents avoided high-popular helpers and high-popular adolescents avoided both low- and high-popular helpers. [→ van Rijsewijk 2016](#van-rijsewijk-2016)

## Evidence

### van Rijsewijk 2016

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

RSiena meta-analysis: popularity ego and alter effects were nonsignificant (b = 0.15 and b = -0.41), but the similarity effect was significant (b = 0.79, odds = 2.21).

> "Popularity did not predict variation in receiving and giving help (b ! .15, SE ! .21, odds ! 1.16; b ! #.41, SE ! .25, odds ! .67). However, the similarity effect was significant (b ! .79, SE ! .12, odds ! 2.21)."

### van Rijsewijk 2016 (2)

van Rijsewijk, L., Dijkstra, J. K., Pattiselanno, K., Steglich, C., & Veenstra, R. (2016). Who Helps Whom? Investigating the Development of Adolescent Prosocial Relationships. Developmental Psychology, 52(6), 894-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000106

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Selection table for popularity: low-popular adolescents favored low-popular helpers (.29) over high-popular (-1.20); high-popular adolescents avoided both low-popular (-.25) and high-popular (-.15) helpers, so "high-popular adolescents did not receive help from similarly and dissimilarly popular peers."

> "low-popular adolescents were more likely to report low-popular peers (.29) than high-popular peers (#1.20) as helpers. High-popular adolescents did not report both low- and high-popular peers as helpers (#.25 and #.15)."
