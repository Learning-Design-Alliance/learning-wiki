## Evidence

### Luca Girlanda 2009

Luca Girlanda. (2009). Echoes from the Moon. arXiv preprint. https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.3367 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0903.3367](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0903.3367)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

Measurement activity in which 10 student groups analyzed the echo of the word "over" in an Apollo communication using Audacity; the students' averaged delay "∆ t = (2.620 ±0.007) s" translates into the printed distance. A maximum-likelihood fit would yield ∆t = (2.616±0.001) s and dEM = (3.921±0.002)·10^8 m.

> "As a final result, without aﬀording a thorough statistical analysis, students agreed to take the average of the 10 measurements with the maximum shift from average as uncertainty, ∆ t = (2.620 ±0.007) s. This translates into dEM = (3.93±0.01)·108 m"

### Luca Girlanda 2009 (2)

Luca Girlanda. (2009). Echoes from the Moon. arXiv preprint. https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.3367 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0903.3367](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0903.3367)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r2`

First round of the activity: 10 groups of two or three students timed reply delays with chronometers during the Apollo 11 Armstrong conversation, using tapescripts. Because the tape was recorded at Houston, only Armstrong's delays carry the flight time, giving the printed upper bound.

> "Only the delays in Armstrong’s replies are aﬀected by the time of ﬂight of the radio signal, since the tape was recorded at Houston. From the minimum delay in Ar mstrong’s replies (last column of the 2nd row) an upper bound for the Earth-Moon dis tance was found, dEM < (4.5 ± 0.7) · 108 m."
