## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Average group participation tends to decrease with increased heterogeneity, while the highest-scoring student's participation increases with increasing heterogeneity. [→ Joss Ives 2016](#joss-ives-2016)

## Evidence

### Joss Ives 2016

Joss Ives, Matias de Jong Van Lier, Nutifafa Kwaku Sumah, and Jared B. Stang. (2016). Examining Student Participation in Two-Phase Collaborative Exams through Video Analysis. [doi:10.1119/perc.2016.pr.038](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2016.pr.038)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Figure 4 (left panel) analysis of 27 groups placed in three heterogeneity levels (nine groups each) by standard deviation of solo-phase grades, showing the divergent participation trends.

> "we see that average participation score of the group tends to decrease with increased heterogeneity. We also ﬁnd this trend holds for the lowest scoring student in the group, as measured by their score in the solo phase. However, when looking at the highest scoring student in the group, we see that their participation increases with increasing heterogeneity."

### Joss Ives 2016 (2)

Joss Ives, Matias de Jong Van Lier, Nutifafa Kwaku Sumah, and Jared B. Stang. (2016). Examining Student Participation in Two-Phase Collaborative Exams through Video Analysis. [doi:10.1119/perc.2016.pr.038](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2016.pr.038)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Figure 4 (right panel) subset analysis excluding questions with partial marks, comparing correct vs incorrect students' participation across heterogeneity levels; no effect size printed.

> "in groups of Low and Medium heterogeneity, there is no signiﬁcant difference between students that answered a question correct or incorrect, but in the groups of High heterogeneity, the students with the correct answer participate more."
