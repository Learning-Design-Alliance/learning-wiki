# Medical students using the e-learning module scored significantly higher on the reproductive health knowledge questionnaire than students receiving traditional lectures only

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` E-learning participants (n = 140, mean 15.40 ± 2.46) scored significantly higher on the RH knowledge questionnaire than non-participants (n = 155, mean 11.97 ± 2.78), P < 0.001. [→ Abdelhai R 2012](#abdelhai-r-2012)
`q3 i?` Among e-learning participants there was no difference between males and females in knowledge scores (P = 0.48). [→ Abdelhai R 2012 (2)](#abdelhai-r-2012-2)

## Evidence

### Abdelhai R 2012

Abdelhai R, Yassin S, Ahmad MF, Fors UGH. (2012). An e-learning reproductive health module to support improved student learning and interaction: a prospective interventional study at a medical school in Egypt. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6920-12-11

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Student's t-test comparison of anonymous paper-based RH knowledge questionnaire scores between 140 e-learning participants (15.40 ± 2.46) and 155 traditional-lecture students (11.97 ± 2.78); the comparison was "highly statistical significant", with no sex difference among e-learning students (P = 0.48).

> "we found a highly statistical significant improvement for students in the e-learning group (P < 0.001). No difference was found among males and females attending the e-learning module, regarding their scores (P = 0.48)."

### Abdelhai R 2012 (2)

Abdelhai R, Yassin S, Ahmad MF, Fors UGH. (2012). An e-learning reproductive health module to support improved student learning and interaction: a prospective interventional study at a medical school in Egypt. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6920-12-11

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

In the traditional-lecture group only, females (12.40 ± 2.53, n = 80) scored slightly higher than males (11.52 ± 2.97, n = 75), a borderline significant difference (P = 0.049); no such sex difference appeared in the e-learning group.

> "females attending the regular lectures had slightly higher mean knowledge scores than males and this finding was border line significant [Table 2]."
