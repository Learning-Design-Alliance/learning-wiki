## Evidence

### Kyle M. Whitcomb and Chandralekha Singh 2021

Kyle M. Whitcomb and Chandralekha Singh. (2021). Inequity exemplified by underrepresented minority students receiving lower grades and having higher rates of attrition across STEM disciplines. Originally published 2020. [doi:10.1080/09500693.2021.1900623](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2021.1900623)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Observational analysis of institutional records; mean yearly GPA of degree-earners versus droppers plotted in Fig. 8. The article reports degree-earners' GPA is "roughly 0.6 grade points higher than those that dropped", representing one to two letter grades.

> "For chemistry, computer science, engineering, mathematics, and physics and astronomy majors (Figs. 8a and 8b), those that earned a degree in this set of majors have a GPA of roughly 0.6 grade points higher than those that dropped."

### Kyle M. Whitcomb and Chandralekha Singh 2021 (2)

Kyle M. Whitcomb and Chandralekha Singh. (2021). Inequity exemplified by underrepresented minority students receiving lower grades and having higher rates of attrition across STEM disciplines. Originally published 2020. [doi:10.1080/09500693.2021.1900623](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2021.1900623)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Same analysis for the second STEM cluster (Figs. 8c and 8d). The article reports a smaller gap of "roughly 0.2 grade points higher" for degree-earners, with roughly 26% of these majors dropping.

> "For biological science, economics, geology and environmental science, neuroscience, and statistics majors (Fig. 8c and 8d), those that earned a degree in this set of majors have a GPA of roughly 0.2 grade points higher than those that dropped, with roughly 26% of majors dropping."
