# Self Regulated Learning Strategies Improve Achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i1`–`i2`

Teaching learners to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own learning (self-regulated learning, or SRL) raises academic achievement across domains and age groups. The claim covers explicit strategy training in Zimmerman's three phases — forethought, performance, and self-reflection — not merely exposure to study-skills content.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 84 intervention studies in primary and secondary school, training students in self-regulated learning produced an average effect of 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use and motivation combined, with larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training and in mathematics than in reading/writing. [→ Dignath & Büttner 2008](#dignath-buttner-2008)

`q3 i1` In elementary and secondary students, measured self-regulated learning is only weakly correlated with academic achievement (metacognitive processes r = 0.20; cognitive strategies r = 0.11), and the correlation varies by process, subject, grade level and how SRL and achievement are measured. [→ Dent & Koenka 2016](#dent-koenka-2016)

## Evidence

### Dignath & Büttner 2008

Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level. *Metacognition and Learning, 3*(3), 231–264. [doi:10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)

`q4 · meta-analysis of intervention studies` · `i2 · medium-to-large effect, average ES = 0.69` · `n=84 studies (357 effect sizes)`

Two separate meta-analyses pooled self-regulated learning training studies: 49 with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students, 357 effect sizes in all. Outcomes were academic performance, strategy use and motivation, and the 0.69 average covers all three together. The abstract does not give a separate figure for achievement. Meta-regression found larger effects when researchers, not regular teachers, delivered the training, and in mathematics compared with reading/writing or other subjects. The theory behind the programme and the type of strategy taught had different effects at the two school levels. Read as abstract only.

### Dent & Koenka 2016

Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 28*(3), 425–474. [doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8)

`q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies` · `i1 · small correlation, r = 0.20 (metacognitive processes), r = 0.11 (cognitive strategies)` · `n=?`

Two meta-analyses of elementary and secondary school students related achievement to (1) metacognitive processes of self-regulated learning and (2) use of cognitive strategies. Both overall correlations were small: r = 0.20 for metacognitive processes and r = 0.11 for cognitive strategies. The correlations differed significantly by the specific process or strategy, academic subject, grade level, type of SRL measure and type of achievement measure. This is the correlational arm of the claim. It supports a real but modest association, not a causal effect. The number of studies was not in the abstract. Read as abstract only.

## Discussion

SRL interventions typically train one or more of three phases from Zimmerman's cyclical model: forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, self-instruction, attention control), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, attributing outcomes to strategy use rather than fixed ability). The claim as stated is broad; the strength of the effect depends heavily on which strategies are trained, how explicitly, and for whom. Until Evidence entries are added, treat the claim as plausible but under-documented.

Key moderators to document when evidence is added:

- **Strategy type.** Meta-analytic work (e.g., Dignath & Büttner on elementary classrooms; Hattie, Biggs & Purdie across levels) suggests effects are larger when training is embedded in subject-matter instruction rather than delivered as a standalone study-skills course [~M]. Generic "learning-to-learn" courses show weak transfer [-M].
- **Learner age and prior knowledge.** Younger learners and novices benefit from highly explicit, teacher-modeled strategy instruction; older learners can profit from more self-directed formats [~M]. Very low-prior-knowledge learners may lack the domain resources to deploy strategies effectively [-W].
- **Duration and practice.** One-shot workshops show little durable effect [-W]; sustained training with practice and feedback in authentic tasks is the condition under which achievement gains appear [+M].

Boundary conditions: SRL strategy training presumes learners have some metacognitive capacity and motivational readiness; it does not substitute for adequate prior knowledge or [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). For novices, heavy self-directed demands can itself impose extraneous load — see [Cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) [-M]. This parallels the expertise-reversal pattern documented for scaffolding generally: supports that help novices can burden them differently as competence grows.

Design implication: pair explicit SRL strategy instruction with [clear structure](clear-structure-improves-learning.md) and modeling, then fade the scaffolds as learners internalize the regulatory cycle. [Assessment for learning](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) provides natural opportunities to practice the self-reflection phase, since feedback invites learners to evaluate outcomes against goals and attribute success or failure to strategy use. Concrete, trainable strategies that map onto the three phases include [goal setting](../strategies/achievable_micro-goals.md) and planning in forethought, [annotating](annotating-improves-learning.md) and self-questioning during performance, and structured [reflection prompts](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md) during self-reflection.

Open questions for future enrichment: whether effects persist after training ends, whether gains transfer across domains or remain domain-bound, and how SRL training interacts with [adaptive learning](adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) environments that externalize some regulatory decisions.
