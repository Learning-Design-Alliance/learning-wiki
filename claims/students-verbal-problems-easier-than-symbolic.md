# Ninth grade algebra students solve verbally presented problems better than symbolically presented problems

## Subclaims

`q3 i?` Students who completed a year of formal algebra instruction performed significantly better on verbally presented problems than on symbolic equations, in both samples. [→ Nathan 2001](#nathan-2001)

`q3 i?` Beginning algebra students performed worse on equations than on matched word problems, implying greater knowledge deficits for comprehending algebraic expressions than English sentences. [→ Koedinger 2012](#koedinger-2012)

## Evidence

### Nathan 2001

Nathan, M. J., Koedinger, K. R., & Alibali, M. W. (2001). Expert Blind Spot: When Content Knowledge Eclipses Pedagogical Content Knowledge. Proceeding of the Third International Conference on Cognitive Science. https://www.google.com/search?q=%22Expert+Blind+Spot%22+Nathan+Koedinger+Alibali

`q3 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Research on problem-solving performance of ninth grade students in two samples (n1 = 76, n2 = 171) who had completed a year of formal algebra instruction. The article reports "statistically significant advantages for verbal problems in both samples (p < .01)"; no effect size is printed.

> "Students’ performance on equations is less than 30%, while verbal problems are solved correctly over 50% of the time, leading to statistically significant advantages for verbal problems in both samples (p < .01)."

### Koedinger 2012

Koedinger, K.R., Corbett, A.C., & Perfetti, C. (2012). The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction (KLI) Framework: Bridging the Science-Practice Chasm to Enhance Robust Student Learning. Cognitive Science, 36(5), 757-798. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01245.x

`q3 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Contrast-based assessment analysis reported by the article (Koedinger and Nathan, 2004): comparing performance on algebra problems presented in words versus matched equations. The result illustrates how contrasting Assessment Events infers Knowledge Components, and how instructors may incorrectly conclude equations are easier because they look simpler.

> "In contrast to teachers’ beliefs, beginning algebra students performed worse on equations than word problems and this contrast led the researchers to infer that students have greater knowledge deficits for comprehending algebraic expressions than corresponding English sentences."
