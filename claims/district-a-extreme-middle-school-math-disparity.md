# The most extreme district disparity: District A's high-poverty middle schools had about 1 in 20 highest-performing math teachers versus about 12 in 20 in the lowest-poverty quintile

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r2` · `q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` In District A middle school math, prevalence of highest-performing teachers ranged from about 5 percent in the two highest-poverty quintiles to about 60 percent in the lowest-poverty quintile, a statistically significant divergence from equal access. [→ Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max 2011](#steven-glazerman-and-jeffrey-max-2011)

## Evidence

### Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max 2011

Steven Glazerman and Jeffrey Max. (2011). Do Low-Income Students Have Equal Access to the Highest-Performing Teachers? NCEE Evaluation Brief, NCEE 2011-4016. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20114016/pdf/20114016_app.pdf

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Figure 2 analysis for District A middle school math teachers (N=235 teachers as printed); the chi-square test of no relationship between quintile and percent highest-performing was rejected at the 0.05 level.

> "About 1 in 20 teachers in the first two high- poverty quintiles were highest -performing, whereas about 12 in 20 teachers in the top quintile schools were highest-performing. Under perfectly equal access, all quintiles would have 4 of 20 teachers who were from this highest-performing group."
