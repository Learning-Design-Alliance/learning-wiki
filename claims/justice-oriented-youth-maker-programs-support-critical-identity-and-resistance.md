# Justice-oriented youth maker programs support redefinition of entrepreneurialism and resistance to structural misrecognition

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

A two-year critical ethnography of 12 youth in a community STEM-making program found that all five youth teams redefined entrepreneurialism toward community benefit rather than profit, grounded their designs in community-rooted problems, and collectively recognized and resisted racialized structural misrecognition at a public entrepreneurial fair, sustaining and deepening this orientation over more than a year of follow-up.

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` All five youth project teams redefined entrepreneurialism as community benefit rather than profit when producing required business-plan documents, e.g. writing "We do not want to make money from it. What we want to do is provide opportunities to the kids in our community" — a reorientation sustained in follow-up interviews conducted more than a year later. [→ Greenberg et al. 2020](#greenberg-et-al-2020)

`q3 i2` All five teams identified their design problems through community ethnography (surveys, interviews, observation) rooted in their own lived experience of historicized injustice — e.g., a bus-shelter heating design informed by a team member's mother's work as a bus driver, and a library-access design informed by mapping community bus routes and 119 community-member surveys. [→ Greenberg et al. 2020](#greenberg-et-al-2020)

`q3 i2` Youth explicitly named and collectively resisted structural misrecognition at a public entrepreneurial fair — noting the fair's racial and economic homogeneity, and pushing back when judges characterized their high-tech nonprofit projects as lacking market "style and flash" — including refusing organizers' attempt to physically separate the group's tables. [→ Greenberg et al. 2020](#greenberg-et-al-2020)

`q3 i2` Rather than accept mentors' suggestion to stop attending after a difficult first year, youth argued to return with an explicitly sharper justice focus, and did so the following year with new projects (a youth-made STEM YouTube channel, a homelessness-data-sharing app) — evidence of sustained rather than one-time engagement. [→ Greenberg et al. 2020](#greenberg-et-al-2020)

## Evidence

### Greenberg et al. 2020

Greenberg, D., Calabrese Barton, A., Tan, E., & Archer, L. (2020). Redefining entrepreneurialism in the maker movement: A critical youth approach. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 29*(4-5), 471-510. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2020.1749633](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2020.1749633)

`q3 · two-year critical ethnography with multiple data sources (field notes, artifact interviews, video, youth-produced business plans, 1+ year follow-up interviews) and open coding across two analytic passes; single program, no comparison group, no quantified outcome measures` · `i2 · a clear, well-documented, and longitudinally sustained qualitative pattern of identity change and structural resistance across all five youth teams, not a quantified or causally isolated effect` · `n=12 youth ages 11-14, majority Black/youth of Color, low-income community, one afterschool STEM program running 10+ years`

Twelve youth in a decade-old community STEM-making program ("Green Club") were followed for two years through the [Critical Maker-Entrepreneurialism Program](../patterns/critical-maker-entrepreneurialism-program.md) sequence, culminating in five teams presenting designs (a heated boot, a solar-powered book/maker-kit library, a heated bus shelter, an emergency phone-alert button, and a STEM YouTube series) at a citywide entrepreneurial fair. Analysis of business-plan documents, field notes, and interviews (including a 1+ year follow-up) traces consistent redefinition of entrepreneurship toward community benefit, grounded problem identification, collective resistance to misrecognition, and sustained re-engagement, interpreted through [Critical Maker-Entrepreneurialism](../theories/critical-maker-entrepreneurialism.md).

## Discussion
This is a rich, well-triangulated single-program qualitative study, not a comparative or causal design: there is no comparison group of youth in a conventional (non-justice-oriented) maker-entrepreneurship program, so the claims describe what this program's participants did and became, not what any maker-entrepreneurship program would produce. The longitudinal follow-up (documented shifts sustained 1+ year later, and a deliberate return to the fair the following year with a sharper justice focus) is a genuine strength that distinguishes this from a single-moment case study.
