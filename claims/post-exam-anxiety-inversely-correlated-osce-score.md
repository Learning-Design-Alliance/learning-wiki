# Post-exam anxiety is inversely correlated with OSCE score: students with the lowest post-OSCE anxiety achieved better scores

## Evidence

### Massalou Damien 2025

Massalou Damien, Doyen Jérôme, Almairac Fabien, Rohrlich Pierre-Simon, Fournier Jean-Paul, Vandersteen Clair, Sadaghianloo Nirvana. (2025). Can relaxation exercises improve students’ OSCE grades: a prospective study. Canadian Medical Education Journal. https://doi.org/10.36834/cmej.77739

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Correlational analysis in the same prospective study of 199 students. The article reports "Spearman’s rank coefficient of -0.18, p = 0.01" between final score and post-exam anxiety VAS; the article prints no standardised effect size in d/g/η² form, so impact is coded i? (no magnitude reported; not a null effect).

> "Interestingly, the final score was inversely correlated with the final anxiety VAS, measured after the exam (Spearman’s rank coefficient of -0.18, p = 0.01): students with the lowest anxiety VAS post-OSCE had greater success rates on their final OSCE."

### Massalou Damien 2025 (2)

Massalou Damien, Doyen Jérôme, Almairac Fabien, Rohrlich Pierre-Simon, Fournier Jean-Paul, Vandersteen Clair, Sadaghianloo Nirvana. (2025). Can relaxation exercises improve students’ OSCE grades: a prospective study. Canadian Medical Education Journal. https://doi.org/10.36834/cmej.77739

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Linear regression including factors with p > 0.1 (post-exam VAS and gender). The article reports "B = -0.1 [95%CI: -0.178; -0.023], p = 0.01"; the unstandardised coefficient is not an effect size.

> "The multivariate analysis indicated post-exam anxiety VAS to be the only predictive factor for exam score (B = -0.1 [95%CI: -0.178; -0.023], p = 0.01, linear regression)."
