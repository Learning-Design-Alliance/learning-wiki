# Rejected and disliked children communicate less responsively and less coherently than popular or liked peers

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Rejected children demonstrated turn-taking styles including irrelevant turns, interruptions, simultaneous talking, and non-contingent responding, whereas popular children alternated turns and displayed coherent discourse. [→ Femke van der Wilt 2019](#femke-van-der-wilt-2019)
`q2 i?` Disliked children were more likely than other children to demand and suggest themes and roles, reject others' ideas, and describe their own activity during social pretend play. [→ Femke van der Wilt 2019 (2)](#femke-van-der-wilt-2019-2)

## Evidence

### Femke van der Wilt 2019

Femke van der Wilt, Chiel van der Veen, Claudia van Kruistum, Bert van Oers (2019). Why Do Children Become Rejected by Their Peers? A Review of Studies into the Relationship Between Oral Communicative Competence and Sociometric Status in Childhood. Educational Psychology Review (2019) 31:699–724. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09479-z

`q2 · i?`

The review reports, from the study of Black and Logan (1995) (N = 43 children aged 2.00–5.00, observations of peer interactions, comparative cross-sectional analysis), that "Rejected children demonstrated turn-taking styles" marked by irrelevant and non-contingent turns while popular children showed coherent discourse.

> "Rejected children demonstrated turn-taking styles that included irrelevant turns, interruptions, simultaneous talking, and non-contingent responding; popular chil- dren alternated turns, provided explanations to peers, and were more likely to display coherent discourse"

### Femke van der Wilt 2019 (2)

Femke van der Wilt, Chiel van der Veen, Claudia van Kruistum, Bert van Oers (2019). Why Do Children Become Rejected by Their Peers? A Review of Studies into the Relationship Between Oral Communicative Competence and Sociometric Status in Childhood. Educational Psychology Review (2019) 31:699–724. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09479-z

`q2 · i?`

The review reports Black's (1992) study of preschoolers' negotiation during social pretend play (N = 68, observations of play entry, comparative cross-sectional design): "disliked children were more likely to demand and suggest themes and roles" and to reject peers' ideas.

> "results indicated that, compared with other children, disliked children were more likely to demand and suggest themes and roles, to reject others ' ideas and to spend a fairly substantial proportion of the conversation describing their own activity or contributions to play."
