# Under sequential presentation with Likert ratings for every word, no memory benefit emerged for items judged likely to be forgotten; negative ratings predicted the poorest recall

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r2` · `q3` · `i3` large

## Subclaims
`q3 i3` In Experiment 4, words rated -3 (most likely to forget) were recalled worst and below the no-judgment control (d = -0.91), with no positive reactivity for forget items. [→ Dillon H. Murphy 2023](#dillon-h-murphy-2023)

## Evidence

### Dillon H. Murphy 2023

Dillon H. Murphy, Vered Halamish, Matthew G. Rhodes, & Alan D. Castel. (2023). How evaluating memorability can lead to Unintended Consequences. Metacognition and Learning (2023) 18:375–403. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-023-09332-3

`q3 · i3` · `causal · r2`

Experiment 4 with 161 undergraduates viewing 16 words sequentially for 5 s each; groups made 7-point (n = 49) or 3-point (n = 62) Likert memorability ratings or received an interval (n = 50). Compared to control recall (M = 0.54), "words given a -3 were most poorly recalled" and +3 words better recalled.

> "words given a -3 were most poorly recalled (M = 0.28, SD = 0.29), [t(31) = -5.14, p < .001, d = − 0.91] and words given a + 3 were better recalled (M = 0.73, SD = 0.32), [t(31) = 3.37, p = .002, d = 0.60]"
