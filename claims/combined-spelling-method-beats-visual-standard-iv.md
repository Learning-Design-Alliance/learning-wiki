`q2 i?` After learning, Set A (combined method) averaged 17.9 words correct versus 15.1 for Set B (visual method), a difference of 2.8 words that the author calls highly significant at eight times its probable error. [→ Winch 1913](#winch-1913)

## Evidence

### Winch 1913

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 4*(10), 579–592. [doi:10.1037/h0076021](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076021)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Controlled experiment with 54 Standard IV boys in a London school; each boy learned one 30-word list by a combined method with directed attention and the other by a silent visual method. The author reports "the difference between the means, namely, 2.8" as eight times its probable error; no standardized effect size is printed.

> "The difference between the means, namely, 2.8, is 8 times its 'probable error,' and is therefore a highly 'significant' differ ence."

### Winch 1913 (2)

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental researches on learning to spell. Part II. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 4*(10), 579–592. [doi:10.1037/h0076021](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076021)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Preliminary dictation test in the same Standard IV experiment, used to check that the two 30-word lists were of similar difficulty before learning; the author reports the correlation between lists as +.81, a probable error of the difference of .20, and "no 'significant' difference between the two lists before learning." A non-significant difference does not prove exact equality. The author also cautions that the later advantage of Set A may come from directed attention rather than from the combination of motor, auditory and visual memories, and Set A's lessons fell more often in the morning (judged by the author a very slight advantage).

> "The average number of words correctly spelt in Set A before learning was 4.3, for Set B it was 4.4."
