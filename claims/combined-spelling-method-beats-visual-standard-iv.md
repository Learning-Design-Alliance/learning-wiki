# For Standard IV boys (average age 11), a combined articulatory-auditory-visual spelling method with directed attention yields higher dictation scores than a silent visual method without directed attention

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` After learning, Set A (combined method) averaged 17.9 words correct versus 15.1 for Set B (visual method), a difference of 2.8 words that the author calls highly significant at eight times its probable error. [→ Winch 1913](#winch-1913)
`q2 i?` Before learning the two word lists were equal in difficulty (4.3 vs 4.4 words correct; no significant difference), so the post-learning difference is attributable to the methods. [→ Winch 1913 (2)](#winch-1913-2)

## Evidence

### Winch 1913

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental Researches on Learning to Spell, Part II. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076021

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Controlled experiment with 54 Standard IV boys in a London school; each boy learned one 30-word list by a combined method with directed attention and the other by a silent visual method. The author reports "the difference between the means, namely, 2.8" as eight times its probable error; no standardized effect size is printed.

> "The difference between the means, namely, 2.8, is 8 times its 'probable error,' and is therefore a highly 'significant' differ ence."

### Winch 1913 (2)

Winch, W. H. (1913). Experimental Researches on Learning to Spell, Part II. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076021

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Preliminary dictation test in the same Standard IV experiment established list equality before learning; the author reports the correlation between lists as +.81 and states there is "no 'significant' difference between the two lists before learning."

> "The average number of words correctly spelt in Set A before learning was 4.3, for Set B it was 4.4."
