Evidence¶

Sunny Yu 2026¶

Sunny Yu, Myra Cheng, Ahmad Jabbar, Ilia Sucholutsky, Katherine M. Collins, Dan Jurafsky, Robert D. Hawkins. (2026). Cognitive offloading and the speedup illusion in human-AI interaction. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23177 doi:10.48550/arxiv.2605.23177

q3 · i? · causal · r3

Preregistered between-subjects behavioral study (prediction and completion samples, N=1237) fitting a linear mixed-effects model of predicted vs. actual times. The article reports "the actual completion time was almost one minute greater than the predicted time" for AI assistance, holding across all four task categories and both difficulty levels. Overall, actual AI-assisted and independent completion times did not differ, but AI assistance did make difficult tasks faster, by 26.14 seconds (SE = 11.9, p < .05); no difference was found for easy tasks.

"the actual completion time was almost one minute greater than the predicted time (β=57.8,SE=8.79,z=6.57,p<0.001). The speedup illusion holds across all four task categories and both difficulty levels."

Sunny Yu 2026 (2)¶

Sunny Yu, Myra Cheng, Ahmad Jabbar, Ilia Sucholutsky, Katherine M. Collins, Dan Jurafsky, Robert D. Hawkins. (2026). The efficiency-gain illusion: People underestimate the rate of AI use and overestimate its benefits on simple tasks. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22687 doi:10.48550/arxiv.2605.22687

q3 · i? · causal · r3

Pre-registered Study 2 (prediction sample N=600, completion sample N=1001 on Prolific) compared predicted versus actual times for independent and AI-assisted completion. The speedup illusion held "across all four task categories and both difficulty levels" per the article.

"Specifically, participants expected AI to save more time (55.7 seconds) than it actually did (7.5 seconds) (Equation (1);β=−48.33,p<0.001). This difference primarily arises from miscalibration about AI-assisted performance, i.e., ˆtA(τ) versust A(τ): the actual completion time with AI (86.2 seconds) is nearly double the predicted time with AI (43.3 seconds) (β= 42.9,p<0.001)."

  • Prompting is a major source of AI-assisted cognitive effort: copying prompts reduces effort but not time, and verbose model responses can make AI-assisted completion slower than predicted — related
  • AI assistance reduces subjective mental effort across all tasks even when it does not reduce completion time, dissociating time and effort — related
  • AI assistance provides no significant overall time savings and can slow completion on easy task variants, with chat-interface friction (prompting time) dominating on trivial tasks — related
  • People overestimate how much mental effort AI assistance alleviates (offloading illusion), driven by overestimating the effort of independent completion while AI-assisted effort is accurately estimated — related
  • Practicing with AI did not create an illusion of mastery: participants reported learning and skill levels similar to other conditions — related
  • Educators benefit from AI chatbots through time-saving administrative assistance and improved pedagogy — reports the opposite
  • Students' actual task selections deviate about half a difficulty level and half a support level from SRLTS model-based selections — related
  • Prior AI use carries over: brief AI exposure increases subsequent AI adoption and exacerbates the speedup illusion, while independent exposure does not reduce AI use — related
  • People use AI on simple tasks at a rate significantly higher than they predict for themselves (self-estimate miscalibration), especially on easy task variants — related
  • AI-related placebo effects lead users to overestimate their own abilities while or after being assisted by an AI — related
