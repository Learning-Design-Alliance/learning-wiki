# Accumulated deliberate practice is associated with performance, explaining a domain-dependent share of its variance: about a quarter in games, 4% in education and under 1% in professions

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 1 quant-synthesis `r?`, 1 theoretical `r3` · `q2`–`q3` · n=not established from abstract
<!-- deprecated title (2026-10-05, overstated its evidence): Deliberate Practice Improves Performance -->

Individual differences in accumulated deliberate practice (structured, effortful practice on well-defined subskills at the edge of current ability, with feedback and repetition) are correlated with individual differences in performance, but explain far less of them than originally proposed, and much less in education and the professions than in games, music and sports. The one meta-analysis recorded here (Macnamara et al. 2014) relates practice accumulation to performance across studies; it is not an experiment, so it does not show that deliberate practice causes larger gains than repetition or experience. The second entry (Wedlock & Binnie 2025) reports the same education figure second-hand.

### Macnamara et al. 2014

Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., & Oswald, F. L. (2014). Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis. *Psychological Science, 25*(8), 1608–1618. [doi:10.1177/0956797614535810](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)

`q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies (relates accumulated practice to performance; no manipulation)` · `i? · variance explained, not an effect size: R²=.01–.26 by domain (about a quarter in games, 4% in education, under 1% in professions)` · `n=not established from abstract` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A meta-analysis covering all major domains in which deliberate practice has been investigated (music, games, sports, education, and professions), testing whether individual differences in deliberate-practice accumulation account for individual differences in performance. Deliberate practice explained the largest share of variance in domains with well-defined tasks and immediate feedback — games (26%), music (21%), and sports (18%) — and a much smaller share in education (4%) and professions (under 1%). The authors conclude that deliberate practice is an important predictor of performance but explains far less of it than originally proposed, and that its explanatory power is strongly domain-dependent — directly supporting this page's own "Domain dependence" discussion rather than the stronger, domain-general version of the claim.

The authors attribute the modest figure to a simplified definition of practice that included all structured activity hours.

## Discussion

*Evidence markers that stood beside statements with no claim link in this Discussion were removed on 2026-10-05: nothing on this page backed them. Statements below without a link are the construct's standard account or design reasoning, not findings recorded here; the domain figures are Macnamara et al. (2014)'s.*

**What counts as deliberate practice.** The construct, as articulated by Ericsson and colleagues, is narrower than "practice" generally: it requires (a) a well-defined goal, (b) full concentration on tasks just beyond current competence, (c) immediate informative feedback, and (d) repetition with refinement. Accumulated hours of ordinary experience or play correlate with performance far more weakly than accumulated deliberate practice. Designers should therefore distinguish practice *volume* from practice *structure* — a well-designed 30-minute feedback-rich drill cycle can outperform hours of unstructured repetition.

**Domain dependence.** The strength of the deliberate-practice–performance relationship varies sharply by domain. It appears strongest in games, music, and sports, where tasks are well-defined and feedback is immediate, and weakest in education and the professions, where performance criteria are fuzzy and feedback loops are long. Claims about deliberate practice should be scoped to the domain in question rather than generalized.

**Prior ability as a moderator.** A substantial share of performance variance in many domains is attributable to factors preceding practice — starting age, general cognitive ability, and innate talent indicators — so deliberate practice explains performance differences but rarely exhausts them. Practice history and prior ability interact: learners with stronger domain-relevant prerequisites often extract more from identical practice opportunities.

**Design implications.** Effective deliberate practice in learning environments maps onto several established mechanisms: isolating subskills before integration (see [Chunking reduces working memory load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)) [+S], calibrating difficulty to the learner's current level (see [Adaptive learning improves outcomes](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md)) [+M], and closing the loop with timely, specific feedback (see [Feedback improves learning outcomes](feedback-improves-learning.md)) [+S]. Retrieval-based and spaced formats are natural vehicles for the repetition-with-refinement requirement (see [Testing effect: retrieval practice improves retention](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) and [Spaced practice improves long-term retention](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md)). Without these supports, extended practice time alone yields diminishing returns.

**Constraints on application.** Deliberate practice is effortful and cognitively taxing; sustained full-concentration sessions are fatiguing, and session length beyond roughly an hour tends to show diminishing returns for most learners. It also presupposes well-defined performance criteria — in ill-structured domains, learners may practice the wrong subskills with high commitment. Finally, difficulty calibration is fragile: practice pitched too far beyond current competence produces overload and discouragement rather than growth, while practice pitched too far below it produces mere repetition.

**Open questions.** The field lacks consensus on how much of expert performance deliberate practice can explain, and on how to operationalize "deliberate" practice for measurement in classroom settings. Two entries are recorded: one meta-analysis of correlational studies (Macnamara et al. 2014, read from its abstract) and a framework article reporting the same education figure second-hand. Missing are experiments that compare deliberate practice with an equal amount of ordinary repetition, especially in classroom settings. <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale): Studies still need to be added to the Evidence section before this claim can carry an evidence-strength rating. -->
