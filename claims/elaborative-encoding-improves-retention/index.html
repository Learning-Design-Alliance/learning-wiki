Elaborative Encoding Improves Retention¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · quant-synthesis r? · q4 · i2 medium · n=69 effect sizes (64 research reports)

Elaborative encoding — connecting new material to prior knowledge through self-explanation, examples, analogies, and "why" and "how" questions — produces more durable memory traces than rote rehearsal.

Bisra et al. 2018¶

Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 30(3), 703–725. doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x

q4 · well-powered meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect, g=0.55 · n=69 effect sizes (64 research reports) · quant-synthesis · r?

A systematic search of bibliographic databases identified 69 effect sizes from 64 research reports comparing learning outcomes for participants who received self-explanation prompts against comparison conditions while studying or solving problems. A random-effects model gave an overall weighted mean effect size of Hedges' g = 0.55, and the authors coded 20 moderator variables (task type — solving problems, studying worked examples, studying text — subject area, education level, type of inducement, and treatment duration), concluding self-explanation prompts are a potentially powerful intervention across a range of instructional conditions.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. Elaboration is typically explained through the depth-of-processing framework: richer, more meaningful processing creates more retrieval routes than shallow repetition. In practice, elaboration overlaps with several related operations — activation of prior knowledge, analogical reasoning, and chunking — so isolating its unique contribution is difficult. Elaborative interrogation ("why is this true?") and self-explanation are the two most-studied operationalizations; both ask learners to integrate new material with what they already know rather than re-read it.

Boundary conditions. Elaboration is effortful and depends on working-memory capacity; learners with low prior knowledge may generate inaccurate elaborations that are then encoded and later retrieved as errors. Quality of elaboration matters more than quantity — self-generated connections to relevant prior knowledge support retention, while forced or superficial elaboration ("this word reminds me of my dog") may add processing load without adding meaning. This connects to cognitive overload: elaboration demands working-memory resources that overload can consume, so prompting learners to elaborate dense or unfamiliar material can backfire. Designers should therefore scaffold elaboration — provide partial prompts, worked examples of good elaborations, or advance organizers that supply the knowledge to connect to — rather than issuing open-ended "explain this to yourself" instructions to novices.

Moderators to expect. When evidence is populated, likely moderators include prior knowledge (elaboration should benefit learners who have relevant knowledge to connect to), accuracy of self-generated elaborations (errors encoded during elaboration are later retrieved as errors), and outcome type — elaboration plausibly supports retention and comprehension more than it supports transfer, where analogical reasoning across cases does more work. Time pressure is a further moderator: elaborative strategies are slower than restudy, so under fixed study time the benefit can shrink or reverse.

Open questions. The evidence base for this claim still needs to be populated — studies comparing elaborative interrogation and self-explanation against restudy, and work on when elaboration helps retention versus transfer, should be added before this page is treated as authoritative.
