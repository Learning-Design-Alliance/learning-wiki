## Evidence

### Carter 2016

Carter, B. E., and Wiles, J. R. (2016). Comparing effects of comedic and authoritative video presentations on student knowledge and attitudes about climate change. Bioscene, 42(1), 16-24. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1103782

`q2 · i1` · `causal · r1`

Correlational analysis of survey and HICCK scores in the quasi-experiment (Figure 3): pre-intervention r = 0.151 (p < 0.001), post-intervention r = 0.199 (p < 0.001), both weak. The r values are printed effect sizes.

> "the correlation between pre-intervention, self-reported understanding of climate change and actual measured understanding of climate change science, while positive and significant (r of 0.151, p < 0.001), is weaker than expected."

### Carter 2016 (2)

Carter, B. E., and Wiles, J. R. (2016). Comparing effects of comedic and authoritative video presentations on student knowledge and attitudes about climate change. Bioscene, 42(1), 16-24. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1103782

`q2 · i1` · `causal · r1`

Group-specific correlations: the IPCC group showed the largest post-intervention increase (pre r = 0.167, p = 0.0103; post r = 0.25, p < 0.001), yet measured understanding explained only 6.25% of variance in self-reported understanding.

> "The correlation between self-reported and measured understandings of climate change were greater post-intervention for the IPCC group than either of the other groups, although only 6.25% of the variance in self-reported understanding is explained by measured understanding."
