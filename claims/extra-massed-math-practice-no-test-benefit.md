# Doubling the number of same-kind problems worked in one practice session did not raise one-week test scores on a newly learned mathematics procedure

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

> **Currency** · newest study 2007 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

The authors call this extra, immediate practice of the same kind of problem after a learner can already solve it an *overlearning strategy*. The claim is a failure to detect a benefit in one small experiment, not evidence that extra practice never helps.

## Subclaims

`q3 i?` Undergraduates who worked four permutation problems in one session scored 49% on a test one week later, and those who worked only two scored 46%, a difference the authors report as not reliable (p = 0.8); those who worked the same four problems split across two sessions a week apart scored 74%. ## Evidence

### Rohrer & Taylor 2007

Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. *Instructional Science, 35*(6), 481–498. [doi:10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)

`q3 · randomised experiment, not pre-registered` · `i? · no effect size printed for the Massers against Light Massers comparison` · `n=60 analysed (66 completed all sessions)` · `causal · r2`

Experiment 1 of the article. Undergraduates at a US university learned, from a three-minute tutorial and worked examples, to count the distinct orderings of a letter string with a repeated letter. Massers worked four practice problems in one session and Light Massers two; both were tested on five new problems one week later (a third group, Spacers, split the four problems over two sessions). To make the comparison one of practice beyond first success, the analysis kept only the 60 students who solved at least one of the first two practice problems. Massers (49%, SE 10%) and Light Massers (46%, SE 7%) did not differ reliably (Holm–Sidak, p = 0.8), while Spacers (74%) beat both; the authors argue the null is not a ceiling effect because the Spacers did much better. They add that a much larger amount of extra practice might help. Group sizes are not printed. See [Spaced practice improves retention](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) for the spacing result from the same experiment.

## Discussion

The comparison doubled practice from two problems to four, so it says nothing about larger amounts; the authors themselves allow that a tenfold increase might boost scores, and argue it would cost too much study time to be efficient. They also cite an earlier experiment of their own (Rohrer & Taylor, 2006) in which a threefold increase had no effect on 1-week or 4-week tests, which this page does not record as a separate entry.
