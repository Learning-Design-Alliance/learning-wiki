# Participants' feedback-skipping decisions diverge from the optimal computer algorithm: they skip after some incorrect responses and fail to skip after many correct responses

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r2` · `q3`

## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Participants skipped feedback after an average of 15% of incorrect responses, while the computer never did, and failed to skip after roughly one in three correct responses, while the computer always skipped those. [→ Matthew Jensen Hays 2010](#matthew-jensen-hays-2010)

## Evidence

### Matthew Jensen Hays 2010

Matthew Jensen Hays, Nate Kornell, and Robert A. Bjork. (2010). The costs and benefits of providing feedback during learning. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review. https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.17.6.797

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Skipping-behavior analysis across the first four cycles of the skipping-permitted lists; both divergence types were reliable in the printed statistics, yet overall skipping frequency did not differ by controller.

> "the participants chose to skip feedback after an average of 15% (SD 5 16%) of their incorrect responses. The computer never skipped this feedback."
