# EEQ response patterns are statistically similar between two institutions on three of four sub-questions, but differ significantly on the Carnot working-substance question

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · qualitative `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Fisher's exact tests show School 1 and School 2 response distributions are statistically similar (p > 0.10) on three of four EEQ sub-questions. [→ Trevor I. Smith 2015](#trevor-i-smith-2015)
`q2 i?` On part (b), the Carnot-engine working-substance question, the two populations differ significantly (p = 0.001), attributable largely to School 2 students claiming insufficient information (11 vs. 0 at School 1). [→ Trevor I. Smith 2015 (2)](#trevor-i-smith-2015-2)

## Evidence

### Trevor I. Smith 2015

Trevor I. Smith, Warren M. Christensen, Donald B. Mountcastle, and John R. Thompson. (2015). Identifying Student Diﬃculties with Entropy, Heat Engines, and the Carnot Cycle. [doi:10.1103/physrevstper.11.020116](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevstper.11.020116)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Written EEQ pretest comparisons between School 1 (N = 32) and School 2 (N = 38) using Fisher's exact tests on full response distributions and on distributions with incorrect answers combined; the article reports p > 0.10 for three of four sub-questions, with no effect size printed.

> "The data from School 2 appear visually similar to that from School 1, and a Fisher's exact test shows that the two populations are statistically similar in their response patterns for three out of four sub-questions (p >0.10, see Table III)"

### Trevor I. Smith 2015 (2)

Trevor I. Smith, Warren M. Christensen, Donald B. Mountcastle, and John R. Thompson. (2015). Identifying Student Diﬃculties with Entropy, Heat Engines, and the Carnot Cycle. [doi:10.1103/physrevstper.11.020116](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevstper.11.020116)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r2`

Fisher's exact test on EEQ part (b) responses comparing School 1 and School 2; the article reports p = 0.001 and attributes the difference to 11 School 2 students versus 0 School 1 students answering "other." A post-hoc test with those students removed yielded p = 0.175. No effect size is printed.

> "the two populations are statistically signiﬁcantly diﬀer- ent (p = 0.001, see Table III). The most salient diﬀerence between the two distributions is the large proportion of School 2 students (11 compared to 0 at School 1) who claimed that there is not enough information to answer the question"
