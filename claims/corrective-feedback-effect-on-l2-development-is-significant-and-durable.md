# Corrective feedback on second-language errors has a significant effect on L2 development that is maintained on delayed tests

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r2` · `q3` · `i2` medium

Corrective feedback here is a teacher's or interlocutor's response to a learner's error in the target language (recasts, prompts, explicit correction and similar moves). The claim is about feedback as a whole; which type works best is a separate question (see Related Claims).

## Subclaims
`q3 i2` Across 15 classroom quasi-experimental studies (N = 827), oral corrective feedback had a medium effect against control groups (d = 0.74), with a between-group mean of 0.63 at immediate and 0.84 at delayed posttests, and no significant decline between them. ## Evidence

### Lyster & Saito 2010

Lyster, R., & Saito, K. (2010). Oral feedback in classroom SLA: A meta-analysis. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 32*(2), 265–302. [doi:10.1017/S0272263109990520](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520)

`q3 · meta-analysis of classroom quasi-experimental studies` · `i2 · medium effect, d=0.74 (between-group)` · `n=15 studies (N=827)` · `quant-synthesis · r2`

Meta-analysis restricted to 15 classroom-based quasi-experimental studies of oral corrective feedback in teacher–student interaction (N = 827), chosen because laboratory results were not taken to predict classroom effectiveness. Against control groups, corrective feedback had d = 0.74; against learners' own pretests it had d = 0.91, though control groups also gained (d = 0.39). Between-group contrasts gave a mean of 0.63 at immediate posttests and 0.84 at delayed posttests, and the overlapping confidence intervals indicate no significant decline. Effects were larger for prompts than recasts, largest on free constructed-response measures, and not different between second- and foreign-language classrooms; long treatments (d = 1.13) beat short-to-medium ones (d = 0.57), and a regression on age found younger learners benefiting more than older ones, so adults (mean age above 23; 140 of the 827 learners) are the group for whom the pooled effect is least representative, and the authors caution that few child and adult studies were available. Full text read.

> "Both immediate and delayed effects of CF thus exhibit medium-to-large effect sizes"

### Li 2010

Li, S. (2010). The effectiveness of corrective feedback in SLA: A meta-analysis. *Language Learning, 60*(2), 309–365. [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x)

`q3 · meta-analysis (abstract only)` · `i?` · `n=33 studies` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Meta-analysis of 33 primary studies of corrective feedback in second language acquisition (22 published studies and 11 Ph.D. dissertations), coded for 17 substantive and methodological features. It found a medium overall effect for corrective feedback that was maintained over time; implicit feedback's effect was better maintained than explicit feedback's; laboratory studies showed larger effects than classroom studies, shorter treatments larger effects than longer ones, and foreign-language contexts larger effects than second-language ones; published studies did not show larger effects than dissertations. Only the abstract was read: it prints no effect size, confidence interval or participant count, so "medium" is the author's word and is not coded here.

> "there was a medium overall effect for corrective feedback and the effect was maintained over time"

## Discussion

Both syntheses find the effect of corrective feedback durable, and they overlap in time and topic, so some primary studies are probably in both; the abstract of Li (2010) does not list its studies, so the overlap could not be checked. They disagree on treatment length: Li (2010) reports larger effects for shorter treatments, while Lyster and Saito (2010), in classrooms only, found long treatments larger than short-to-medium ones and not distinguishable from brief ones. On setting they also differ: Li found larger effects in foreign-language contexts, Lyster and Saito found setting not to be a contributing factor. Lyster and Saito's age result (younger learners benefit more) is a caution for designs aimed at adults, who were a minority of their pooled learners.
