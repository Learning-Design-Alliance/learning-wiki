## Evidence

### Lin 2016

Lin, S.-Y. and Singh, C. (2016). Using Analogies to Learn Introductory Physics. Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Pittsburgh. Originally published 2008. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.06388](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.06388)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Group comparison in a recitation-quiz study of 251 calculus- and algebra-based introductory physics students assigned to comparison, intervention 1, and intervention 2 conditions. The study reports "significantly higher (with p values less than 0.05)" for the named groups versus the comparison group; Tables 2 and 3 print the means.

> "Intervention 2 students in both courses and intervention 1 students in the algebra-based course on average scored significantly higher (with p values less than 0.05) than those in the comparison group"

### Lin 2016 (2)

Lin, S.-Y. and Singh, C. (2016). Using Analogies to Learn Introductory Physics. Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of Pittsburgh. Originally published 2008. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1602.06388](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1602.06388)

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Null finding for intervention 1 in the calculus-based course: no significant improvement on average versus the comparison group; equivalence was not tested. The authors attribute this to bottom students in the group not processing the solutions deeply.

> "Intervention 1 students in the calculus-based course, on the other hand, didn’t show a significant improvement on average as compared to the comparison group."
