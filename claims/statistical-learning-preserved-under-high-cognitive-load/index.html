Evidence¶

Marta Isabel Garrido 2016¶

Marta Isabel Garrido, Chee Leong James Teng, Jeremy Alexander Taylor, Elise Genevieve Rowe and Jason Brett Mattingley. (2016). Surprise responses in the human brain demonstrate statistical learning under high concurrent cognitive demand. npj Science of Learning. https://doi.org/10.1038/npjscilearn.2016.6

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Experiment 2 single-channel FCz analysis in 17 participants performing a concurrent N-back task: main effect of surprise, F(1,16) = 43.02, P = 7×10−6, and surprise-by-variance interaction, F(1,16) = 22.83, P = 2.05×10−4, showing learning "regardless of cognitive load".

"we again found larger responses to odds than to means, regardless of cognitive load (main effect of surprise, F(1,16) = 43.02, P =7×1 0 − 6) and this difference was again larger under the narrow than the broad distribution (surprise by variance interaction, F(1,16) = 22.83, P = 2.05 × 10− 4"

Marta Isabel Garrido, Chee Leong James Teng, Jeremy Alexander Taylor, Elise Genevieve Rowe and Jason Brett Mattingley. (2016). Surprise responses in the human brain demonstrate statistical learning under high concurrent cognitive demand. npj Science of Learning. https://doi.org/10.1038/npjscilearn.2016.6

q3 · i? · causal · r1

Spatiotemporal follow-up in Experiment 2 split by load found a fronto-central surprise cluster in both conditions (peaking at 105 ms high load, 95 ms low load, FWE-corrected); low load additionally showed later frontal clusters, and the surprise-by-variance cluster was "less significant under the high-load condition".

"We found a main effect of surprise in both load conditions, with a common fronto-central cluster peaking at about 100 ms (105 ms and 95 ms for high load and low load, respectively, both at P o 0.05 FWE-corrected"

Related Claims¶

  • Conscious awareness of the regularity is not necessary for an early neuronal signature of global statistical violation, as participants showed surprise-by-variance effects while reporting no awareness — related
  • Concurrent cognitive load was successfully manipulated: participants were slower and less accurate on the high-load (2-back) than low-load (1-back) working memory task — related
  • Prediction-error responses to physically identical outliers are larger in a narrow than a broad contextual distribution, reflecting sensitivity to contextual variance — related
  • No reliable reduction in auditory prediction-error magnitude under high versus low cognitive load, with the load-by-surprise interaction absent in frequentist and Bayesian analyses — related
  • The absence of a load effect may reflect cross-modality design: visual load may spare auditory resources, and load was not sustained to full capacity — related
