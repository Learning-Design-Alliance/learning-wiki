# Self-determination instruction improves outcomes

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Explicitly teaching learners about their basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — and how to satisfy them improves motivational and learning outcomes.

## Discussion

This claim concerns *instruction about* self-determination — helping learners understand [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) and the needs it posits — rather than the broader claim that autonomy-supportive teaching environments improve motivation, which is covered separately in [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) The two are related but distinct: an instructor can design an autonomy-supportive environment without ever naming the theory, and can teach the theory without changing their practice. Evidence for the environment-level claim is comparatively strong; evidence for the instruction-level claim is not yet represented here, and the two should not be conflated when judging this page.

Boundary conditions to watch once evidence is added: didactic instruction about motivation can be counterproductive if it feels like another demand rather than a resource [~M], and need-support effects are known to depend on learners' age, cultural context, and prior sense of competence [~M]. Until studies are added here, treat the strength of this claim as undetermined.

A plausible mechanism, to be tested against the eventual evidence: naming the needs gives learners a vocabulary for noticing when a task thwarts autonomy, competence, or relatedness, and strategies for reframing or restructuring their engagement — a self-regulatory move analogous to how [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) instruction makes strategic processes explicit. If the instruction instead reads as exhortation ("be more autonomous"), it may undermine the very autonomy it describes; framing matters as much as content.

Designers should also distinguish teaching the theory from teaching need-satisfying *strategies*. The strongest candidate interventions would pair conceptual explanation with guided application — e.g., learners audit a course task for need-support and practice reframing it — rather than lecture-only exposure to SDT concepts. This mirrors the general finding that strategy instruction works best when paired with supported practice rather than delivered as information alone.

Open questions for future enrichment: (1) whether instruction about SDT adds benefit *on top of* an already autonomy-supportive environment or only compensates for a controlling one; (2) whether effects persist beyond the immediate post-test; (3) whether younger learners have the metacognitive maturity to benefit from explicit need-vocabulary at all.
