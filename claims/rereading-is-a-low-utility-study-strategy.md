# Rereading Is A Low Utility Study Strategy

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Rereading — passively restudying text or notes — is one of the most commonly used study strategies, but it produces less durable learning than strategies that require learners to actively reconstruct material. The scope of this claim is rereading as a self-directed study technique, compared against alternative strategies under equivalent study time.

## Discussion

**Why rereading feels effective but underperforms.** Rereading creates a familiarity with the material that learners easily mistake for mastery — the material reads smoothly on a second pass, so it *feels* well learned. This is a classic metacomprehension failure: fluency during restudy is used as a proxy for learning, but familiarity is not the same as the ability to retrieve and use information later. Strategies that force retrieval or transformation of the material (self-testing, explaining, summarizing from memory) align the study activity with what a test or application demands — the principle of [transfer-appropriate processing](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) — which is why they consistently outperform restudy at equal time on task.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The low-utility verdict is strongest for massed rereading — reading the same text twice in immediate succession — which adds little beyond the first reading. Spaced rereading (with a delay between readings) is somewhat better than massed rereading, consistent with [spacing](../principles/spaced-practice.md) effects generally, and a single careful reading combined with other strategies may be a reasonable component of a study routine. Rereading may also serve a legitimate initial-encoding function for complex or unfamiliar material before more demanding strategies are applied — a first pass builds the base that [retrieval practice](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) then strengthens. The claim should therefore be read as a comparison against active alternatives, not as "rereading has zero value under all conditions."

**Constraints on the claim.** The low-utility verdict weakens or reverses under several conditions. When total study time is *not* held constant, a second reading can outperform a single briefer active episode [-M], so comparisons must be time-matched to be meaningful. For learners with very weak background knowledge or reading skill, a restudy pass may support comprehension enough that later active strategies become viable [~M] — rereading is a poor *primary* strategy but not necessarily a harmful *supplementary* one. And because rereading's subjective fluency inflates learners' confidence, interventions that merely warn students against it often fail [~M]: the illusion of mastery persists unless a concrete replacement strategy is supplied.

**Practical implication.** Because rereading is cheap, familiar, and feels productive, learners default to it. Simply telling students rereading is ineffective rarely changes behavior; replacing it with a concrete alternative — e.g., [annotating](annotating-improves-learning.md) during a first reading, or closed-book retrieval and self-explanation afterward — is more effective than prohibition alone. Framing the first reading as preparation for active study, rather than as study itself, helps learners budget their time accordingly. Instructors can support this by building low-stakes quizzing or [retrieval](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) opportunities into courses, which displaces rereading without requiring students to change habits on their own.
