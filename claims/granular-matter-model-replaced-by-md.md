# Students commonly hold a granular-matter mental model of gases, in which molecules rub and give off heat, and extended consideration of molecular dynamics leads most students to replace it with an energy-conserving model

`q1 i?` When asked what happens to the temperature of a compressed gas, students typically say it increases because molecules rub against each other and give off heat, reflecting a granular matter mental model. [→ Jan Tobochnik and Harvey Gould 2007](#jan-tobochnik-and-harvey-gould-2007)

## Evidence

### Jan Tobochnik and Harvey Gould 2007

Jan Tobochnik and Harvey Gould. (2007). Teaching statistical physics by thinking about models and algorithms. arXiv:0712.3488. https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.3488 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0712.3488](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0712.3488)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r1`

The authors state, based on interviews by one of them with students who took standard introductory physics and chemistry courses, that students' microscopic explanation is that 'the molecules rub against each other and give oﬀ heat.' No effect size is reported.

> "If students are asked what happens to the temperature when a gas is compressed, they will likely say that it increases. Their microscopic explanation will likely be that the molecules rub against each other and give oﬀ heat."

### Jan Tobochnik and Harvey Gould 2007 (2)

Jan Tobochnik and Harvey Gould. (2007). Teaching statistical physics by thinking about models and algorithms. arXiv:0712.3488. https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.3488 [doi:10.48550/arXiv.0712.3488](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.0712.3488)

`q1 · i?` · `design · r1`

The authors report their own teaching experience over several weeks of molecular dynamics contexts; this is an unquantified instructor observation, not a controlled study, and no effect size is printed.

> "Our experience has been that a consideration of molecular dynamics in various contexts over several weeks leads to most students replacing their granular matter model by one in which energy is conserved."
