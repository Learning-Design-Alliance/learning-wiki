# Expressive Writing Improves Exam Performance

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Brief expressive writing about exam-related worries, completed shortly before a high-stakes test, can offload anxious thoughts and free working-memory resources for the exam task itself. The claim concerns *performance* under pressure — helping students show what they already know — not the acquisition of new knowledge.

## Discussion

**Proposed mechanism.** The claim rests on a working-memory account: test anxiety consumes working-memory capacity with intrusive, ruminative worry, and writing those worries down externalizes them so they no longer compete with task-relevant processing. This aligns with the broader finding that cognitive overload degrades learning and performance — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) — and with evidence that reducing unnecessary load frees capacity for the target task, as in [Chunking reduces working memory load.](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md).

**Boundary conditions.** The effect is expected to be strongest for students who are chronically anxious about tests and for high-stakes situations; for low-anxiety students, writing about worries may be neutral or even counterproductive by directing attention to failure. Timing matters — the writing must occur close to the exam, before the pressure peaks, and should focus on thoughts and feelings rather than essay-quality prose or study content. The intervention is a performance lever, not a learning lever: it changes how well students can *express* what they know under pressure, not what they know — unlike [Active learning improves exam performance.](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md), which operates on encoding.

**Practical implementation.** In classroom terms, the intervention is cheap and scalable: roughly ten minutes of unstructured writing about worries immediately before an exam, framed as a routine opportunity to "download" concerns rather than as a therapeutic exercise. Framing matters — if students perceive the writing as an assessment or as an implicit signal that they are expected to be anxious, the offloading benefit may be undermined.

**Open questions.** Key studies still need to be added to the Evidence section before this claim can carry an evidence-strength rating. Open issues include dosage (how long a writing session must be), durability across repeated exams, and whether effects hold in low-stakes or online assessment contexts. Replication across age groups and subject domains is also needed before the claim can be generalized beyond the populations in which it was first tested.
