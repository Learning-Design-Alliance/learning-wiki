# Creating computational literature develops computational literacy even when code modification is minor

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Computational literacy has three interdependent pillars — material (technical coding fluency), cognitive (using computation to reason about a disciplinary problem), and social (communicating computational work) — and students can develop the cognitive and social pillars substantially through [computational essay writing](../patterns/computational-essay-writing.md) even when their actual code changes are small, provided they have example computational literature to draw on.

## Subclaims
`q1 i1` Two physics students reluctant toward coding made only minor modifications to a provided simulation, yet developed sophisticated modeling practices, generated their own investigation question, and clearly communicated their reasoning and findings in their essay. [→ Odden & Zwicki 2025](#odden-zwicki-2025)

`q1 i0` Both pairs studied explicitly credited provided example essays and code seeds with helping them understand the assignment genre and formulate their investigation question; without that literature, both pairs reported they would not have understood what was expected. [→ Odden & Zwicki 2025](#odden-zwicki-2025)

`q1 i0` Two pairs with similar prior programming experience but different initial dispositions toward coding developed different strengths — one leaned toward conceptual sensemaking and interpretation, the other toward code implementation and visualization — suggesting the task supports multiple valid paths to computational literacy rather than a single one. [→ Odden & Zwicki 2025](#odden-zwicki-2025)

## Evidence

### Odden & Zwicki 2025

Odden, T. O. B., & Zwicki, B. (2025). How physics students build computational literacy by creating computational literature. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 34*(5), 700-742. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2494791](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2494791)

`q1 · case study of two student pairs, interviews and artifact analysis, no comparison condition` · `i0-i1 · qualitative evidence of learning inferred from artifacts and interviews, not a measured pre/post gain` · `n=4 undergraduate physics students (two pairs)`

Pairs of physics students extended a provided physics simulation (code seed) and wrote a computational essay documenting an original investigation. One pair, reluctant toward programming, made only minor code changes but spent substantial time on physics comprehension, comparing candidate models and interpreting anomalous results; interviews and their essay showed developed epistemic agency and clear communication despite limited coding depth. A second pair, more enthusiastic about coding, produced a more code- and visualization-heavy essay with less interpretive discussion. Both pairs explicitly credited the provided example essays and code seeds with making the open-ended assignment tractable.

## Discussion
This is a single-course case study (q1) with two student pairs (n=4) and no comparison condition, control group, or formal pre/post assessment — learning is inferred from interviews and the essays students produced, not measured directly. It was also the first open-ended computational project these students had done, so the observed growth could partly reflect novelty rather than the specific instructional design. The finding that different pairs developed different strengths (interpretation vs. implementation) suggests the task accommodates varied entry dispositions, but this is an observed pattern in two cases, not a tested design principle. Generalizability beyond this one physics course is unknown.
