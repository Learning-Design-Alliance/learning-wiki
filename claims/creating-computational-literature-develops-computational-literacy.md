# In a qualitative case study of two student pairs, creating computational literature was associated with computational literacy development even when code modification was minor

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · qualitative `r?` · `q1` · n=4 undergraduate physics students (two pairs)
> **Currency** · newest study 2025 · checked against its sources 2026-11-06 (automated, 1 of 1 entry) · not reviewed by a person

Computational literacy has three interdependent pillars — material (technical coding fluency), cognitive (using computation to reason about a disciplinary problem), and social (communicating computational work) — and students can develop the cognitive and social pillars substantially through computational essay writing even when their actual code changes are small, provided they have example computational literature to draw on.

## Evidence

### Odden & Zwickl 2025

Odden, T. O. B., & Zwickl, B. (2025). How physics students build computational literacy by creating computational literature. *Journal of the Learning Sciences, 34*(5), 700-742. [https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2494791](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2025.2494791)

`q1 · case study of two student pairs, interviews and artifact analysis, no comparison condition` · `i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may` · `n=4 undergraduate physics students (two pairs)` · `qualitative · r?`

Pairs of physics students extended a provided physics simulation (code seed) and wrote a computational essay documenting an original investigation. One pair, reluctant toward programming, made only minor code changes but spent substantial time on physics comprehension, comparing candidate models and interpreting anomalous results; interviews and their essay showed sophisticated modeling and clear communication despite limited coding depth. A second pair, more enthusiastic about coding, produced a more code- and visualization-heavy essay with less interpretive discussion. Both pairs engaged in what the authors call epistemic negotiation among their interests, the assignment's goals, their tools and their existing computational literacy, and both chose their question from a provided example simulation and its suggested questions. The second pair (Linda and Amos) spent significant time reading the example essays to understand the genre, and one said that at first "I really had no idea what we had to do"; the first pair (Norah and Natasha) is described working from the example code and its suggested question, not as crediting the example essays. The article, preprinted on arXiv as "How Physics Students Develop Disciplinary Computational Literacy", carries the journal title above in Crossref.

## Discussion
This is a single-course case study (q1) with two student pairs (n=4) and no comparison condition, control group, or formal pre/post assessment — learning is inferred from interviews and the essays students produced, not measured directly. It was also the first open-ended computational project these students had done, so the observed growth could partly reflect novelty rather than the specific instructional design. The finding that different pairs developed different strengths (interpretation vs. implementation) suggests the task accommodates varied entry dispositions, but this is an observed pattern in two cases, not a tested design principle. Generalizability beyond this one physics course is unknown.
