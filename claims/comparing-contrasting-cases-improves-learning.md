# Comparing Contrasting Cases Improves Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 studies · `q3`–`q4` · `i2` medium

Learners who compare two or more cases that differ on key features — side by side, with attention directed to what varies and why — learn the underlying principles better than learners who study the same cases one at a time.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 57 experiments and 336 tests, case-comparison activities produced greater learning than sequential or single-case study, non-analogous cases, traditional instruction and controls (d = .50), with larger benefits when learners are asked to find similarities, when the principle is given after the comparison, for perceptual content, and on immediate tests. [→ Alfieri et al. 2013](#alfieri-et-al-2013)

`q3 i?` Undergraduate novices who compared two negotiation cases on one page were more than twice as likely to transfer the principle to a new negotiation as those who studied the same two cases separately (48% vs 19%). [→ Gentner et al. 2003](#gentner-et-al-2003)

`q3 i?` Seventh-graders who compared alternative algebra solution methods side by side gained more procedural knowledge and flexibility than peers who reflected on the same methods one at a time, but gained no more conceptual knowledge. [→ Rittle-Johnson & Star 2007](#rittle-johnson-star-2007)

## Evidence

### Alfieri et al. 2013

Alfieri, L., Nokes-Malach, T. J., & Schunn, C. D. (2013). Learning through case comparisons: A meta-analytic review. *Educational Psychologist, 48*(2), 87–113. [doi:10.1080/00461520.2013.775712](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)

`q4 · meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, d=0.50, 95% CI [.44, .56]` · `n=57 experiments (336 tests)`

A random-effects meta-analysis of 57 laboratory and classroom experiments (336 tests) that compared learning from case-comparison activities with other ways of studying cases (one case, cases in sequence, non-analogous cases) and with traditional instruction or control conditions. Comparison led to greater learning overall, d = .50. Of 15 moderators tested, four reliably changed the effect: asking learners to find similarities, presenting the principle after the comparison, perceptual content, and testing immediately were all associated with larger gains. The pooled estimate mixes several kinds of comparison condition, so it is not solely a "compare vs. study the same cases one at a time" effect.

### Gentner et al. 2003

Gentner, D., Loewenstein, J., & Thompson, L. (2003). Learning and transfer: A general role for analogical encoding. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 95*(2), 393–408. [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393)

`q3 · peer-reviewed randomised experiment (3 experiments)` · `i? · no standardised effect size reported; 48% vs 19% transfer, χ²(1, N=128)=11.85` · `n=128 (Experiment 2)`

In Experiment 2, 128 undergraduates learning negotiation strategies were randomly assigned either to read two example cases on one page and describe their similarities, or to read and describe the same two cases on separate pages. On a later test negotiation, 48% of the comparison group used the target principle against 19% of the separate-cases group, and comparers were also more likely to state the principle in full. Experiment 1 found a benefit of comparison over no case study, and Experiment 3 found that more comparison support raised transfer in a face-to-face negotiation.

### Rittle-Johnson & Star 2007

Rittle-Johnson, B., & Star, J. R. (2007). Does comparing solution methods facilitate conceptual and procedural knowledge? An experimental study on learning to solve equations. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 99*(3), 561–574. [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.561](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.561)

`q3 · peer-reviewed randomised experiment` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract read` · `n=70`

Seventy seventh-grade students learning to solve algebra equations were randomly assigned to compare and contrast alternative solution methods or to reflect on the same methods one at a time. At posttest the compare group had made greater gains in procedural knowledge and procedural flexibility, and comparable gains in conceptual knowledge. This qualifies the claim: here comparison helped learners use and choose methods, not their grasp of the underlying concepts. Only the abstract was read.

## Discussion

**Why comparison helps.** Contrasting cases make the deep structure of a domain visible: features that remain constant across cases are likely central to the principle, while features that vary are incidental. Comparison also prompts learners to notice dimensions they would otherwise overlook, supporting [analogical reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) and transfer to novel situations. This aligns with the broader claim that [analogical reasoning improves transfer](analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md).

**Multiple cases and cognitive flexibility.** Studying multiple cases with varied surface features is a core prescription of cognitive flexibility theory; the specific benefit claimed here depends on learners actively *comparing* the cases rather than merely encountering them sequentially — see [Cognitive flexibility theory: multiple cases support transfer.](cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md).

**Boundary conditions.** Comparison is demanding: simultaneously holding two cases in working memory can overload novices, so the benefit likely depends on case familiarity, the salience of the contrasting features, and instructional support such as prompts naming what to compare — consistent with [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) concerns. Poorly chosen cases that differ on too many dimensions invite surface-level alignment rather than structural insight. Comparison should also be distinguished from simply reading multiple examples; without explicit comparison activity, learners often process cases independently and miss the contrast. Side-by-side spatial alignment of cases appears to matter: sequential presentation of the same cases tends to yield weaker structural alignment than simultaneous, aligned presentation.

**Expertise considerations.** As with worked examples, the value of guided comparison likely follows an expertise-reversal pattern: novices benefit from explicit prompts naming the contrasting dimensions, while advanced learners may compare spontaneously and find such scaffolding redundant. See [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md).

**Open questions.** The evidence base for this page has not yet been populated; the strength of the claim, its moderators (novice vs. advanced learners, domain, case format), and its effect sizes need to be established from the literature before the claim can be rated.
