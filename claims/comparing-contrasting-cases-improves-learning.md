# Comparing Contrasting Cases Improves Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · none recorded yet

Learners who compare two or more cases that differ on key features — side by side, with attention directed to what varies and why — learn the underlying principles better than learners who study the same cases one at a time.

## Discussion

**Why comparison helps.** Contrasting cases make the deep structure of a domain visible: features that remain constant across cases are likely central to the principle, while features that vary are incidental. Comparison also prompts learners to notice dimensions they would otherwise overlook, supporting [analogical reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) and transfer to novel situations. This aligns with the broader claim that [analogical reasoning improves transfer](analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md).

**Multiple cases and cognitive flexibility.** Studying multiple cases with varied surface features is a core prescription of cognitive flexibility theory; the specific benefit claimed here depends on learners actively *comparing* the cases rather than merely encountering them sequentially — see [Cognitive flexibility theory: multiple cases support transfer.](cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md).

**Boundary conditions.** Comparison is demanding: simultaneously holding two cases in working memory can overload novices, so the benefit likely depends on case familiarity, the salience of the contrasting features, and instructional support such as prompts naming what to compare — consistent with [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) concerns. Poorly chosen cases that differ on too many dimensions invite surface-level alignment rather than structural insight. Comparison should also be distinguished from simply reading multiple examples; without explicit comparison activity, learners often process cases independently and miss the contrast. Side-by-side spatial alignment of cases appears to matter: sequential presentation of the same cases tends to yield weaker structural alignment than simultaneous, aligned presentation.

**Expertise considerations.** As with worked examples, the value of guided comparison likely follows an expertise-reversal pattern: novices benefit from explicit prompts naming the contrasting dimensions, while advanced learners may compare spontaneously and find such scaffolding redundant. See [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md).

**Open questions.** The evidence base for this page has not yet been populated; the strength of the claim, its moderators (novice vs. advanced learners, domain, case format), and its effect sizes need to be established from the literature before the claim can be rated.
