# Self-regulated learning improves achievement

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r?`, 1 review `r3` · `q3`–`q4` · `i1`–`i2`

Learners who plan, monitor, and evaluate their own learning — and who are explicitly taught to do so — achieve better outcomes than learners who study the same material without these self-regulatory processes.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 84 intervention studies in primary and secondary school, training students in self-regulated learning produced an average effect of 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use and motivation combined, with larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training and in mathematics than in reading/writing. [→ Dignath & Büttner 2008](#dignath-buttner-2008)

`q3 i1` In elementary and secondary students, measured self-regulated learning is only weakly correlated with academic achievement (metacognitive processes r = 0.20; cognitive strategies r = 0.11), and the correlation varies by process, subject, grade level and how SRL and achievement are measured. [→ Dent & Koenka 2016](#dent-koenka-2016)

`q3 i?` In online learning environments, a systematic review of 35 studies found that most supports for self-regulated learning (prompts, integrated support systems, prompts combined with feedback) improved learner performance, while the two studies of feedback alone did not, integrated systems helped only when learners actually used them, and no pooled effect was computed. [→ Wong et al. 2019](#wong-et-al-2019)

## Evidence

### Dignath & Büttner 2008

Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level. *Metacognition and Learning, 3*(3), 231–264. [doi:10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x)

`q4 · meta-analysis of intervention studies` · `i2 · medium-to-large effect, average ES = 0.69` · `n=84 studies (357 effect sizes)` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Two separate meta-analyses pooled self-regulated learning training studies: 49 with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students, 357 effect sizes in all. Outcomes were academic performance, strategy use and motivation, and the 0.69 average covers all three together. The abstract does not give a separate figure for achievement. Meta-regression found larger effects when researchers, not regular teachers, delivered the training, and in mathematics compared with reading/writing or other subjects. The theory behind the programme and the type of strategy taught had different effects at the two school levels. Read as abstract only.

### Dent & Koenka 2016

Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 28*(3), 425–474. [doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8)

`q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies` · `i1 · small correlation, r = 0.20 (metacognitive processes), r = 0.11 (cognitive strategies)` · `n=?` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Two meta-analyses of elementary and secondary school students related achievement to (1) metacognitive processes of self-regulated learning and (2) use of cognitive strategies. Both overall correlations were small: r = 0.20 for metacognitive processes and r = 0.11 for cognitive strategies. The correlations differed significantly by the specific process or strategy, academic subject, grade level, type of SRL measure and type of achievement measure. This is the correlational arm of the claim. It supports a real but modest association, not a causal effect. The number of studies was not in the abstract. Read as abstract only.

### Wong et al. 2019

Wong, J., Baars, M., Davis, D., Van Der Zee, T., Houben, G.-J., & Paas, F. (2019). Supporting Self-Regulated Learning in Online Learning Environments and MOOCs: A Systematic Review. *International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction, 35*(4–5), 356–373. [doi:10.1080/10447318.2018.1543084](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2018.1543084)

`q3 · systematic review, narrative synthesis, no pooled estimate` · `i? · no pooled effect size reported` · `n=35 studies` · `review · r3`

A systematic review of 35 studies of approaches to support self-regulated learning in online learning environments, from seventh graders to working adults (23 at undergraduate level), grouped as prompts (14), integrated support systems (10), feedback (2), prompts with feedback (4) and other approaches (5). Most studies found positive effects on learner performance; neither feedback-only study found an effect on learning performance, and in one integrated-system study learners performed better only when they used the support provided. Few studies examined learner differences, and those that did suggest learners with lower prior knowledge, cognitive ability or metacognitive ability need additional or differentiated support. Very few experimental studies had been run in MOOCs themselves. Read in full (open access, Erasmus University repository).

## Discussion

Self-regulated learning (SRL) is not a single technique but a cycle of forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, self-control), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, adaptive adjustment). The claim covers both (a) correlations between learners' existing self-regulatory skill and achievement, and (b) causal effects of explicitly training or scaffolding SRL strategies. These are distinct claims with distinct evidence bases: correlational studies cannot rule out that stronger students self-regulate more, while training studies vary widely in dosage, duration, and whether strategies are taught generically or embedded in a specific domain.

Key moderators to establish when evidence is added: the learner's age and prior achievement (younger and lower-achieving learners typically have the most room to gain from SRL instruction, because they default to passive rehearsal); whether support is a standalone curriculum or woven into subject-matter instruction (embedded support usually transfers better); and whether the strategies taught are actually appropriate to the task — teaching monitoring strategies without accompanying control strategies can leave learners aware of their confusion but unable to act on it. SRL also interacts with motivation: strategies are only deployed when learners value the goal and expect success, which links this claim to motivational mechanisms such as autonomy and competence support (see [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) and [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md)).

Boundary conditions expected from the broader literature: benefits shrink for highly expert learners who already regulate effectively, and poorly designed strategy training (short, decontextualized, or focused on surface behaviors like highlighting) can produce no gain or even displace more effective study time. Measurement is a further open question — much of the literature relies on self-report questionnaires, which correlate only modestly with trace or online measures of actual regulatory behavior, so the correlational arm of this claim may look stronger or weaker depending on how SRL is operationalized. The theoretical framing lives in [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

**Evidence status.** Three studies are recorded on this page: a meta-analysis of SRL training in primary and secondary school (Dignath & Büttner 2008), whose average of 0.69 combines performance, strategy use and motivation; a meta-analysis of the correlation between measured SRL and achievement (Dent & Koenka 2016, r = 0.11–0.20); and a systematic review of SRL supports in online learning with no pooled effect (Wong et al. 2019). Not yet recorded here: an effect of SRL training on achievement alone, and one that distinguishes embedded from standalone instruction.

*Merged from “Self Regulated Learning Improves Academic Outcomes” (self-regulated-learning-improves-academic-outcomes):* Self-regulated learning (SRL) is typically described as a cyclical process with three phases: forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, self-control of attention and effort), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, adaptive adjustment after feedback). Interventions operationalize these phases through [goal setting](../elements/goal-setting.md), planning templates, self-questioning prompts, and structured [reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md) routines embedded in subject-matter instruction.

Important moderators shape when and how strongly SRL instruction pays off:

- **Novice advantage.** Novices often lack the domain knowledge to judge their own understanding accurately, so explicit strategy instruction and external scaffolds (prompts, planning templates, checklists) tend to matter most early in learning; as expertise develops, learners can internalize regulation and external supports may become redundant — a pattern consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M].
- **Support vs. autonomy.** SRL thrives when learners have meaningful choices about goals, strategies, and pacing; environments that support [autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) foster the motivation that regulation requires, whereas purely compliance-driven settings can undermine it [~M].
- **Accuracy of self-assessment.** Regulation is only as good as the monitoring it rests on. Learners frequently misjudge their own comprehension, so interventions that pair self-monitoring with accurate external feedback — [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) practices — are more effective than reflection prompts alone [~S].
- **Domain specificity.** Regulatory strategies transfer imperfectly across domains; embedding strategy instruction within authentic subject-matter tasks outperforms teaching generic "study skills" in isolation [~M].
- **Cognitive cost of regulation.** Self-monitoring and strategy switching consume working-memory resources; for novices already near [cognitive load](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) limits, heavy regulatory demands during [practice](../elements/practice.md) can compete with learning the content itself [~M].

Constraints on the claim's scope: SRL instruction shows weak or null effects when delivered as decontextualized study-skills training [-W], when learners lack the motivation to apply strategies (regulation without [intrinsic motivation](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) tends to decay) [-M], and when self-report-based "regulation" reflects learners describing desirable behaviors rather than actually enacting them — a measurement problem that complicates much of the supporting literature [~W].

Open questions include how durable SRL gains are over time, how best to measure regulation (self-report questionnaires versus trace data from learning platforms), and how [adaptive learning](../principles/adaptive-learning.md) technologies should balance learner control against algorithmic scaffolding.

*Merged from “Self-regulated learning improves outcomes” (self-regulated-learning-improves-outcomes):* SRL is typically described as a cyclical process of forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, self-control), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, adaptive adjustment) — see [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). The claim that SRL training improves outcomes is broadly supported in the literature [+M] — meta-analyses of SRL interventions report positive effects on academic performance, with the largest gains when strategy instruction is embedded in subject-matter content rather than taught as a generic study-skills course [~M]. However, no specific studies have yet been added to this page's Evidence section, so the strength and scope of the claim cannot be finalized here.

Key moderators and boundary conditions to document when evidence is added:

- **Novice dependence.** SRL strategies impose demands on working memory and prior knowledge; learners with weak domain knowledge may benefit less from self-directed strategy use until foundational skills are in place [~M] — consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md). This mirrors the pattern seen in [cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md): self-imposed strategic demands compete with the intrinsic demands of the task itself.
- **Strategy quality matters.** Not all self-regulatory activity is beneficial. Superficial strategies (e.g., rereading, highlighting) show weak or null effects [-M], while elaborative and metacognitive strategies (self-explanation, retrieval practice, planning) show stronger ones [+M]. An SRL claim that aggregates all strategies will overstate the effect — compare [Learner highlighting of text gives a small memory benefit but no reliable comprehension benefit, and evidence on other forms of annotation is not yet recorded here](annotating-improves-learning.md), which isolates one concrete strategy with its own evidence base.
- **Measurement heterogeneity.** Studies measure SRL via self-report questionnaires, think-aloud protocols, and trace data, which correlate poorly with each other; effect sizes vary substantially by measure [~M]. Cross-study synthesis should treat measurement type as a moderator.
- **Training vs. disposition.** Interventions that *teach* SRL strategies differ from studies that merely *measure* SRL and correlate it with achievement; causal claims rest on the former. Correlational designs cannot rule out that stronger learners self-regulate more because they find the material easier.
- **Motivational preconditions.** Strategy instruction is unlikely to succeed where learners lack the motivation to deploy strategies [~W]; autonomy-supportive contexts — see [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) — are a plausible enabling condition rather than a substitute for strategy training.
- **Duration and transfer.** Short single-session interventions tend to produce smaller and less durable effects than extended training with opportunities to apply strategies across tasks [~M]; whether trained strategies transfer beyond the trained domain remains an open question.

**Design implication.** Until evidence entries are added, practitioners should treat this claim as a family-level generalization: embed strategy instruction in authentic subject-matter tasks, model the forethought–performance–reflection cycle explicitly, and avoid equating any self-directed activity with effective self-regulation.

*Merged from “Self Regulated Learning Strategies Improve Achievement” (self-regulated-learning-strategies-improve-achievement):* SRL interventions typically train one or more of three phases from Zimmerman's cyclical model: forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, self-instruction, attention control), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, attributing outcomes to strategy use rather than fixed ability). The claim as stated is broad; the strength of the effect depends heavily on which strategies are trained, how explicitly, and for whom. Until Evidence entries are added, treat the claim as plausible but under-documented.

Key moderators to document when evidence is added:

- **Strategy type.** Meta-analytic work (e.g., Dignath & Büttner on elementary classrooms; Hattie, Biggs & Purdie across levels) suggests effects are larger when training is embedded in subject-matter instruction rather than delivered as a standalone study-skills course [~M]. Generic "learning-to-learn" courses show weak transfer [-M].
- **Learner age and prior knowledge.** Younger learners and novices benefit from highly explicit, teacher-modeled strategy instruction; older learners can profit from more self-directed formats [~M]. Very low-prior-knowledge learners may lack the domain resources to deploy strategies effectively [-W].
- **Duration and practice.** One-shot workshops show little durable effect [-W]; sustained training with practice and feedback in authentic tasks is the condition under which achievement gains appear [+M].

Boundary conditions: SRL strategy training presumes learners have some metacognitive capacity and motivational readiness; it does not substitute for adequate prior knowledge or [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). For novices, heavy self-directed demands can itself impose extraneous load — see [Cognitive load reduction improves learning](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) [-M]. This parallels the expertise-reversal pattern documented for scaffolding generally: supports that help novices can burden them differently as competence grows.

Design implication: pair explicit SRL strategy instruction with [clear structure](clear-structure-improves-learning.md) and modeling, then fade the scaffolds as learners internalize the regulatory cycle. [Assessment for learning](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) provides natural opportunities to practice the self-reflection phase, since feedback invites learners to evaluate outcomes against goals and attribute success or failure to strategy use. Concrete, trainable strategies that map onto the three phases include [goal setting](../strategies/achievable_micro-goals.md) and planning in forethought, [annotating](annotating-improves-learning.md) and self-questioning during performance, and structured [reflection prompts](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md) during self-reflection.

Open questions for future enrichment: whether effects persist after training ends, whether gains transfer across domains or remain domain-bound, and how SRL training interacts with [adaptive learning](adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) environments that externalize some regulatory decisions.

*Merged from “Self Regulated Learning Strategies Improve Performance” (self-regulated-learning-strategies-improve-performance):* Self-regulated learning (SRL) strategies span the full learning cycle: forethought (goal setting, strategic planning), performance (self-monitoring, attention control, help-seeking), and self-reflection (self-evaluation, adaptive strategy adjustment). Interventions typically work by making these processes explicit — through modeling, prompts, checklists, or structured reflection routines — rather than by merely describing them.

Several boundary conditions are well established in the literature. SRL strategy training shows the strongest effects when strategies are embedded in a specific subject domain rather than taught as generic study skills; decontextualized "learning-to-learn" courses tend to produce weak transfer [~M]. Effects are generally larger for younger and lower-achieving learners, who have more room to adopt effective strategies, while already-skilled learners may gain little — a pattern consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Strategy training also imposes its own cognitive cost: prompting learners to plan and monitor while simultaneously processing new content can overload working memory if the strategies themselves are not yet automatized [~M], so scaffolds should be faded as strategy use becomes routine — see [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) and [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md).

A further moderator is duration and dosage: one-off strategy instruction rarely changes behavior, whereas interventions that cycle modeling, guided practice, and feedback over weeks are more likely to produce habitual strategy use [~M]. Prompt-based supports (e.g., embedded planning and reflection prompts in online learning) show positive but smaller and more variable effects than face-to-face training, partly because prompts are easy to ignore without accountability structures [~W].

Open questions include how durable the gains are once prompts and scaffolds are removed, and how well SRL interventions transfer from controlled studies to ordinary classroom conditions, where teacher time and learner motivation vary widely. The training meta-analysis recorded above found larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training.

*Merged from “Self Regulated Learning Strategy Instruction Improves Achievement” (self-regulated-learning-strategy-instruction-improves-achievement):* SRL instruction is typically delivered through one of two routes: embedding strategy instruction within subject-matter teaching (e.g., teaching students to plan, monitor, and evaluate while solving math problems), or teaching strategies as a separate study-skills curriculum. The embedded route is generally considered more promising because strategies are practiced with real content, whereas decontextualized study-skills courses often fail to transfer — a boundary condition that any future evidence entries should distinguish.

Key moderators to attend to when evidence is added: learner age and prior knowledge (younger and lower-achieving students tend to benefit more from highly explicit, scaffolded strategy instruction, consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)); duration and dosage (brief one-off workshops on "learning styles" or generic tips show little effect, while sustained practice with feedback is the design that meta-analyses credit); and which phase of SRL is targeted (forethought, performance, or self-reflection). Instruction that combines [cognitive strategies](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) with metacognitive monitoring and motivational components (e.g., self-efficacy) is typically stronger than strategy training alone, and connects to broader motivation claims such as [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md).

The theoretical grounding is [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) as a cyclical process (Zimmerman's forethought–performance–reflection model), in which learners who set goals, monitor comprehension, and adjust tactics use study time more effectively. Note also that strategy instruction interacts with [cognitive load](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): novices may need strategies modeled and scaffolded before they can apply them without overloading working memory.

**Measurement caveat.** Much SRL research relies on self-report questionnaires, which correlate only weakly with actual strategy use; studies that measure online traces, think-alouds, or performance outcomes should be weighted more heavily when evidence entries are added.

**Boundary conditions.** SRL instruction is unlikely to help when learners already use effective strategies (redundancy), when the strategies taught are generic rather than tied to the domain's demands, or when instruction is delivered as information-only without guided practice and feedback. Claims that generic "study skills" courses alone raise achievement should be treated skeptically relative to claims about embedded, scaffolded strategy instruction.

**Open questions.** Whether SRL instruction produces durable, transferable strategy use or only near-term gains in the trained domain remains contested; how much learner autonomy should accompany scaffolding (over-scaffolding may undercut the development of self-regulation itself); and whether effects differ across achievement levels once floor and ceiling effects are controlled.

*Merged from “Self Regulation Strategy Instruction Improves Academic Performance” (self-regulation-strategy-instruction-improves-academic-performance):* Self-regulation strategy instruction sits within the broader tradition described in [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): learners who set goals, monitor comprehension, and adjust their approach allocate effort more effectively than learners who rely on implicit habits. Instruction that makes these strategies explicit — modeling, guided practice, and fading — is generally distinguished from merely encouraging students to "try harder"; the claim here concerns strategy instruction specifically, not motivational exhortation.

Several boundary conditions are plausible from general reasoning about scope. First, strategy instruction should interact with learner expertise: novices may benefit from tightly scaffolded, domain-specific strategies, while more advanced learners may find prescriptive strategy routines redundant, consistent with the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) documented for other scaffolds. Second, generic study-skill training delivered outside any content domain has historically shown weaker effects than strategies embedded in authentic academic tasks — the strategy must be practiced on the material learners are actually trying to master. Third, benefits likely depend on learners actually adopting the strategies; instruction that produces knowledge about strategies without sustained practice in using them should not be expected to improve performance.

Open questions that evidence entries should address include: which strategy families (cognitive, metacognitive, resource management) drive the largest gains; how durable the effects are after instruction ends; and how effects vary by age band and subject domain.

**Online and self-paced settings (Wong et al. 2019).** The two meta-analyses above are of primary and secondary school students. The online-learning review extends the direction of the claim to online environments but qualifies it: providing support is not sufficient on its own (learners gained only when they used the support), feedback alone showed no effect on performance, and the review computed no effect size, so it cannot say how large the gain is.

*Merged from “Self-regulation strategy instruction improves achievement” (self-regulation-strategy-instruction-improves-achievement):* **Mechanism.** Self-regulation strategy instruction works by making learners active managers of their own cognition: they set goals, select strategies, monitor comprehension and progress, and adjust effort or approach when feedback indicates a mismatch. This aligns with the broader model described in [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). Strategy instruction is closely related to [metacognitive prompts and reflection](../claims/metacognitive-prompts-improve-learning.md), which operationalize the monitoring step.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** Effects are generally strongest when strategies are taught explicitly, modeled, and practiced within subject-matter content rather than as generic study-skills sessions detached from coursework. Benefits also depend on learners having enough prior knowledge to monitor meaningfully — novices who lack domain knowledge may misjudge their understanding, and strategy instruction cannot substitute for [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) during initial skill acquisition. Younger learners and low-achieving students typically need more scaffolding and external prompts before self-regulation becomes self-sustaining.

**Open questions.** How durable strategy gains are after instruction ends, and how much training is needed before learners transfer strategies to new domains, remain under-established in the current evidence base for this claim. The training meta-analysis above reports larger effects when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered the training, so classroom-scale effects may be smaller than the pooled estimate.
