# Desirable Difficulties Enhance Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 studies · `q4` pre-registered or meta-analytic · `i2` medium

Introducing certain effortful conditions during learning — such as retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving — can slow visible performance during instruction while improving long-term retention and transfer. The claim is about *desirable* difficulties specifically: conditions that are effortful but surmountable, not obstacles that exceed the learner's capacity.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` Across 222 classroom studies with 48,478 students, practice testing (quizzing) raised academic achievement by a medium amount compared with restudying and other strategies, g = 0.499. [→ Yang et al. 2021](#yang-et-al-2021)

`q4 i2` Spacing retrieval practice out over time produced much better final retention than massing it (g = 0.74, 29 studies), but expanding intervals were no better than uniform ones (g = 0.034). [→ Latimier et al. 2020](#latimier-et-al-2020)

`q4 i2` Interleaving produced a moderate overall benefit for inductive (category) learning (g = 0.42, 59 studies), but it depended on the material: blocking beat interleaving for words (g = -0.39), and results for expository texts were ambiguous. [→ Brunmair & Richter 2019](#brunmair-richter-2019)

## Evidence

### Yang et al. 2021

Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. *Psychological Bulletin, 147*(4), 399–435. [doi:10.1037/bul0000309](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)

`q4 · systematic review and meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.499` · `n=222 studies (48,478 students)`

A systematic review and meta-analysis of the testing effect in real classrooms rather than the laboratory, pooling 222 independent studies with 48,478 students. Taking quizzes or practice tests on studied material ([retrieval practice](../elements/retrieval-practice.md)) raised academic achievement by a medium amount compared with restudying and other learning strategies. The size of the benefit varied with what the control group did, whether the practice and final tests had the same format, whether corrective feedback was given, how many times learners were tested, and the study design.

### Latimier et al. 2020

Latimier, A., Peyre, H., & Ramus, F. (2020). A Meta-Analytic Review of the Benefit of Spacing out Retrieval Practice Episodes on Retention. *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(3), 959–987. [doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)

`q4 · meta-analysis (robust variance estimation)` · `i2 · medium-to-large effect, g=0.74` · `n=29 studies (39 + 54 effect sizes)`

A meta-analysis of 29 studies on spaced retrieval practice, which the authors describe as combining two "desirable difficulties": retrieval practice and spacing. Spreading repeated retrieval of the same content over time gave a strong retention advantage over massing it (g = 0.74, 39 effect sizes). Expanding the gaps between retrievals was no better than keeping them even (g = 0.034, 54 effect sizes), although expanding schedules helped more when items were tested more often.

### Brunmair & Richter 2019

Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. *Psychological Bulletin, 145*(11), 1029–1052. [doi:10.1037/bul0000209](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)

`q4 · multilevel meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.42` · `n=59 studies (158 samples, 238 effect sizes)`

A multilevel meta-analysis comparing interleaved with blocked presentation of items for inductive (category) learning, across 59 studies. Interleaving helped by a moderate amount overall, most for paintings and other visual materials (g = 0.67) and less for mathematical tasks (g = 0.34). It **qualifies** the claim: for word materials blocking beat interleaving (g = -0.39), and effects for expository texts and tastes were not significant. Interleaving helped more when categories were similar to each other and items within a category were less similar, so the difficulty is desirable only for some materials.

## Discussion

**The difficulty must be surmountable.** Desirable difficulties work because effortful retrieval and discrimination trigger deeper processing and strengthen memory traces — but only when learners can actually overcome the difficulty. If the challenge exceeds available working-memory resources, it becomes an undesirable difficulty and performance degrades, consistent with [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) and [Chunking reduces working memory load.](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) The designer's task is to keep difficulty within the learner's zone of capability, which is the same constraint that governs [Cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md) and underpins [Cognitive load reduction improves learning.](cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) — reducing extraneous load is precisely what frees capacity so that the intrinsic difficulty of retrieval and discrimination can be productive rather than overwhelming.

**Expertise reversal.** The same difficulty that helps novices can hinder advanced learners. As expertise grows, conditions that force extra effort on already-mastered material become redundant or counterproductive — see [Expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) and [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md). Difficulty should be faded or re-targeted as competence develops, not held constant.

**Transfer appropriateness.** A difficulty is most desirable when it mirrors the conditions of the eventual test or use: practicing under varied, delayed, or interleaved conditions prepares learners for retrieval situations that blocked, immediate practice does not. Feedback must still be available so that errors made under difficulty are corrected rather than reinforced.

**Fluency misjudgment.** Learners judge their learning by momentary fluency, so they often prefer easier strategies that produce worse long-term outcomes — and may resist conditions that feel harder. Designers should expect short-term performance dips, communicate the rationale for effortful practice, and use assessments that reward delayed retention rather than immediate performance; productive struggle connects to [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md)

**Evidence status.** No specific studies are catalogued in the Evidence section yet; the framework originates with Robert Bjork's work on desirable difficulties, and key experimental and meta-analytic support (particularly for retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving) still needs to be added before this claim can be rated.

**Open questions.** How much difficulty remains desirable as expertise grows, and how difficulty should be calibrated dynamically over the course of instruction, remain open design questions. Adaptive approaches that adjust challenge to current learner state — see [Adaptive learning improves outcomes.](adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) — are one candidate mechanism, but their fit with deliberately *under*-scaffolding practice has not been established here.
