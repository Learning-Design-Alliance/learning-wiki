## Evidence

### Rusk A 2012

Rusk A, Smith N, Menya D, Obala A, Simiyu C, Khwa-Otsyula B, O'Meara W. (2012). Does anti-malarial drug knowledge predict anti-malarial dispensing practice in drug outlets? A survey of medicine retailers in western Kenya. Malaria Journal. https://doi.org/10.1186/1475-2875-11-263

`q2 · i2` · `associational · r2`

Cross-sectional survey analysis of 115 retailers using unadjusted odds ratios: 65% identified AL correctly, and participants with health training had "odds of knowing the recommended treatment were higher" (OR = 3.26, 95% CI 1.23 – 8.63; p = 0.017). Both pharmacy-trained and nurse/midwifery-trained retailers outperformed the untrained.

> "Most of the population surveyed correctly identified the first-line treatment (65%), but the odds of knowing the recommended treatment were higher amongst participants with health training (OR = 3.26, 95% CI 1.23 – 8.63; p = 0.017)."

### Rusk A 2012 (2)

Rusk A, Smith N, Menya D, Obala A, Simiyu C, Khwa-Otsyula B, O'Meara W. (2012). Does anti-malarial drug knowledge predict anti-malarial dispensing practice in drug outlets? A survey of medicine retailers in western Kenya. Malaria Journal. https://doi.org/10.1186/1475-2875-11-263

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Survey analysis found education level "was not related to anti-malarial dug knowledge" (as printed): 62% of secondary-or-below versus 68% of above-secondary retailers identified AL correctly; the table shows OR = 1.27 (0.56 - 2.87; 0.572), non-significant, no effect size reported.

> "Level of education was not related to anti-malarial dug knowledge. Sixty-two percent of those with a secondary education or below identified AL as the MOH recommended first-line therapy for malaria, and 68% of those with above a secondary education identified the first-line anti-malarial medication."
