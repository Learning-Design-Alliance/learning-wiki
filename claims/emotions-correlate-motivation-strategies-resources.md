# Positive academic emotions correlate positively with interest, effort, flexible learning strategies, and perceived self-regulation, while boredom and hopelessness correlate negatively with all of these motivational variables

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · associational `r1`–`r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` In a sample of 230 university students, positive emotions (enjoyment, hope, pride) correlated positively with study interest, effort, elaboration, and self-regulation, whereas boredom and hopelessness correlated negatively with all motivational variables and effort. [→ Reinhard Pekrun 2002](#reinhard-pekrun-2002)
`q2 i?` Negative emotions correlated positively with task-irrelevant thinking, with boredom showing especially strong relations because mental withdrawal and daydreaming are strongly tied to it. [→ Reinhard Pekrun 2002 (2)](#reinhard-pekrun-2002-2)

## Evidence

### Reinhard Pekrun 2002

Reinhard Pekrun, Thomas Goetz, Wolfram Titz, & Raymond P. Perry. (2002). Academic Emotions in Students' Self-Regulated Learning and Achievement: A Program of Qualitative and Quantitative Research. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3702_4

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Cross-sectional correlational analysis of trait learning-related emotions and components of self-regulated learning in 230 university students (Titz, 2001, Study 2), reported in Table 4 with exemplary correlations such as enjoyment-study interest r = .62.

> "In contrast, the negative deactivating emotions of boredom and hopelessness correlated negatively with all of these motivational variables and with self-reported effort (cf. Table 4), thus suggesting that these two emotions may be detrimental for students' academic motivation."

### Reinhard Pekrun 2002 (2)

Reinhard Pekrun, Thomas Goetz, Wolfram Titz, & Raymond P. Perry. (2002). Academic Emotions in Students' Self-Regulated Learning and Achievement: A Program of Qualitative and Quantitative Research. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3702_4

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Same correlational dataset (N = 230 university students). The authors report relations of other negative emotions to irrelevant thinking are at least as strong as for test anxiety, exemplified by boredom's r = .72 with irrelevant thinking in Table 4.

> "Negative emotions, on the other hand, correlated positively with task-irrelevant thinking. In test anxiety research, irrelevant thinking has of-ten been defined as a component of this specific emotion (cf. Hodapp & Benson, 1997)."
