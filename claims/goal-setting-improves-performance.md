## Discussion

**The claim is underspecified as stated.** "Goal setting" and "performance" each hide the moderators that determine whether the effect holds. The goal-setting literature conventionally distinguishes specific, challenging goals from vague intentions ("do your best"), and distinguishes learning goals (focused on strategy acquisition) from performance goals (focused on outcome attainment) — with the latter sometimes counterproductive on complex or novel tasks where early errors are informative. Until evidence is curated here, the page cannot say which version of the claim is supported, at what strength, or for which learners.

**Boundary conditions to test when evidence is added.** Plausible moderators include: goal specificity and difficulty; whether goals are assigned or self-set (see [Autonomy Supports Intrinsic Motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) and [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md)); whether learners receive feedback on progress toward the goal (see [Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md)); task complexity, which interacts with goal type in ways consistent with [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md); and goal proximity, where proximal subgoals may sustain effort better than distant targets (see [Achievable Micro-Goals](../strategies/achievable_micro-goals.md)). Goal setting is also a core phase of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md), so effects may be mediated by learners' monitoring and strategy adjustment rather than by the goal itself.

**Open questions.** Does the effect hold for novices as well as experienced learners? Does it transfer beyond the specific task the goal targets? Are effects on motivation durable or limited to short, monitored tasks? These should be answered by the Evidence entries added in a future enrichment pass.
