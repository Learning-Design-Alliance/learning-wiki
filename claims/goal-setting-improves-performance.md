## Subclaims

`q3 i2` Across 125 goal-setting studies on task performance, specific difficult (and hard vs. easy) goals raised performance, but the effect shrank with task complexity, from d = .76 on simple tasks to d = .42 on complex ones. [→ Wood et al. 1987](#wood-et-al-1987)

`q4 i1` Across 141 papers of randomized trials (384 effect sizes, N = 16,523), setting a goal had a small unique effect on behavior change, d = .34, which was larger when goals were difficult, set publicly, or set for a group. ## Evidence

### Wood et al. 1987

Wood, R. E., Mento, A. J., & Locke, E. A. (1987). Task complexity as a moderator of goal effects: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 72*(3), 416–425. [doi:10.1037/0021-9010.72.3.416](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.72.3.416)

`q3 · meta-analysis (1987, not pre-registered)` · `i2 · medium effect, d=.42 (complex tasks) to d=.76 (simple tasks)` · `n=125 studies`

A meta-analysis of goal-setting studies published from 1966 to 1985 on how task complexity changes the effect of goals. The authors ran three sets of analyses: hard versus easy goals, specific difficult goals versus "do your best" or no goal, and all studies combined. Goals improved performance in every set, but most on simple tasks such as reaction time and brainstorming (d = .76) and least on complex tasks such as business simulations, scientific and engineering work, and faculty research productivity (d = .42). Read as abstract only. It supports the claim for specific, challenging goals, and says the effect is weaker on the complex, novel work that learning often involves.

### Epton et al. 2017

Epton, T., Currie, S., & Armitage, C. J. (2017). Unique effects of setting goals on behavior change: Systematic review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 85*(12), 1182–1198. [doi:10.1037/ccp0000260](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000260)

`q4 · systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials` · `i1 · small effect, d=.34 (95% CI .28–.41)` · `n=141 papers, 384 effect sizes, 16,523 participants`

A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials that isolated goal setting from other parts of a behavior change intervention. Goal setting had a small positive effect across behaviors (d = .34). It worked best when the goal was difficult, set publicly, or a group goal. There was weaker evidence that it worked better with external monitoring by others and with face-to-face delivery. Read as abstract only. The outcomes are behaviors, mainly in health settings, rather than learning or academic performance, so they bear on the claim only by analogy.

## Discussion

**The claim is underspecified as stated.** "Goal setting" and "performance" each hide the moderators that determine whether the effect holds. The goal-setting literature conventionally distinguishes specific, challenging goals from vague intentions ("do your best"), and distinguishes learning goals (focused on strategy acquisition) from performance goals (focused on outcome attainment) — with the latter sometimes counterproductive on complex or novel tasks where early errors are informative. Until evidence is curated here, the page cannot say which version of the claim is supported, at what strength, or for which learners.

**Boundary conditions to test when evidence is added.** Plausible moderators include: goal specificity and difficulty; whether goals are assigned or self-set (see [Autonomy Supports Intrinsic Motivation](autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) and [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md)); whether learners receive feedback on progress toward the goal (see [Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md)); task complexity, which interacts with goal type in ways consistent with [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md); and goal proximity, where proximal subgoals may sustain effort better than distant targets (see [Achievable Micro-Goals](../strategies/achievable_micro-goals.md)). Goal setting is also a core phase of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md), so effects may be mediated by learners' monitoring and strategy adjustment rather than by the goal itself.

**Open questions.** Does the effect hold for novices as well as experienced learners? Does it transfer beyond the specific task the goal targets? Are effects on motivation durable or limited to short, monitored tasks? These should be answered by the Evidence entries added in a future enrichment pass.
