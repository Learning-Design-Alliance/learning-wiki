Evidence¶

Madelon van den Boer 2022¶

Madelon van den Boer, Loes Bazen, Elise de Bree. (2022). The Same yet Different: Oral and Silent Reading in Children and Adolescents with Dyslexia. Journal of Psycholinguistic Research. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-022-09856-w

q3 · i3 · associational · r2

Study 1 correlations: oral–silent r = 0.922 overall, r = 0.862 for children with dyslexia and r = 0.840 for typical readers (all p < 0.001), indicating the two modes are largely interchangeable in primary school children.

"Oral and silent text reading fluency were very strongly correlated for the total group of participants (r = 0.922, p < 0.001)"

Madelon van den Boer 2022 (2)¶

Madelon van den Boer, Loes Bazen, Elise de Bree. (2022). The Same yet Different: Oral and Silent Reading in Children and Adolescents with Dyslexia. Journal of Psycholinguistic Research. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-022-09856-w

q3 · i3 · associational · r1

Study 2 correlations: oral–silent r = 0.784 overall, r = 0.646 (dyslexia) and r = 0.676 (typical). For typical readers the standardized One Minute Test correlated with oral but not silent text reading (Fisher's z = 2.597, p < 0.001); for adolescents with dyslexia the two correlations were similar (Fisher's z = 0.799, p = 0.212).

"for typical readers the correlation with oral reading was stronger than with silent reading (oral: r = 0.662, p = 0.001; silent: r = 0.283, p = 0.191; Fisher's z = 2.597, p < 0.001)"

Related Claims¶

  • For children and adolescents with dyslexia, the silent reading deficit is as large as or larger than the oral reading deficit — related
  • Children with dyslexia read texts significantly more slowly than typical readers in both oral and silent reading — related
  • Adolescents read silently faster than orally, and this oral–silent difference is smaller for adolescents with dyslexia than for typical readers — related
  • The oral–silent reading difference increases with reading experience, plausibly because a less extensive orthographic lexicon forces dyslexic readers to rely on phonological processing during silent reading — related
  • For children in Grades 3–5, with and without dyslexia, oral and silent text reading fluency do not differ — related
  • Word-level reading fluency is positively associated with intertextual integration among K-12 students, including readers with developmental dyslexia — related
