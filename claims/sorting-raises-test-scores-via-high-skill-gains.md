# Average test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skill, driven by large learning gains for high-skilled students and slightly lower scores for low-skilled students

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Overall test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skills because high-skilled students show large learning gains under sorting. [→ Elaine Allensworth and Takako Nomi 2014](#elaine-allensworth-and-takako-nomi-2014)
`q2 i?` Low-skilled students have slightly lower test scores with sorting, and on average the gains for high-achievers offset the losses for low-achievers. [→ Elaine Allensworth and Takako Nomi 2014](#elaine-allensworth-and-takako-nomi-2014)

## Evidence

### Elaine Allensworth and Takako Nomi 2014

Elaine Allensworth and Takako Nomi. (2014). Skill-Based Sorting in the Era of College Prep for All. University of Chicago Consortium on Chicago School Research. https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/skill-based-sorting-era-college-prep-all-costs-and-benefits

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r?`

The CCSR report compares Chicago Public Schools reforms that sorted algebra students by ability versus de-sorted them. It reports that sorting raises overall test scores, quoting that "test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skills," with no effect sizes printed in the release.

> "Overall, test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skills due to large benefits for high-skilled students' learning gains. Low-skilled students have slightly lower test scores with sorting, while high-skilled students have substantially higher test scores."
