Evidence¶

Watson et al. 2017¶

Watson, A., Timperio, A., Brown, H., Best, K., & Hesketh, K. D. (2017). Effect of classroom-based physical activity interventions on academic and physical activity outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis. International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity, 14(1), 114. doi:10.1186/s12966-017-0569-9

q3 · systematic review and meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect on classroom behaviour, SMD=0.60 (95% CI 0.20–1.00) · n=39 studies reviewed (16 meta-analysed; 4 in the classroom-behaviour pool) · quant-synthesis · r2

A systematic review of classroom-based physical activity interventions (active breaks and physically active lessons) in primary-school children. The pooled estimate from four studies found a medium positive effect on observed on-task and off-task behaviour. Most studies measuring behaviour (10 of 12) reported an acute gain, but the few with longer follow-up suggested it may fade. Too few studies measured chronic effects to separate acute from chronic results in the pooled estimate. Cognitive functions such as executive function showed no significant pooled effect, and the authors warn that high heterogeneity in interventions and outcomes rules out firm conclusions.

Daly-Smith et al. 2018¶

Daly-Smith, A. J., Zwolinsky, S., McKenna, J., Tomporowski, P. D., Defeyter, M. A., & Manley, A. (2018). Systematic review of acute physically active learning and classroom movement breaks on children's physical activity, cognition, academic performance and classroom behaviour: understanding critical design features. BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, 4(1), e000341. doi:10.1136/bmjsem-2018-000341

q3 · systematic review (PROSPERO-registered), no meta-analysis · i? · no pooled effect size reported · n=18 studies (10 physically active learning, 8 classroom movement breaks) · review · r2

A registered systematic review of single (acute) bouts of classroom movement breaks and physically active learning in children and adolescents aged 4 to 17. Risk-of-bias scores were low to medium (33% to 64.3%). Classroom behaviour, measured mostly as time on task, improved after a bout in 9 of 11 interventions, depending on its duration and intensity. There was no consistent evidence of better cognition or academic performance. Attention was tested in three or more studies and improved after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after longer 10–12-minute moderate-to-vigorous bouts. This qualifies the claim: the evidence is for on-task behaviour more than for attention as measured by cognitive tests.

Discussion¶

Scope and mechanism. The claim concerns acute effects: a single bout of moderate physical activity transiently elevates arousal and executive attention, which can translate into more on-task behavior immediately afterward. This is distinct from claims about fitness or chronic exercise, and distinct from effects on memory consolidation — see Acute exercise timing affects memory for how the timing of activity relative to learning changes the outcome.

Dose and intensity matter. In the review recorded here (Daly-Smith et al. 2018), attention tests improved after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after longer 10–12-minute moderate-to-vigorous bouts, while time on task improved after bouts of more than 10 minutes or shorter, more intense 5-minute bouts. That very vigorous activity immediately before academic tasks leaves students aroused but not better focused is unsourced on this page and runs against that attention finding. Overly long activity bouts consume instructional time that may not be recovered. This dose-sensitivity parallels the way cognitive load management treats any intervention: the same activity can help or hinder depending on intensity and placement.

Boundary conditions. Benefits are most consistently reported for younger learners and for students with elevated attentional or self-regulation difficulties (unsourced on this page: neither review recorded here reports this moderation); for already-engaged learners the effect may be small or undetectable. Activities that are themselves cognitively demanding (e.g., complex coordination tasks) may compete for the same executive resources the activity is meant to refresh — see Cognitive overload degrades learning.

Classroom integration. Movement breaks work best as a deliberate part of clear lesson structure — a predictable transition between segments — rather than as unstructured downtime. Embedding movement into content itself (active lessons where students move to demonstrate or retrieve material) may combine the attentional reset with active learning benefits, though the two mechanisms should not be conflated.

Open questions. Whether repeated daily activity breaks produce cumulative gains in attention or achievement, and how effects vary by subject area and task type, remain under-studied. The two reviews recorded here cover mostly acute effects in primary-school children; neither establishes a gain on attention tests that generalises across doses.
