# Learning nonsense syllables backward takes a higher total-repetition ratio than forward, suggesting interference between habits

## Subclaims
`q1 i?` In a six-subject nonsense-syllable experiment, the ratio of total repetitions to first-learning repetitions averaged 1.2 forward and 1.4 backward, which the author attributes to interference from the forward habit. [→ Pyle 1917](#pyle-1917)

## Evidence

### Pyle 1917

Pyle, W. H. (1917). Retention as Related to Repetition. The Journal of Educational Psychology.

`q1 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Six subjects learned 25 nonsense syllables forward to automatization by the Ebbinghaus metronome method, then backward. The printed average ratios are 1.2 forward and 1.4 backward; the interference account is the author's interpretation of this small dataset.

> "That the learning backward was relatively harder to carry to the point of automatization was doubtless due to the interference of the habit of saying them forward."
