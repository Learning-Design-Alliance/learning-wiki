Feedback that answers three questions (Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next?) improves learning¶

Claim · All claims

Currency · newest study 2007 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

Feedback is most effective when it tells learners the goal, their current status relative to that goal, and the next actionable step. The three questions — goal, gap, next step — form a loop that connects instruction to learning: without a goal, performance information has no reference point; without gap information, learners cannot calibrate; without a next step, feedback stops at evaluation.

Hattie Timperley 2007¶

This is the paper's origin: Hattie and Timperley propose a model in which effective feedback answers three questions — "Where am I going?", "How am I doing?", and "Where to next?" — operating across four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self), and argue this reduces the discrepancy between current understanding and a learning goal. They synthesize prior empirical and theoretical work on feedback's effect on learning and achievement to build the model rather than reporting a new primary study or a pooled quantitative estimate specific to the three-question structure. Re-read in full text (2026-10-21). The review calls the three questions feed up, feed back and feed forward, and says of "Where to next?" that the feed-forward question "can have some of the most powerful impacts on learning"; it also notes that too often students learn that the answer to "Where to next?" is simply "more" tasks. It prints effect sizes only from earlier meta-analyses it summarises (its Tables 1–3), none of which tests the three-question structure, so the i? stands.

Discussion¶

Why the loop closes. The framework's power lies in closing the loop between instruction and learning: without a clear goal ("Where am I going?"), performance information has no reference point; without gap information ("How am I doing?"), learners cannot calibrate; without a next step ("Where to next?"), feedback stops at evaluation rather than guiding improvement. Feedback that only rewards or grades, without answering the third question, is often described in the literature as the least effective form — which is why action-oriented feedback centers on the "where to next" step specifically.

Moderators to pin down. Three boundary conditions are commonly discussed: (a) feedback directing attention to the self rather than the task tends to be less effective; (b) detailed feedback may diminish or reverse in value for highly proficient learners — an expertise-reversal pattern analogous to Cognitive Load Theory and the expertise reversal effect — so feedback detail should fade as proficiency grows; and (c) feedback must arrive while learners can still act on it — timing and opportunity to act are moderators, not afterthoughts, linking the claim to assessment for learning practice generally. The framework also presupposes that learners monitor and act on the information, placing it inside self-regulated learning: the three questions map onto goal setting, self-monitoring, and strategy adjustment.

Status of the evidence. The three-question framing is a widely used organizing heuristic in the formative assessment literature, but no supporting studies have yet been pinned to this page, so the claim's strength cannot be rated. The related claim Assessment for learning improves achievement shares the goal–gap–next-step loop.

Open questions. Whether the three questions must be answered explicitly by the teacher or can be partially answered by learners through self-assessment; how the framework interacts with grading (grades appended to comments may wash out the benefit of the comments); whether "where to next" must be immediately actionable or can be a longer-term learning intention; and whether the three questions operate as a package or whether the third question carries most of the effect on its own.

Merged from “Feedback Answers Three Questions” (feedback-answers-three-questions): The three-question framing treats feedback as information about the gap between a current state and a target state, not as praise, blame, or a verdict. Each question implies a different design decision:

  • Where am I going? requires that goals and success criteria be visible to learners before performance, connecting feedback to assessment for learning and to clear structure. Feedback against unclear or unshared goals tends to be interpreted idiosyncratically.
  • How am I going? requires evidence of current performance relative to the goal — which is why feedback is tightly coupled with assessment and with check-ins that surface progress during, not after, learning.
  • Where to next? is the action-oriented component: feedback must suggest a next step the learner can take. This aligns with action-oriented feedback and supports self-regulated learning, since learners who know the next move can begin managing their own progress.

A key boundary condition in the feedback literature is the level at which feedback is directed. Feedback about the task and about the processes used to do the task is generally more useful than feedback directed at the person ("you're so smart"), which can steer attention toward self-protection rather than strategy adjustment. This interacts with learner expertise: novices typically need task-level feedback, while more advanced learners benefit more from process and self-regulation levels — a moderation consistent with the expertise reversal effect.

Constraints on the model follow from these boundary conditions. Feedback loses effectiveness when it directs attention to the self rather than the task, when goals are unshared or ambiguous so that "how am I going?" has no stable reference point, and when learners lack the prerequisite knowledge to act on "where to next?" guidance — in that case, next-step advice becomes noise rather than direction. Automated or rubric-driven feedback environments often answer "how am I going?" adequately but fail at "where to next?", producing evaluation without direction — see Checklist evaluation ineffective online for a related boundary case.

Open questions this page should eventually resolve with cited evidence: (1) the relative effect sizes of the three questions when manipulated independently; (2) whether "where to next?" feedback loses its advantage when learners lack the prerequisite knowledge to act on it; and (3) how the model plays out in digital environments, where automated feedback can answer "how am I going?" well but struggles with "where to next?"
