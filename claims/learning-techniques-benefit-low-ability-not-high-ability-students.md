## Evidence

### Donoghue & Hattie 2021

Donoghue, G. M., & Hattie, J. A. C. (2021). A Meta-Analysis of Ten Learning Techniques. *Frontiers in Education, 6*, 581216. [doi:10.3389/feduc.2021.581216](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216)

`q3 · meta-analysis (19 studies reporting ability)` · `i2 · medium effect for low-ability students, d = 0.47; d = -0.11 for high-ability` · `n=19 studies` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Only 6 of the 242 studies examined high-ability students and 13 low-ability students. Across techniques, low-ability students gained d = 0.47 (SE 0.15, 58 effects) and high-ability students d = -0.11 (SE 0.10, 28 effects), with negative effects for interleaved practice and summarization in the high-ability group.

> "Across all techniques, the mean effect on High ability students was -0.11 (SE = 0.10, N = 28) for Low ability students was 0.47, SE = 0.15, N = 58."

## Discussion

This rests on 19 studies and is consistent with expertise-reversal accounts, where support that helps novices is redundant or harmful for more knowledgeable learners. It is a reason to adapt technique prompts to prior knowledge rather than a verdict on any one technique.
