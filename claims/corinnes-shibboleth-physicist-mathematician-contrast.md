# Physicists and mathematicians tend to interpret the same symbolic function differently, as shown by the coordinate-transformation 'shibboleth' problem

## Subclaims

`q1 i?` Faced with rewriting T(x,y) = k(x2 + y2) in polar coordinates, physicists tend to treat T as a physical function of the point in space and answer T(r,theta) = kr2, while mathematicians treat it as a mathematical functional dependence and answer k(r2 + theta2). [→ Edward F. Redish and Ayush Gupta 2010](#edward-f-redish-and-ayush-gupta-2010)

`q1 i?` On a coordinate-transformation problem, physicists tend to treat T as a physical function representing temperature at a point in space while mathematicians tend to treat it as an abstract functional dependence. [→ Edward F. Redish and Eric Kuo 2014](#edward-f-redish-and-eric-kuo-2014)

## Evidence

### Edward F. Redish and Ayush Gupta 2010

Edward F. Redish and Ayush Gupta. (2010). Making Meaning with Math in Physics: A Semantic Analysis. Department of Physics, University of Maryland, College Park. Originally published 2009. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1002.0472](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1002.0472)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Theoretical illustration (anecdote from the Vector Calculus Bridge project) contrasting interpretations of one function across coordinate systems. The authors report "Physicists tend to think of T as a physical function" whereas mathematicians focus on functional form and treat variables as dummy variables; the roles are explicitly exaggerated to illustrate the point.

> "Physicists tend to think of T as a physical function - one that represents the temperature (in whatever units) at a particular point in space (in whatever coordinates)."

### Edward F. Redish and Eric Kuo 2014

Edward F. Redish and Eric Kuo. (2014). Language of physics, language of math: Disciplinary culture and dynamic epistemology. [doi:10.1007/s11191-015-9749-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11191-015-9749-7)

`q1 · i?` · `theoretical · r3`

Analytical illustration from the essay (Corinne's Shibboleth): the authors describe how physicists read the temperature function physically while mathematicians read the same expression as abstract dependence of a value on two independent variables, with "Physicists tend to think of T as a physical function" and mathematicians writing T(r,θ) = k(r2 + θ2).

> "Physicists tend to think of T as a physical function – one that represents the temperature (in whatever units) at a particular point in space (in whatever coordinates)."
