Evidence¶

Fyfe et al. 2014¶

Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., Son, J. Y., & Goldstone, R. L. (2014). Concreteness fading in mathematics and science instruction: A systematic review. Educational Psychology Review, 26(1), 9–25. doi:10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3

q3 · systematic review · i? · no effect size reported · n=N/A (review) · review · r?

A systematic review of the "concreteness fading" literature in mathematics and science instruction. The authors reject the simple dichotomy of "concrete vs. abstract materials" and instead argue that instruction should begin with concrete materials and then be explicitly and gradually faded toward abstract symbols. They identify the mechanism as connection-building: concrete materials let learners interpret otherwise-opaque symbols, but only if learners are guided to strip away the concrete surface features and see the generalizable structure the symbols encode — i.e., the concrete material itself is not what teaches the concept.

Discussion¶

The connection is the mechanism, not the material. Manipulatives are frequently adopted on the assumption that "hands-on" activity is inherently effective. The research literature suggests otherwise: benefits depend on whether the learner perceives and internalizes the mapping between the manipulative and the target concept. Without explicit bridging — teacher talk, gestures, labels, or drawn representations that link the object to the symbolic notation — learners may manipulate objects fluently while learning little about the underlying mathematics. This is consistent with broader findings that cognitive load reduction improves learning only when the reduced load is redirected toward sense-making rather than toward unguided exploration.

Concrete objects can distract from structure. Richly detailed, realistic manipulatives can draw attention to incidental perceptual features at the expense of the mathematical structure they are meant to embody. More bland or schematic representations often support transfer better (unsourced on this page), because they make the relevant structure more salient and the irrelevant surface features less so. This parallels the general principle that clear structure improves learning.

Fading from concrete to abstract. A recurring design recommendation is a gradual progression: begin with concrete objects, then move through diagrammatic or iconic representations, and finally reach symbolic notation, with explicit connections drawn at each transition. This mirrors the fading logic in worked examples with practice improve transfer and the scaffolding orientation of cognitive load management.

Manipulatives as analogy. A manipulative functions as a concrete analogy for an abstract structure, and analogy research is clear that the benefit lies in the mapping, not the source domain: learners left to induce the mapping on their own often align surface features instead of relational structure. Explicitly labeling correspondences ("this rod is one ten; these unit cubes are ones") is what converts object play into conceptual learning — see analogical reasoning improves transfer.

Long-term retention caveat. Knowledge anchored in a single concrete representation can remain tied to the specific objects used, making transfer to novel contexts harder unless multiple varied representations are used. This aligns with the argument in cognitive flexibility theory: multiple cases that varied presentations support flexible transfer.

Moderators to specify. Learner age and prior knowledge are widely hypothesized to moderate the effect — younger learners with less symbolic fluency may benefit more from concrete starting points, while older or more knowledgeable learners may find them redundant or distracting (an expertise-reversal pattern analogous to worked examples can become redundant or counterproductive for advanced learners). These moderators still need well-specified experimental evidence on this page.

Open questions. The precise conditions under which manipulatives outperform — or underperform — purely symbolic instruction remain unsettled. Studies are needed that compare manipulative-based instruction with and without explicit concept-connection supports, holding instructional time constant.

Merged from “Hands On Without Conceptual Integration Insufficient” (hands-on-without-conceptual-integration-insufficient): This claim concerns a boundary condition on active learning: the effectiveness of manipulatives, simulations, labs, and other embodied activities depends on whether learners extract and connect the intended concepts. Activity that remains at the level of procedure or play — without prompts to explain, predict, or relate actions to underlying principles — tends to leave the conceptual content unlearned. Mechanisms plausibly include working-memory limits (cognitive overload degrades learning): hands-on tasks can impose extraneous load from manipulating equipment, crowding out the sense-making the activity was designed to support. This aligns with cognitive load theory and with guidance effects in discovery-style instruction.

Design responses include structuring the activity around explicit learning goals, adding prompts for prediction and explanation, and following activity with consolidation — e.g., through activation of relevant prior ideas before the task, or assessment for learning that surfaces misconceptions afterward. Case-based and anchored instruction approaches similarly pair experience with explicit conceptual framing.

Open questions: how much guidance is optimal (over-guidance may trigger its own expertise-reversal problems — see expertise reversal effect), and whether the effect differs across domains (physical science manipulatives vs. simulated environments). The claim is a negative/boundary claim: it does not assert that hands-on activity fails in general, only that it fails without conceptual integration, so supporting evidence would come from comparisons of guided versus unguided versions of the same activity.
