## Evidence

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2010

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2010). Using Analogy to Solve a Three-Step Physics Problem. PERC Proceedings. [doi:10.1063/1.3515228](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3515228)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Before-and-after examination of intervention 2 students' work in the calculus-based course shows the solved example's main benefit was principle invocation, especially CM. Most students who improved were those who initially missed CM but invoked it after reading the solved problem.

> "Examination of students’ work in intervention 2 before and after they read the snowboard problem indicates that the solved example is most useful in helping students invoke the correct principles, in particular the principle of CM, which was more likely to be ignored by the students who didn’t receive the solved snowboard problem."

### Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh 2010 (2)

Shih-Yin Lin and Chandralekha Singh. (2010). Using Analogy to Solve a Three-Step Physics Problem. PERC Proceedings. [doi:10.1063/1.3515228](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3515228)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Analysis of students' written work shows that even after successfully applying CM and finding the post-collision speed was half the pre-collision speed, many students still solved the final answer assuming mechanical energy was conserved during the whole process, failing to connect sub-problems.

> "Many students didn’t make use of the CM principle to relate h f to h o; they still used their original idea (e.g., that the mechanical energy is conserved during the whole process) to solve for the final answer even though they have found that the speed of putties stuck together after the collision is half of the initial speed of putty A right before the collision by applying the principle of CM successfully."
