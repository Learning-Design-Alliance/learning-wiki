Decorative Illustrations Do Not Improve Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 3 studies · 2 causal r2, 1 quant-synthesis r? · q3 · i1 small

This claim concerns decorative illustrations — images included for visual appeal, engagement, or page decoration rather than to carry instructional content. It does not apply to illustrations that explain or structure the material itself.

Evidence¶

Sundararajan & Adesope 2020¶

Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020). Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect. Educational Psychology Review, 32(3), 707–734. doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4

q3 · meta-analysis · i? · no pooled effect size in the abstract · n=not stated in abstract · quant-synthesis · r?

This is a meta-analysis of experiments comparing learning materials with and without seductive details, meaning interesting but irrelevant text or images. It concludes that adding such details can hinder learning. The size of the effect depended on how the seductive details were presented, the type of image used for comparison, the delivery format, language, subject, learner pacing, the type of recall question and how the manipulation was checked. It covers seductive details in general, not only decorative illustrations. Only the abstract was read, so no pooled estimate or study count is recorded here.

Lenzner et al. 2013¶

Lenzner, A., Schnotz, W., & Müller, A. (2013). The role of decorative pictures in learning. Instructional Science, 41(5), 811–831. doi:10.1007/s11251-012-9256-z

q3 · three peer-reviewed experiments (incl. eye tracking) · i? · no effect size in the abstract · n=7th and 8th graders, three experiments (sample sizes not in abstract) · causal · r?

Three experiments with 7th and 8th grade students compared decorative pictures (chosen mainly to look appealing) with instructional pictures (chosen to inform). Eye tracking showed that learners looked at decorative pictures briefly at first and then mostly ignored them. The pictures improved mood, alertness and calmness and made the material feel less difficult, but they did not raise situational interest. For learning, decorative pictures were neither harmful nor helpful overall. They did, however, reduce the benefit of instructional pictures (the multimedia effect), most strongly for learners with lower prior knowledge. This supports the "does not improve" claim and argues against a blanket "harms" version.

González et al. 2019¶

González, F. M., Saux, G., & Burin, D. (2019). The decorative images' seductive effect in e-learning depends on attentional inhibition. Australasian Journal of Educational Technology, 35(3). doi:10.14742/ajet.4577

q3 · within-subjects counterbalanced experiments · i1 · small effect, η²=.04 (Study 1) · n=126 (Study 1); 65 (Study 2) · causal · r2

In Study 1, 126 first-year undergraduates in a Moodle course read texts with and without decorative, irrelevant images. Each student saw both conditions, in counterbalanced order. Comprehension was a little lower with the images (M = 9.24 vs 9.54; F(1,123) = 4.25, p = .041, η² = .04), and the authors call the effect "very small in magnitude". In Study 2, 65 undergraduates read harder texts spread across several screens. The images had no overall effect (F(1,53) = 0.02), but they interacted with attentional inhibitory control (F(1,53) = 9.18, η² = .15): students less able to ignore irrelevant visual input understood less when the images were present.

Discussion¶

Scope of the claim. The claim is deliberately narrow: it asserts that adding decorative images does not improve learning outcomes. It does not assert that decorative images actively harm learning in all cases, nor does it say anything about illustrations that serve an instructional function (e.g., diagrams, labeled figures, or worked-example visuals — see Demonstration).

Relationship to the coherence principle. The strongest theoretical framing for this claim is the coherence principle: instruction should exclude material that does not support the learning goal, because irrelevant material consumes working-memory capacity without contributing to schema construction — see Irrelevant or extraneous materials hurt learning (coherence principle). Under this account, decorative illustrations are a form of extraneous processing: they attract attention and occupy limited working memory while adding no referential content. This connects to the broader architecture described in Cognitive Load Theory and the claim that cognitive overload degrades learning.

Why the intuition persists. Decorative illustrations are widely believed to boost engagement, interest, and motivation, and learners often report liking them. The claim highlights a recurring dissociation in multimedia learning research between preference/affect and measured learning outcomes: what learners enjoy is not necessarily what they learn from. Designers should not treat learner approval of visuals as evidence of instructional value. This is a recurring trap in design review: aesthetic polish is easy to perceive and praise, while its instructional cost — split attention, displaced working-memory resources — is invisible without outcome measures.

Boundary conditions and open questions. Plausible moderators include learner expertise (novices may be more vulnerable to distraction by irrelevant graphics, while experts can ignore them — consistent with the expertise reversal effect), the salience and animation of the decoration, and whether the image is adjacent to essential content. Whether decorative images impair learning — rather than merely failing to help — is a separate, stronger claim, and the entries above split on it: Lenzner et al. (2013) found decorative pictures neither helped nor harmed overall but weakened the benefit of instructional pictures for lower-prior-knowledge learners, and González et al. (2019) found a very small harm (η² = .04) that, on harder texts, appeared only in students with low attentional inhibition. The distinction also matters for Dual Coding Theory: dual coding predicts benefits when visuals are referentially connected to verbal content, a condition decorative images by definition fail to meet. A practical design heuristic follows: before adding an illustration, ask what referential connection it makes to the content; if the honest answer is "none," the burden of proof lies with the decoration, not with its removal.

Evidence status. Three studies are recorded: a meta-analysis of seductive details in general (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, abstract only, no pooled estimate recorded) and two sets of experiments on decorative images specifically (Lenzner et al. 2013 with 7th and 8th graders; González et al. 2019 with undergraduates). None found that decorative images improved learning; whether they harm it depends on the learner and the material. The learner-expertise moderator suggested above has partial support from Lenzner et al. (lower prior knowledge) and González et al. (attentional inhibition); salience, animation and adjacency are untested here.
