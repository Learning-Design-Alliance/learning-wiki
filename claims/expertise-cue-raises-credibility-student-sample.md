# In a student sample, a science text attributed to a high-expertise author is rated more credible than the identical text attributed to a low-expertise author, with no effect of author gender

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · causal `r3` · `q3` · `i1` small
> **Currency** · newest study 2026 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

## Subclaims
`q3 i1` Study 1 found a small significant main effect of author expertise on perceived message credibility (partial η² = 0.03), supporting the high-over-low-expertise hypothesis. [→ Ulsperger 2026](#ulsperger-2026)

## Evidence

### Ulsperger 2026

Ulsperger, A., Pfannenschwarz, L., Schetla, C., Poletilo, K., Strecker, L., Egner, M., & Kimmerle, J. (2026). Authors' academic but not personal expertise or gender affect message credibility in science communication. Scientific Reports, 16, 18000. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55148-x

`q3 · i1` · `causal · r3`

Preregistered online experiment (Study 1, n = 203) with a 2 × 2 between-groups design varying alleged author expertise and gender. The two-way ANOVA showed a small expertise main effect: "partial η2 = 0.03", with means M = 4.85 (high) versus M = 4.47 (low).

> "The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of author expertise, F(1, 199) = 5.38, p = 0.021, partial η2 = 0.03. The results indicate that the text by authors with alleged high expertise was rated as more credible (M = 4.85, SD = 1.09) than the identical text by authors with alleged low expertise (M = 4.47, SD = 1.27)"
