# On distractor-driven science items, instruction can first strengthen students' attraction to an alternative conception before that preference declines

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · associational `r2` · `q2` · n=1,250 students, grades 8–12

On items whose distractors match common student ideas, the share of students choosing an alternative conception is not a simple decreasing function of understanding.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` For items with highly attractive distractors based on alternative conceptions, instruction appeared to strengthen support for those conceptions before the preference eventually declined, an unusual psychometric profile. [→ Sadler 1998](#sadler-1998)

`q2 i?` The author reads this as support for the view that such ideas may be markers of progress toward scientific understanding rather than impediments to learning, and argues that distractor-driven instruments have a psychometric profile very different from conventional tests. [→ Sadler 1998](#sadler-1998)

## Evidence

### Sadler 1998

Sadler, P. M. (1998). Psychometric models of student conceptions in science: Reconciling qualitative studies and distractor-driven assessment instruments. *Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 35*(3), 265–296. [doi:10.1002/(SICI)1098-2736(199803)35:3<265::AID-TEA3>3.0.CO;2-P](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1098-2736(199803)35:3<265::AID-TEA3>3.0.CO;2-P)

`q2 · pre/post psychometric study` · `i? · no effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=1,250 students, grades 8–12` · `associational · r2`

Multiple-choice astronomy items whose distractors captured alternative conceptions identified in qualitative research were given to 1,250 students in grades 8 to 12 at the start and end of their introductory astronomy courses. Psychometric modelling placed the appeal of the various ideas on a single scale and showed the rise-then-fall profile for attractive distractors, as well as the ages at which particular conceptions are most prevalent. There is no comparison group; the rise is a pattern across the ability scale and the pre/post administrations, not a manipulated effect. Coded from the published abstract; the full text was not read.

## Discussion

A rise in the number of students choosing an alternative-conception distractor after a unit is not, on this evidence, necessarily a sign that teaching failed: it may mark learners moving from no idea to a partial one. That reading fits the [knowledge-in-pieces](../theories/knowledge-in-pieces.md) view of misconceptions as steps in a refinement. For [misconception probes](../elements/misconception-probes.md) it means a single pre/post comparison of distractor choices can mislead.
