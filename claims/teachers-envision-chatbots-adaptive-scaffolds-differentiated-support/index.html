Evidence¶

Riahi 2026¶

Riahi, B., Ozturk, D., Guth, A., Li, J., Singh, D. P., Tian, X., Chiu, J., Lytle, N., Barnes, T., & Cateté, V. (2026). Will It Teach as Intended? How Teachers Configure Educational AI Chatbots. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29993 doi:10.48550/arxiv.2609.29993

q2 · i? · qualitative · r?

Focus-group interview thematic analysis during summer 2026 PD workshops with 27 middle school teachers. Teachers envisioned adjusting the form and level of assistance based on students' needs, with examples including a fractions tutor that could "give hints for answers, correct misconceptions, [and] use visuals."

"Approximately 15 teachers described chatbots as adaptive instructional supports that could respond to differences in students' prior knowledge, skill level, accessibility needs, or the type of learning support required."

Related Claims¶

  • Teachers envisioned chatbots as extending teacher capacity and availability by providing additional access to support and redistributing planning workload — related
  • Participants envisioned switchable AI roles — strategic advisor, cross-domain translator, and perspective challenger — whose expansion destabilized perceived neutrality and raised authority and accountability concerns — related
  • Expert scenarios envision AI saturation as augmenting rather than displacing human pedagogical work (the more-human paradox) — related
  • Teachers envision AI companion literacy as shared work across stakeholders, starting early and spiraling across grade levels, but disagree on which level owns it — related
  • Teachers sought to preserve student thinking and authorship by bounding chatbot answer-giving and maintaining teacher control over scope — related
  • The chatbot often provided more scaffold types than requested, and supplying classified question scope to the chatbot raised scaffold precision above .60 — related
  • Educators benefit from AI chatbots through time-saving administrative assistance and improved pedagogy — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Teachers designed tasks with dilemmas, unfamiliar scenarios, and differentiation intended to support transfer, without assessing capability — related
