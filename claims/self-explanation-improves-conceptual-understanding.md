# Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r?`, 1 causal `r1`, 1 associational `r1` · `q2`–`q4` · `i2` medium
<!-- deprecated title (2026-10-05, overstated its evidence): Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance. -->

Generating explanations for oneself while studying worked examples or solving problems helps learners bridge gaps in their knowledge, integrate new information with prior schemas, and monitor their own comprehension.

## Subclaims

`q2 i?` Students who generate more self-explanations while studying worked examples perform significantly better on subsequent problem-solving tasks. [→ Chi et al. 1989](#chi-et-al-1989)
`q3 i?` Prompted self-explanation leads to moderate improvements in conceptual knowledge and procedural transfer in mathematics. [→ Rittle-Johnson 2017](#rittle-johnson-2017)

`q4 i2` Across 69 effect sizes from 64 reports, prompting learners to self-explain while studying or solving problems improved learning outcomes by a moderate average amount (g = .55), across task types, subject areas and levels of education. [→ Bisra et al. 2018](#bisra-et-al-2018)

`q3 i?` In mathematics, prompted self-explanation gives a small-to-moderate immediate improvement in procedural knowledge, conceptual knowledge and procedural transfer, but evidence for gains in classroom settings or after a delay is much more limited. [→ Rittle-Johnson 2017](#rittle-johnson-2017)

`q2 i?` Eighth-grade students prompted to self-explain each line of a text on the circulatory system gained more from pretest to posttest than students who read it twice, and those who explained most built the most accurate mental models. [→ Chi et al. 1994](#chi-et-al-1994)

### Bisra et al. 2018

Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing Self-Explanation: a Meta-Analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 30*(3), 703–725. [doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)

`q4 · meta-analysis (random effects)` · `i2 · medium effect, g=.55` · `n=69 effect sizes from 64 research reports` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

A systematic search and meta-analysis of studies in which participants received self-explanation prompts while studying or solving problems, compared with learners who did not. The random-effects weighted mean effect was g = .55. The authors coded 20 moderators, including the type of task (solving problems, studying [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md), studying text), subject area, level of education, type of prompt and treatment duration, and conclude that self-explanation prompts are a potentially powerful intervention across a range of instructional conditions. Read from the abstract only; moderator results are not reported here.

### Chi et al. 1994

Chi, M. T. H., De Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & Lavancher, C. (1994). Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding. *Cognitive Science, 18*(3), 439–477. [doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3)

`q2 · small controlled experiment, two groups` · `i? · no effect size in the abstract` · `n=24 students` · `causal · r1`

Fourteen eighth-grade students were asked, with no extensive training, to self-explain after reading each line of an expository passage on the human circulatory system; ten control students read the same text twice without prompts. All were tested before and after. The prompted group gained more from pretest to posttest, and within it the students who generated many self-explanations learned with greater understanding than those who generated few. All high explainers reached the correct mental model of the circulatory system, while many unprompted students and low explainers did not.

## Discussion

The effectiveness of self-explanation can be limited by a learner's prior knowledge; if a learner lacks the necessary background to generate a correct explanation, they may reinforce misconceptions.

*Merged from “Self-explanation improves learning” (self-explanation-improves-learning):* **Mechanism.** Self-explanation is thought to work by closing gaps in mental models: when learners must state *why* a step follows or *how* an example relates to a principle, they generate inferences that passive study leaves implicit [+M]. This aligns with the broader account in [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — explanation prompts direct limited working-memory resources toward schema construction rather than surface features, in a manner related to how [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The effect is generally strongest for novices, who have the most gaps to close [+M]; for advanced learners, explanation prompts can become redundant or even disruptive, consistent with the [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Prompt quality also matters: open prompts ("explain this to yourself") can leave unprepared learners explaining superficial features [~M], while more structured or scaffolded prompts tend to yield more productive explanations [+M]. Because generating explanations adds effort, poorly timed or excessive prompting risks overload — the same failure mode described in [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M].

**Relation to worked examples.** Self-explanation prompts are frequently layered onto [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md), where they prompt learners to justify each step rather than passively read it [+M]. The two strategies are complementary: examples reduce search during early learning, while explanation prompts ensure the example's underlying principles are actually extracted rather than copied — see [Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes](worked-examples-example-problem-sequences.md).

**Relation to other generative strategies.** Self-explanation belongs to a family of generative techniques — [annotating](annotating-improves-learning.md), summarizing, questioning — that share a common logic: learning improves when learners actively construct connections rather than receive them [+M]. What distinguishes self-explanation is its focus on *justifying* steps and *relating* representations, which makes it especially well suited to worked examples, [case-based learning](case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md), and step-by-step procedures where each step has an underlying rationale worth articulating.

Domain boundaries and the durability of gains over time are still thinly documented: Rittle-Johnson 2017 finds evidence after a delay or in classrooms much more limited, and the moderator results of Bisra et al. are not recorded here. Key questions for future enrichment: how self-explanation compares with other generative strategies of equal time-on-task, whether prompted explanation produces durable gains or only momentary engagement, and how prompt structure should be faded as expertise develops.

*Merged from “Eliciting Self Explanations Improves Understanding” (eliciting-self-explanations-improves-understanding):* **Mechanism.** Self-explanation prompts are usually understood through generative-learning logic: producing an explanation forces the learner to connect new information to what they already know, exposing gaps and repairing flawed mental models. This places self-explanation close to [Activation](activation-improves-learning.md) and [Analogical reasoning](analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) as integration-supporting techniques.

**Costs and boundary conditions.** Generating explanations consumes working-memory resources, so for novices or very high-element-interactivity material the prompt itself can add load rather than support integration [~M] — the same trade-off that governs [Chunking](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) and [Cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) more broadly. When material is already well understood, explaining it can become redundant and yield little or no benefit, echoing the expertise-reversal pattern described in [Expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Prompt design matters: open-ended "explain this" prompts can produce shallow restatements, while prompts that target principles or underlying reasons are more likely to generate productive explanations [~M].

**Design implications.** Practitioners should (a) attach explanation prompts to specific steps or statements rather than asking for global summaries, (b) scaffold early prompts with sentence starters or principle cues and fade them as competence grows, and (c) avoid prompting explanation of material learners can already recite fluently — at that point the prompt is redundant effort with no integration payoff [~M]. Self-explanation pairs naturally with [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md) and with [case-based learning](case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md), where prompts can direct attention to why a case or solution step unfolds as it does.

**Open questions.** The durability of self-explanation effects over delayed tests, their interaction with learner expertise, and the relative value of self-generated versus instructional explanations remain active areas of investigation. <!-- deprecated (2026-10-05, stale): Studies still need to be added to the Evidence section before the strength of this claim can be rated. --> The four studies recorded above test immediate learning; none of them settles delayed retention or the interaction with expertise.

*Merged from “Self Explanation Prompts Improve Learning” (self-explanation-prompts-improve-learning):* **Mechanism.** Self-explanation prompts work by forcing learners to go beyond passive restatement: they map new information onto prior knowledge, expose gaps in understanding, and help learners infer the underlying principles behind worked steps or textual claims. This aligns with the broader generative-learning position that learning improves when learners actively construct connections rather than receive explanations — see [Activation improves learning](activation-improves-learning.md) [+M] and [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

**Boundary conditions.** The benefit is strongest for novices, who otherwise skip self-explanation or do it shallowly. For more advanced learners, prompts can become redundant or interrupt fluent processing — the same expertise-reversal pattern documented for [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md) and formalized in [expertise reversal](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]. Prompt design also matters: open-ended "explain why" prompts can overload limited working memory if the material is high in element interactivity, in which case providing the key explanation (an "assisted" or scaffolded prompt) can outperform pure self-generation [~M]. This connects to [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S] and [Chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S], and situates the strategy within [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).

**Prompt placement and format.** Prompts can be attached to worked steps, textual passages, or multimedia segments. Principle-based prompts ("what principle does this step illustrate?") and gap-targeted prompts tend to focus explanation on the deep structure of the material, whereas vague prompts ("explain this to yourself") risk eliciting paraphrase rather than genuine elaboration [~W]. Written explanations impose more load than oral or prompted-selection formats, so designers of [annotating](annotating-improves-learning.md)-style tasks should weigh response format against material complexity.

**Open questions.** The durability of gains on delayed tests, and how prompt type (principle-based vs. goal-based vs. gap-targeted) moderates outcomes, are not settled by the evidence recorded above.

*Merged from “Self Explanation Prompts Improve Learning From Worked Examples” (self-explanation-prompts-improve-learning-from-worked-examples):* **Mechanism.** Self-explanation prompts are thought to work by directing attention to the underlying principles and rationale of each solution step, helping learners build schemas that connect the example to domain knowledge — consistent with [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) accounts of worked-example learning. Without prompts, learners often passively read examples and fail to engage in the inferential processing that produces transferable schemas; prompts convert example study from reading into an active, generative act, in line with [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of relevant prior knowledge.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The benefit of self-explanation prompts is widely understood to interact with learner expertise: for novices, prompts scaffold productive processing, but for more advanced learners the same prompts can impose extraneous load or become redundant — the expertise reversal pattern described in [Worked examples can become redundant or counterproductive for advanced learners.](worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) and formalized in the [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md). Prompt design also matters: open-ended "why" prompts can be demanding, and learners who generate poor or shallow explanations may gain little; the added processing can itself be a source of [cognitive overload](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) when examples are complex or working-memory resources are stretched. Designers should consider providing partial explanations, fading prompts over time, and pairing prompts with [worked examples](../elements/demonstration.md) interleaved with [practice](../elements/practice.md) rather than examples alone. Prompt timing is another open design variable: prompts attached to every step may disrupt the smooth reading of a solution, whereas prompts placed after complete solution steps tend to preserve the example's coherence while still eliciting explanation.

**Open questions.** Optimal prompt formats and the durability of gains across domains are not settled by the evidence recorded above, which concerns self-explanation in general rather than worked examples specifically.
