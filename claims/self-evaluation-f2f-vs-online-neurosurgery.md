## Subclaims
`q3 i?` Self-evaluation scores for learning motivation, learning more targeted and more interesting, clinical thinking ability, clinician-patient communication skills, and teamwork skills were significantly higher in the face-to-face group (P = 0.005, 0.031, 0.003, 0.005, 0.015). ## Evidence

### Shen Jun 2025

Shen Jun, Zhang Shaolin, Sun Dongdong, Ge Ruixiang, Chen Sansong, Fang Jincheng, An Qian. (2025). Comparison of face-to-face teaching and online teaching in neurosurgery education for medical students. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-06817-4

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Post-course questionnaire self-evaluation (5-point items) comparing 87 face-to-face and 84 online students. Five items favored face-to-face teaching with the printed P-values; no effect sizes were reported.

> "These scores were significantly higher than those in the online group, which were 3.64 ± 1.04, 3.58 ± 1.01, 3.62 ± 0.92, 3.61 ± 0.91, and 3.61 ± 0.97 (P = 0.005, P = 0.031, P = 0.003, P = 0.005, and P = 0.015, respectively)."

### Shen Jun 2025 (2)

Shen Jun, Zhang Shaolin, Sun Dongdong, Ge Ruixiang, Chen Sansong, Fang Jincheng, An Qian. (2025). Comparison of face-to-face teaching and online teaching in neurosurgery education for medical students. BMC Medical Education. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-025-06817-4

`q3 · i?` · `causal · r1`

Two of the seven self-evaluation items showed no significant difference between groups; the article suggests online teaching achieves comparable outcomes only in self-learning skills and basic knowledge mastery.

> "However, there was no significant difference in self-learning skills and basic knowledge mastery scores between the face-to-face and online groups (P = 0.941 and P = 0.789, respectively)."
