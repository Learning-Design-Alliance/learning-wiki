# Even students who choose the wrong answer on a multiple-choice physics question can generate the correct explanation when prompted for alternatives

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` On Two Boats Q1, 11 of the 13 students who believed strongly in the incorrect answer provided a valid explanation for the correct answer when prompted. [→ Nolan K. Weinlader 2019](#nolan-k-weinlader-2019)

## Evidence

### Nolan K. Weinlader 2019

Nolan K. Weinlader, Eric Kuo, Benjamin M. Rottman, and Timothy J. Nokes-Malach. (2019). A new approach for uncovering student resources with multiple-choice questions. Originally published 2018. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1909.01455](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1909.01455)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r3`

Interview study in which 16 undergraduates generated explanations for every option of the Two Boats questions. Using part 3 dominant ratings, of the 13 students favoring incorrect choice C on Q1, 11 "provided a valid explanation for why B might be the answer" (the correct choice B).

> "Of the 13 students who believed strongly in choice C, 11 provided a valid explanation for why B might be the answer."

### Nolan K. Weinlader 2019 (2)

Nolan K. Weinlader, Eric Kuo, Benjamin M. Rottman, and Timothy J. Nokes-Malach. (2019). A new approach for uncovering student resources with multiple-choice questions. Originally published 2018. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1909.01455](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1909.01455)

`q2 · i?` · `qualitative · r3`

Same interview study, Two Boats Q2, where the majority chose incorrect option A. When generating explanations for correct answer C, "three of those students gave the correct explanation" that equal peak heights mean equal flight times, replicating the Q1 pattern with fewer students.

> "Yet, when generating explanations for answer C (the correct answer) , three of those students gave the correct explanation: because the shells reach the same peak height, they hit at the same time."
