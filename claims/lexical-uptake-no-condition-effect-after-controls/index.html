Evidence¶

Russell Scheinberg 2026¶

Russell Scheinberg, Ameeta Agrawal, Tetyana Sydorenko, Kalab Kahsay, Nina Vyatkina, Griet Boone. (2026). What Changes When the Interlocutor Is an AI? Interactional Fluency and Linguistic Uptake in L2 Spoken Dialogue. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22225

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Mixed-effects regression on lexical adoption from the within-participant study; the AI produced over twice as many novel lemmas (M=79.6 vs. 36.4). "Pool size (β= 0.10,p < .001) and learner word count (β= 0.005,p <.001) were the significant predictors".

"However, a mixed-effects regression con- trolling for pool size and learner word count found no sig- nificant condition effect (β=−1.77,SE= 0.95,z=−1.87, p=.062)."

Russell Scheinberg 2026 (2)¶

Russell Scheinberg, Ameeta Agrawal, Tetyana Sydorenko, Kalab Kahsay, Nina Vyatkina, Griet Boone. (2026). What Changes When the Interlocutor Is an AI? Interactional Fluency and Linguistic Uptake in L2 Spoken Dialogue. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22225

q3 · i2 · causal · r2

Position-matched Welch's t-tests comparing adoption at the same sequential position to disentangle partner type from task order (Group 1 n=46, Group 2 n=32). The effect held at position 2 but not position 1.

"In position 2—where the depleted vocabulary pool provides a more sensitive test—AI participants adopted significantly more words (M= 4.6 vs. 2.6;d= 0.72,p=.003). In position 1, the direction is consistent but nonsignificant (d= 0.42,p=.079)."
