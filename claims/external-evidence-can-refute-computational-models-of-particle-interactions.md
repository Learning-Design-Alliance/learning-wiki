# External empirical evidence can refute a computational model of particle interactions when the model's own simulation cannot

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · `q1` argument or single case · `i2` medium · n=18 consented students (of 29 in the class), one sixth-grade classroom, one experienced teacher, 6-day unit

When a sixth-grade class's computational simulation of particle diffusion could not visually distinguish between a correct theory and a plausible but incorrect "infect" theory, a physical evaporation experiment that directly tested the underlying particle-level claim shifted a substantial share of students away from the incorrect theory.

## Subclaims
`q1-q2 i2` Of 18 consented students whose reasoning was tracked across a 6-day diffusion unit, 7 (39%) explicitly cited a physical evaporation experiment — in which evaporated water/food-coloring mixture turned clear, directly contradicting the prediction of an "infect" theory that ink particles chemically bond with water particles — as the specific evidence that changed their theory preference. [→ Wagh et al. 2025](#wagh-et-al-2025)

`q1-q2 i1` The teacher recognized, mid-unit, that the class's own agent-based simulation could not adjudicate between the "infect" theory and the correct diffusion theory because both looked visually similar when run, and deliberately introduced the external evaporation experiment specifically because it tested a claim the simulation itself could not test. [→ Wagh et al. 2025](#wagh-et-al-2025)

`q1-q2 · single-classroom qualitative observation with systematic interaction-analysis coding (three researchers, 296 coded utterances, 8/296 disagreements resolved by majority) and triangulated video/artifact data; no comparison classroom or controlled test of the evaporation experiment's specific causal effect` `i2 · a clear qualitative pattern with a directly quantified student-response count (7 of 18) tied to a specific, well-documented instructional moment, though from one classroom with no independent replication` `n=18 consented students (of 29 in the class), one sixth-grade classroom, one experienced teacher, 6-day unit`

In a sixth-grade computational-modeling unit on particle diffusion using [Three Practices for Ontological Alignment in Computational Modeling](../patterns/three-practices-for-ontological-alignment-in-computational-modeling.md), the teacher recognized that the class's agent-based simulation could not visually distinguish between two competing student theories and introduced a physical evaporation experiment whose result directly contradicted one theory's prediction. Post-unit written reflections from 18 consented students were analyzed for whether and how they referenced this experiment in explaining a change in their thinking.

## Discussion
This is a single-classroom qualitative study without a comparison condition, so the claim documents what happened in this specific instructional sequence, not a general or causally-isolated effect of introducing external evidence. The 39% figure reflects only students who explicitly mentioned the experiment in a written reflection; it likely undercounts students whose thinking was influenced without stating so directly, and may also reflect recency bias (the experiment occurred late in the unit). No independent measure verifies that these students' post-experiment theories were durable beyond the unit's assessment.
