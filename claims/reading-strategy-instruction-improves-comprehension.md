# Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis `r?` · `q3`–`q4` · `i0`–`i2`
> **Currency** · newest study 2025 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

Explicit teaching of reading strategies — planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's understanding of text — improves learners' comprehension beyond what general reading practice produces.

## Subclaims

[→ Ueno et al. 2025](#ueno-et-al-2025)

## Evidence

### Ueno et al. 2025

Ueno, S., Ikeda, M., Takeuchi, O., & Teng, M. F. (2025). Impact of reading strategy instruction on improvement of strategy use and reading comprehension: A meta-analysis. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 37*(2), 227–250. [doi:10.64152/10125/67505](https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/67505)

`q4 · meta-analysis (27 studies)` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.72` · `n=20 articles / 26 effect sizes (2,352 participants), between-group comparison` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Meta-analysis of 27 studies of reading-strategy (RS) instruction for English L2/EFL learners. Comparing experimental groups to control groups at posttest, reading comprehension improved with a medium-to-large effect (g = 0.72, k = 20 articles, 95% CI [0.49, 0.95], p < .001); a separate within-group (pre/post) comparison across 24 articles gave g = 0.95. RS use itself also improved substantially (g = 0.62–1.24 across designs). Moderator analyses found larger effects in ESL than EFL settings and some variation by educational level and proficiency, but effects were significant across nearly every subgroup.

### Okkinga et al. 2018

Okkinga, M., van Steensel, R., van Gelderen, A. J. S., van Schooten, E., Sleegers, P. J. C., & Arends, L. R. (2018). Effectiveness of reading-strategy interventions in whole classrooms: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 30*(4), 1215–1239. [doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9445-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9445-7)

`q3 · meta-analysis (52 studies)` · `i0 · negligible effect on standardized tests, d=.186` · `n=52 studies / 125 effect sizes` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

Meta-analysis of 52 studies (125 effect sizes) testing reading-strategy interventions delivered in *whole classrooms* by regular teachers, rather than in the small researcher-led tutoring groups most prior comprehension-strategy research used. On standardized reading-comprehension tests the pooled effect was very small (Cohen's d = .186); on researcher-developed comprehension tests it was small (d = .431); on measures of strategic ability it was medium (d = .786). Effects were larger when the researcher rather than the classroom teacher delivered the training and when the intervention included explicit goal-setting — evidence that ecological validity (real teachers, real classrooms) substantially shrinks the effect relative to controlled studies.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Strategy instruction is typically explained through self-regulated learning: skilled readers actively plan, monitor, and repair their understanding, while struggling readers often lack these procedures rather than the underlying language skill. Teaching strategies makes these otherwise invisible processes explicit and practicable, connecting to [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

**What "instruction" means matters.** The claim is strongest for *explicit, teacher-modeled* instruction — the teacher names the strategy, demonstrates its use, guides practice, and gradually releases responsibility (unsourced on this page; Okkinga et al. 2018 found larger effects when the researcher rather than the classroom teacher delivered the training). Simply telling learners that strategies exist, or assigning strategy-labelled activities without modeling, produces much weaker effects (unsourced on this page). Strategies such as summarizing, questioning, predicting, and clarifying are the most consistently studied.

**Transfer and maintenance.** A recurring boundary condition is whether strategy use persists after instruction ends. Effects are strongest on trained texts and near-transfer tasks, as Okkinga et al. 2018's larger effects on researcher-developed than on standardized tests suggest; durable, independent use requires extended practice across varied texts, not a single instructional unit.

**Interaction with decoding.** Strategy instruction cannot compensate for weak word recognition (unsourced on this page). If decoding is effortful, working-memory resources are consumed before comprehension strategies can operate — see [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension](automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) and [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md). Strategy instruction is most effective once basic decoding is reasonably automatic.

**Expertise reversal.** For already-skilled readers, extensive strategy scaffolding can become redundant or even counterproductive (unsourced on this page), mirroring the [Expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md). Instruction should fade as readers internalize the strategies and begin deploying them flexibly and independently.

**Open questions.** The relative effectiveness of single-strategy versus multiple-strategy (packages) instruction, optimal timing across grade levels, and how much explicit modeling is needed before independent practice remain actively debated.
