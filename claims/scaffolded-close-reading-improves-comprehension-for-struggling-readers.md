# Scaffolded Close Reading Improves Comprehension For Struggling Readers

Struggling readers comprehend challenging texts better when close reading is scaffolded — teacher-guided text-dependent questioning, repeated readings, and gradual release of responsibility — rather than assigned as independent work.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** Close reading asks readers to attend to text structure, vocabulary, and author's craft at a depth that exceeds the independent capability of struggling readers. Scaffolds — such as [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the text into manageable segments, [annotating](../principles/annotating.md), teacher modeling of text-dependent questions, and [activating prior knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) — reduce the working-memory burden so attention can go to meaning-making rather than decoding. This aligns with [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and with the claim that [automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md).

**Gradual release as the operative scaffold.** The claim's power lies less in any single technique than in the sequence: teacher modeling ("I do"), guided collaborative work ("we do"), then independent application ("you do"). For struggling readers, skipping the modeled and guided phases and assigning complex text cold produces frustration and disengagement rather than comprehension growth; the scaffold is the temporary support plus its planned removal, not permanent hand-holding.

**Boundary conditions.** The claim is scoped to struggling readers; for proficient readers, heavy scaffolding may be unnecessary or redundant, consistent with expertise-reversal reasoning (see [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)). Scaffolds should be faded as competence develops, moving toward independent reading through gradual release. Close reading of a single text also trades breadth for depth; it should complement, not replace, volume of independent reading — struggling readers in particular need both intensive scaffolded work with complex text and abundant accessible reading to build fluency and vocabulary.

**Open questions.** The evidence base for this claim still needs to be populated — studies are needed to establish effect sizes, the relative value of specific scaffolds (questioning vs. annotation vs. repeated reading), and durability of gains across text types and grade levels. Until then, treat this claim as a well-motivated synthesis of adjacent evidence (cognitive load, annotation, chunking, fluency) rather than a directly tested finding.
