# Classroom Design Affects Learning Progress

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)

Physical characteristics of a classroom — light, air quality, temperature, acoustics, layout, and visual complexity — measurably affect how much learning progress students make in it, independently of instruction.

## Discussion
The strongest work in this area attributes effects to a set of distinguishable physical factors rather than to "good design" as a whole, which matters for instructional decisions: the actionable finding is which factors carry weight, not that environment matters in general. Naturalness factors (daylight, air quality, temperature) and individualization factors (flexibility, ownership of the space) recur as the largest contributors, while visual complexity behaves non-monotonically — some display supports learning and heavy display appears to compete for attention.

Two cautions apply to how this claim gets used. First, most of the evidence is observational: classrooms are not randomly assigned, and schools with better facilities differ in many other ways, so effect estimates are vulnerable to confounding even where multi-level models control for intake. Second, environmental effects are small relative to instructional ones, so this claim justifies attention to the room, not investment in the room at the expense of teaching quality.

This page is a draft: the specific studies, effect sizes, and DOIs still need to be gathered and verified before any figure from this literature is quoted. Barrett and colleagues' multi-level analysis of UK primary classrooms is the obvious starting point, and its citation should be confirmed against the record rather than reproduced from memory.
