# Scaffolding does not preferentially benefit female students; apparent preferential gain is an artifact of achievement-level composition

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Average exam-score gains from increased scaffolding were 6.6% for female and 5.2% for male students, with neither gender difference statistically significant. [→ Hillary Dawkins 2017](#hillary-dawkins-2017)
`q2 i?` Scaffolding gains depend on achievement level, greatest for intermediate students; expected gender gains computed from level composition (6.2% female, 5.8% male) match actual gains. [→ Hillary Dawkins 2017 (2)](#hillary-dawkins-2017-2)

## Evidence

### Hillary Dawkins 2017

Hillary Dawkins, Holly Hedgeland, and Sally Jordan. (2017). The impact of scaffolding and question structure on the gender gap. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.13.020117](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.13.020117)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Observational analysis of exam responses from Open University modules S207 (2012-2014) and S217 (2015), with questions scored by scaffolding elements into low and high groups. The article reports "The average gain is 6.6% for female students, and 5.2% for male students."

> "The average gain is 6.6% for female students, and 5.2% for male students."

### Hillary Dawkins 2017 (2)

Hillary Dawkins, Holly Hedgeland, and Sally Jordan. (2017). The impact of scaffolding and question structure on the gender gap. [doi:10.1103/physrevphyseducres.13.020117](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevphyseducres.13.020117)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Analysis of exam performance by achievement level (1-4) assigned by overall module performance, with N males 414/732/577/297 and N females 85/248/151/71 across levels 1-4. Gains by level were 0.33, 7.0, 8.7, and 5.4 percentage points; the article concludes preferential female gain is "simply an artifact of gain dependency on level."

> "As expected, scaﬀolding provides the greatest gains to the intermediate students."
