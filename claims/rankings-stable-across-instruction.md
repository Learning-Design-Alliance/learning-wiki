## Evidence

### Trevor I. Smith 2024

Trevor I. Smith, Mohammad A. Kazmi, Richard R. Sarles, III, Joshua A. Sbrana, Cody W. Soper, and Nasrine Bendjilali. (2024). Comparing item response theory models for ranking incorrect response options. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Numerical comparison of NRM rankings from AllPre (N = 11,494), AllPost (N = 10,769), and Combined (N = 22,263) data sets. The article reports the highest and lowest incorrect responses are always the same, suggesting rankings may be stable across instruction.

> "The highest and lowest incorrect responses are always the same, and any dis-crepancies are localized to two or three adjacent responses, suggesting that these responses may be more similar than suggested by the calculated Hedges' g values."

### Trevor I. Smith 2024 (2)

Trevor I. Smith, Mohammad A. Kazmi, Richard R. Sarles, III, Joshua A. Sbrana, Cody W. Soper, and Nasrine Bendjilali. (2024). Comparing item response theory models for ranking incorrect response options. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.05231)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Response-frequency comparison across pre- and post-instruction subsets shown in Fig. 1(b) for item 19, with similar trends for other items with consistent highest-ranked incorrect responses. The article suggests learning may be occurring even though students are not choosing the correct response.

> "students in both DS1 and DS2 are more likely to choose the highest-ranked incorrect response to item 19 (A) after in-struction than before instruction."
