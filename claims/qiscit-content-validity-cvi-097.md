# Iterative expert content validation produced strong content validity for QISCIT, with average CVI rising to 0.97 and expert accuracy to 91% in the final 31-item version

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` After two rounds of feedback from 11 QIS experts, the final 31-item QISCIT achieved an average item-level content validity index of 0.97 and 91% expert accuracy. [→ Kelley Durkin 2026](#kelley-durkin-2026)

## Evidence

### Kelley Durkin 2026

Kelley Durkin, Manshuo Lin, Michael H. Kolodrubetz, Ryan P. McMahan. (2026). QISCIT: A validated concept inventory assessment for quantum information science. arXiv. Originally published 2025. [doi:10.1119/5.0287731](https://doi.org/10.1119/5.0287731)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r3`

Second round of expert content validation of QISCIT: 11 QIS experts rated each item's content relevance from 1 to 4, with items accepted at a 0.78 CVI threshold. After dropping three items, "the average CVI across the remaining 31 items was even higher (0.97), and the accuracy further increased (91%)."

> "After removing these three questions, the average CVI across the remaining 31 items was even higher (0.97), and the accuracy further increased (91%)."

### Kelley Durkin 2026 (2)

Kelley Durkin, Manshuo Lin, Michael H. Kolodrubetz, Ryan P. McMahan. (2026). QISCIT: A validated concept inventory assessment for quantum information science. arXiv. Originally published 2025. [doi:10.1119/5.0287731](https://doi.org/10.1119/5.0287731)

`q2 · i?` · `design · r3`

Across the two expert-review rounds and final version, the authors report overall expert accuracy rising from 87% to 89% to 91% as low-CVI, low-accuracy, or confusingly worded questions were revised or dropped. The article reports these as descriptive percentages with no effect size.

> "By revising or removing these questions, our overall accuracy went from 87% in the first round to 89% in the second round to 91% in the final version (obtained by dropping problematic questions from the second round)."
