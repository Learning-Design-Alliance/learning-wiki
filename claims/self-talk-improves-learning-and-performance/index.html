Self Talk Improves Learning And Performance¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 quant-synthesis r?, 1 review r? · q3 · i2 medium

Instructional or motivational self-talk — verbalizing task-relevant cues or encouragement to oneself — can improve attention, technique, and performance on learning and motor tasks.

Evidence¶

Hatzigeorgiadis et al. 2011¶

Hatzigeorgiadis, A., Zourbanos, N., Galanis, E., & Theodorakis, Y. (2011). Self-Talk and Sports Performance: A Meta-Analysis. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 6(4), 348–356. doi:10.1177/1745691611413136

q3 · peer-reviewed meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect, ES=.48 · n=32 studies (62 effect sizes) · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analytic review of self-talk interventions on task performance, pooling 32 studies yielding 62 effect sizes, mostly in sport settings. The overall effect was positive and moderate (ES = .48). Moderator analyses found self-talk interventions were more effective for fine (versus gross) motor tasks and for novel (versus well-learned) tasks; instructional self-talk outperformed motivational self-talk specifically on fine-motor tasks; and interventions that included explicit self-talk training were more effective than those that did not.

Tod et al. 2011¶

Tod, D., Hardy, J., & Oliver, E. (2011). Effects of Self-Talk: A Systematic Review. Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 33(5), 666–687. doi:10.1123/jsep.33.5.666

q3 · systematic review · i? · no pooled effect size reported · n=47 studies · review · r?

A systematic review of 47 studies on the self-talk–performance relationship, including "second-generation" questions about mediators and moderators. Results indicated beneficial effects of positive, instructional, and motivational self-talk on performance. Two findings challenged popular assumptions: negative self-talk did not impede performance, and evidence for differential effects of instructional versus motivational self-talk by task type was inconsistent. Mediation analyses found cognitive and behavioral factors had the most consistent relationships with self-talk's effect.

Discussion¶

Self-talk research distinguishes two broad forms with different mechanisms. Instructional self-talk (cue words or phrases describing the action, e.g., "elbow up" in basketball shooting) is thought to direct attention to task-relevant features and support skill acquisition, and is generally most useful during early learning when learners must consciously control movement or procedure. Motivational self-talk ("I can do this") is thought to sustain effort, confidence, and persistence, and tends to matter more in endurance, anxiety-provoking, or high-pressure situations.

Boundary conditions follow from this distinction. Overly verbose self-talk can itself consume working-memory resources, so cue words should be short and task-relevant — consistent with cognitive load management and the broader finding that cognitive load reduction improves learning. As skill develops, instructional self-talk may become redundant or even disruptive (an expertise-reversal-like pattern, cf. expertise reversal effect), because it forces conscious control over actions that are becoming automatic. Negative or self-critical self-talk is associated with worse performance and higher anxiety, so the valence of the internal dialogue matters as much as its presence.

Open questions include how durable the effects are beyond immediate performance, how self-talk interacts with self-regulated learning strategies more broadly, and whether effects transfer from motor tasks (where most evidence originates) to complex academic learning. The evidence recorded above comes from sport and motor performance (ES = .48 in the meta-analysis); whether self-talk improves academic learning is not tested by it.
