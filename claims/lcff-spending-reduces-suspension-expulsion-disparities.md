# LCFF-induced spending increases reduced suspensions and expulsions, with larger effects for boys and the most pronounced effects for Black students

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study (2 entries) · causal `r2` · `q2`

## Subclaims
`q2 i?` A $1,000 per-pupil spending increase over 3 consecutive years reduced the likelihood of suspension or expulsion in a high school year by 5–6 percentage points for boys and 3 points for girls. [→ Johnson 2023](#johnson-2023)
`q2 i?` For Black students, the same $1,000 increase (grades 8–10) reduced the likelihood of suspension or expulsion in 10th grade by 8 percentage points for Black boys and 5 points for Black girls. [→ Johnson 2023 (2)](#johnson-2023-2)

## Evidence

### Johnson 2023

Johnson, R. C. (2023). School funding effectiveness: Evidence from California's Local Control Funding Formula. Learning Policy Institute. https://doi.org/10.54300/529.194

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Quasi-experimental analysis of disciplinary incidents across grades 3–10, using LCFF-induced spending variation. Effects were greater for boys than girls and larger in high school than elementary and middle school.

> "a $1,000 increase in per-pupil spending experienced for 3 consecutive years was associated with a 5 to 6 percentage-point reduction in the likelihood of being suspended or expelled in a given year of high school for boys and a 3 percentage-point reduction for girls."

### Johnson 2023 (2)

Johnson, R. C. (2023). School funding effectiveness: Evidence from California's Local Control Funding Formula. Learning Policy Institute. https://doi.org/10.54300/529.194

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Subgroup analysis of the same disciplinary outcome for Black students, the group the article identifies as having the most pronounced impacts. No standardised effect size is printed for these estimates.

> "a $1,000 increase in per-pupil spending experienced for 3 consecutive years (grades 8–10) was associated with an 8 percentage-point reduction in the likelihood of suspension or expulsion in high school (10th grade) among Black boys and a 5 percentage-point reduction in the probability of suspension or expulsion for Black girls."
