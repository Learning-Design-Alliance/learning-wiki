## Evidence

### Giles Westerfield 2015

Giles Westerfield, Antonija Mitrovic & Mark Billinghurst. (2015). Intelligent Augmented Reality Training for Motherboard Assembly. Int J Artif Intell Educ 25:157–172. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-014-0032-x

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

In the evaluation study, 16 university students were randomly allocated to the intelligent AR tutor or the traditional AR tutor. Post-test performance of the experimental group was "significantly higher than that of the control group (t(14) = 3.374, p<.005)". Means were 9.13 (SD 1.13) versus 6.63 (SD 1.77) out of 10.

> "The post-test performance of the experimental group is significantly higher than that of the control group (t(14) = 3.374, p<.005). This is less than the Bonferroni-corrected value of .0083 (.05/6), so the intelligent AR tutor produced a significantly better learning outcome than the non- intelligent AR tutor."

### Giles Westerfield 2015 (2)

Giles Westerfield, Antonija Mitrovic & Mark Billinghurst. (2015). Intelligent Augmented Reality Training for Motherboard Assembly. Int J Artif Intell Educ 25:157–172. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-014-0032-x

`q2 · i3` · `causal · r1`

Normalized learning gains were 0.66 (SD 0.26) for the experimental group and 0.40 (SD 0.21) for the control group. The article reports "a significant difference between the normalized learning gains of the two groups (t(14) = 2.198, p<.05)" with an effect size of "Cohen 'sd " = 0.981, a large effect.

> "There is also a significant difference between the normalized learning gains of the two groups (t(14) = 2.198, p<.05). The effect size (Cohen 'sd ) is 0.981, which is a significant improvement."
