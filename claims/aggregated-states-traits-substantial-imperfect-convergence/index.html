Evidence¶

Keller 2025¶

Keller, M. M., Yanagida, T., Lüdtke, O., & Goetz, T. (2025). How Similar Are Students' Aggregated State Emotions to Their Self-Reported Trait Emotions? Results from a Measurement Burst Design Across Three School Years. Educational Psychology Review. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-09995-1

q3 · i3 · associational · r2

Longitudinal measurement burst study of N = 161 students followed from grade 9 to 11; bivariate person-level correlations of aggregated states with traits per emotion and wave, Fisher-z averaged across waves. The study reports "for boredom r = 0.608" and shame r = 0.391, all correlations statistically significant at p = 0.05.

"The across-waves averaged correlations between aggregated states and self-reported traits for enjoyment were r = 0.424, for anger r = 0.488, for pride r = 0.520, for anxiety r = 0.535, for shame r = 0.391, and for boredom r = 0.608."

Related Claims¶

  • Most variance in students' momentary classroom emotions lies at the within-person level, yet person-aggregated state measures are fairly reliable (ICC(2) from 0.735 to 0.901) — related
  • Long-term stability of aggregated state emotions does not significantly differ from that of self-reported trait emotions over one- and two-year spans — related
  • Self-reported trait emotions were rated significantly higher than aggregated states for some emotions and timepoints (anger, pride, shame, boredom), but the memory-experience gap did not hold uniformly and anxiety showed the reverse descriptive pattern — related
  • Aggregated state emotions show no superior incremental predictive validity over self-reported traits for academic outcomes; traits corrected for states were only systematically correlated with criteria for enjoyment and somewhat for pride — related
  • Students report statistically significant levels of boredom during mathematics tests, for both trait and state measures — related
  • Within-domain emotion structure only partially matches the valence–activation model: anger relates more strongly to boredom than to anxiety, and enjoyment–boredom and anger–boredom correlations are conspicuously strong — related
