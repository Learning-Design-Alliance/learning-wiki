# Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3` peer-reviewed experiment · `i2` medium

Preparing to teach and teaching improve the tutor's own learning. (Whether tutors gain *more* than their tutees is often said but is not established by the evidence below, which does not compare the two.) Preparing to teach, explaining aloud, and responding to a learner's questions drive the tutor to organize, elaborate, and fill gaps in their own understanding.

## Subclaims

`q3 i2` Across 28 studies, teaching after preparing to teach improved the tutor's domain knowledge relative to studying without a teaching expectancy (g = 0.56), with preparing-to-teach alone giving a smaller benefit (g = 0.35) and interactive teaching outperforming non-interactive teaching. [→ Kobayashi 2019](#kobayashi-2019)

## Evidence

### Kobayashi 2019

Kobayashi, K. (2019). Learning by preparing-to-teach and teaching: A meta-analysis. *Japanese Psychological Research, 61*(3), 192–203. [doi:10.1111/jpr.12221](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpr.12221)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.56 (teaching with preparing-to-teach); g=0.35 (preparing-to-teach alone)` · `n=28 studies`

This meta-analysis pooled 28 studies comparing learners who prepared to teach, or prepared and then actually taught, against learners who simply studied the same material with no expectation of teaching. Preparing to teach produced a small-to-medium gain in domain knowledge, and preparing and then teaching produced a larger, medium gain; both helped deep as well as surface learning and persisted after a delay. The benefit was larger when the teaching was interactive (responding to a learner) than when it was non-interactive, and even the mere expectation of interactive teaching helped more than expecting to teach non-interactively. This supports the page's distinction between "prepare to teach" as a weaker intervention and "teach and respond to questions" as a stronger one. Read from the abstract only.

### Cohen et al. 1982

Cohen, P. A., Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1982). Educational outcomes of tutoring: A meta-analysis of findings. *American Educational Research Journal, 19*(2), 237–248. [doi:10.3102/00028312019002237](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237)

`q3 · meta-analysis of programme evaluations` · `i? · no effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=65 studies`

This meta-analysis synthesised 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programmes. Besides benefiting the students who were tutored, the programmes had positive effects on the children who served as tutors: tutors came to understand the tutored subject matter better and developed more positive attitudes toward it. Tutoring had little or no effect on the self-esteem of either tutors or tutees. The abstract does not compare the size of tutor gains with tutee gains, so it does not bear on the page's statement that tutors often learn more than tutees. Read from the abstract only.

## Discussion

**Why teaching works on the tutor.** The expected effect is usually explained by the preparatory and generative demands of teaching: anticipating a learner's questions forces deeper processing, and explaining aloud exposes gaps that silent study conceals. This places learning-by-teaching close to other generative activities — see [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) — in that the benefit accrues from what the tutor must retrieve, organize, and articulate, not from the social role itself. Explaining aloud is also a form of self-explanation, which links this claim to the broader family of elaboration effects; the tutor must reconstruct material in their own words rather than reread it.

**Expectation matters.** A plausible moderator is whether the tutor expects to teach. If the tutor does not actually teach afterward, much of the preparatory deep processing may not occur, so the effect should be strongest when teaching genuinely happens. Designers should therefore treat "prepare to teach" as a distinct, weaker intervention than "teach and respond to questions."

**Boundary conditions.** The effect should depend on the tutor having enough baseline knowledge to explain meaningfully; a tutor with severe knowledge gaps may simply rehearse misconceptions, and the tutee inherits those errors. The quality of the tutee's engagement (asking questions, requesting explanations) is also likely to moderate how much gap-filling the tutor performs — a passive tutee reduces teaching to recitation, stripping out the retrieval and elaboration that drive the effect. Tutee confusion or probing questions may be the single most valuable feature of the interaction for the tutor's own learning, since they force the tutor to confront and repair incomplete understanding.

**Design implications.** Peer-tutoring and teach-back formats should be structured so the tutor must retrieve and reconstruct rather than read aloud: assign the tutor material they have studied but not mastered, prompt the tutee to ask genuine questions, and build in a step where the tutor checks the tutee's understanding and diagnoses errors. Reciprocal role rotation (each pair member alternates tutor/tutee) lets both learners capture the tutor-side benefit while limiting the time any one learner spends explaining material they barely know. Where full peer tutoring is impractical, structured explanation tasks — teach-backs, jigsaw expert roles, or explaining to a hypothetical novice — approximate the generative demands without requiring a real tutee, though the absence of genuine probing questions likely weakens the gap-filling component. Because explaining imposes its own generative load, tutors with weak baseline knowledge may need [cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md) supports (e.g., reference sheets, scripted prompts) so that the effort goes into reasoning rather than coping; the same expertise-sensitivity is captured by the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md), which predicts that supports helpful for novice tutors become redundant for knowledgeable ones.

**Open questions.** No evidence entries have been added to this page yet; the specific studies, effect sizes, and moderators still need to be sourced before the claim can be rated. Key open questions include whether the effect holds across age groups and domains, how it compares to equivalent-time generative activities such as writing summaries or self-explaining, and whether structured tutoring protocols (e.g., scripted roles, tutee question prompts) amplify or dampen the tutor-side benefit.
