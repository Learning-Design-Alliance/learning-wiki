# Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q3` peer-reviewed experiment · `i2` medium

Preparing to teach and teaching improve the tutor's own learning. (Whether tutors gain *more* than their tutees is often said but is not established by the evidence below, which does not compare the two.) Preparing to teach, explaining aloud, and responding to a learner's questions drive the tutor to organize, elaborate, and fill gaps in their own understanding.

## Subclaims

`q3 i2` Across 28 studies, teaching after preparing to teach improved the tutor's domain knowledge relative to studying without a teaching expectancy (g = 0.56), with preparing-to-teach alone giving a smaller benefit (g = 0.35) and interactive teaching outperforming non-interactive teaching. [→ Kobayashi 2019](#kobayashi-2019)

`q3 i?` In a meta-analysis of 65 evaluations of school tutoring programmes, the children who served as tutors gained a better understanding of the tutored subject matter, alongside the gains of the tutees; the abstract reports no effect size for tutors. [→ Cohen et al. 1982](#cohen-et-al-1982)

## Evidence

### Kobayashi 2019

Kobayashi, K. (2019). Learning by preparing-to-teach and teaching: A meta-analysis. *Japanese Psychological Research, 61*(3), 192–203. [doi:10.1111/jpr.12221](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpr.12221)

`q3 · meta-analysis` · `i2 · medium effect, g=0.56 (teaching with preparing-to-teach); g=0.35 (preparing-to-teach alone)` · `n=28 studies`

This meta-analysis pooled 28 studies comparing learners who prepared to teach, or prepared and then actually taught, against learners who simply studied the same material with no expectation of teaching. Preparing to teach produced a small-to-medium gain in domain knowledge, and preparing and then teaching produced a larger, medium gain; both helped deep as well as surface learning and persisted after a delay. The benefit was larger when the teaching was interactive (responding to a learner) than when it was non-interactive, and even the mere expectation of interactive teaching helped more than expecting to teach non-interactively. This supports the page's distinction between "prepare to teach" as a weaker intervention and "teach and respond to questions" as a stronger one. Read from the abstract only.

### Cohen et al. 1982

Cohen, P. A., Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1982). Educational outcomes of tutoring: A meta-analysis of findings. *American Educational Research Journal, 19*(2), 237–248. [doi:10.3102/00028312019002237](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237)

`q3 · meta-analysis of programme evaluations` · `i? · no effect size reported in the abstract` · `n=65 studies`

This meta-analysis synthesised 65 independent evaluations of school tutoring programmes. Besides benefiting the students who were tutored, the programmes had positive effects on the children who served as tutors: tutors came to understand the tutored subject matter better and developed more positive attitudes toward it. Tutoring had little or no effect on the self-esteem of either tutors or tutees. The abstract does not compare the size of tutor gains with tutee gains, so it does not bear on the page's statement that tutors often learn more than tutees. Read from the abstract only.

## Discussion

**Why teaching works on the tutor.** The expected effect is usually explained by the preparatory and generative demands of teaching: anticipating a learner's questions forces deeper processing, and explaining aloud exposes gaps that silent study conceals. This places learning-by-teaching close to other generative activities — see [Active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) — in that the benefit accrues from what the tutor must retrieve, organize, and articulate, not from the social role itself. Explaining aloud is also a form of self-explanation, which links this claim to the broader family of elaboration effects; the tutor must reconstruct material in their own words rather than reread it.

**Expectation matters.** A plausible moderator is whether the tutor expects to teach. If the tutor does not actually teach afterward, much of the preparatory deep processing may not occur, so the effect should be strongest when teaching genuinely happens. Designers should therefore treat "prepare to teach" as a distinct, weaker intervention than "teach and respond to questions."

**Boundary conditions.** The effect should depend on the tutor having enough baseline knowledge to explain meaningfully; a tutor with severe knowledge gaps may simply rehearse misconceptions, and the tutee inherits those errors. The quality of the tutee's engagement (asking questions, requesting explanations) is also likely to moderate how much gap-filling the tutor performs — a passive tutee reduces teaching to recitation, stripping out the retrieval and elaboration that drive the effect. Tutee confusion or probing questions may be the single most valuable feature of the interaction for the tutor's own learning, since they force the tutor to confront and repair incomplete understanding.

**Design implications.** Peer-tutoring and teach-back formats should be structured so the tutor must retrieve and reconstruct rather than read aloud: assign the tutor material they have studied but not mastered, prompt the tutee to ask genuine questions, and build in a step where the tutor checks the tutee's understanding and diagnoses errors. Reciprocal role rotation (each pair member alternates tutor/tutee) lets both learners capture the tutor-side benefit while limiting the time any one learner spends explaining material they barely know. Where full peer tutoring is impractical, structured explanation tasks — teach-backs, jigsaw expert roles, or explaining to a hypothetical novice — approximate the generative demands without requiring a real tutee, though the absence of genuine probing questions likely weakens the gap-filling component. Because explaining imposes its own generative load, tutors with weak baseline knowledge may need [cognitive load management](cognitive-load-management.md) supports (e.g., reference sheets, scripted prompts) so that the effort goes into reasoning rather than coping; the same expertise-sensitivity is captured by the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md), which predicts that supports helpful for novice tutors become redundant for knowledgeable ones.

**Open questions.** No evidence entries have been added to this page yet; the specific studies, effect sizes, and moderators still need to be sourced before the claim can be rated. Key open questions include whether the effect holds across age groups and domains, how it compares to equivalent-time generative activities such as writing summaries or self-explaining, and whether structured tutoring protocols (e.g., scripted roles, tutee question prompts) amplify or dampen the tutor-side benefit.

*Merged from “Learning By Teaching Improves Learning” (learning-by-teaching-improves-learning):* **Mechanisms.** The learning-by-teaching effect is typically attributed to two complementary processes. First, *preparation to teach* drives deeper processing: expecting to explain material to someone else prompts learners to organize content, identify gaps, and rehearse retrieval rather than passively reread [+M]. Second, *actual teaching* forces generative elaboration — the tutor must reconstruct explanations, answer questions, and monitor a tutee's comprehension, which exposes the tutor's own misconceptions and triggers repair [+M]. Both routes are consistent with generative-processing accounts in [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) and with the constructive character of [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md).

**Preparation vs. enactment.** A recurring open question is how much of the benefit comes from expecting to teach versus teaching itself. Some findings suggest that much of the advantage can be obtained from preparation alone [+M], while the enactment adds benefits mainly when tutors receive feedback, must answer questions, or must adapt explanations to a tutee's needs [~M]. Designers should therefore not assume that merely assigning a teaching role guarantees deep processing; the task must require retrieval, organization, and explanation.

**Boundary conditions.** The effect is strongest when the to-be-taught material is conceptually demanding, when tutors have adequate baseline knowledge to explain it, and when teaching involves interactive dialogue rather than one-way presentation [~M]. Teaching without preparation, or teaching procedural material the tutor has not yet automatized, can produce shallow "illusion of knowing" effects or overload the tutor [-M] — a direct risk under [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) Structured formats — reciprocal peer tutoring, teach-backs, and jigsaw-style [Collaborative Learning](../patterns/collaborative-learning.md) arrangements — tend to manage these risks better than unstructured "explain it to your partner" prompts [+W].

**Relation to other effects.** Learning by teaching overlaps with [Active Learning](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) as a broad category, but the specific claim here is that the *teaching role itself* — not merely activity or interaction — drives the benefit. It also connects to retrieval practice and elaboration: preparing an explanation is, in effect, a demanding retrieval and organization task, linking the effect to [Retrieval practice improves retention.](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) Where the tutor is a novice explaining to a peer, the interaction also resembles the reciprocal dynamics described in [Reciprocal Teaching](../elements/reciprocal-teaching.md), though that pattern emphasizes structured dialogue roles rather than the tutor's learning gain per se.

**Open questions.** Key unresolved issues include: (a) the relative contribution of expectancy versus enactment, (b) whether benefits accrue equally to tutees and tutors, and (c) how tutor expertise moderates the effect — very low-knowledge tutors may lack the resources to explain at all, while high-knowledge tutors may gain little from restating what they already know. Well-controlled studies isolating these moderators are needed before the claim can be rated for evidence strength.

*Merged from “Learning By Teaching Improves Mastery” (learning-by-teaching-improves-mastery):* **Mechanism.** The expected benefit rests on two complementary processes: (a) *expectation-driven preparation* — anticipating that one will teach prompts deeper, more organized encoding than anticipating a test [+W]; and (b) *retrieval and explanation during teaching itself*, which forces the learner to reconstruct, verbalize, and repair gaps in understanding [+M]. The "protégé effect" literature suggests the expectation alone contributes meaningfully, so designers should not assume the benefit requires an actual audience. Teaching is also a maximally generative activity, consistent with the broader finding that [active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M].

**Boundary conditions.** The effect is most plausible when the to-be-taught material is conceptually structured (explanatory rather than rote), when learners have adequate base knowledge to explain it, and when teaching tasks require generative elaboration rather than reading a script aloud. If the teaching task can be satisfied by recitation or shallow paraphrase, the mastery benefit should shrink [~M]. Learners low in prior knowledge may also experience overload when asked to explain before they can comprehend — sequencing study before teach is safer than teach-before-study, consistent with [cognitive load theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) [-M]. This mirrors the expertise-reversal pattern documented for other generative methods: activities that help knowledgeable learners can burden novices.

**Relation to retrieval practice.** Explaining material to another person is a generative retrieval event, so part of any observed benefit may overlap with the well-established effect of [retrieval practice improves retention](retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Well-designed comparisons match study time and retrieval demands across conditions; until such evidence entries are added, the unique contribution of the *teaching* frame beyond retrieval practice remains open.

**Design implications.** Where the claim is used, structure teaching tasks so they demand explanation, justification, and error correction rather than recitation; provide source material and adequate study time before the teaching episode; and consider "teach-to-prepare" manipulations (learners told they will teach, then tested without teaching) as a low-cost variant when logistics of real audiences are prohibitive [+W]. Peer tutoring formats can operationalize the claim at scale — see [collaborative learning improves outcomes](collaborative-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]. Reciprocal role-reversal structures (each tutee later tutors) distribute the generative burden and prevent the tutor/tutee asymmetry from becoming a fixed ability signal within a class.

**Open questions.** How durable the benefit is over long retention intervals, how it compares to equivalent-time retrieval practice, and whether benefits transfer to far transfer rather than near recall all remain under-established until evidence entries are added. It is also unresolved whether the expectation-to-teach effect holds when learners repeatedly expect to teach but are never asked to — a common outcome in classroom implementations where teaching episodes get cut for time [~W].
