## Subclaims

## Evidence

### Cacey S. Stevens 2015

Cacey S. Stevens, Michael Marder, and Sidney R. Nagel. (2015). Patterns in Illinois Educational School Data. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Weighted-deviation analysis of 2013 math exam data (Fig. 4b, Table III): the average difference Δ between Chicago passing fractions and the state best-fit line was computed, weighted by test takers. The authors find Chicago school types "perform better, on average, than expected" at every grade, with neighborhood schools improving up to grade 8.

> "At every grade level, all Chicago school types, including neighborhood schools, perform better, on average, than expected based on achievement of the rest of the state."

### Cacey S. Stevens 2015 (2)

Cacey S. Stevens, Michael Marder, and Sidney R. Nagel. (2015). Patterns in Illinois Educational School Data. arXiv preprint. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1502.02057)

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r2`

Comparison at 11th grade (Fig. 4a) using the 2013 Math PSAE, restricted to 103 of 105 Chicago career, charter, neighborhood, magnet and military schools with at least 48% low-income students, because data below 48% poverty is sparse in Chicago.

> "When restricting attention to the range of 48-100% poverty concentration, we ﬁnd that Chicago high schools have signiﬁcantly higher scores than schools in the rest of Illinois, except for the highest poverty schools where the diﬀerence vanishes on average."
