# Generation Effect Improves Retention

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 studies · `q4` meta-analysis and originating experiments · `i2` medium · moderated by material

Information that learners actively generate (completing a word, producing an answer, deriving a rule) is remembered better than information they passively read, even when generation takes longer or produces errors.

## Subclaims

`q3 i2` Across five experiments, words the learner generated from a cue and a rule were recognised and recalled better than words simply read, with study time and materials otherwise matched. [→ Slamecka & Graf 1978](#slamecka-graf-1978)

`q4 i2` Meta-analysis confirmed a positive overall generation effect and found it strongly moderated — by the type of material, the generation task, and whether the test was recall or recognition. [→ Bertsch et al. 2007](#bertsch-et-al-2007)

`q4 i2` The effect is best attested for simple verbal materials generated from a rule. Evidence for complex or novel material, where the learner may not be able to generate anything correct, is much weaker. [→ Bertsch et al. 2007](#bertsch-et-al-2007)

## Evidence

### Slamecka & Graf 1978

Slamecka, N. J., & Graf, P. (1978). The generation effect: Delineation of a phenomenon. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 4*(6), 592–604. [doi:10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592)

`q3` · `i2`

The originating demonstration: participants either read word pairs or generated the second word from the first plus a rule (a rhyme, a synonym, a category member). Generated words were better retained across five experiments and several test formats. The paper names the phenomenon and establishes its boundary conditions in the same place.

### Bertsch et al. 2007

Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., & McDaniel, M. A. (2007). The generation effect: A meta-analytic review. *Memory & Cognition, 35*(2), 201–210. [doi:10.3758/BF03193441](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441)

`q4` · `i2`

A meta-analytic review of the generation-effect literature. The pooled effect was positive and reliable, and the moderator analysis is the more useful result for design: effects varied substantially with material type and generation task, and the conditions producing the largest effects were not the ones most like authentic instruction.

## Discussion

The generation effect is typically explained as a consequence of deeper processing: producing an answer forces the learner to activate prior knowledge and elaborate the target item, creating richer retrieval routes than reading provides. A key boundary condition is that generation imposes working-memory demands, so the benefit depends on the learner having sufficient knowledge to generate a plausible response — for complete novices, unguided generation can overload working memory and degrade learning, consistent with [cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]. This makes the effect closely related to the expertise-reversal pattern described in [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md): generation tasks that help knowledgeable learners may hinder novices, and scaffolds such as partial cues or worked examples can restore the benefit by reducing the generation burden (see [chunking reduces working memory load](chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)) [~W].

Design implications follow directly from this boundary condition. For learners with adequate prior knowledge, designers should prefer tasks that require producing an answer — cloze deletion, prediction before explanation, deriving a rule from instances — over tasks that require only recognizing or rereading [~M]. For novices, the same tasks should be scaffolded: partial cues, word banks, or a worked example preceding the generation task preserve the benefit while limiting the search space [~W]. Generation also pairs naturally with [activation](activation-improves-learning.md), since producing an answer is itself an act of retrieving relevant prior knowledge, and it is one of the mechanisms behind why [active learning improves exam performance](active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md).

Open questions include how durable the effect is over long retention intervals, how it interacts with feedback when learners generate incorrect answers, and whether the effect transfers from simple items (e.g., word completion) to complex, integrated knowledge. Because no evidence entries have been added yet, the strength and scope of these claims remain to be established by future enrichment passes.
