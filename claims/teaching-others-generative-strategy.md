# Teaching Others Generative Strategy

Preparing to teach material to others is a generative learning strategy: it prompts learners to organize, elaborate, and restructure content, which can produce deeper processing than studying for a test alone.

## Discussion

**Mechanism.** The expected benefit comes from generative processing: anticipating explanation to another person forces learners to identify main ideas, build connections, and monitor their own understanding. This aligns with the broader family of generative strategies described in [Annotating improves learning](annotating-improves-learning.md) and [Activation improves learning](activation-improves-learning.md). Teaching also sits within the retrieval family — explaining from memory is a form of retrieval practice, which is where much of its effect likely originates.

**Expectation vs. actual teaching.** A key moderator in the literature is whether learners actually teach or merely expect to teach. Expecting to teach can induce deeper study, but some findings suggest that the expectation alone, without retrieval or interaction, may encourage shallow re-reading rather than genuine organization. Actual teaching, especially with questions or confusion from the tutee, is more likely to drive the elaborative benefit. Designers should therefore build in a genuine audience — a peer, a tutee, or even a simulated one — rather than relying on the mere instruction "study this as if you will teach it."

**Boundary conditions.** Benefits should be strongest when the material is conceptually demanding enough to reward restructuring, when learners have adequate base knowledge to explain coherently (otherwise explanation may collapse into parroting), and when the teaching episode includes retrieval and feedback rather than reading aloud from notes. For very low-knowledge learners, the demands of composing an explanation may add extraneous load — see [Cognitive overload degrades learning](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md). This parallels the expertise-reversal pattern documented for other scaffolds: the strategy that helps a moderately knowledgeable learner restructure content can overwhelm a novice.

**Comparison with related strategies.** Teaching others is the interpersonal analogue of [Self-explanation improves learning](self-explanation-improves-learning.md), but adds social accountability and the possibility of tutee questions that expose gaps. Whether the social component adds benefit beyond explaining aloud to oneself is an open empirical question that this page's evidence base has not yet resolved. Peer teaching is frequently embedded in [Collaborative learning improves outcomes](collaborative-learning-improves-outcomes.md) structures, which makes isolating the specific contribution of the teaching role difficult.

**Open questions.** The evidence base for this page has not yet been populated with specific studies; effect sizes, moderating conditions, and comparisons with other generative strategies (e.g., self-explanation, summarizing) remain to be documented. Until controlled studies are added, this claim should be treated as a plausible, theory-grounded hypothesis rather than an established effect.
