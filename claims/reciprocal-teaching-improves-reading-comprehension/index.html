Evidence¶

Rosenshine and Meister 1994¶

Rosenshine, B., & Meister, C. (1994). Reciprocal Teaching: A Review of the Research. Review of Educational Research, 64(4), 479–530. doi:10.3102/00346543064004479

q3 · systematic review of quasi-/experimental studies · i1 · small effect, median d=.32 on standardized tests (d=.88 on experimenter-developed tests) · n=16 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

This review synthesizes sixteen quantitative studies of reciprocal teaching — a dialogue-based routine teaching students to summarize, question, clarify, and predict as they read — drawn from published journal articles and unpublished dissertations. Effects favored reciprocal teaching overall, but the size of the effect depended heavily on the outcome measure: a median effect size of .32 when comprehension was assessed with standardized, norm-referenced tests, versus a much larger .88 when assessed with tests the experimenters themselves developed (which tend to align more closely with the taught strategies). The review also discusses which of the four strategies were most helpful and how instructional quality moderated outcomes.

Discussion¶

The mechanism is usually attributed to two components working together: explicit strategy instruction (the four routines give learners concrete comprehension moves) and scaffolded social practice (students articulate their thinking, receive feedback, and gradually assume the teacher's role) [+M]. This places reciprocal teaching at the intersection of Cognitive Load Theory — the strategies reduce unguided comprehension effort — and Self-Regulated Learning, since learners monitor and repair their own understanding while reading [+M]. The gradual handover of the discussion-leader role is an application of scaffolding and fading, consistent with Cognitive Apprenticeship: modeling, coaching, then independent student-led practice.

Boundary conditions to watch: the routine requires sustained practice over multiple sessions to take hold [~M], and the dialogue format depends on students having sufficient decoding fluency to free attention for comprehension — see Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [~M]. Groups that drift off-task without strong teacher facilitation tend to lose the benefit [-M], and the approach is generally less effective for learners still struggling at the word-recognition level [-M]. Implementation quality is a major moderator: effective enactments include explicit teacher modeling of each strategy, clear role structure within groups, and ongoing monitoring of group dialogue rather than unstructured peer discussion [~M].

Open questions include how much teacher modeling is optimal before handover, how well effects transfer to unfamiliar text types, and whether the four-strategy package is necessary or whether individual strategies (particularly summarizing and questioning) carry most of the benefit.

Single-source limitation. The one review recorded above was read as an abstract. Its effect on standardized comprehension tests (median .32) is much smaller than on tests the researchers wrote themselves (.88), so the size of the benefit depends heavily on how comprehension is measured.
