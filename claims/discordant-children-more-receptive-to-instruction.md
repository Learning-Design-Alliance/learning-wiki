## Subclaims
`q2 i?` Discordant children, who express different procedures in speech and gesture, were more likely to benefit from instruction in equivalence than concordant children, despite neither group solving problems correctly before training. ## Evidence

### Alibali and Goldin-Meadow 1993

Martha Wagner Alibali and Susan Goldin-Meadow. (1993). Gesture-Speech Mismatch and Mechanisms of Learning: What the Hands Reveal about a Child's State of Mind. Cognitive Psychology 25, 468-523. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/001002859390017M

`q2 · i?` · `associational · r1`

The article reports, citing Perry et al. (1988), a study of 9- to 10-year-olds solving and explaining six equivalence problems, in which "discordant" children were "more likely to benefit from instruction in equivalence" than concordant children; no effect size is printed.

> "the children who produced many gesture-speech mismatches in their explanations (labeled “discordant” children by Perry et al.) were more likely to benefit from instruction in equivalence than the children who produced few gesture-speech mismatches"
