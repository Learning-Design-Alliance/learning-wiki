# Deaf children's abstract-counting errors reflect the rule-bound base-5 structure of Belgian French Sign Language: they stop at production-rule boundaries and make fewer omission errors than hearing children

## Evidence

### Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem 2002

Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem. (2002). Counting in Sign Language. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1006/jecp.2002.2660

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Descriptive error analysis of first errors in the abstract counting task: 64% of hearing children versus 24% of deaf children made omission errors. Percentages are descriptive; no statistical test is printed.

> "This kind of error was more common in hearing children than in the deaf children (64% of the hearing children made omission errors compared to 24% of the deaf children)."

### Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem 2002 (2)

Jacqueline Leybaert and Marie-Noëlle Van Cutsem. (2002). Counting in Sign Language. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1006/jecp.2002.2660

`q3 · i?` · `associational · r1`

Descriptive analysis of stopping points: deaf children's stops clustered at BFSL rule-transition points (after 5 and after 15), whereas hearing children's stops varied more widely across the sequence.

> "Most of the deaf children stopped counting in the portion of the number sequence where a new production rule should be adopted, (i.e., after 5 and after 15)."
