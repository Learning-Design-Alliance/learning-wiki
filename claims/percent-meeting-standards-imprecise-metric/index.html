Evidence¶

Luppescu 2011¶

Luppescu, S., Allensworth, E. M., Moore, P., de la Torre, M., Murphy, J., with Jagesic, S. (2011). Trends in Chicago's Schools Across Three Eras of Reform. Consortium on Chicago School Research, University of Chicago. https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/trends-chicagos-schools-across-three-eras-reform-full-report

q2 · i? · associational · r3

Methodological argument in Chapter 2 (Issue 2) about dichotomous reporting metrics. The report also notes the wide "meets" band (e.g., grade-three reading 191–227, roughly the 50th to 90th percentile) has no singular meaning for later outcomes. No effect size is printed.

"Small improvements in test scores can result in many more students meeting norms/standards if there are many students close to the cut-off score, while large overall improvements in test scores can go unnoticed if there are few students with scores close to the cut-off."

Luppescu 2011 (2)¶

Luppescu, S., Allensworth, E. M., Moore, P., de la Torre, M., Murphy, J., with Jagesic, S. (2011). Trends in Chicago's Schools Across Three Eras of Reform. Consortium on Chicago School Research, University of Chicago. https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/trends-chicagos-schools-across-three-eras-reform-full-report

q2 · i? · associational · r2

Re-analysis of Era 2 score distributions reported in Chapter 2. The widespread belief that the district improved low achievers more than high achievers arose because many students scored near the 25th percentile and few near the 75th, so equal growth moved many across the lower cut-off. No effect size is printed.

"In fact, there was fairly equal growth among both low- and high-achieving students during the time period—the district was not better at educating low-skill than high-skill students."
