# Learners Misjudge Spacing Benefits

Learners systematically predict that massed practice will serve them better than spaced practice, even though spacing reliably produces stronger long-term retention — a metacognitive error that leads them to choose study schedules that undermine their own learning.

## Discussion

**The illusion of massing.** Because massed practice produces fluent, effortless performance in the moment, learners interpret that fluency as evidence of learning. Spaced practice feels harder and yields worse immediate performance, so learners judge it less effective even when it produces substantially better delayed retention. This is a case of a desirable difficulty being misread as a defect: conditions that impair short-term performance can enhance long-term learning, and learners' metacognitive monitoring is tuned to short-term performance, not durable retention. The same subjective-experience trap appears in [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md), where the felt ease of a study condition diverges from what it actually produces.

**Design implications.** Because learners cannot be trusted to schedule their own review, spacing should be built into the learning environment rather than delegated to learner choice — fixed review schedules, automatically resurfaced material, and curricula that revisit topics across weeks. This aligns with the design stance taken in [Spaced practice improves long-term retention](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md): the effectiveness of spacing is well documented, but its benefits are only realized when the schedule is imposed or scaffolded rather than left to learner preference. Where learners do choose, explicitly teaching them about the dissociation between performance and learning can partially correct their scheduling preferences, though calibration of judgments of learning remains imperfect even after instruction.

**Boundary conditions.** The misjudgment is strongest for verbal and skill-based material where massed practice feels smooth, and weakest where feedback makes forgetting vivid. Very long spacing gaps can genuinely impair learning, so the claim applies to learners' *relative* misjudgment of moderate spacing versus massing, not to the claim that any spacing is always better. The claim is also about metacognitive judgment and scheduling choice; it does not by itself establish the size of the spacing effect on retention, which must be documented separately in [Spaced practice improves long-term retention](spaced-practice-improves-retention.md).
