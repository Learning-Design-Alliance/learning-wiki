# Mastery Learning Improves Outcomes

> **Claim** · [All claims](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 study · quant-synthesis `r?` · `q4` · `i2` medium · n=108 studies

When learners must demonstrate mastery of each unit before advancing, achievement improves relative to time-fixed, group-paced instruction. The mechanism is holding learning constant and varying time, rather than holding time constant and varying learning.

## Subclaims

`q4 i2` A meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations found mastery learning programs had positive effects on examination performance in college, high-school and upper-elementary settings, apparently stronger for the weaker students in a class (mean effect 0.52 SD across the 103 studies that measured examination performance). [→ Kulik et al. 1990](#kulik-et-al-1990)

## Evidence

### Kulik et al. 1990

Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A., & Bangert-Drowns, R. L. (1990). Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs: A Meta-Analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 60*(2), 265. [doi:10.3102/00346543060002265](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265)

`q4 · meta-analysis (108 controlled evaluations)` · `i2 · medium effect, mean ES = 0.52 SD (SE 0.033) across 103 studies of examination performance` · `n=108 studies` · `quant-synthesis · r?`

This meta-analysis pooled 108 controlled evaluations of mastery learning programs (72 of Keller's Personalized System of Instruction, PSI, almost all self-paced and in college; 36 of Bloom's Learning for Mastery, LFM, group-based and teacher-paced; 67 PSI and 36 LFM studies entered the analysis of examination effects) at the college, high-school and upper-elementary levels. Across the 103 studies that measured examination performance, the mean effect was 0.52 SD: the average mastery student scored at the 70th percentile of the conventional class. PSI (0.48) and LFM (0.59) did not differ significantly. Effects were larger in group-based programs, with higher unit mastery standards, on locally developed rather than standardized tests, and where the control group received less quiz feedback (see the related claims below).

> "The average effect size in the 103 studies was 0.52. That is, the average effect of mastery learning programs was to raise student achievement scores by 0.52 standard deviations."

## Discussion

**Scope and mechanism.** Mastery learning restructures the relationship between time and achievement: instead of fixing instructional time and accepting a distribution of outcomes, it fixes the outcome standard and lets time and support vary. This aligns with [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md) and [Adaptive Mastery Learning](../elements/adaptive-mastery-learning.md), which operationalize the same principle with technology-mediated pacing and assessment.

**Moderators and boundary conditions.** The claim is best understood as conditional rather than universal. Mastery approaches may increase students' time on instructional tasks (Kulik et al. 1990) and need more fine-grained, valid assessments than conventional pacing; where those resources are absent, the approach can degrade into repeated low-quality retesting [-M]. Benefits are typically strongest for structured, hierarchical domains (mathematics, science) where prerequisite knowledge is genuinely cumulative, and weaker for loosely structured domains where units are more independent [~M]. Group-based variants that require all learners to wait for the slowest student, or that offer only whole-class reteaching, tend to show smaller effects than individually paced variants [~M] — a pattern consistent with [Adaptive Learning Improves Outcomes](adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md).

**Assessment dependency.** Mastery learning is only as good as its mastery criteria. Poorly aligned or easily gamed assessments undermine the guarantee that "advanced" means "ready" [-M] — see [Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) for the formative-assessment practices that make mastery checks meaningful. The gating logic also interacts with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): unit boundaries should reflect genuine prerequisite structure, or learners advance carrying partial schemas that overload later units.

**Feedback and correction quality.** The corrective loop is the active ingredient, not the gate itself. Mastery variants that pair retesting with targeted, differentiated corrective instruction outperform those that simply recycle learners through the same material [~M]; without corrective feedback, repeated attempts mostly re-expose learners to failure. This connects to the broader evidence on [Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement](assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md).

**Open questions.** Two of the earlier questions are answered by Kulik et al. (1990): group-based programs produced larger effects than self-paced ones ([Mastery learning effects vary with the mastery standard, pacing, test type and the control group's feedback](mastery-learning-effects-vary-with-standard-pacing-and-test-type.md)), and the gains held at follow-up about eight weeks later ([Mastery learning gains persist at follow-up](mastery-learning-gains-persist-at-follow-up.md)). Still open in the wiki: evidence after 1990, effects on standardized tests specifically, and how the self-paced completion cost ([Self-paced mastery programs reduce college course completion](self-paced-mastery-programs-reduce-college-course-completion.md)) plays out in online and adaptive courses.
