Feedback Answers Three Questions¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · review r? · q2 · n=N/A (a synthesis, not a study of individual participants)

Effective feedback answers three questions for the learner: Where am I going? (the goal), How am I going? (current progress relative to that goal), and Where to next? (what actions will close the gap). Feedback that addresses only one of these — or that directs attention to the self rather than the task — is less likely to improve learning.

This is the paper's origin: Hattie and Timperley propose a model in which effective feedback answers three questions — "Where am I going?", "How am I doing?", and "Where to next?" — operating across four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self), and argue this reduces the discrepancy between current understanding and a learning goal. They synthesize prior empirical and theoretical work on feedback's effect on learning and achievement to build the model rather than reporting a new primary study or a pooled quantitative estimate specific to the three-question structure. Discussion¶

The three-question framing treats feedback as information about the gap between a current state and a target state, not as praise, blame, or a verdict. Each question implies a different design decision:

  • Where am I going? requires that goals and success criteria be visible to learners before performance, connecting feedback to assessment for learning and to clear structure. Feedback against unclear or unshared goals tends to be interpreted idiosyncratically.
  • How am I going? requires evidence of current performance relative to the goal — which is why feedback is tightly coupled with assessment and with check-ins that surface progress during, not after, learning.
  • Where to next? is the action-oriented component: feedback must suggest a next step the learner can take. This aligns with action-oriented feedback and supports self-regulated learning, since learners who know the next move can begin managing their own progress.

A key boundary condition in the feedback literature is the level at which feedback is directed. Feedback about the task and about the processes used to do the task is generally more useful than feedback directed at the person ("you're so smart"), which can steer attention toward self-protection rather than strategy adjustment. This interacts with learner expertise: novices typically need task-level feedback, while more advanced learners benefit more from process and self-regulation levels — a moderation consistent with the expertise reversal effect.

Constraints on the model follow from these boundary conditions. Feedback loses effectiveness when it directs attention to the self rather than the task, when goals are unshared or ambiguous so that "how am I going?" has no stable reference point, and when learners lack the prerequisite knowledge to act on "where to next?" guidance — in that case, next-step advice becomes noise rather than direction. Automated or rubric-driven feedback environments often answer "how am I going?" adequately but fail at "where to next?", producing evaluation without direction — see Checklist evaluation ineffective online for a related boundary case.

Open questions this page should eventually resolve with cited evidence: (1) the relative effect sizes of the three questions when manipulated independently; (2) whether "where to next?" feedback loses its advantage when learners lack the prerequisite knowledge to act on it; and (3) how the model plays out in digital environments, where automated feedback can answer "how am I going?" well but struggles with "where to next?"
