## Evidence

### Porter 2020

Porter, C. D., Brown, J., Smith, J. R., Simmons, A., Nieberding, M., Ayers, A., & Orban, C. (2020). A controlled study of virtual reality in first-year magnetostatics. Originally published 2019. [doi:10.1119/perc.2019.pr.porter](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2019.pr.porter)

`q2 · i?` · `causal · r2`

Repeated measures analysis in SPSS of the randomized three-treatment study found a significant treatment-by-sex interaction on both the entire item set and the superposition subscale; only p < 0.01 is printed, no effect size.

> "when both treatment type and student sex are added as between-subject factors, there is a statistically significant dependence on treatment, sex, and a statistically significant interaction between treatment and sex (with p < 0.01 in all cases)."

### Porter 2020 (2)

Porter, C. D., Brown, J., Smith, J. R., Simmons, A., Nieberding, M., Ayers, A., & Orban, C. (2020). A controlled study of virtual reality in first-year magnetostatics. Originally published 2019. [doi:10.1119/perc.2019.pr.porter](https://doi.org/10.1119/perc.2019.pr.porter)

`q2 · i1` · `causal · r2`

Bootstrapping (100,000 samples with replacement) of actual student scores estimated gender-gap-in-gains effect sizes by treatment (Table III: VR-WebGL d = 0.39, VR-Images d = 0.18, WebGL-Images d = 0.20); the authors stress these compare "very small gains".

> "It must be emphasized that these effect sizes are based on comparisons between very small gains. What is worth noting is that females are not disadvantaged by the VR treatment compared to other treatments."
