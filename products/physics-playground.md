## Description
Physics Playground is a 2D computer-based physics learning game developed by Shute and colleagues in which students draw simple machines to solve problems while gameplay logs support stealth assessment of physics understanding and persistence.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Physics Playground (PP): a 2D physics game with stealth assessment**: Physics Playground is a problem-based, 2-dimensional game in which each problem's goal is hitting a red balloon with a green ball by drawing simple machines (ramp, lever, springboard, pendulum). The version used had seven playgrounds with 74 problems arrayed easy to difficult, nonlinear free choice of problems, and gold trophies for efficient solutions and silver trophies for other solutions. The authors note that "Across various studies (e.g., Kim & Shute, 2015; Shute et al., 2015; [Shute & Ventura, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-3185-5_25); Wang, 2017), the game has been shown to support students' acquisition of physics concepts via gameplay." (Rahimi et al. (2021))
- **Physics Playground game with creativity stealth assessment**: Physics Playground is a computer-based game designed to assess and support conceptual understanding of physics principles. Players draw objects (ramps, levers, pendulums, springboards) that become active and interact to move a ball to a balloon, operationalizing Newton's laws. The game includes hundreds of levels solvable in multiple ways, encouraging creative solutions. A Bayesian-network-based stealth assessment estimates fluency, flexibility, and originality from gameplay indicators such as number of agents drawn, variety of agents attempted, and deviation of ball trajectory from expected solutions. (Shute et al. (2020))
- **Physics Playground immersive VR physics learning application**: Physics Playground is an educational, fully immersive VR application consisting of "a virtual laboratory where users can engage in several physics experiments". A robot guide named Emanon introduces each phenomenon through vocal dialogues with subtitles; users then manipulate physics variables on control panels and run experiments in four rooms covering gravity and friction, projectile motion, planetary orbits, and escape velocity, framed by a Mars-mission narrative. Blackboards present the underlying formulas, and most environment objects are grabbable to enhance realism and immersion. (Battipede et al. (2024))
