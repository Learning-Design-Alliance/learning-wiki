## Description
A computerized real-time classroom-observation instrument developed at UC Davis to code and quantify instructor–student interaction patterns for research and teaching-assistant or faculty professional development.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **RIOT: Real-time Instructor Observing Tool for coding instructor-student classroom interactions**: The Real-time Instructor Observing Tool (RIOT) is a computerized classroom-observation instrument developed at UC Davis to document instructor-student interactions in discussion/lab sections. It differentiates talking at students, dialoguing with students, observing students, and not interacting, each with subcategories; the authors describe it as 'a computer program that allows us to code during actual classroom observations, to time and quantify the instructor-student interactions.' It serves research and TA professional development. (Emily A. West et al. (2012))
- **Real-time Instructor Observation Tool (RIOT)**: The RIOT is a classroom observation tool, originally developed at UC Davis and later made publicly available by a team at San José State University, that "quantiﬁes and times these interactions, providing a more illustrative view of what actually takes place in a given TA's classroom." It categorizes instructor actions continuously in real time into four major groups — talking at students, shared instructor-student dialogue, observing students, and not interacting — each with subdivisions, logged with interaction type, start time, and duration. This study used it to code TA-student interactions, with inter-observer agreement of 88.5% and Cohen's kappa of 82.5%. (Eric Matthew Hickok (2015))
- **Real-time Instructor Observing Tool (RIOT)**: The RIOT is a computer-based tool, originally developed at UC Davis and later transformed into a free publicly-available web-based app at San José State University, for monitoring and classifying instructor-student interactions. It "quantifies and times instructor-student interactions", categorizing them continuously in real time into four major groups: talking at students, shared instructor-student dialogue, observing students, and not interacting, each with subdivisions and target (individual, small group, whole class). It automatically generates charts after each observation. The authors chose it because it accounts for small-group time and gives a complete continuous picture of TA time use, enabling direct comparison to CLASP observations. (Eric M. Hickok et al. (2025))
- **RIOT 2: an observational protocol for classrooms with multiple members of an instructional team**: The RIOT 2 is a tablet/computer-based observational tool adapted from the RIOT that allows observers to continuously record instructor or LA behaviors over time, producing timelines and time-allocation breakdowns. Its major change is adding "INST" columns to both sides of the interface so that "LA-instructor interactions" can be categorized, while keeping the original behavior rows unchanged. It is hosted on Google App Engine and available for public use. (Gina M. Quan et al. (2026))
- **Original RIOT: real-time continuous recording of single-instructor behaviors**: The Real-time Instructor Observational Tool (RIOT), initially developed at UC Davis, lets a trained observer continuously record what an instructor is doing over time, yielding "a quantifiable breakdown of time spent on different instructor behaviors." It includes 15 instructor behaviors in 4 categories and differs from protocols that capture only presence, not duration, of behaviors. (Gina M. Quan et al. (2026))
- **Real-time observational tools trade validation capability for efficiency and lighter consent burden**: The article argues that real-time observational tools like the RIOT offer practical advantages: "The RIOT is time-efficient to use; training an observer can happen in an hour or less," and data are classified in real time, faster than video analysis. Because only instructor behavior is stored, only instructor and LA consent is needed, which benefits observations of large classes. The tradeoff is the inability to recode the same class for validation. (Gina M. Quan et al. (2026))
