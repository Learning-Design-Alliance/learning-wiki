# Ten Cate's five supervision levels for entrustable professional activities

> **Product or Programme** · [All products and programmes](index.md)

## Description
A five-level entrustment framework used in health-professions education to rate the supervision required for a trainee's performance of an activity, from observation-only to supervising colleagues.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Ten Cate's five supervision levels for entrustable professional activities as an assessment framework for procedural skill training outcomes**: The study operationalizes skill performance and trust using Ten Cate's five supervision levels for entrustable professional activities, ranging from "Observation but not execution, even with direct supervision" through "execution with direct, proactive supervision", "execution with reactive supervision, i.e., on request and quickly available", and "supervision at a distance and/or post hoc" to "supervision provided by the trainee to colleagues". Fellows self-assess expected supervision levels and consultants assess required supervision levels before and after training, making entrustment the outcome measure of training effectiveness. (Zante Bjoern (2020))
- **Entrustable Professional Activity supervision levels as a lens for interpreting logbook autonomy as evidence of entrustment**: The article applies the entrustable professional activity framework, in which an EPA is "a unit of activity undertaken which the trainee could be trusted to complete," to surgical training. It maps ten Cate's five supervision levels onto the UK eLogbook supervision codes (Assisting; Supervised, Trainer Scrubbed; Supervised, Trainer Unscrubbed; Performed; Training a more junior trainee) and distinguishes ad hoc from summative entrustment decisions. On this account, a trainee routinely recorded as performing a procedure unsupervised has plausibly been entrusted with it, so longitudinal logbook supervision patterns across a whole cohort can evidence changing entrustment decisions alongside formal competency assessments. (Elsey EJ et al. (2018))
