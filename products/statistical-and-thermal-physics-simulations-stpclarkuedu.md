## Description
A collection maintained by Jan Tobochnik and colleagues of approximately forty modifiable open-source simulations for teaching statistical and thermal physics, accompanied by instructor-adaptable instructional materials and notes.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Approximately forty open-source simulations of statistical and thermal physics systems with modifiable instructional material**: The authors maintain a collection of simulations available from stp.clarku.edu: 'Approximately forty (open source) simulations of systems of interest in statistical and thermal physics are available from <stp.clarku.edu>.' The simulations come with related instructional material that can be easily modified by instructors, and the site also hosts notes by the authors on statistical and thermal physics incorporating simulations and algorithm discussions throughout the text. The simulations are also available as an EPAPS document. (Jan Tobochnik et al. (2007))
- **Java applet dice-throwing simulation programs for teaching statistical thermal physics**: A set of Java applet simulation programs that simulate throwing large numbers of dice with fixed total value to demonstrate the statistical foundation of thermal physics. The article states: "We report several simulation programs 1 which can be used to teach the statistical foundation of thermal physics in the introductory college physics courses." Dice represent atoms with equally spaced energy levels, and the total dice value is the system energy. The programs were written to supplement an introductory physics textbook and are runnable in a Java-enabled browser. (Koo-Chul Lee (1999))
- **Dice-throwing analogy as a method for teaching the statistical basis of thermodynamics**: The article's organizing framework models a physical system of N paramagnetic atoms with six equally spaced energy levels as N dice whose total value is the total energy. Only two principles are involved: energy conservation, put in by hand (a microcanonical ensemble), and the "principle of equal a priori probability" of accessible microscopic states, implemented via the microcanonical Monte Carlo technique. The article argues this makes statistical mechanics teachable to freshmen: "teaching statistical mechanics in the introductory college physics courses is not at all diﬃcult if we employ a proper method." The framework underlies all four simulation applets. (Koo-Chul Lee (1999))
