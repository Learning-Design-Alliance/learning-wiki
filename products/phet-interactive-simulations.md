## Description
Free, interactive science and mathematics simulation software developed and distributed by the PhET Interactive Simulations project at the University of Colorado Boulder.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **PhET suite of 18 quantum mechanics simulations organized into fundamental principles, historical experiments, and applications**: The PhET Project released a free, openly available suite of interactive, game-like quantum mechanics simulations: "We now have a suite of 18 simulations on various aspects of quantum phenomena." The simulations "fall into three broad categories": fundamental principles (e.g., Quantum Tunneling and Wave Packets, Quantum Bound States, Quantum Wave Interference), key ideas in historical experiments (e.g., Photoelectric Effect, Davisson Germer: Electron Diffraction, Models of the Hydrogen Atom), and applications (e.g., Lasers, Simplified MRI, Semiconductors), as listed in Table I. Each simulation has a web page with a description, learning goals, sample activities, and Tips for Teachers covering approximations, common difficulties, and classroom-use suggestions. (S. B. McKagan et al. (2008))
- **PhET simulation design framework: visualization, interactivity, context, and effective use of computation to build mental models**: The article organizes its simulation design around four key features. As stated in the conclusion: "The key features of PhET simulations - visualization, interactivity, context, and effective use of computations - are particularly effective for helping students understand the abstract and counterintuitive concepts of quantum mechanics." Visualization shows unobservable microscopic processes; interactivity directly couples students' actions to animations, which interviews show helps students "establish cause-and-eﬀect relationships"; real-world contexts ground abstract physics in everyday experience (e.g., MRI, discharge lamps); and fast hidden calculations let students "focus on the concepts rather than the math." The framework is grounded in research on how people learn, including reducing cognitive load by starting simulations in simple states. (S. B. McKagan et al. (2008))
- **Implicit scaffolding framework: guiding learners through affordances and constraints without explicit instruction**: Implicit scaffolding is the article's intellectual tool for designing learning environments that guide students without explicit instructions, built on a constructivist model of learning, human-computer interaction design principles, and over 10 years of PhET research and implementation. Like a well-designed door, sims "afford actions that are productive for learning, and constrain actions that might be confusing"; through affordances, constraints, cues, intelligible representations, and feedback, correct usage is "guaranteed by design." In short, implicit scaffolding "guides without students feeling guided," enabling students to ask their own questions and circumvent Meno's paradox. (Noah S. Podolefsky (2013))
- **Electric Field Hockey interactive simulation (PhET)**: The Electric Field Hockey is a PhET interactive simulation that "simulates individual electric charges and their interaction according to electrostatic theories (e.g., Coulomb's law) and Newtonian kinetics." Students drag positive (red) and negative (blue) source charges onto a simulated field to make a positive test-charge puck hit a goal, observing attraction, repulsion, and the puck's trajectory. It offers four gradient challenge levels (practice plus levels 1-3 with walls), optional field-line and trace representations, and adjustable puck mass. (Cao (2008))
- **PhET interactive simulations as assessment and learning environment for blended math-science sensemaking**: PhET simulations (Acceleration and Beer's Law) served as the interactive context for interviews probing MSS levels. They offer "a dynamic and interactive assessment environment that allows for accumulation of new evidence and feedback associated with changing parameters of the system in question." The article argues they lower cognitive load, minimize prior-knowledge requirements, and provide accessible representations supporting MSS engagement across diverse backgrounds. (Kaldaras et al. (2024))
- **PhET interactive simulations as representational supports for engaging marginalized learners in MSS**: PhET interactive computer simulations are used as the exploration environment in which students complete MSS tasks across framework levels. The article states that "By limiting cognitive load and providing intuitive visual representations with a minimum of text to interpret, the simulations are effective for students with a wide range of educational backgrounds." They let students manipulate parameters while focusing exploration on the most relevant variables, offering a simplified-but-not-too-simplified system for exploring mathematical aspects of a phenomenon without requiring prior knowledge. Prior work by the authors found PhET simulations foster authentic engagement in the dynamic MSS process and offer valuable supports for historically marginalized learners. (Kaldaras et al. (2024))
- **Projectile Data Lab (PDL): a noise-enhanced PhET simulation for measurement uncertainty**: Projectile Data Lab is a new PhET simulation featuring statistical noise and measurement tools in the context of projectile motion, designed so students explore "systems with uncertainty, the representation of variability, and the connection of physical apparatus with both the source and magnitude of variability". It has four screens — Variability, Sources, Measures, and Sampling — each layering on new capabilities to reduce initial cognitive load, with implicit scaffolding throughout. Since publication in May 2024 it has been run over 850,000 times and translated into 35 languages. (Qiaoyi Liu et al. (2025))
- **PhET interactive quantum mechanics simulations for the reformed course**: A suite of interactive computer simulations on quantum mechanics developed for this course as part of the PhET Project, incorporated into lecture, clicker questions, and homework with guided inquiry activities. The simulations "provide visual representations of abstract concepts and microscopic processes that cannot be directly observed" and help students build mental models. They reduce cognitive load by focusing attention on essential features, e.g., the Photoelectric Effect simulation illustrates the variable voltage supply as a battery with a slider. (S. B. McKagan et al. (2006))
