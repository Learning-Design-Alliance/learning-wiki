## Description
Free, interactive science and mathematics simulation software developed and distributed by the PhET Interactive Simulations project at the University of Colorado Boulder.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **PhET suite of 18 quantum mechanics simulations organized into fundamental principles, historical experiments, and applications**: The PhET Project released a free, openly available suite of interactive, game-like quantum mechanics simulations: "We now have a suite of 18 simulations on various aspects of quantum phenomena." The simulations "fall into three broad categories": fundamental principles (e.g., Quantum Tunneling and Wave Packets, Quantum Bound States, Quantum Wave Interference), key ideas in historical experiments (e.g., Photoelectric Effect, Davisson Germer: Electron Diffraction, Models of the Hydrogen Atom), and applications (e.g., Lasers, Simplified MRI, Semiconductors), as listed in Table I. Each simulation has a web page with a description, learning goals, sample activities, and Tips for Teachers covering approximations, common difficulties, and classroom-use suggestions. (S. B. McKagan et al. (2008))
- **PhET simulation design framework: visualization, interactivity, context, and effective use of computation to build mental models**: The article organizes its simulation design around four key features. As stated in the conclusion: "The key features of PhET simulations - visualization, interactivity, context, and effective use of computations - are particularly effective for helping students understand the abstract and counterintuitive concepts of quantum mechanics." Visualization shows unobservable microscopic processes; interactivity directly couples students' actions to animations, which interviews show helps students "establish cause-and-eﬀect relationships"; real-world contexts ground abstract physics in everyday experience (e.g., MRI, discharge lamps); and fast hidden calculations let students "focus on the concepts rather than the math." The framework is grounded in research on how people learn, including reducing cognitive load by starting simulations in simple states. (S. B. McKagan et al. (2008))
