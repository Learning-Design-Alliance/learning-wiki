## Description
A commercially published astronomy workbook by Prather and colleagues that provides Socratic-dialogue, collaborative classroom activities for introductory astronomy learners.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Lecture-Tutorials for Introductory Astronomy workbook**: Lecture-Tutorials for Introductory Astronomy (Prather et al. 2008, Second edition) is a commercially published workbook of collaborative, Socratic-dialogue classroom activities covering topics commonly taught in Astro 101 courses. The article describes how "the textbook provides a detailed exposition on these topics, while the Lecture-Tutorial uses a Socratic-dialogue approach of asking questions, and requires explanations to engage the student actively in grappling with the material". In the studied course the workbook was required throughout, used in class after mini-lectures, with unfinished activities completed as homework. (Rudolph (2011))
- **Lecture-Tutorials: sequenced Socratic pair activities completed in lecture after a short lecture**: The Lecture-Tutorials for Introductory Astronomy are "pencil and paper activities, designed to be completed by pairs of students in 10 to 25 minutes, working together in lecture hall settings after hearing a short lecture". They are driven by "carefully sequenced Socratic questions posed in ordinary language", of increasing difficulty, requiring students to examine scientific representations, graphs, and data sets, engaging them in critical discourse and evaluation of their developing ideas. The pattern operates within lecture courses as a collaborative, active-engagement insert rather than replacing lecture entirely. (An Investigation into the Correlations between Students’ Written Responses to Lecture-Tutorial Questions et al. (2014))
- **Light and Matter Lecture-Tutorial on molecular vibrations, rotations, and synchrotron radiation**: A three-part Lecture-Tutorial: Part I covers emission and absorption by molecular vibrations (infrared photons) and rotations (radio photons) using arcs and arrows; Part II covers synchrotron radiation, whose continuous non-thermal spectrum is not temperature-dependent; Part III applies these ideas to real telescopes (ALMA, Chandra, Spitzer, GALEX) and culminating error-correction tasks requiring students to "re-represent all incorrect information" across linked representations. (Wallace et al. (2015))
