# Team-Based Learning (TBL)

> **Product or Programme** · [All products and programmes](index.md)

## Description
A structured collaborative-learning framework supported by the Team-Based Learning Collaborative that organizes preparation, readiness assurance, feedback, team application activities, and peer evaluation.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **The standardized TBL process framework: a prescribed sequence of steps plus four key components**: The article describes Team-Based Learning as "a highly structured form of learning, involving the performance of a prescribed sequence of steps and the implementation of key components." The sequence runs pre-class preparation, readiness assurance (IRAT, TRAT, immediate feedback and clarification), problem-solving activities, and a closing summary. The framework's four key components are "carefully formed and managed teams, frequent and timely feedback, problem-solving activities, and student peer evaluation." Application activities follow the 4 S's: Significant problem, Same problem, Specific choice, Simultaneous reporting. The study implemented this full framework in a 6-week systems course and assessed its effect on student perception. (Andrews-Dickert Rebecca et al. (2024))
- **Team-based learning framework: individual and team readiness assurance followed by team application exercises**: The article describes team-based learning as a learner-centered strategy fostering active learning, collaboration, critical thinking, and problem solving, in which "students working in teams to apply their knowledge to solve complex, real-world problems". Its framework comprises three components: preparation against clear learning objectives, a readiness assurance process involving individual readiness assurance tests (iRATs) and team readiness assurance tests (tRATs) with immediate instructor clarification, and team application exercises (tAPPs). The study measures performance separately on each component. (Shoair OA (2024))
- **Structured TBL session format: advance preparation, iRAT, tRAT, instructor clarification, and team application**: The TBL session implemented in this study was an 85-minute in-class sequence conducted per the literature: teams of five to six members formed by the instructor, an individual readiness assurance test of ten single-best-answer MCQs (15 minutes), the same test as a team readiness assurance test (15 minutes), instructor clarification review (15 minutes), then a 40-minute team application. In team application, "Different scenarios, with all relevant details, were provided to all teams" under rules requiring identical scenarios, practice-relevant problems, specific choices, and simultaneous answer display with defense. Students most often named team application as TBL's most interesting element. (Imran M et al. (2022))
- **Team-based learning (TBL) four-stage instructional process**: Team-based learning is an active-learning method characterized as "a four-step process that facilitates student learning and participation before and during class": student preparation, readiness assurance, application, and peer assessment. Students allocated to teams prepare by reading or watching a short lecture; they then take an individual readiness assurance test (iRAT) and a team test (tRAT) using the immediate-feedback assessment technique (IFAT), followed by team work on significant cases with simultaneous specific answers. In the article, this process replaced four weeks of lectures in a physiotherapy course. (Jeno Lucas M. et al. (2017))
- **Modified two-phase team-based learning intervention with IRAT and GRAT using an audience response system**: The article implemented a modified TBL focusing on Phases 1 and 2 of the pure TBL method: students read preparatory material independently, then completed an Individual Readiness Assurance Test on clinical scenarios using an electronic audience response system, followed by a Group Readiness Assurance Test in randomly assigned teams of 4-5 students. Teams discussed for three minutes, "simultaneously display their consensus answer on a card", justified answers, and could appeal. The same design served as the active intervention against a self-reading passive learning control in neurological emergencies and neurological localization. (Tan Nigel CK et al. (2011))
