# Einstein-First program

> **Product or Programme** · [All products and programmes](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 17 claims (16 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (5 causal, 3 design, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 10 report an effect size · 16 claims rest on one study

## Description
A school physics curriculum programme developed by Kaur et al. that teaches Einsteinian physics through models, analogies, thought experiments, and small-group activities, supported by professional development for teachers.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Teaching Einsteinian physics through models, analogies and small-group classroom activities**: The Einstein-First program focuses on "developing simple, inexpensive activities that use analogies and models to teach the concepts of Einsteinian physics." All classes used small group activities working with models and analogies, and students were encouraged to participate in group discussions. The program introduces curved space first, then warped time through real and thought experiments with clocks, before introducing Newtonian and Euclidean approximations as useful tools. (Kaur et al. (2016))
- **Einstein-First 20-lesson programme structure: 15-minute introduction, 15-minute group activity, 15-minute discussion and worksheets, using physical models and analogies**: The Einstein-First programme is a 20-lesson curriculum that teaches curved space, gravity, duality of light, quantum interference, the uncertainty principle and related concepts through physical models and analogies rather than mathematics. "Twenty lessons were structured with carefully designed models and analogies to assist with students' understanding." Each lesson divides into three 15-minute phases: material presentation, group activity, and class discussion with worksheet completion. The authors contend it is possible to learn the modern paradigm at a young age using appropriate and carefully designed materials. (Tejinder Kaur et al. (2017))
- **Einstein-First showcase program combining presentations, role-play, model interferometers, light experiments and a space-time simulator**: The Einstein-First showcase program teaches the fundamentals of Einsteinian physics and gravitational wave detection through presentations and hands-on activities, so that participants "learn through role-playing, models and analogies, discussions, video presentations, lab tours and practical experiments". Its five core activities are a video/interactive presentation, a scripted role-play of Hertz's and Einstein's discoveries, a model interferometer built from a laser pointer and hanging mirrors, simple light-interference experiments, and a lycra-sheet space-time simulator. The Public Outreach Workshop added spatially linked signage and an external lab tour of the Gingin facility. (Foppoli et al. (2018))
- **One-day Einsteinian physics intervention program (role play, toy interferometer, three laser experiments)**: A one-day outreach program delivered at the Australian International Gravitational-wave Observatory site combining a science role play (Hertz, Einstein, Feynman), a toy model interferometer with nerf-gun photon demonstrations, meeting young researchers, and three low-cost laser experiments: a tabletop Michelson interferometer, soap film interference, and measuring hair thickness by diffraction. The program "engages students to learn quantum properties of light through a succession of activities and experiments in the context of gravitational waves", targeting two core and three derived concepts. (Rahul K. Choudhary et al. (2017))
- **A 6-10 lesson quantum physics program that reverses the traditional order by putting particle-like behaviour first**: The Einstein-First quantum component is a program of 6-10 lessons that begins with the particle nature of light rather than classical waves. The article states, "In this paper we present a program of 6 - 10 lessons that reverses the traditional order, putting particle-like behaviour first." It combines physical models, thought experiments, videos of water waves and single-photon interference, and hands-on laser experiments, with minimal mathematics, and treats the wave nature as an emergent statistical pattern of photon arrivals. (Kaur et al. (2017))
- **Einstein-First primary intervention combining hands-on lessons, a reading play and a Gravity Discovery Centre excursion**: The initial 2011 intervention for Year 6 students (aged 10-11) consisted of "six 45-minute lessons, a reading play, and an excursion to a Gravity Discov- ery Centre". Its three aspects encompass the Einstein-First aim of "a simple and explicit introduction to Einsteinian physics concepts th rough hands- on and interactive activities", covering space-time, curvature, the warping of time and photons. The pattern provides a conceptual basis students can build on throughout schooling. (Kyla Adams et al. (2021))
- **Einstein-First hypothesis that early introduction of Einsteinian physics prevents later contradictions and fosters positive attitudes**: The Einstein-First collaboration's organizing hypothesis is that "early introduction of Einst einian physics is ben- eﬁcial because it prevents later contradictions, increases stude nt motivation and fosters a positive attitude towards science". It holds that introducing a new Einsteinian paradigm equips students with language, concepts and knowledge relevant to the modern world, and aims to prevent conceptual conflicts by teaching modern concepts from early in a student's science education. (Kyla Adams et al. (2021))
- **Einstein First early-modern-physics teaching sequence with models, analogies and historical context**: A lesson-sequence pattern in which language and concepts of modern physics are introduced before or alongside classical physics, using hands-on models and analogies rather than mathematics. The article reports that "in primary school it is impos sible teaching modern physics using maths formulas: hence, the whole intervention was developed through the use of lan guage" plus models and analogies, and a focus on the historical context of Galileo and Einstein. The 15-hour sequence moved from a spacetime simulator to curved-surface activities with balloons, aiming to provoke conflict between Euclidean intuitions and the modern view. (Matteo Luca Ruggiero et al. (2021))
- **Nine-topic Einstein-First program using role-plays, hands-on activities, and models/analogies for Year 3**: The Einstein-First program delivered nine 60-minute lessons to Year 3 students, each including a 20-minute PowerPoint introduction, a 20-minute small-group activity, and a final 20-minute worksheet/discussion segment. Topics covered space, time, light, and gravity through activities such as a spacetime simulator with rolling marbles, Nerf gun photon photography, soap film interference, and a 'History of Light' role play. The program was followed by a full-day excursion to the Gravity Discovery Centre for Class 1, featuring a 'How to make Gold' role play based on the 2017 neutron star merger observation. (Tejinder Kaur et al. (2021))
- **Paradigm conflict between Newtonian and Einsteinian physics as rationale for early Einsteinian instruction**: The article argues that Newtonian physics is defined by Euclidean geometry, in which space is an absolute grid and time absolute and unchangeable, whereas in Einsteinian physics space is deformed by matter, time changes with proximity to masses and relative speed, and gravity on Earth is caused predominantly by the stretching of time. Because the paradigms directly contradict each other, the authors argue that early learning of Newtonian physics presents a significant obstacle to later learning of the modern paradigm, and the Einstein-First curriculum is designed to prevent cognitive confusion by introducing Einsteinian concepts from an early age. (Tejinder Kaur et al. (2021))
- **Spiral learning progression introducing Einsteinian concepts from Years 3 to 10**: The Einstein-First curriculum is organised as a spiral in which core concepts such as photons and phonons are introduced in Years 3–6 through toys and Einsteinian language, then revisited at increasing sophistication in Years 7–10. The article states: "The spiral learning approach allows for introduction, development and reinforcement of Einsteinian concepts. By revisiting and building on concepts at different grade levels, students are given multiple opportunities to understand and internalise them." Figures 2 and 3 map the progression from core to derived concepts, including associated mathematics such as vectors, probability and powers of ten. (Kaur et al. (2023))
- **Maths for Einstein's Universe: a five-module activity-based mathematics program for ages 7-12 linked to Einsteinian physics**: MEU is an out-of-school and in-school program developed by the Einstein-First project that shifts attention from traditional arithmetic to logarithmic thinking, estimation, vectors, probability and curved-space geometry for students aged 7-12. "The program entitledMaths for Einstein 's Universe includes five modules covering extreme numbers, estimation, probability, vectors and curved space geometry taught through group activities, games and plays." The modules are sequenced so each builds on pre-knowledge from earlier modules and complement the Einstein-First science learning progression from Year 3 to Year 10. Each module can be delivered at higher or lower complexity by age group. (Anastasia Popkova et al. (2023))
- **MEU teacher training via micro-credential courses and workshops reaching 63 teachers**: Because initial trials revealed teachers needed additional support, the program created micro-credential courses and specialised workshops. Table 4 records five courses totalling 46.5 hours for 63 participants, including pre-service teachers and primary and secondary teachers. "We also collected data on teacher's responses to our teacher training courses that consisted of micro-credential courses (micro-credentials, 2023) and training workshops." Teachers initially lacked confidence but, after gaining experience, expressed a desire to continue implementing the program independently. (Anastasia Popkova et al. (2023))
