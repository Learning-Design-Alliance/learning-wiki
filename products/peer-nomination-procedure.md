# Peer nomination procedure

> **Product or Programme** · [All products and programmes](index.md)

## Description
A peer-report sociometric assessment developed by Moreno and adapted by Coie et al. in which children nominate liked and disliked peers to derive acceptance, rejection, social impact, social preference, and sociometric-status groups.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Peer nomination procedure for assessing children's sociometric status**: The peer nomination procedure, developed by Moreno and adapted by Coie et al. (1982), asks children to nominate up to three peers they like and three they dislike. Nominations are used to compute acceptance, rejection, social impact, and social preference, which in turn create the sociometric groups (popular, rejected, neglected, controversial, average). The review reports it is "a reliable and valid method for measuring children 's social position", used in 16 of the 25 reviewed studies, and recommends it remain part of future studies. (Femke van der Wilt et al. (2019))
- **Peer nomination (sociometric) methodology for assessing classroom citizenship behaviors and relationships**: Peer nomination methods ask children to nominate classmates on behavioral and affective items, so that "children act as multiple observers of classmates' typical behaviors and of their relationships with others." Behavioral items describe interpersonal behavior (e.g., who helps others) and affective items capture relationships (e.g., whom classmates like most). The review proposes this approach as a systematic, quantitative complement to self-report measures in citizenship research, mapping classroom social dynamics into a social network. (Krijnen et al. (2024))
- **Peer-nomination classroom survey protocol for measuring perceived classmate ability**: A survey instrument, informed by Grunspan et al.'s method, in which each participant receives a roster of classmates identified "by first name and included a last initial when there was more than one person with the same first name" and checks names in response to four questions: who they study with, whom they seek help from, who stands out as knowledgeable, and who are the best students (ranked 1–5). The proportion of women selected in each category, out of each participant's total selections, served as the outcome measure. Classmates listed as "other" were gender-coded from pronouns in the participant's description. (Bloodhart et al. (2020))
