# User experience design tools for courseware: persona creation kit, journey maps, and ecosystem maps

## Description
WestEd's freely available suite of participant-data-informed tools helps postsecondary gateway-math courseware designers examine learner and faculty needs, experiences, and ecosystems.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **User experience design tools for courseware: persona creation kit, journey maps, and ecosystem maps**: The WestEd team developed freely available user experience design tools grounded in participant data for courseware designers and developers. These include "a modular, deck-based user persona creation kit" with eight student and faculty archetypes plus capacity, challenge, and design thought cards; eight journey maps visualizing a semester-long student experience with friction points and courseware opportunities; and two ecosystem maps illustrating the people, processes, features, and resources that positively or negatively impact success in math courses. (Higgin et al. (2025))
- **Personas: fictional named user archetypes synthesized from qualitative interview data**: Personas are a design method, rooted in ethnography and human-computer interaction, in which rich qualitative data about users are synthesized into archetypes. The article defines them as "fictional, detailed archetypical characters that represent grouping of behaviors, goals and motivations observed and identified during the research phase". They are usually named and given pictures so design teams can reason about them as people, and are revisited throughout a project to keep the product user-centered. (Adrian Madsen et al. (2012))
- **Persona methodology: life-like user archetypes synthesized from real users' goals and motivations**: Persona methodology treats a design as meaningful only when people use it to achieve specific goals: personas are 'life-like models, whose characteristics are driven by the various goals and motivations of real or potential users.' They are 'synthesized patterns of rich behaviors and motivations from an amalgamation of users,' not descriptions of individuals, and they evoke designers' empathy to promote user-centered design. The authors repurpose the method to represent undergraduate researchers for research-program design. (Tra Huynh et al. (2019))
