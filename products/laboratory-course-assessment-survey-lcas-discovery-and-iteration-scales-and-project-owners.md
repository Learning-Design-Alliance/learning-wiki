# Laboratory Course Assessment Survey (LCAS) discovery and iteration scales and Project Ownership Survey (POS) cognitive ownership scale

## Description
A student survey battery combining LCAS discovery and iteration scales with the POS cognitive ownership scale to measure how learners experience course design features, used here with undergraduate life-science laboratory students.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Student survey measures of CURE design features (LCAS discovery and iteration; POS cognitive ownership)**: A student survey battery used to independently confirm that courses described by instructors as CUREs were experienced as more CURE-like than non-CUREs. The authors "collected data using two scales from the Laboratory Course Assessment Survey (LCAS; Corwin, Runyon et al., 2015) and one scale from the Project Ownership Survey (POS; Hanauer and Dolan, 2014)": a 5-item discovery scale, a 6-item iteration scale, and an 8-item cognitive ownership scale, administered via Qualtrics with attention checks to a final analytic sample of 462 students from the 48 courses. (Zajic et al (2026))
- **Laboratory Course Assessment Survey (LCAS), a 17-item instrument for biology lab course design**: The LCAS is a 17-item Likert-type survey measuring students' perceptions of three biology lab course design features: collaboration, discovery and relevance, and iteration. Developed through cognitive interviews, item refinement from an initial 55-item pool, and iterative exploratory factor analysis with 213 undergraduates in 16 courses at 11 US institutions, it achieved Cronbach's alpha above 0.8 on all three scales and factor loadings consistently above 0.4. The authors report it "is suited for characterizing and comparing undergraduate biology lab courses." (Corwin Lisa A. et al. (2015))
- **Three-dimension framework of research lab course design: collaboration, discovery and relevance, and iteration**: The article proposes that undergraduate biology lab courses can be characterized by student-perceived design features drawn from Corwin Auchincloss et al.'s CURE dimensions. Exploratory factor analysis of a 17-item instrument yielded three scales: collaboration (six items on working together and metacognitive reflection), discovery and relevance (five items combining opportunities to generate new knowledge with relevance to communities beyond the classroom), and iteration (six items on time to revise, repeat, or extend work). The authors state the discovery and relevance scale "places strong emphasis on this by specifying that discovery must be new to students, instructors, and communities outside the course." (Corwin Lisa A. et al. (2015))
