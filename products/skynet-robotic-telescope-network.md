## Description
A UNC-Chapel Hill-operated robotic telescope network and web portal that enables students and professional astronomers to control telescopes and collect astronomical data remotely.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Skynet Robotic Telescope Network used for introductory astronomy labs**: Skynet is UNC-CH's robotic telescope network, the centerpiece of the reformed introductory astronomy laboratory course ASTR 101L. Six of the eight new labs utilize it; students use Skynet to collect their own data, for example "to measure the distance to an asteroid using parallax measured simultaneously by Skynet telescopes in different hemispheres". Since its inception in 2004 it has grown to one of the largest robotic telescope networks in the world, with nearly 30 optical telescopes ranging from 14 to 40 inches, a 20-meter radio telescope, all controlled through a web-based portal used by professional astronomers and students alike; approximately 50,000 students have used it to date. (Adam S. Trotter et al. (2018))
- **Skynet robotic telescope network and web interfaces as shared research-grade observing infrastructure for students**: Skynet is "sophisticated telescope control and queue scheduling software" controlling a globally distributed network of robotic telescopes that students use "right alongside the professionals". Participating institutions contribute 10% of each telescope's time in lieu of fees, and students submit requests through a web interface at skynet.unc.edu, with the backend acquiring and returning images through the same interface. The network has grown to about 20 optical telescopes (14–40 inches) across four continents plus a 20m radio telescope, and its data are published in peer-reviewed journals roughly every 20 days. (Daniel E. Reichart (2021))
- **Global network of intermediate-sized radio telescopes integrated into Skynet for student access**: To support MWU!, the project is integrating a global network of radio telescopes into Skynet: Arecibo Observatory's 12m in Puerto Rico, 4.5m, 12m, and two contingent 26m telescopes at PARI in North Carolina, and 14m, 26m, and 30m telescopes at two Australian locations managed by the University of Tasmania. The article states "This is the first time that multiple, diverse radio telescopes will be broadly, and easily, accessible through a single interface", likely establishing a standard other radio telescopes will adopt so their unused observing time can be used by students. This requires some generalization of Skynet's backend software. (Daniel E. Reichart (2021))
