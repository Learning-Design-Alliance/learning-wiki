## Description
A reformed calculus-based introductory physics curriculum developed for teaching fundamental principles, microscopic models, coherence across physics domains, and computer modeling.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Matter & Interactions (M&I) curriculum for introductory electromagnetism**: The Matter & Interactions curriculum is a reformed calculus-based introductory physics curriculum that, per the article, "diﬀers from the traditional calculus-based curriculum in its emphasis on fundamental physical principles, microscopic models of matter, coherence in linking diﬀerent domains of physics, and computer modeling." For E&M it reorganizes the traditional topic sequence by increasing early emphasis on the vector field concept and on the effects of fields on matter at the microscopic level, introducing magnetic fields earlier and treating flux and Gauss's law later than traditional courses. The article evaluates it against traditional curricula using the BEMA across four universities. (Kohlmyer et al. (2009))
- **The Matter and Interactions curriculum organizes mechanics knowledge around three fundamental conservation-law principles as central knowledge**: The M&I curriculum, developed by Chabay and Sherwood, aims to improve student understanding by "organizing mechanics knowledge around three fundamental principles (conservation laws)". This design reflects using a small amount of central knowledge as the starting point for expansion by systematically specifying associations with more detailed subordinate knowledge, giving mechanics an explicit global hierarchical structure that should support more ready and flexible retrieval than a many-formulas approach. (Keith R Bujak et al. (2008))
- **Matter & Interactions I: Modern Mechanics — a textbook that treats air resistance correctly**: The article identifies one recommended exception among introductory texts: "One book treats air resistance correctly: Matter & Interactions I: Modern Mechanics" by Chabay and Sherwood (John Wiley, 2002). In its problem 5.3, students numerically integrate a baseball trajectory with and without air resistance, compare high-altitude (Denver) and sea-level effects, and see that the effect is about a factor of 2. The authors highly recommend it and its companion electricity-and-magnetism volume to teachers. (Sanjoy Mahajan et al. (2006))
