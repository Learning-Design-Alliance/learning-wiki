## Description
NANOGrav's five-level education and public-outreach framework maps audiences and methods to escalating goals from inspiring the public to training researchers for participation in gravitational-wave astronomy.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **NANOGrav EPO pyramid: a five-level framework (inspire, inform, engage, educate, train) linking audiences and methods to escalating engagement goals**: The EPO pyramid is NANOGrav's organizing framework for education and public outreach, described as demonstrating "the importance of initiating broad and inclusive outreach efforts if one wants to ultimately build a well-trained and diverse scientiﬁc community." It has three dimensions: audiences ranging from the general public and media through K-8, high-school and undergraduate students, grad students and post-docs, to peers; methods such as social media, exhibits, SPOT presentations, pulsar searching, and special sessions; and a vertical progression of goals from inspiring a broad range of people to training students and junior researchers for direct involvement in NANOGrav. Pyramid level sizes reflect intended audience sizes, and several initiatives are designed to bridge external and internal audiences at the Engage level. (NANOGrav Education and Outreach: Growing a Diverse and Inclusive Collaboration for Low-Fre)
- **The SPOT near-peer role-model outreach model**: SPOT is a train-the-trainer outreach model in which graduate student managers recruit and train paid undergraduate presenters who deliver interactive, customized space-science presentations in K-12 classrooms and community programs. The model rests on near-peer mentoring: "SPOT takes advantage of both the excitement of space and the natural way college students serve as role models for children." Presenters introduce themselves and their majors, answer questions about higher education, and highlight local research, so young students can envision themselves as future scientists. The article calls the result "a win-win program" because presenters simultaneously gain science-communication and leadership experience. (Des Jardins et al. (2020))
