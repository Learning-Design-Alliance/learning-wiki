- **AI-Enabled EdTech Adoption Cycle (vision and planning, implementation, ongoing evaluation, scaling and sustaining)**: Appendix 1 of the brief outlines a four-phase adoption cycle for AI-enabled edtech: vision and planning, implementation, ongoing evaluation, and scaling and sustaining, each with key challenges and recommendations. It states that "Planning for AI-enabled edtech requires districts to move beyond reactive purchasing decisions and establish strategic frameworks that align technology with clear learning objectives and pedagogical goals." AI-enabled tools require specialized evaluation criteria including algorithmic transparency, bias mitigation, and data privacy. (Ruiz et al. (2025))
- **Four-phase AI-enabled edtech adoption cycle for districts**: The article organizes district adoption of AI-enabled edtech into a cycle of four phases: vision and planning, implementation, ongoing evaluation, and scaling and sustaining. Each phase pairs a goal statement with key challenges and recommendations, e.g. planning requires districts to "move beyond reactive purchasing decisions and establish strategic frameworks that align technology with clear learning objectives and pedagogical goals." The cycle frames adoption as continuous rather than a one-time purchase. (Ruiz et al. (2025))
- **Six-step edtech procurement process from needs analysis to sustain-and-scale**: The guide structures district edtech procurement as six steps: Analyze & Inventory, Discover & Decide, Design & Plan, Mobilize & Implement, Evaluate & Reflect, and Sustain & Scale. Each step frames key questions and points to practical resources and templates. The guide says the steps "represent a comprehensive process that districts and schools can engage with to discover, pilot, and procure edtech tools to meet a given need," grounding the articulated need in data before exploring solutions and returning to Discover & Decide if a pilot shows a tool is not the right fit. (Digital Promise (2026))

It describes four models: a Large-Scale Pilot Study getting more than 350 students to use the tool and measuring outcomes, useful for detecting differences in effects for special populations; a Small-Scale Pilot Study with a smaller group when conditions must be carefully controlled; Live Testing, where students practice using the tool while usage and feedback are observed; and Demonstration and Feedback for teachers when piloting with students is not allowed. Evidence-collection methods include embedded assessments, baseline/endline tests, propensity score matching with a control (Digital Promise (2026))

# EdTech Pilot Navigator with four pilot models (Resource E)

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Resource E is a quick-reference guide and planning template for choosing a pilot type before formal procurement. It describes four models: a Large-Scale Pilot Study getting more than 350 students to use the tool and measuring outcomes, useful for detecting differences in effects for special populations; a Small-Scale Pilot Study with a smaller group when conditions must be carefully controlled; Live Testing, where students practice using the tool while usage and feedback are observed; and Demonstration and Feedback for teachers when piloting with students is not allowed. Evidence-collection methods include embedded assessments, baseline/endline tests, propensity score matching with a control group, surveys, observation, and usage data.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Pilots build on what districts already know from external evidence and EdTech Index validations
#### Constraints
- Demonstration and Feedback applies when piloting with students is not allowed under district policies or when teacher buy-in must be secured first

### Target Learners
- K-12 students and teachers participating in district edtech pilots

### Target Learning Goals
- Testing whether a tool is usable in local classrooms and whether research-based impact claims translate into practice
