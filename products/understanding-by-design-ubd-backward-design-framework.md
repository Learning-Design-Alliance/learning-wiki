# Understanding by Design (UbD) backward design framework

## Description
A curriculum-design framework developed by Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe that guides educators to plan from desired results through evidence and learning experiences.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **Understanding by Design (UbD) and its backward design approach**: Understanding by Design is a unit-design framework in which teachers act as designers who plan for meaningful, transferable learning rather than content coverage. It is dominated by the backward design approach, in which teachers determine which evidences they want to reach before planning what and how to teach. The framework consists of three stages: desired results, evidence, and learning plan, using GRASPS performance-task criteria and WHERETO principles. In this study, teachers integrated UbD into EFL Reading and Listening & Speaking courses to target student motivation and knowledge transfer. (Yurtseven et al. (2015))
- **Backward design / assessment-driven design of curricula**: Backward design is a curriculum-development framework in which learning objectives are identified first and the curriculum is engineered end-to-beginning to achieve them; the article describes developing the final summative assessment at the start as an implementation of it. Under ISEE the approach evolved into "assessment-driven design", focusing on the culminating assessment task and its rubric. The article operationalizes it as a five-step cycle: draft LOs, build a LO-assessing conceptual multiple-choice pre-/post-quiz, design the curriculum backwards, implement with formative and summative use of the quiz questions, then reflect and revise. It is presented as iterative, with LOs revised as the curriculum emerges. (Cooksey et al. (2022))
- **Understanding by Design three-tier importance filter for learning outcomes**: The article applies Wiggins and McTighe's Understanding by Design filter, ranking learning outcomes as 1) "worth being familiar with", 2) "important to know and do", or 3) "enduring understanding", with importance rising by tier. It lets teachers emphasize an objective that ranks low on Bloom's taxonomy yet is an enduring concept in its field, which the authors use to classify cybersecurity outcomes when asking how AI tools change curricula. (Peter Jamieson et al. (2023))
