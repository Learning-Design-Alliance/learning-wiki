# Modeling instruction

## Description
A research-based, guided-inquiry science-teaching pedagogy in which students collaboratively develop, test, defend, and deploy models, supported by teacher facilitation and professional training.

## Components
<!-- A programme's own frameworks, tools, indicators and instruments, as its sources describe them -->
- **The Modeling Instruction modeling cycle: constructing and testing models through multiple representations**: Modeling Instruction employs "a coherent framework of scientifically testing the limits of physical models (i.e., 'the modeling cycle')" by engaging students in constructing and comparing different representations of physical phenomena. Each cycle is built on modules that promote scientific thinking through observation, experimentation, and discourse; students construct a working model able to describe observed phenomena and predict similar but not-yet-observed ones. The article builds its computational integration on this curriculum, which it reports is used in approximately 10% of U.S. high school physics classrooms. (Caballero et al. (2012))
- **Modeling Instruction organizes student activities around a modeling cycle in which students observe, develop models, and evaluate them against further observations**: Modeling Instruction (Hestenes 1987) employs "a theoretical framework (the modeling cycle) around which student activities are organized." Students "engage in open-ended experimental and theoretical procedures while making real-world observations," propose measurements to describe patterns, develop representative models, then "test their models against additional observations to evaluate their model's capacity to explain the observed phenomena." The observation-development-evaluation cycle is repeated for new phenomena, and students "utilize several scientific practices during a single cycle: developing and using models, finding patterns, testing hypotheses." (Hoskinson et al. (2013))
- **The Modeling Instruction modeling cycle: Model Development and Model Deployment stages**: The Modeling Instruction (MI) curriculum organizes high school physics around "scientific models" taught through a two-stage "modeling cycle." Students begin in the Model Development stage with a pre-lab demonstration, a "paradigm experiment" in small groups, and a post-lab discussion of each group's successes and failings. Model Deployment then uses worksheets, quizzes, and a lab practicum in which students apply the model to a real-world problem such as predicting where two constant-velocity carts collide. At the end of each cycle, a new phenomenon that the current model cannot explain motivates the next model, mirroring professional model revision. (Aiken (2013))
- **Modeling Instruction classroom structured as an interactive learning community producing dense, reciprocal networks**: Modeling Instruction organizes instruction around "a handful of scientiﬁc models that represent the core content of physics" in an interactive learning community, replacing lectures with structured peer interaction. Its course structure — group work, common exams and lab reports, and board meetings — yields classroom networks with relatively high reciprocity and transitivity (MTr = 0.38, Mρ↔ = 0.49) and density that increases over the semester. (Zwolak et al. (2016))
- **Modeling Instruction collaborative classroom structure: small groups, board meetings, and flexible seating that build a community of learners**: Modeling Instruction organizes the introductory physics classroom so that students work in small groups of three on experiments and problem solving, come together in instructor-moderated board meetings of about 18 to 21 students to present solutions and reach consensus from experimental evidence, and sit at round tables that are re-assigned every 3-4 weeks. The article states that everything in MI, "From the instructional design (“learning by doing”) to the ﬂow of the activities (small group work, multigroup discussions, a whole class wrap up) to the ﬂexible classroom space," is designed to promote development of a community of learners. Cross-group interaction is explicitly encouraged during board meetings and occurs regularly in unstructured form, which produced the network development the study observed. (Williams et al. (2021))
- **Modeling theory of instruction: physicists reason from models, and instruction should build model-centered knowledge bases**: Modeling theory holds that expert physicists reason from mental constructs called models, built through representational tools such as graphs, diagrams, and formulae, and coordinated into general models applying to broad classes of situations. The article states that "The modeling theory of instruction is centered on the idea that physicists reason from mental constructs known as models." It extends this to instruction, designing university physics so students develop model-centered knowledge bases resembling those of practicing scientists, with declarative, procedural, and experiential knowledge attached to each model. (Eric Brewe (2006))
