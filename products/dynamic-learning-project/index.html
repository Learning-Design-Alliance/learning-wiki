Dynamic Learning Project¶

Product or Programme · All products and programmes Evidence · 16 claims (16 for) · 4 studies (2 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), q2 · 0 of 4 report an effect size · 16 claims rest on one study

Description¶

A school-based instructional technology coaching programme run by Digital Promise that places full-time coaches in underserved schools to support teachers through iterative coaching cycles, modeling, co-teaching, and reflection.

Components¶

  • Dynamic Learning Project (DLP) coaching model: The DLP is a school-based instructional technology coaching program that placed one site-based, full-time coach in each of 50 underserved schools for one year. Coaches ran "four eight-week coaching cycles" in which teachers selected, tackled, and reflected on classroom challenges, supported by classroom modeling, co-teaching, and observations. Coaches and principals received mentoring, a Summer and Winter Institute, and membership in a Professional Learning Network. In the pilot year more than 1,110 of 2,250 teachers participated. (Bakhshaei et al. (2018))
  • Five cornerstones of an effective classroom technology coaching program: The report proposes that five aspects of the DLP account for its success: partnership, active learning, personalization, sustained support, and content flexibility. Partnership means a voluntary, non-evaluative, school-based collaboration among administrators, coaches, and teachers in which "teachers and coaches co-constructed lessons." Active learning structures coaching around learning by doing on each teacher's own classroom challenge; personalization fits support to teacher, school, and district context; sustained support spans weeks and months; and content flexibility lets coaches support subjects outside their own expertise by treating the teacher as the content expert. (Bakhshaei et al. (2019))

Claims¶

  • Coaches reported significantly greater confidence in their coaching skills by year end, rising from 62 to 89 percent rating themselves high or extremely high [+W]
  • DLP teachers reported significant increases in using technology for both content and pedagogy, but no significant difference from non-DLP teachers in basic technology skills [+W]
  • Teachers coached for more than one cycle reported more powerful technology use and greater confidence, though a breadth-versus-depth tradeoff remains open [+W]
  • Principals' confidence in leadership skills related to instructional coaching rose from 60 to 95 percent over the pilot year [+W]
  • Teachers reported increasing encouragement of risk-taking while coaches were less confident teachers actually took risks [+W]
  • After one year of DLP coaching, coached teachers reported more frequent and more powerful technology use than non-participating colleagues [+W]
  • DLP participants reported more frequent meaningful coaching activities than other coaching programs, but fewer opportunities to observe other teachers [+W]
  • Dynamic Learning Project research found teachers who worked with edtech coaches used technology more frequently and felt more confident than peers who did not [+W]
  • DLP-coached teachers reported greater increases in frequency of technology use than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • DLP participants reported more frequent meaningful coaching activities than other coaching programs, but fewer opportunities to observe other teachers [+M]
  • Coaches and principals in the DLP grew in confidence and skills over the year [+W]
  • More DLP teachers reported strong ability to select and use technology for pedagogy and for their content area than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • DLP teachers reported higher rates of impactful student technology use and greater perceived positive impact on student engagement and learning than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • DLP teachers reported more confidence than non-DLP teachers in using technology to develop students' six 21st-century skill categories [+W]
  • Effective coaches require content expertise, classroom experience, technology proficiency, flexible scheduling, partnership orientation, and interpersonal skills [+W]
  • Four relational components—positive feedback, rapport, mutual respect, and reciprocal trust—form the relational framework of coaching [+W]
  • Teachers and coaches in the qualitative study perceived that coaching increased teacher knowledge and practice, with sustainable practices and positive student learning outcomes [+W]
