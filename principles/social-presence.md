# Social Presence

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), `q3` · 1 of 2 report an effect size

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **being perceived as a real person by the others in a course**, and how a design makes that possible where it does not happen by itself. For learners in a group course that runs through a medium, usually `adult` and `postsecondary`, often `online-instructor-led` or blended, over `days-weeks` or a `term-plus`, whose part in shared discussion or group work is thin or guarded (they read but rarely post, agree but never question, leave group work to others, or drift away), designed chances to be seen and heard as a person are expected to raise their willingness to take part openly, including disagreeing and showing unfinished work, and through that to support `persistence`, satisfaction and the quality of the group's discussion. The design moves are introductions tied to the course, an instructor who is visible and answers by name, small stable groups, structured replies to named peers, norms that allow warmth and disagreement, and some synchronous contact. The expected change in each learner's understanding (`conceptual-understanding`, `general-achievement`) is indirect: it runs through what the discussion and group work then do.

The relationship is conditional on the medium (in a `classroom` the cues come for free and the design matters less), on there being a shared task that needs open discussion, on the time the group stays together, and on the learners' own norms about self-disclosure. **No claim in the wiki tests it.** Nothing here compares learners in courses designed for more and less social presence on participation, persistence or learning. What the wiki holds is survey associations from two small online graduate programmes (social presence rated high, and the presence most associated with self-reported self- and co-regulation), correlational syntheses linking warm teacher–student relationships with engagement, a randomised belonging intervention that worked only where the setting gave students real chances to belong, teachers' and designers' reports that online community takes deliberate effort, and a review reporting that an instructor's face in lecture videos raised perceived learning but not measured performance. The old page also cited a meta-analysis of online courses (Richardson et al., 2017) reporting correlations of about .54 with satisfaction and .45 with perceived learning; the wiki has no claim page for it, and both outcomes are self-reports. So the design below is a labelled proposal.

How the converted siblings sit around this: [Community of Inquiry](community-of-inquiry.md) holds the model of a discussion community in which social, cognitive and teaching presence together move inquiry towards a resolution; social presence is one of its three presences, and this page treats it on its own, inside that model, as the condition for open communication and trust. It does not repeat CoI's model of facilitation or of cognitive presence. [Instructor Accessibility](instructor-accessibility.md) owns the route from a stuck learner to the instructor (channels, reply windows, outreach); this page uses the instructor's visibility only for what it does to the group's sense that real people are present. The [Online Course Design](../patterns/online-course-design.md) pattern places presence-building in a whole course sequence. [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) and [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) own when joint work and discussion change what each learner can do.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (introductions and profiles, instructor visibility and responsiveness, structured interaction, norms that allow affective expression, and its cautions about forced disclosure, sociability without purpose and discussion-board mandates), kept as a concrete default for an online or blended course of 6–15 weeks. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Open with introductions tied to the course, in the first week.** Ask each learner for a short post (about 100–150 words, or a 60–90-second audio or video clip if they prefer) answering course-relevant prompts: what brings you here, one experience you have had with the topic, one thing you hope to be able to do. Ask each to reply to two classmates by name, saying what they have in common or would like to ask. The learner chooses what to disclose; a photo, a face on video and personal details are optional, and a professional introduction is always enough. Untested; from the earlier page, whose constraints warn that forced disclosure can reduce trust. Recipes: [Introducing yourself via video](../strategies/introducing_yourself_via_video.md), [Icebreakers and introductions](../strategies/icebreakers-and-introductions.md).
2. **Make the instructor visible as a person from day one.** A personal welcome message to each learner or the group before the course starts; then a weekly announcement of 2–3 minutes (video, audio or text) that names specific posts and contributions from the past week and says what comes next; replies in the forum signed by name within a stated window. Untested as a design. E-teachers in one interview study reported that online community needed deliberate, planned effort that physical co-presence supplies ([Creating community and interacting with students online required deliberate, planned effort unlike spontaneous face-to-face co-presence](../claims/online-community-requires-conscious-effort.md) [+W]). Spend the effort on replies and contact rather than on the instructor's face in every content video: a review reports that a talking head raised learners' sense of learning but not measured performance ([Human embodiment in video](../claims/human-embodiment-video-presence-effects.md) [~M]). How fast to reply is on [Instructor Accessibility](instructor-accessibility.md). Recipe: [Welcome messages](../strategies/welcome-messages.md).
3. **Put learners in small stable groups early.** Groups of 4–6 that stay together for at least the first three or four weeks, with a group space, before any reshuffle. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's call for small-group work and its constraint that familiarity takes sustained contact.
4. **Structure replies, not only posts.** Each week ask for one initial post and two named replies to classmates that do one of three things: build on the post, ask a real question about it, or connect it to the replier's own experience. Interaction counts went with higher posttest scores in one online small-group study ([Higher numbers of interactions are associated with higher posttest scores in asynchronous online small group work](../claims/interaction-quantity-correlates-posttest-online-groups.md) [+W]; correlational, so stronger learners may simply post more). The reply frame is untested; the earlier page says unstructured forums and posting mandates rarely produce presence on their own.
5. **State norms that allow warmth and disagreement, and model them.** At the start, say that humour, saying how a task felt and disagreeing with a reason are welcome, and that questions are expected, not a sign of weakness; the instructor's own posts show it (a brief personal remark, a "I was wrong about this", a respectful challenge). Untested; from the earlier page. Recipe: [Discussion norms](../strategies/discussion-norms.md).
6. **Add synchronous contact, about once a fortnight.** A 45–60-minute web meeting for open discussion of the content, not for administration, with cameras optional and a text chat alongside. In one redesigned online teacher-education unit, fortnightly web conferences refocused from assessment talk to open-ended conversation were reported as highly successful in engaging students and forming their community ([Fortnightly web conferences with open-ended discussion supported students' academic and social engagement and community formation](../claims/web-conferences-support-online-community.md) [+W]; student comments, no comparison). Where learners need to be corrected (for example in a language course), use the live channel: in one study of second-language learners, discussion boards produced no corrective feedback at all, against text chat and video ([Discussion board interactions generated no negative feedback at all](../claims/discussion-board-no-negative-feedback-synchrony-required.md) [-W]). Recipe: [Increase synchronous teacher presence](../strategies/increase_synchronous_teacher_presence.md).
7. **Tie every social activity to the task within a week or two.** Use what introductions surfaced (learners' experience, workplaces, languages) in the first cases or discussion questions, and say so. Social exchange with no task purpose is what the earlier page warned can turn into chat and lower perceived rigour. Untested; the task side is modelled on [Community of Inquiry](community-of-inquiry.md).
8. **Move the source of presence from instructor to peers across the course.** A default arc, untested: in weeks 1–2 the instructor leads (welcome, introductions, frequent named replies, the first live meeting); in the middle of the course peers take rotating roles in each group (opener, questioner, summariser), the instructor replies less often and mainly at synthesis points, and live meetings are partly run by groups; in the last third groups use the trust they have built to critique each other's drafts and plans, and the instructor appears for the final synthesis and to close the community. Move a group to the peer-led stage when every member has posted and replied at least twice and some replies question or build on others rather than only agree (a default of about a third of replies; set the threshold locally and record it). Keep a group on instructor-led support longer if members are still silent.
9. **Watch for who is missing, from week 2.** If a learner has not introduced themselves or posted by the end of the second week, send one short private message by name that offers a lower-risk way in (reply in their small group only, an audio post, a five-minute call). Untested; the outreach itself is on [Instructor Accessibility](instructor-accessibility.md).
10. **Judge the design on participation and learning separately.** Record who posts and replies, what replies do, and whether learners stay; judge learning on an individual task at the horizon the objective sets. A high rating of social presence is not evidence that learners learned more ([social presence's association is with self-reported regulation](../claims/social-presence-strongest-metacognition-association-cbi.md) [~M]).

When a condition is not met: if the course is short (one session or a week), skip profiles and do one paired introduction tied to the first task; if the group changes every week (open enrolment, a drop-in course), keep the instructor's named replies and a standing small group for anyone who stays more than two weeks; if no one can moderate a forum, do not open one, and use small-group work in the live sessions instead.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **The medium, and how much of it is live.** In a room, people already see each other; in an asynchronous text course, nothing about them is visible unless the design invites it. If unstated, ask: will learners ever see or hear each other live, and on what devices?
- **How long the group stays together, and how big it is.** Trust between peers needs repeated contact with the same people; a one-off workshop or a 2,000-learner course cannot rely on it. If unstated, ask: how many learners, in what groups, for how many weeks?
- **What learners are willing to show, and to whom.** Norms about self-disclosure, emotional expression and talking to a teacher differ by culture, age, profession and past experience of school, and some learners have reasons to keep their lives private. If unstated, ask: what do learners already share with each other, and is anything off limits?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behaviour in the course: who posts, replies and attends live sessions, when and how often; whether replies name peers and build, question or merely agree; whether anyone discloses anything about themselves or their work; who drops out or goes quiet; learners' own survey ratings; and each learner's individual performance on the target. "Low social presence", "does not feel safe" and "feels isolated" are inferred states, and the same surface pattern can come from several of them. Record the prompt, the channel, the group membership and every instructor move.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A learner reads every thread but rarely posts | Fear of public error or of being judged; no time; does not see what posting gives them; language or literacy barrier | Ask privately what would make posting easier and offer a lower-risk mode (small group, audio, draft first); compare their private answer with the group's level and see which mode they take up. |
| Introductions are warm, but later discussion is polite agreement | Norms reward agreement; the prompts have one obvious answer; learners do not know each other well enough yet to disagree | Pose a question with two defensible answers and, separately, model a respectful challenge; see which, if either, brings questioning replies. |
| Lively social talk, little about the task | Social activity has no link to the task; the task is too easy or unclear; learners value the contact more than the content | Tie the next prompt to the introductions' content; ask learners privately what they want from the group. |
| Presence ratings high, individual test results unchanged | Presence supports satisfaction rather than learning here; the discussion targets a different capability from the test; others carried the reasoning | Give an individual probe on the reasoning discussed; check whether the test and the discussion target the same capability. |
| A learner withdraws after posting something personal | Disclosure felt exposed or got no response; a peer's reply was dismissive; unrelated life events | Check the replies their post got; ask privately; move personal sharing to small groups or make it optional. |
| Remote learners in a mixed group stay silent while in-room learners talk | They cannot hear or be heard; the room's conversation moves too fast; they are not known to the room | Give remote learners the first turn in the next discussion and a shared chat; see whether participation changes. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking a learner about their participation can itself change it, so record each probe.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests social-presence design: none assigns learners to courses or activities that differ in social presence and measures participation, persistence or learning. The claims below are associations, reports and neighbouring findings.

- [Social presence shows the strongest association with metacognition in an online case-based course, while teaching presence shows no significant relationship](../claims/social-presence-strongest-metacognition-association-cbi.md) [~M]: a survey of 47 graduate students in one online case-based instructional design course; social presence had the strongest association with self-reported self-regulation and co-regulation, then cognitive presence, while teaching presence's correlations (.096 and .228) were not significant. Associational (`r1`), self-report on both sides, no learning outcome. It is the most direct evidence here that social presence matters, and it is a correlation in one course.
- [Co-regulation shows stronger relationships with the three CoI presences than self-regulation in an online case-based course](../claims/co-regulation-stronger-presence-relationships-than-self-regulation.md) [+W]: the same survey; co-regulation correlated .653 with social presence against .397 for self-regulation. Consistent with social presence being about the group's regulation of its work more than each learner's; predicts nothing about outcomes.
- [Students in an accelerated online MBA program experience their courses as a community of inquiry, with all three CoI presences rated above 4.0 on a 5-point scale](../claims/mba-students-experience-courses-as-coi.md) [+W]: CoI Survey ratings from 21 MBA students (32 completed surveys); social presence was rated highest (M = 4.24). A description of perceptions in one programme.
- [Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of correlational and longitudinal studies, one of 119 studies (learner-centred teacher variables with student outcomes at a mean r = .31, higher for affective and behavioural outcomes) and one of 99 studies from preschool to high school (relationships with engagement at medium-to-large strength, with achievement at small-to-medium). It supports the instructor-as-person part of the design for school learners; neither synthesis tests whether improving relationships raises engagement, and neither is about online courses.
- [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]: two randomised experiments with college entrants (92 students over three years; 26,911 students at 22 institutions), read from abstracts. A brief message framing belonging worries as common and temporary raised GPA or first-year completion for groups that had historically progressed at lower rates, **but only where the setting gave those groups opportunities to belong**. It qualifies this page: a message alone does not make up for a course in which there is no real group to belong to.
- [Human embodiment in video: perceived social presence benefits learning ratings, but instructor-face inclusion shows no significant learning-performance difference](../claims/human-embodiment-video-presence-effects.md) [~M]: a narrative review's second-hand reports: students judged lecture videos with the instructor visible to offer more learning, while a comparison of more and less talking-head presence found no significant difference in learning performance (equivalence not tested). It limits the design: making the instructor feel present can change how learners rate a course without changing what they learn.
- [Peer monitoring among MBA learners goes beyond CoI social presence by involving professionally relevant evaluation of peers' contributions](../claims/peer-monitoring-exceeds-social-presence.md) [~W]: interviews with six MBA students, who read introductions to find contributing colleagues and judged peers by what they could offer. It qualifies the construct for professional learners: the social side of presence may be less about affirmation and comfort than about judging who is worth learning from.
- [Creating community and interacting with students online required deliberate, planned effort unlike spontaneous face-to-face co-presence](../claims/online-community-requires-conscious-effort.md) [+W] and [Fortnightly web conferences with open-ended discussion supported students' academic and social engagement and community formation](../claims/web-conferences-support-online-community.md) [+W]: teachers' interview reports (web-based high school) and student comments in one redesigned online teacher-education unit; no comparisons. They support designing for presence deliberately, and fortnightly live meetings as one way to do it.
- [Discussion board interactions generated no negative feedback at all, suggesting synchrony is required to foster corrective feedback](../claims/discussion-board-no-negative-feedback-synchrony-required.md) [-W]: in one study of second-language learners' negotiated interaction, the board produced no explicit correction, recasts or self-correction, against 61 occurrences in text chat and 58 in videoconferencing. It limits asynchronous forums as the only channel where correction matters.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (graduate and MBA students' ratings, school pupils' relationships, college entrants' persistence, video viewers' judgements), so do not rank them. Taken together: social presence is perceived, goes with self-reported regulation and satisfaction, and probably needs deliberate design online; whether designing for it raises learning is not shown, and making presence felt can change ratings without changing performance.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective names the capability the course should build and how it will be judged on an individual task; social presence is a means to it, and a course can be warm and still miss it. Elicit separately what each learner wants from the others: company in a lonely course, contacts in their profession, help when stuck, to be left alone to get on with it, or to keep their private life private. Agreement and divergence are both observations, and a high presence rating does not show the learner shares the designer's objective.

For example, a working nurse in an online leadership course may value peers who can tell her how a policy played out on their wards, while the designer's objective is that she can analyse a change proposal with the course's framework. Professional introductions that surface each learner's ward and role serve the first; a group case where each member applies the framework to a colleague's real proposal can serve both, and an individual analysis checks the designer's objective rather than the group's warmth.

## What would revise this model?

The model would gain support if comparisons of otherwise equal online courses, with and without the designed presence moves, showed more learners staying, more open and critical discussion, and better individual performance on the target. It would weaken if such comparisons raised presence ratings and satisfaction but left persistence and learning unchanged, which would make social presence a description of a pleasant course rather than a condition for learning; the video review already shows ratings and performance coming apart. If presence for professionals turns out to rest on judging peers' usefulness rather than on warmth, the introductions and norms should be redesigned around expertise. If forced or public disclosure lowers participation for some groups, those steps should be made private by default. Do not protect the model by calling every silent learner "low presence" after the fact.

Perceived presence, participation, satisfaction, persistence and individual learning are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish that designing for social presence causes any of them, how much is enough, or how long a group must stay together.

## Examples

### Validated
- Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu (2017) meta-analyzed 51 studies of online courses and found social presence significantly related to student satisfaction (r ≈ .54) and perceived learning (r ≈ .45), with instructor presence behaviors among the strongest predictors [+S].

### Illustrative
- **Introductions with media profiles** — a standard first-week activity in online courses (e.g., Canvas or Moodle introduction forums) where learners post a short video or annotated photo; establishes identity cues that text alone omits.
- **Instructor video announcements** — weekly personalized video or audio messages in LMS-based courses; a widely recommended practice in online teaching handbooks for making the instructor feel present rather than textual.
- **Peer response protocols in discussion forums** — requiring named, substantive replies to classmates (e.g., Piazza or Perusall threads) converts broadcast posting into dialogue, the interaction pattern most associated with cohesion.
- **[Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md)** — reclaiming synchronous time for interaction and discussion increases the interpersonal cues available compared with lecture-transmission formats.

## Description
Social presence is the degree to which participants in a learning environment perceive each other as "real" people — with personality, emotion, and intent — rather than as anonymous or mechanical interlocutors. The principle recommends deliberately designing for social presence through personal introductions, affective and cohesive communication, instructor visibility and responsiveness, and activities that surface learners' identities and voices. It is most consequential in mediated environments (online, blended, asynchronous), where cues that make people feel present are naturally attenuated.

- Sustained, high-quality discourse and peer critique
