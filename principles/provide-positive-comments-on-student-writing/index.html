Provide positive comments alongside constructive criticism when responding to student writing¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 21 claims (5 for, 11 mixed, 5 against) · 23 studies (6 causal, 6 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 2 design, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 4 of 23 report an effect size · 16 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns what a teacher's response to a student's draft says about what works in it, beside the critique. For a student writer (an adolescent or adult, a novice or intermediate writer in the genre, including second-language writers) who will revise the draft, a response that names specific strengths of this draft (what the writing does and why it works for its reader: "this example makes your claim concrete", not "good job" or "you are a gifted writer") alongside a small number of prioritised critical comments is expected to keep the writer reading and acting on the critique (motivation-affect, persistence into revision) and so to produce a better revised draft (skill-performance on a writing-quality judgement), compared with critique alone, with praise in place of critique, or with generic or person-directed praise. The goal is a complex-skill (composing a text); a secondary target is the writer's self-confidence and motivation to write (attitude-motivation), which the earlier page named.

The relationship is conditional on four things. The positive comment must carry information about the work: feedback helps on average and helps more the more information it carries, and feedback that turns attention to the person helps least and can lower performance. Praise must not replace critique: a strength comment frames the critique, and a response with praise and no usable critique withholds the feedback the writer needs. Praise for ability is a risk when failure may follow: children praised for being smart did worse after a later failure than children praised for effort. The writer must have a chance to act on the response (another draft), or neither strength nor critique can change the text. It is proposed for classroom, online-instructor-led and online-self-paced settings over days-weeks or term-plus, with teacher, peer or automated comments on drafts.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship: none compares writing feedback with and without specific positive comments, or measures a revised draft against the balance of praise and critique. The earlier page rests on one practitioner article (Shvidko 2020), which has no claim page here. What the claims test are neighbouring parts: what any feedback must carry to help (two meta-analyses), what praise for ability does to children after failure (one set of experiments and one review), whether verbal praise undermines free-choice engagement as tangible rewards do (two competing meta-analyses), and how automated feedback distributes praise and critique (two studies of AI-generated feedback, one of them on student essays). Two brief "affirmation" findings that the earlier page cited bear on the identity-threat reason it gave, and both limit it.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page, so a designer can go to the one that holds each decision. Process-based Writing holds the draft–respond–revise cycle and the reader stance in responding; this page holds one move inside the response, naming strengths. Wise Feedback Across Difference holds critique framed with a high standard and an assurance that the learner can reach it, for learners who may doubt the giver's intent; its "positive comments" part is this page's move, and its framing should be added when that doubt is likely. Strengths-Based Approach holds how a learner's demonstrated capabilities are named and built on across tasks; a strength comment on a draft is one such naming, at the grain of one text. Feedback Loops holds whether a comment reaches a next attempt that is checked again. The structured peer review pattern holds the peer routine in which students give each other praise and critique.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher or designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Read for what works before marking what does not. On a first read, mark two or three places where the draft does something well for its reader. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("acknowledge positive aspects of student work and highlight the strengths").
  2. Make each strength comment specific to the text. Name the move and its effect ("Your second paragraph answers the objection a sceptical reader would raise"), not the person ("You're a natural writer") and not a bare tick. Short marginal remarks ("Well said", "Good point"), which the earlier page reports can build motivation, are fine as additions beside a passage, not as the whole response. Basis: feedback carrying more information helps more, and feedback aimed at the self helps least (Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [+S]; Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels [+S]); untested for strength comments on writing.
  3. Praise the choice or the work, never the writer's ability. Comment on decisions the writer made (a strategy, a revision, effort spent on evidence) rather than talent. Basis: Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [-S] (fifth graders on reasoning problems, not writing); carried to older writers untested.
  4. Give the critique, prioritised: two or three comments, the most important first. Choose what the next draft most needs (usually ideas, organisation or evidence on an early draft), and say what to do, not only what is wrong. Basis for keeping critique in: LLM feedback for students marked by race, language, disability, gender, or motivation shows positive feedback bias and feedback withholding bias — more praise and less substantive critique than for unmarked counterparts [-M] (AI-generated feedback on 600 essays; shows the risk, not its effect on learning). The number of comments is an untested proposal.
  5. Do not set a praise-to-critique ratio by rule, and do not make praise automatic. Match strength comments to what the draft actually does; a draft with little that works gets one honest strength and focused critique, not invented praise. Basis: The agent's praise was indiscriminate affirmation: validation followed about half of participant turns regardless of what the participant said [-M] (a reflection agent in a randomized trial, not writing feedback; it shows what content-blind praise looks like, not its effect). The earlier page notes that the balance "may not always be easy to achieve".
  6. End with the next step. Close the response with what to do in the next draft and, where the writer is likely to doubt your intent, an explicit statement of the standard and that you think they can meet it (modelled in Wise Feedback Across Difference). Untested proposal here.
  7. Check what the writer did with it. On the next draft, look first at whether the prioritised comments were acted on, and name any improvement as the first strength comment ("The new topic sentence now tells the reader where this is going"). Untested proposal; links progress to the strength comments.
  8. Plan the arc across drafts and the term, not one response. A default arc, all quantities untested proposals:
  9. First draft of a new genre (early in a term): 5–10 minutes per draft; two or three strength comments on ideas and structure, two or three critical comments on the biggest issue, one next step. No line editing.
  10. Second draft: about 5 minutes; open with one comment naming what improved since the first draft, then one or two critical comments, which may now reach paragraphs and sentences.
  11. Final draft: a short summary of what works and one target for the next piece of writing; grade separately.
  12. Across the term: move the source of the strength comments from the teacher to the writer. From the second or third assignment, ask writers to mark what they think works and what they want help with before submitting; respond to their choices, agreeing, adding or correcting. Peer responses using PQP (Praise-Question-Polish) can supply the first round once writers have seen several teacher responses.
  13. Moving on: a writer whose own strength notes match yours on two consecutive drafts, and who acts on critique without prompting, needs fewer teacher strength comments; keep them specific when you do give them.

When a condition is not met: if there is no further draft, give the strength and the one change for the next piece of writing rather than a list of faults; if the writer only reads the grade, return comments before the grade or without it (see the table below).

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether the writer will revise this draft. Comments on a draft that will be revised can be acted on; comments on a final text are read, if at all, as a justification of the grade. If unstated, ask: will the writer produce another version of this text, and when?
  • How the writer is likely to read critique. A confident writer may need few strength comments; a writer with low confidence, a second-language writer, or one with reason to doubt the teacher's intent may stop reading at the first criticism. If unstated, ask: how has this writer responded to critique before, and do they trust the person giving it?
  • Who writes the comments, and with how much time. A teacher with ten minutes per draft, a peer, and an AI tool produce different balances of praise and critique. If unstated, ask: who will respond to each draft, and how long do they have?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: the comments given (how many strengths, whether each names something in the text, how many critical comments and which were marked priority), whether the writer opened or read them, what changed between drafts, and what the writer says about the response. "Discouraged", "defensive" and "open to feedback" are inferred states.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
The revised draft changes nothing the critique asked for The comments were not read (grade only); the critique was unclear; the writer read the critique as a judgement of them and disengaged; no time to revise Ask the writer to say, in their own words, what the main comment asked; check whether the grade was returned with the comments.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares writing feedback with and without specific positive comments, or measures revised drafts or writing motivation against the mix of praise and critique. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

  • Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [+S]: across 435 educational studies, feedback had a medium average effect (d = 0.48) with high heterogeneity; reinforcement or punishment gave d = 0.24 against d = 0.99 for high-information feedback. A second meta-analysis across settings found d = .41 on average, with more than a third of interventions lowering performance. Its studies table counts 7 distinct studies behind it and its narrower claims. It supports making a strength comment informational; it does not test praise inside writing feedback.
  • Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels [+S]: the same two meta-analyses, read for the level of feedback: effectiveness declined as feedback drew attention toward the self. Both support the level ordering only indirectly (one coded information content, the other its own attention levels). It is the reason strength comments name the work, not the writer.
  • Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [-S]: fifth graders praised for intelligence after success chose performance over learning goals far more often than effort-praised children (69% against 12%), and their scores dropped after a failure while effort-praised children's rose (Mueller & Dweck 1998, 128 in Study 1); a review finds the harm moderated by age and gender and that sincere process praise can enhance motivation. It limits the page: praise of the writer's ability is the form to avoid. It is about reasoning problems, not writing feedback.
  • Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [+M]: of two competing meta-analyses, one reports that verbal praise increases intrinsic motivation and the negative effect is confined to expected tangible rewards. Its studies table counts 8 distinct studies, including the praise studies above. It answers one worry (that praise in feedback undermines motivation to write) for free-choice engagement on laboratory tasks; writing classes are not tested.
  • LLM feedback for students marked by race, language, disability, gender, or motivation shows positive feedback bias and feedback withholding bias — more praise and less substantive critique than for unmarked counterparts [-M]: AI-generated feedback on the same 600 essays gave students described as Black, Hispanic, Asian, female, unmotivated or learning-disabled more praise and less constructive criticism. It is the limiting claim on the automated side: praise added to a response can displace critique, and does so unevenly. It measures the comments, not their effect on writers.
  • Non-layered AI feedback produced significantly higher post-test performance than layered AI feedback after controlling for pre-test [-M]: in one randomized online higher-education lesson (100 against 99 students), layered feedback, whose first layer students rated as more encouraging, gave lower adjusted post-test scores (10.40 against 11.24) than elaborated feedback given at once. Its companion claims report the encouragement ratings (Layered AI feedback was perceived as more encouraging and independence-supporting but imposed higher mental effort [~M]) and the negative total effect (The total effect of layered feedback on learning outcomes was significantly negative, with an unmediated negative direct effect [-M]). It qualifies the model: feedback felt as more encouraging did not produce more learning here. Encouragement was not manipulated on its own, and the task was not writing.

Writers, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (children's reasoning problems, laboratory tasks, AI comments on essays, an online lesson), so do not rank them. None measures the quality of a revised draft.

The earlier page cited two findings for its reason, that feedback can threaten a writer's identity and that affirmation buffers the threat. Both limit rather than support it:

  • A brief values-affirmation writing exercise raised African American seventh-graders' grades in two small field experiments, but a large same-district replication found no effect [~S]: a 15-minute values-writing exercise, not a teacher's positive comment, with seventh graders; two small experiments found grade gains and a larger replication of the same protocol found none (d = -0.07). It does not test praise in feedback, and its own page treats the claim as mixed. The earlier page cited it as [+W].
  • The agent's praise was indiscriminate affirmation: validation followed about half of participant turns regardless of what the participant said [-M]: a reflection agent praised about half of participants' turns (48–57%) whatever they had said. It shows what affirmation detached from content looks like; it measures no effect on learning. The earlier page cited it as [-W].

The evidence by question¶

  • Does praise help or harm learners' motivation and later performance, and which kind of praise? Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [+M]
  • Does automated feedback on student work substitute praise for critique, and for whom? LLM feedback for students marked by race, language, disability, gender, or motivation shows positive feedback bias and feedback withholding bias — more praise and less substantive critique than for unmarked counterparts [-M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims found while converting this page that bear on it at a distance. None tests positive comments on writing.

  • Praise focused on global intelligence has negative effects on future learning behavior compared with praise about effort [-M] — the Mueller & Dweck experiments as a practice guide reports them; possibly the same claim as the praise-for-intelligence claim above.
  • Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [~M] — second-hand: ability feedback for rapid early learning raised self-efficacy more than effort feedback; it sits in tension with the praise-for-intelligence finding and suggests the timing of the comment matters.
  • Feedback about self as a person is the least effective feedback level; task, process, and self-regulation feedback support learning and combine for greater impact [~M] — a primer's summary of Hattie and Timperley; the same point as the level claim above, second-hand.
  • Positive reinforcement and praise correlate positively with learning while criticism correlates negatively [+W] — a 1985 conference paper reporting older correlational research second-hand, with no coefficients.
  • Smaller classes related to more interactions; task-directed and praising interactions linked to more time on task and higher achievement, but organisational and personal interactions rose contrary to expectations [+W] — grade 1 classes, correlational, reported by a review; praising interactions went with time on task.
  • A 1970 teacher self-analysis programme asserts, without data, that teacher responses including verbal rewards can close off pupil thinking [~W] — a 1970 training programme's assertion, no data; spoken classroom responses, not written comments.
  • Motivation prompts shift feedback tone: unmotivated students receive enthusiastic, collaborative encouragement while motivated students receive direct, higher-level critique [~M] and Gender prompts shift feedback stance: female students receive affective, first-person, empathy-oriented feedback while male students receive objective, task-oriented critique [~M] — the same essay-feedback study as the withholding-bias claim, by student description.
  • Perceived independence did not mediate a significant indirect effect of layered AI feedback on learning performance [~M] — from the layered-feedback experiment; the page's title states an encouragement pathway, but its recorded entry reports only the non-significant pathway through perceived independence, so it is not used in the model.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The teacher's objective is usually a better revised text and, over a term, a writer who can judge their own drafts. Elicit separately what the writer wants from a response: a grade, reassurance, to know whether the piece works for its reader, or to get a job application or report accepted. A writer who wants only the grade may not read comments at all; one who wants reassurance may read only the strengths. Agreement and divergence are both observations: ask the writer what they want feedback on before responding, and respond to that as well as to your priority.

For example, a second-language university student may want to know whether their English "sounds natural", while the teacher's priority for this draft is the argument's structure. Name a strength in the structure, give the structural critique, and add one comment on the language point the writer asked about, so the writer's question is answered without displacing the priority.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of the same critique with and without specific strength comments, on comparable writers, show no difference in revision quality or in whether writers act on the critique. If generic praise ("good job") and specific strength comments produce the same revisions and the same motivation to write, the information requirement here is a preference, not a condition. If strength comments reduce the critique writers act on (by signalling that the draft is good enough), the default should give critique first. If writers who doubt the responder's intent respond to the standard-and-assurance framing but not to strength comments, the move belongs to Wise Feedback Across Difference rather than here.

Writers' reported confidence, whether they read the comments, what changed in the next draft, and the quality of later independent writing are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no number of strength comments, no ratio of praise to critique, and no effect of either on writing.
