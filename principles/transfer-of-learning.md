# Transfer of Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 17 claims (8 for, 9 mixed) · 17 studies (7 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 3 theoretical, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 17 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the most general relationship in the transfer family: whether what a learner has learned in one setting is used in a new task, situation or problem. Consider a learner who can do a task, or state a principle, in the form it was taught. Their starting response on a new case might be to treat it as unfamiliar, answer from its surface features, apply the taught method where it does not fit, or succeed only when told "this is like last week". The expectation is that instruction which **shows the same underlying structure in several surface forms, asks the learner to compare those forms and name what they share, and then has them retrieve and use the structure on changed items** produces more `near-transfer` to new cases of the same kind than practising one version of the task in one context. The target is a `principle`, `concept`, `procedure` or `complex-skill` whose structure can appear in more than one surface form. Evidence for `far-transfer` to a different domain, and for gains at `delayed-retention`, is thin, and the strongest results are near and short-term.

**No claim in the wiki tests this general relationship as stated**, that is, instruction across varied contexts against instruction in one context, with a transfer test. The idea that varied encoding produces decontextualised knowledge reaches the wiki only second-hand, through a review, and the whole-task argument is a training-design proposal that no recorded study tests. What the wiki does test are four configurations inside the relationship, each with its own comparison: comparing two provided cases against studying them separately, mixing problem types against blocking them, retrieval practice against restudy on new questions, and faded worked examples with principle prompts. All four measure transfer on study-defined tasks close to the training domain. Two experiments show far transfer staying at the floor in every condition. Whether the learner's original learning is secure, whether the varied cases really share the structure, and what counts as "new" for the objective must be established locally.

How the converted siblings sit inside this. [Analogical Reasoning](analogical-reasoning.md) owns mapping one provided or familiar case onto a new one, and holds the case-comparison evidence in detail. [Cognitive Flexibility](cognitive-flexibility.md) owns holding the same knowledge in several cases, perspectives or representations so it can be reassembled for an ill-structured situation. [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) owns retrieval as such. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) and [Worked Examples](worked-examples.md) own how the original learning is secured. This page does not repeat their models. It says what a designer adds when transfer itself is the objective, and how to tell transfer from recall. The [4C/ID pattern](../patterns/4cid-four-component-instructional-design.md) and the [case-based learning pattern](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) hold converted policies for whole-task and case-based designs. The [Hugging and Bridging for Transfer](../strategies/hugging-and-bridging-for-transfer.md) strategy holds two teacher techniques taken from the earlier version of this page.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (Schunk; Souza; Perkins and Salomon), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Evidence status is stated beside each step. Every quantity is an untested proposal unless a claim is named.

1. **Secure the original learning before asking for transfer.** Souza's first factor is "context and degree of original learning". Before the first transfer item, the learner should do the taught task unaided and explain one step. Untested here as a precondition. How to get there is modelled in [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) and [Worked Examples](worked-examples.md).
2. **Teach each principle through at least two, preferably three, cases that differ in surface and share the structure.** Examples from the earlier page: a proportional-reasoning strategy in recipes, map scales and budgets, or one clinical reasoning routine across different patients. Evidence: [comparing analogous cases beats studying the same cases separately](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+S]. A second-hand review reports that [examples from varied contexts produce decontextualised knowledge](../claims/encoding-variability-decontextualization-transfer.md) [+W]. Two position papers argue that [varied tasks of one type](../claims/task-variety-enables-transfer-same-task-type.md) [+W] and [learning in multiple contexts](../claims/multiple-contexts-generalization-transfer.md) [+W] lead to transfer. A review reports that [repeating one task improved that task and not a new task of the same type](../claims/task-repetition-fluency-complexity-same-task-only.md) [~M]. Two cases rather than one is supported. The number of cases is not settled.
3. **Put the cases side by side and ask what corresponds and what is the same, then state the principle.** Evidence: in the case-comparison meta-analysis the benefit was larger when learners looked for similarities and when the principle came after the comparison ([claim](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+S]). In worked-example learning, [prompts to identify the principle, with backward fading, improved near and far transfer](../claims/fading-and-principle-prompts-improve-probability-transfer.md) [+M] on study-defined probability tasks.
4. **Name the critical attributes and where the structure does not apply.** Include at least one case that looks similar but has a different structure, and ask the learner to say why the principle does not fit. This is from the earlier page (Souza's "critical attributes"; negative transfer) and is an untested proposal. Mixing confusable problem types once each has been introduced is the nearest tested form: [interleaving improves classification of new examples](../claims/interleaving-improves-transfer.md) [~M], strongly for visual categories, less for mathematics, and reversed for word categories.
5. **Practise retrieval on changed items, not the same ones.** Ask application and inference questions in a changed format rather than repeating the taught items. Evidence: [retrieval practice beats restudy on transfer questions](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-transfer.md) [~S], most clearly for application and inference questions and changed formats. Bias-corrected estimates show little transfer without those features.
6. **Prompt bridging in both directions.** While learning, ask "where else could you use this?" (forward-reaching). On a new problem, ask "what earlier problem is this like?" (backward-reaching). Fade the prompt once the learner names a correct source without it. From the earlier page (Perkins and Salomon's high-road transfer; [Hugging and Bridging](../strategies/hugging-and-bridging-for-transfer.md)). Untested proposal.
7. **Keep the whole task visible when the target is a complex skill.** Practise integrated tasks that keep the structure of later performance, not only isolated parts. Evidence: only [a training-design argument that no recorded study tests](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W]. For learners new to a high-interactivity task, practise isolated parts first and integrate them soon after: [part-task practice reduced load for absolute novices](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M].
8. **Judge transfer on its own items.** Score near-transfer items (same structure, new surface) and far-transfer items (different domain or form) separately from recall and from the taught items. Give at least one at a delay of a week or more. Expect far-transfer scores to be low: [all conditions sat at a floor on far transfer in a computer-based engineering study](../claims/far-transfer-floor-effect-and-equal-time.md) [~M], and [no condition raised far-transfer scores in a game-learning experiment](../claims/germane-load-gains-limited-to-identical-and-near-transfer-problems.md) [~W]. Do not read a far-transfer null as a failure of the near-transfer design.
9. **Spread the arc across sessions, with the source of cases moving outward.** The following arc is an untested proposal for a unit of about four to six sessions. **Early (sessions 1–2):** about 20 minutes per session on the taught case and one near variant, with the comparison prompt given and the principle stated by the teacher afterwards. **Middle (sessions 3–4):** about 15 minutes per session on two or three new surface forms, problem types mixed, and retrieval questions in a changed format. The learner states the principle, and the teacher checks it. **Late (sessions 5–6):** about 10–15 minutes per session on unprompted new cases, including one the learner brings from their own work or life, and one far case. Open each session after the first with two retrieval items on earlier cases in a new surface form. Move a learner on from one stage to the next when they solve two of three unprompted near cases in a new surface form and can name the shared structure. Move a learner who already does this at the start straight to the late stage ([expertise reversal](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]). Repeat a near and a far item at least a week after the last session.

When a condition is not met: if only one context can be taught (one workplace procedure, one textbook case), vary the problems within it and add a bridging prompt about where else it applies. Test transfer to the context that matters and do not claim a general gain. If learners cannot yet do the taught task, transfer items will only measure that.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **How far the target situation is from the taught one.** A new surface in the same domain (`near-transfer`) is supported here. A different domain or form (`far-transfer`) is barely moved in the evidence the wiki holds. If the brief does not say, ask: what new situations must the learner handle, and how different are they from the ones in the course?
- **Whether the original learning is secure.** A learner who cannot do the taught case unaided has nothing to transfer, and extra varied cases add load. If the brief does not say, ask: can the learner do one taught item now, unaided, and explain why it works?
- **Whether the target setting supports the use.** In workplaces, transfer depends on cases, equipment and colleagues in the setting as well as on the course. If the brief does not say, ask: will the learner meet the situations, and have the resources, to use this within weeks of the course?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are `novice` on this task, or the task is high in element interactivity | Secure the taught case first with worked examples and guided practice; start with two cases, not four; practise parts briefly before integrating; delay mixing until each type has been introduced | [Part-task practice reduces cognitive load for absolute novices during initial skill acquisition.](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M] (load, not transfer); [interleaving](../claims/interleaving-improves-transfer.md) [~M] (claim page notes novel material can reverse the benefit) |
| Learners are `advanced`, or already solve the taught case unaided | Skip the early stage; give unprompted varied and far cases with selective feedback, and ask learners to generate their own cases | [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M] (review of cognitive-load studies; not about transfer designs) |

| Time is one session only | Teach two cases side by side, prompt the comparison, state the principle, and end with one near-transfer item; do not claim far or delayed transfer | [Analogical Reasoning Improves Transfer](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+S] (immediate tests favoured in the meta-analysis) |

| Stakes are high, or the objective is far transfer to another domain | Add cases from the target domain itself rather than relying on transfer from another; assess on representative target tasks after a delay | [All learners … showed a floor effect on far-transfer problems](../claims/far-transfer-floor-effect-and-equal-time.md) [~M]; [no increases for far-transfer problems](../claims/germane-load-gains-limited-to-identical-and-near-transfer-problems.md) [~W] (one study each, engineering and a game task) |
| `workplace-clinical`, and the course teaches advanced competencies the setting rarely uses | Agree with the workplace when the learner will meet the cases; plan refresher cases or simulation if they will not; report use separately from knowledge | [Transfer of advanced competencies is limited by institutional case exposure, technical platforms, and multidisciplinary maturity rather than training deficiencies](../claims/advanced-competency-transfer-limited-by-institutional-resources.md) [~W] (interviews, one programme in China) |

Ten of the twelve rows cite a claim. Five of them (concept, procedure choice, self-paced, one session, far transfer) rest on comparison studies with a transfer outcome, and even these carry undergraduate laboratory or classroom results to other learners and settings. The other five carry a review, interview studies or a load result to this setting, and they say so. The child and classroom rows are untested proposals.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a new case under stated conditions: the case, how it differs from the taught ones (surface, structure, domain), whether a hint or reminder was given, the answer, and any explanation. "Has abstracted the principle", "knowledge is context-bound" and "negative transfer" are inferred states. Record whether the learner was told that an earlier case was relevant. Success with that hint shows that the learner can apply the principle, not that they can recognise when it applies.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Solves the taught items, fails a new case with a different surface | Learning bound to the taught surface; original learning shallower than it looked; the new case also differs in structure | Give a new case with the taught surface and new numbers, then one with a new surface and the same structure; ask what the case is "like". |
| Solves the new case only when told "this is like the earlier one" | Principle held but not retrieved spontaneously; the hint gave away the method | Give matched new cases with and without the hint, and one that looks like the earlier case but needs a different principle. |
| Applies the taught method to a case it does not fit | Surface similarity triggered it (negative transfer); critical attributes not learned; only one method available | Ask what would have to be true for the method to work here; give a pair of look-alike cases with different structures to sort. |
| States the principle correctly, cannot use it on a new case | Verbal knowledge without a procedure; the new case needs other knowledge; the item is a far case | Give a near case in the taught domain, then the far one; check whether the far case's own domain knowledge is held. |
| Transfers at the end of the unit, not a week later | Little consolidation; the transfer item was close to practice items; interference from later work | Re-test with a fresh case at the delay and record all practice in between. |
| Uses the skill in the course, not on the job | No suitable cases at work; missing equipment or support; the skill was adapted locally and looks different | Ask the learner and a supervisor which cases occurred and what was done; observe one case if possible. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A transfer probe is itself a varied case and teaches as it measures, so record each one. Keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim compares instruction across varied contexts with instruction in one context on a transfer outcome at first hand. The claims below test configurations inside the relationship or limit it. Learners, comparisons and outcomes differ, so do not rank them by effect label.

- [Analogical Reasoning Improves Transfer](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis (`synthesis-experimental`) of 57 experiments (336 tests). Case comparison produced more learning than sequential, single-case or non-analogous case study, traditional instruction and controls (d = .50, 95% CI [.44, .56]). Gains were larger when learners looked for similarities, when the principle followed the comparison, with perceptual content, and on immediate tests (abstract only). The page also records three `randomized` experiments with novice undergraduates learning negotiation. In Experiment 2 (n = 128), 48% of those who compared two cases used the principle in a new negotiation, against 19% of those who studied the same cases separately; no standardised effect size is given. This is the strongest test of "show the structure in two forms and compare" in the wiki. It is near transfer at short horizons, and the details are on [Analogical Reasoning](analogical-reasoning.md).
- [Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-transfer.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 122 experiments (192 effect sizes) found practice testing beat non-testing re-exposure on transfer tests (d = 0.40). It was strongest for application and inference questions, changed test formats and medical-diagnosis problems, and weakest for rearranged items and untested material. Publication-bias corrections often left no positive transfer when none of the favourable moderators was present. In four experiments (Butler 2010), repeated testing beat repeated restudy on new inferential questions a week later, within and across knowledge domains (no effect size in the abstract). This supports retrieval on changed items, and only when retrieval asks for application.
- [Interleaving Improves Transfer](../claims/interleaving-improves-transfer.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 59 studies found interleaved presentation of category examples beat blocked presentation on classifying new examples (g = 0.42). The effect was largest for paintings (g = 0.67), smaller for mathematical tasks (g = 0.34), non-significant for expository texts, and reversed for word categories (g = −0.39). The outcome is discrimination between categories in inductive learning. The title's "transfer" means classifying new examples, not use in a new domain.
- [Backward fading and principle prompts supported transfer in probability learning](../claims/fading-and-principle-prompts-improve-probability-transfer.md) [+M]: a 2×2 `randomized` experiment with 78 university students. Backward fading (against example–problem pairs) and prompts to identify the principle (against none) each improved near and far transfer on study-defined probability tasks, with no interaction. It supports making the principle explicit during example study. "Far" here means a probability task the study defined as far, not a different domain.
- [Encoding variability across varied example contexts produces decontextualization supporting transfer (review reports DiVesta and Peverly)](../claims/encoding-variability-decontextualization-transfer.md) [+W]: a 1986 review reports an experiment that varied the context in which concepts were learned and measured recognition of concept instances in a new context. It is the closest the wiki comes to testing this page's own relationship, and it is second-hand, with no numbers recorded.
- [Task rehearsal improves fluency and complexity on the repeated task but does not transfer to a new task of the same type](../claims/task-repetition-fluency-complexity-same-task-only.md) [~M]: a review reports Bygate (2001): repeating a language task improved fluency and complexity on that task and not on another task of the same type. No effect sizes. It limits the alternative of repeating one task.
- [All learners, regardless of condition, showed a floor effect on far-transfer problems, and instructional time did not differ between treatment groups](../claims/far-transfer-floor-effect-and-equal-time.md) [~M] and [Test-score increases occurred only for identical and near-transfer problems; no increases were found for far-transfer problems in any of the three conditions](../claims/germane-load-gains-limited-to-identical-and-near-transfer-problems.md) [~W]: in a 51-participant computer-based electrical-engineering study, far-transfer means were about 1 of 12 in all four conditions. In a three-condition experiment on a game task (Reversi), no condition raised far-transfer scores (t(20) < 1, n.s.; equivalence not tested). These are the limiting claims: within single studies, the designs that moved near transfer did not move far transfer at all.
- [The closer training content is to authentic clinical scenarios, the smoother its translation into practice](../claims/contextualized-teaching-facilitates-transfer.md) [+W] and [Transfer of advanced competencies is limited by institutional case exposure, technical platforms, and multidisciplinary maturity rather than training deficiencies](../claims/advanced-competency-transfer-limited-by-institutional-resources.md) [~W]: interviews in one neurosurgical specialty-nurse programme. Trainees reported that case-based and simulated content close to real scenarios carried into practice more easily, and that advanced competencies often stayed unused where cases, equipment or teams were lacking. These describe perceived transfer to the job, not measured transfer, and they make the workplace a condition of the relationship.
- [A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W]: one training-design article (van Merriënboer, Kester & Paas, 2006). It argues that methods inducing germane load (high variability, limited guidance) during whole-task practice favour transfer, and that intrinsic load should be lowered early for novices. The earlier version of this page cited it at `[+S]` as though it had been tested. It is a proposal, and the marker is now `[+W]`.

Taken together: comparing provided cases, mixing confusable types, retrieval on application questions, and principle prompts during example study each have experimental or meta-analytic support for near transfer at short horizons, mostly with undergraduates. Varying contexts as such is supported only second-hand and by argument. Far transfer is not moved in the two studies that measured it separately.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims bearing on transfer that the wiki holds and this model does not use. None was cited by the earlier page; each label says what it is.

- [Learning varied tasks of the same type enables transfer to unencountered tasks of that type](../claims/task-variety-enables-transfer-same-task-type.md) [+W]: an assertion in Merrill's (2007) conceptual paper, with no empirical test. Cited in the default design only as argument.
- [Learning in multiple contexts and generalizing across them makes knowledge both specific and general, addressing why transfer is difficult](../claims/multiple-contexts-generalization-transfer.md) [+W]: a theoretical argument in Collins (1988), with no data. Cited in the default design only as argument.
- [Pretraining Improves Transfer](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M]: one `randomized` experiment (n = 93). A video naming a tool's parts before an immersive-VR lesson led to fewer errors on a real-life transfer task than the same lesson without it. It bears on securing the original learning for novices before transfer (step 1 of the default design), not on varied contexts.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually unaided performance on a new case of a stated kind (near or far), with stated aids, at a stated delay. Elicit separately what the learner wants to be able to do and where: pass the course test, handle a situation at work next month, or use the idea in a project of their own. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the test may resist varied cases that slow them down on the taught items. A learner with a specific target situation is the best source of the late-stage cases.

For example, a nurse on a specialty course may want to manage one kind of patient on their own ward, while the designer's objective is reasoning across a family of neurosurgical cases. Use the learner's ward cases as the late transfer items, and record use on the ward separately from scores on course cases, so that success in the course is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if first-hand comparisons of varied-context with single-context instruction, with comparable learners and transfer items scored separately from taught items, show no near-transfer advantage, or one that disappears at a week. If comparison and principle prompts help only when the cases are given side by side, and not when learners must retrieve an earlier case, the bridging step should be dropped or redesigned. If studies that measure far transfer go on finding floors in every condition, the page should stop presenting far transfer as an objective that instructional variation alone can reach, and point to teaching in the target domain. If whole-task and part-task practice are compared on transfer and part-task does as well for novices, step 7 changes. Do not protect the model by calling a failed transfer test "too far" after the fact. Say how far the item was before it is given.

Recall of the taught items, near transfer, far transfer, delayed transfer and use in the learner's own setting are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of cases, no session dose and no criterion for moving on.

## Description
Transfer of learning is the principle of helping learners apply what they have learned in one context to new tasks, situations, or problems. It is useful when instruction aims beyond immediate performance toward later use and adaptation.

Mechanistically, learners store propositions (basic units of knowledge) in long-term memory; when new information enters working memory, long-term memory is searched for associations that combine with it, reinforcing the new information and helping assign it meaning. Learning that occurs across varying contexts creates more of these associative links and encourages generalization of the skill or knowledge (Schunk, 2004), building an associative network learners can draw on for future problem-solving (Souza, 2016). Information in memory is, in this sense, "flexible, interpretive, generically altered, and its recall and transfer are largely context-dependent" (Helfenstein, 2005).

### Transfer Taxonomy
Transfer takes several distinct forms (Schunk, 2004):

| Type | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Positive | Prior learning assists new learning |
| Negative | Prior learning hinders or interferes with new learning |
| Zero | Prior learning has no influence on new learning |
| Near | Many elements overlap between the original learning conditions and the new situation |
| Far | The new situation is very different from the one in which learning occurred |
| Literal | The skill is performed exactly as learned, in a new situation |
| Figural | General knowledge is applied to a new situation, often via analogy or metaphor |
| Low-road | Well-established skills transfer spontaneously, even automatically |
| High-road | The learner consciously and deliberately ("mindfully") evaluates the new situation and applies prior learning to it |
| Forward-reaching | High-road transfer in which learners think about possible other uses *while* learning |
| Backward-reaching | High-road transfer in which learners, in a new situation, think back to previous situations that might apply |

### Factors Affecting Transfer
Souza (2016) identifies four factors that shape how well transfer occurs: **context and degree of original learning** (how well the learner acquired the knowledge in the first place), **similarity** (commonalities between the original learning and the new situation, including environmental and memory cues), **critical attributes** (the characteristics that make something unique and thus recognizable across contexts), and **association** (connections among multiple events, actions, and pieces of information, including the conditions and emotions the learner attaches to them). Learners increase transfer through effective practice and by mindfully **abstracting** knowledge — examining experience for similarities via methods such as seeking underlying principles, building models, and identifying analogies and metaphors.

Transfer is less a deliberate act by the learner than a product of the learning environment; teachers, as part of that environment, are themselves an instrument of transfer, for better or worse (Souza, 2016). See [Hugging and Bridging for Transfer](../strategies/hugging-and-bridging-for-transfer.md) for two concrete teacher techniques (building situational similarity, and building abstraction/connection) aimed directly at promoting positive transfer.

## Implications
Transfer of learning improves when learners encounter underlying structure across varied contexts rather than mastering only one narrow version of a task. [A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) supports keeping the integrated logic of performance visible when the goal is later adaptation rather than isolated success. Whole-task performance, comparison across examples, and prompts that make general principles explicit are especially useful because they help learners see what should carry over when surface details change. The practical implication is to design for variation and abstraction, not just successful completion in the original setting.

- **Transfer is weak when learning stays tied to one narrow context**

## Examples
- After learning a proportional reasoning strategy in recipes, learners apply the same structure to map scales, probability tables, and budgeting scenarios.
- A nursing program asks learners to practice the same clinical reasoning routine across different patient cases so they generalize decision patterns rather than memorize one script.
