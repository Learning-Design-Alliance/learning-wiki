# Curricular Knowledge as a Resource for Responsiveness

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Responsive teaching is often framed as being in tension with structured, research-based curricula — as if following a designed sequence of activities necessarily limits a teacher's ability to attend and build on student thinking in the moment. This principle proposes an additional component of curricular knowledge, beyond knowing what materials exist: knowledge of *why* a curriculum's specific activities and question sequences are designed the way they are — what misconception each section anticipates, what conceptual tool each activity builds, and how sections relate to one another. Teachers who develop this knowledge can use a structured curriculum as a responsive partner rather than a script: deciding when to intervene or hold back, which sections to prioritize under time pressure, how to explain a stuck point using the curriculum's own designed logic, and when to deviate from the sequence because a student's actual thinking doesn't match what the curriculum anticipated.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A research-based curriculum with an identifiable underlying design logic (recurring structures such as elicit-confront-resolve sequences, or a hierarchy of "genesis," "tool," and reinforcement exercises)
- Deliberate professional preparation time devoted to analyzing *why* activities are sequenced as they are, not just what content they cover
- Integration of formal knowledge (curriculum designers' research on common student difficulties) with the teacher's own lived experience as a learner and instructor of the material
#### Constraints
- The instructional moves this knowledge enables (referring students to another section, choosing not to intervene, deviating from the sequence) are small-scale, curriculum-embedded adaptations — they do not by themselves constitute the most flexible, curriculum-independent forms of responsive teaching
- Developing this knowledge took an extended period (documented over roughly 30 weeks) and required sustained formal-lived concept integration, not a single training session

### Target Learners
- Novice or early-career instructors (the source study used undergraduate Learning Assistants) working within a structured, research-validated curriculum

### Target Learning Objectives
- Enacting authentic partnership with a research-based curriculum: adapting it responsively to student thinking while remaining grounded in its designed logic, rather than either rigidly following it or abandoning it

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Responsive teaching (Ball 1993; Hammer 1997; Hammer et al. 2012) — attending to and building on the beginnings of disciplinary thinking in student talk
- Sociocultural theory (Vygotsky) — integration of formal (research-based) and lived (experiential) concepts as the mechanism of knowledge development
- Curriculum use as participation with the text (Remillard 2005) — contrasted with curriculum use as merely following or subverting a script

## Examples
- A Learning Assistant, knowing that a tutorial section was designed to elicit and later confront a specific misconception, chose not to intervene when a student expressed that misconception early — because the curriculum's own later sequence would surface and resolve it.
- A Learning Assistant invented an on-the-fly elicit-confront-resolve sequence (using overlapping transparent pages to test a student's incorrect prediction about wave interference) when a student's actual misconception didn't match what the printed curriculum anticipated.
