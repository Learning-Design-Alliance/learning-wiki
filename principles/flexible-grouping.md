# Flexible Grouping

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 11 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 11 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **who works with whom, for what purpose, for how long, and how that changes**. In a class whose learners differ on the current task (readiness on this skill, an error pattern, interest, language, or the role they can take), and where those differences shift from task to task, forming temporary groups for a stated purpose from recent evidence, and re-forming them when the evidence changes, is expected to give more learners the support or challenge they currently need, more chances to explain and hear explanations, and better individually measured achievement (`general-achievement`, `immediate-performance` on the task grouped for) than either one fixed whole-class format or fixed groups set by a general ability label. Two kinds of group do different jobs: short **similar-need groups** (learners who show the same gap or are ready for the same extension) for targeted teaching, and **mixed groups** (different strengths, languages or readiness) for discussion, projects and peer explanation.

The relationship is conditional on four things: the grouping evidence is recent and specific to the task, not a general ranking; the groups actually change, so that membership does not become a status label (quiet tracking); each group has a purpose, a task that needs the group and explicit success criteria; and every grouping cycle ends with each learner's own unaided check, so that support does not become a fixed placement.

**No claim in the wiki tests flexible grouping itself.** None compares flexible with fixed ability groups, or regrouped small-group teaching with whole-class teaching, and no ability-grouping or tracking study is recorded as a claim; the two studies in this page's Key Sources (Burris et al. 2006 on heterogeneous grouping in mathematics; Castle et al. 2005 on flexible grouping in a high-needs school) have no claim page yet. The claims below test neighbouring parts: support adjusted to each learner's responses (one-to-one), using formative evidence to adapt teaching, group rewards resting on each member's learning, learning by explaining and teaching, explicit goals, and fading support. The rest is a default design, labelled as proposals. The evidence on composition (similar-need versus mixed) recorded here is opinion and observation only.

How the converted siblings sit around this: [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) and [Social Interdependence](social-interdependence.md) hold how a mixed group's work is structured (interdependence and individual accountability) and what that does; this page uses that structure for its mixed groups and does not repeat it. [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) holds when joint work of any structure changes what each member can do. [Adaptive Learning](adaptive-learning.md) holds the general relationship of choosing each learner's next task from their responses; flexible grouping is a group-level form of it, run by a teacher, where the unit of adaptation is a small group rather than one learner. [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) holds how support inside a group is raised and withdrawn.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (purpose for each grouping, ongoing formative evidence, low-stigma movement, tasks that need the group, independent performance kept), kept as a concrete default and extended into an arc across sessions. Each step says what evidence, if any, stands behind it; numbers are untested proposals unless a claim is named.

1. **Name the purpose of each grouping before forming it.** One of: targeted support on a shown gap, extension for those already secure, discussion of a shared question, practice, peer teaching, or an interest or project group. Tell learners the purpose and the success criteria for the session ("by the end, each of you can solve two of these without the worked example"). Explicit, specific criteria: [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M] (a narrative review of goal-setting research, mostly adults at work; carried to group tasks). The purpose list is from the earlier page; untested.
2. **Form similar-need groups from a recent, task-specific check, not from a general label.** Use a short check on the skill in hand (an exit ticket or three to five items, from the last one or two sessions) and sort by what the responses show (the same error, the same missing step, ready to go further). Never form them from last year's placement, a reading level used across all subjects, or a diagnosis. Using elicited evidence to adapt teaching: [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] (K-12 meta-analyses of formative assessment, small average effects; they do not test grouping as the adaptation). The check size and age are untested proposals.
3. **Run a similar-need group short and contingent.** Three to six learners, 10 to 20 minutes, one gap per session; the teacher watches each attempt and gives more help after a failure and less after a success. [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (fourth and fifth graders, one-to-one tutoring of long division; the claim's own Discussion says genuine contingency is scarce with 30 students at once, which is the case for teaching a few at a time). Group size and length are untested proposals.
4. **Make mixed groups depend on every member's learning.** Three or four learners, distinct roles that rotate, a shared product plus a short individual check, and any group recognition computed from members' individual results, not from the product alone. [In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M] (elementary and secondary studies of four weeks or more; Slavin also reports structured-interaction methods that work without rewards). The structure itself is modelled in [Social Interdependence](social-interdependence.md).
5. **Make explaining the work of a mixed group.** Ask each member to explain a step, a reason or a prediction to another member, and rotate who explains, so the learner who already knows the answer explains instead of doing the task for others, and the learner who does not is asked to explain back. [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M] (meta-analyses: preparing to teach and then teaching improved the tutor's own knowledge; school tutoring programmes benefited tutors as well as tutees); [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M] (prompted self-explanation, mostly individual study; carried to explaining to a peer, which it did not test).
6. **Dissolve a support group as soon as its members succeed unaided.** A learner leaves a similar-need group after two consecutive unaided successes on the target (or a passed recheck), and goes to independent, mixed or extension work; do not keep learners who are already secure in a support group. [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~M] (a scaffolding review and three experiments fading worked examples, near transfer; carried from fading within a task to leaving a group); [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M] (cognitive-load studies; gives no crossover point). The two-success rule is an untested proposal.
7. **Keep movement low-stigma and keep a record that would show tracking.** Name groups by task or colour, never by level; let any learner join a support session by choice; vary formats so that no group meets only "the strugglers". Log each session's groups and the reason for each placement. Review the log every two weeks: if the same learners have been in the support group for three cycles in a row, or groups split along language, ethnicity, disability or gender, change the instruction or the grouping rather than the label. A teacher's account of why random grouping appealed (it "disrupted the institutional process of labeling kids") is in [Contrasting workshop practices (random vs. purposeful grouping) framed the problem of labeling students around local context and teacher agency](../claims/grouping-practices-framed-labeling-problem.md) [~W] (one qualitative case of teachers' talk; no learner outcomes). The review rule is an untested proposal.
8. **Assign groups when mixing is the purpose; let learners choose when interest is.** When the purpose is to bring together different languages or backgrounds, the teacher assigns groups, because self-chosen groups tend to sort by similarity. [Students segregated into language cliques whenever allowed to choose peer interactions, despite the 50-50 enrollment design](../claims/language-clique-segregation-peer-choice.md) [~W] (classroom observation in one dual-language school). Interest groups and project choices can be learner-chosen; untested.
9. **End every grouping cycle with individual work.** Each session or task that used groups ends with a short individual check, and the week keeps time for independent work, so that no learner's record rests on group output. [Cooperative learning has drawbacks including slow learners depending on fast achievers, so teachers should stress both cooperative and individual work](../claims/cooperative-learning-versus-individual-endeavors.md) [~W] (an opinion essay, no data); individual accountability as above [+M].
10. **Plan the arc across sessions, not only one lesson.** A default, all untested proposals:
    - *Weeks 1–2 (routines).* Whole-class lesson most of the time, plus one grouping format per lesson. Teach the moves explicitly: how to form a group in under two minutes, the role cards, the signal to regroup, what to do when finished. Form one similar-need group per lesson from the previous day's check; mixed groups use fixed roles the teacher assigns.
    - *Weeks 3–6 (rotation).* A typical 50–60 minute lesson: whole-class model or mini-lesson 10–15 minutes; groups 20–25 minutes (one similar-need group with the teacher while mixed groups practise or discuss with roles); individual check 5–10 minutes, which forms tomorrow's groups. Mixed groups keep membership for one unit or two to three weeks; similar-need groups last one to three sessions.
    - *Weeks 7 onwards (handover).* Learners read their own check results against the criteria and sign up for a support or extension session themselves, with the teacher adding anyone the check flags; interest and project groups become learner-chosen within the teacher's mixing rules; roles rotate without prompting.
    - A learner moves on (out of a support group, into extension) on two consecutive unaided successes; a whole-class reteach replaces small groups when more than about half the class shows the same gap.

When a condition is not met: if there is no recent task-specific evidence, run a short check before grouping or keep the lesson whole-class for that segment; if routines are weak and regrouping costs more than five minutes, use fewer, longer-lived groups until the moves are taught.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the grouping is for, and what evidence it rests on.** Targeted support needs similar-need groups formed from a recent check; discussion and projects need mixed groups with interdependence; neither should be formed from a general ranking. If unstated, ask: what should each group be able to do at the end of the session, and what did the last check on this task show?
- **How much facilitator attention there is.** The evidence for responsive support is one-to-one; with one teacher and many groups, or a volunteer for a few minutes, the support has to come from roles, materials, peers and a short individual check. If unstated, ask: how many adults, for how many minutes, and who sees each learner's work before the next session?
- **How the groups could become labels.** Fixed or visible "levels", self-chosen groups in a class split by language, and support groups that never empty all turn grouping into tracking. If unstated, ask: who decides membership, how often does it change, and what do learners and families see?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are `advanced` on this task, or most already pass the check | Skip the support group for them; form an extension group with a harder or wider task, or place them in mixed groups as explainers with an explaining role, not answer-givers | [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M] (cognitive-load studies, not grouping); [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M] (tutors' own gains) |
| Learners are `child` (primary age) | Fewer formats, taught as routines with visual role cards; similar-need groups with the teacher for 10–15 minutes; other groups do practice they can check themselves | [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (fourth and fifth graders, one-to-one); format and timing untested |
| Learners are `adult`, including adults in a work setting or adult basic education | Group by the task or job role in hand and by what learners say they need, not by test scores; let adults choose to join a support session; use mixed groups to pool workplace experience | untested proposal; the earlier page names adults, including those with learning disabilities and multilingual learners |
| Multilingual learners, or a dual-language class | Assign mixed-language groups for discussion and projects, with a role that needs each language; use short similar-language groups only for a stated language goal, then dissolve them | [Students segregated into language cliques whenever allowed to choose peer interactions, despite the 50-50 enrollment design](../claims/language-clique-segregation-peer-choice.md) [~W] (one dual-language school, observation); the design move is untested |
| Learners with disabilities, low confidence or low literacy | Keep them in mixed groups for shared tasks, with a role matched to a strength (reader, builder, timekeeper, presenter of a drawing); give targeted support in short similar-need sessions that others also join; check unaided work orally or by doing where literacy blocks writing | untested proposal; from the earlier page (participation alongside peers, strengths shown) |
| One teacher, many learners (30 or more), or a class of about 50 | One similar-need group with the teacher per segment while the rest work in mixed groups with roles and self-checkable tasks; rotate which group meets the teacher across the week; whole-class reteach when more than half share a gap | untested proposal; contingent support is shown one-to-one only ([Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]) |
| Scarce facilitator time: a volunteer, a busy shift, a tutor who checks in for a few minutes | Spend the minutes on the similar-need group the last check identified, not on circulating; give mixed groups a printed task card with roles, criteria and an answer check; collect one individual item from everyone for the next check | untested proposal |
| Mixed mode: some learners remote, some in the room; or phone-only | Do not split groups by location by default: put remote learners in mixed groups with a shared document and one in-room member who reads the work aloud, or make one remote group with its own facilitator; for phone-only learners, use text-based roles and a short checkable item at the end | untested proposal |
| `online-self-paced`, no live teacher | Replace live regrouping with branching: a short check routes each learner to a support module or an extension; asynchronous discussion groups are assigned by the course for mixing, and change each unit | untested here; individualised routing is modelled in [Adaptive Learning](adaptive-learning.md) |
| One session only | No regrouping cycle is possible: run a two-minute opening check, one similar-need group with the teacher for 15 minutes while others work in mixed pairs, and an individual exit item; do not claim regrouping effects | untested proposal |
| A fixed short time budget (for example 20 minutes a session) | Keep one grouping format per session: 3–5 minutes check or recap, 10–12 minutes in groups, 3–5 minutes individual check; regroup only between sessions | untested proposal |
| The brief names its own grouping routine, categories, criteria or rubric (its own station rotation, reading levels, competency list or check-in) | Keep that routine and its categories; change only what the evidence here says matters: groups formed from a recent check on the task, membership reviewed at a set interval, an individual check at the end | untested proposal; the formative-evidence step rests on [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] |
| Placement data are private or sensitive (diagnoses, test scores, immigration or family circumstances) | Form groups from task evidence only; never display levels or reasons; let the learner choose whether to tell peers why they are in a session; keep the placement log to the teacher | untested proposal |
| A whole-task performance or product for a real audience (a project, an exhibition, a client report) | Keep the whole task in one mixed group from start to finish; pull members out briefly into similar-need sessions for a component skill, then return them; assess each member's part and the whole | untested proposal; individual accountability as in [In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M] |
| High stakes or an unaided final assessment | Judge readiness only on individual unaided checks, never on group products; ensure support groups end with the same item types as the assessment | untested proposal |
| Teachers or parents resist mixed groups because high achievers "carry" others | Use explaining roles and individual checks so contribution is visible; show the tutor-gain evidence; keep some extension groups | [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]; the concern itself is in [Cooperative learning has drawbacks including slow learners depending on fast achievers, so teachers should stress both cooperative and individual work](../claims/cooperative-learning-versus-individual-endeavors.md) [~W] (opinion) |

Seven of the sixteen rows cite a claim, and none of those claims tests grouping as such: each carries a neighbouring finding (one-to-one tutoring, cognitive-load studies, tutoring and teaching meta-analyses, formative-assessment syntheses, cooperative-learning reviews, one school's observation) to grouping decisions and says so. The other nine rows are untested proposals.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: the check items and each learner's responses, who was placed in which group and why, how long each group ran, the roles taken, the talk (who explained, who asked, who was silent), each learner's individual check afterwards, and the group history over weeks. "Needs support", "is ready for extension", "is carried by the group" and "feels labelled" are inferences. A group product is an observation about the group, not about any member.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| The same learners return to the support group cycle after cycle | The instruction in the group is not addressing the gap; the gap is wider than the check showed (a prerequisite); the check items differ from what was taught; the group has become a placement | Give a check on the prerequisite; look at the attempts made in the group, not only the result; compare their progress on items taught in the group with items not taught there. |
| A learner refuses to join a support session, or performs worse after being placed | Stigma (the group is seen as "low"); the learner disagrees with the check; the session time clashes with something they value | Ask the learner privately; offer the same help as an open session anyone may join; compare with a matched item done alone. |
| A mixed group's product is good but one member fails the individual check | The work was done by others; the member understood in the group but cannot do it alone; the check measures something the task did not | Ask that member to explain one step of the group's product; give a parallel item with the group's notes available, then without. |
| A high-attaining learner in a mixed group is disengaged or doing everyone's work | The task does not need the group; there is no explaining role; the learner values finishing quickly | Change the role to explainer or checker with a rule that others write; give a group task that one person cannot finish alone. |
| Groups formed by choice split by language, friendship or gender | Comfort and shared language; status in the class; the task allows parallel work | Assign groups for the next mixing task and compare participation; record who speaks in each. |
| Regrouping takes most of the lesson | Routines not yet taught; too many formats; groups re-formed every segment | Time the moves; drop to one format per lesson until moves take under two minutes. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A check used to form groups is also practice; record each check and each placement so the history, not one score, guides the next decision.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares flexible grouping with fixed ability grouping or with whole-class teaching, or varies group composition with achievement measured. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]: fourth and fifth graders tutored one-to-one in long division under fully contingent support solved more problems than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition, a quasi-experiment); interactive tutoring of eighth graders gave similar immediate learning and better transfer; a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding. Its Discussion notes most evidence is one-to-one and contingency is scarce with whole classes. It supports teaching a few learners at a time when the aim is responsive support; it does not show that a similar-need group of five gets the one-to-one benefit.
- [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]: K-12 meta-analyses of formative assessment with control conditions find small positive effects (d = .29 across 33 US studies; about .20 across 13 studies, after a published correction), larger where students self-assess. It supports forming groups from elicited evidence; it does not isolate regrouping as the adaptation, and the feature estimates are comparisons across studies.
- [In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]: a best-evidence review of 99 school studies of four weeks or more (median +.32 with group rewards on individual learning against +.07 without), an elementary peer-assisted-learning meta-analysis (d = 0.33, larger with interdependent rewards), and a randomised component study with at-risk 4th and 5th graders. It governs how mixed groups are run; it does not test how they are composed or how often they change.
- [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]: across 28 studies, preparing to teach and then teaching improved the tutor's domain knowledge against studying without that expectation (g = 0.56; preparing alone g = 0.35), more when the teaching was interactive; school tutoring programmes benefited tutors as well as tutees. Abstracts only. It answers the worry that higher-attaining members lose out in mixed groups, when they explain; it does not compare tutor and tutee gains.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 64 reports (g = .55) and a mathematics meta-analysis, both of prompted self-explanation, mostly immediate tests. The earlier page cited it for peer explanation; it tests explaining to oneself, so it is carried, and the marker is lowered from the earlier `[+S]` for that reason.
- [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M]: one narrative review of goal-setting research. The earlier page marked it `[+S]`; one study at q2 caps it at M. It supports explicit success criteria for each group; it is not a study of groups or of learners in classrooms.
- [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~M]: a scaffolding review and three experiments in which fading worked examples into problems favoured near transfer. It is the reason a support group should end; it concerns support within a task, not membership of a group, and gives no timing.
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies and an experiment with electrical trainees. It is the reason not to hold secure learners in support groups; it gives no crossover point.

**Limiting and qualifying claims, all weak.** On composition and labelling the wiki holds only opinion and observation, and they point in different directions:

- [Heterogeneously grouped teams show more benefits than homogeneously formed teams](../claims/heterogeneous-teams-more-benefits-than-homogeneous.md) [+W]: one sentence in an introductory chapter citing earlier research; no study details (`q1`).
- [Homogeneous grouping for reading instruction and heterogeneous grouping for discussion groups each serve distinct purposes](../claims/mixed-homogeneous-heterogeneous-grouping.md) [~W]: an essay arguing from experience for both plans, against all-day heterogeneous grouping (`q1`). It matches this page's split between similar-need and mixed groups, and is no evidence for it.
- [Cooperative learning has drawbacks including slow learners depending on fast achievers, so teachers should stress both cooperative and individual work](../claims/cooperative-learning-versus-individual-endeavors.md) [~W]: the same essay; the reason the default design ends every cycle with individual work.
- [Students segregated into language cliques whenever allowed to choose peer interactions, despite the 50-50 enrollment design](../claims/language-clique-segregation-peer-choice.md) [~W]: observations in one dual-language school (`q2`, qualitative). It limits learner-chosen grouping when mixing is the goal.
- [Contrasting workshop practices (random vs. purposeful grouping) framed the problem of labeling students around local context and teacher agency](../claims/grouping-practices-framed-labeling-problem.md) [~W]: teachers' talk in one professional-development debrief, where one valued random grouping for disrupting the labelling of students amid unofficial tracking. It names the tracking risk; it measures no learner outcome.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these (one-to-one tutoring of children, K-12 syntheses, undergraduates and adults in reviews), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures belonging, motivation or stigma under different grouping policies, which the earlier page named as goals.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims bearing on group work that this page did not cite before, read only for their scope; they concern mixed-group work against individual or competitive work, not regrouping.

- [Collaborative Learning Improves Outcomes](../claims/collaborative-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M] — two meta-analyses (early adolescents; undergraduate STEM small-group learning, d = 0.51 against no group work); bears on using mixed groups at all, not on flexible composition. Owned by [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md).
- [Cooperative Learning Improves Achievement](../claims/cooperative-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] — two meta-analyses of cooperative against individualistic or competitive goal structures; bears on mixed groups' structure, not on regrouping. Owned by [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md).

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually individual unaided performance on the task grouped for, at a stated horizon, and in many settings also participation of every learner in shared work. Elicit separately what learners value: to work with friends, not to be seen as needing help, to get to the harder task, to use their own language, to finish quickly. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being seen in "the help group" may refuse support they need; one who values working with friends may prefer self-chosen groups that defeat a mixing goal.

For example, an adult learner in an English class at work may want to handle phone calls with customers, while the designer's objective for the week is past-tense forms. A similar-need group on past-tense errors serves the objective; a mixed group role-playing a customer call, with that learner in the caller role, tests what the learner came for. Record both, so that a passed grammar check is not read as the capability the learner values.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of regrouped small-group teaching with whole-class teaching, with comparable learners and individual outcomes, show no advantage; if flexible grouping in practice converges on stable groups that behave like ability tracks (the same learners in the same groups across a term), in which case "flexible" is a description of intent only; or if mixed groups with explaining roles show losses for higher-attaining learners on individual measures. If similar-need groups help only when the teacher can give near one-to-one attention, the similar-need step is a staffing decision, not a grouping one. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not flexible enough" after the fact.

Achievement on the grouped task, delayed retention, participation, belonging and the learner's own valued goal are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no group size, duration, regrouping interval or composition rule.

## Description
Flexible grouping is a classroom practice that temporarily places learners together in groups to work collaboratively toward specific learning goals or activities. These groups are adaptive, accommodating learners' evolving needs and interests based on instructor observations, formative assessments, and learner feedback. Encouraging learners to move flexibly between groups allows them to bring their own strengths to the class and provides opportunities to learn with and from each other, thereby strengthening social awareness and relationship skills. This practice supports all adults, including those with learning disabilities and multilingual learners, by allowing them to participate alongside their peers, showcase their strengths, and learn from others intentionally.

## Implications
Flexible grouping is most useful when learner need changes across tasks and over time. Regrouping can make responsive support [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M], peer explanation [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and targeted challenge easier than one fixed whole-class format, but only if groups remain genuinely fluid and tied to a clear purpose. The main design risk is quiet tracking: once groups start signaling status instead of current need, the principle breaks. Strong implementation therefore depends on low-stigma movement, explicit task goals [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S], and regular use of formative evidence to decide who should work together and why. Group-based support should also not become a fixed placement that prevents independent performance [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear instructional purpose for each grouping arrangement: support, challenge, discussion, practice, or peer teaching
- Ongoing formative information so group composition can change in response to learner needs rather than stay fixed
- Norms and routines that let learners move between groups without stigma or confusion
- Tasks that actually benefit from collaboration or differentiated grouping, rather than grouping for its own sake

#### Constraints
- Flexible grouping becomes harmful when it hardens into informal tracking or status labeling
- Constant reconfiguration can waste time or create social instability if routines are weak
- Group quality depends on task design, facilitation, and role clarity; simply placing learners together is not enough
- Learners may need explicit support to collaborate productively across differences in confidence, language, or preparation

### Target Learners
- Learners in heterogeneous classes where needs, strengths, and readiness vary across time and task
- Learners who benefit from occasional targeted support, peer explanation, or challenge groups
- Multilingual learners and learners with disabilities who should participate in shared learning rather than being isolated permanently
- Learners whose motivation and belonging improve when group membership is not fixed by perceived ability

### Target Learning Objectives
- Match support and challenge to learner need on a given task
- Increase opportunities for peer explanation, modeling, and collaborative problem solving
- Support differentiation without locking learners into static categories
- Build social flexibility by allowing learners to contribute different strengths across contexts

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Social learning theory supports learning through observation, modeling, and interaction with different peers
- Social constructivist perspectives support co-construction of understanding through discussion and collaborative work
- Situated and participatory perspectives support the idea that learners can occupy different roles and levels of contribution across tasks
- Differentiation frameworks support adapting groupings to real learner need rather than maintaining static ability groups

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Flexible grouping helps only when groups are genuinely fluid; stable “low” and “high” groups undermine the principle
- Some tasks are better done individually; grouping should serve the task, not replace deliberate task design
- Peer learning is strongest when learners are accountable for explanation and contribution, not when one learner simply carries the work
