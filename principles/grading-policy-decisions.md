# Grading Policy Decisions

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Assigning a grade is rarely a single, self-evident calculation — it bundles several genuinely separable policy decisions, each carrying real tradeoffs.

**Weighting assignments.** Teachers must decide, ideally before a grading period begins, how much each component (weekly quizzes, homework, performance assessments, class participation) contributes to the final grade. This decision itself communicates values to students and parents and shapes effort allocation — a homework component worth only 5 percent signals, accurately, that homework isn't worth much individual effort.

**Whether to include social skills or effort.** Elementary teachers are more likely than middle or high school teachers to fold some social-skills assessment (group cooperation, self-regulation) into a subject grade or report separately (Popham, 2005). Whether to weight effort or improvement is similarly contested: one approach lets students submit drafts and earn credit partly for the degree of improvement shown, but this systematically shortchanges already-high-performing students who have little room left to visibly improve, unless the teacher deliberately designs additional challenge for them. In practice, many teachers use "hodgepodge grading" — blending achievement, effort, growth, attitude, homework, and participation into one number — and a survey of over 8,500 middle and high school students in Virginia found this blended approach broadly supported by both teachers and students in practice (Cross & Frary, 1999), even though it makes any single grade harder to interpret as a pure measure of subject mastery.

**Absolute vs. relative grading.** In **absolute grading**, a grade reflects a fixed standard the teacher set in advance, independent of how the rest of the class performed — if every student meets the criterion for an A, every student gets an A; if none do, none do (Popham, 2005). In **relative grading** ("grading on the curve"), students are ranked against each other, and grades are assigned by rank regardless of the absolute level of performance — useful for compensating when an assessment turns out much easier or harder than intended, but carrying two real costs: an A in one class doesn't necessarily represent the same performance level as an A in another class (since the comparison is only ever within one class), and — more corrosively — Bishop (1999) argues that relative grading gives students a direct personal incentive to *discourage* their classmates from studying hard, since a serious classmate directly makes it harder for everyone else to earn a good relative grade.

**How grades are described.** Traditional letter grades (A-F) are convenient, simple, and easy to average, but reveal nothing about which specific objectives a student has or hasn't met (Linn & Miller, 2005). Pass-fail (or satisfactory/unsatisfactory) systems are simpler still and give students room to explore unfamiliar subjects without risking their overall record, but at the cost of even less information about actual performance — and function especially cleanly under a mastery-learning approach, where "pass" already means demonstrated mastery of every objective by definition. A third option — an explicit checklist of objectives, with each rated (e.g., Proficient/Partially Proficient/Needs Improvement) — communicates strengths and weaknesses far more precisely to students and parents, at the cost of becoming unwieldy if the objective list grows too long to take in at a glance.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Weighting decisions made and communicated before a grading period begins, not improvised as work comes in
- Clarity, stated to students, about which of these decisions (weighting, effort inclusion, absolute-vs-relative, description format) the class's grading actually reflects
#### Constraints
- Relative grading creates a structural incentive for students to want their peers to do worse, not better — a direct conflict with any classroom goal of peer support or collaboration
- Effort/improvement-based grading components can inadvertently penalize the students who most need continued challenge (those already performing well) unless the teacher deliberately designs harder follow-on work for them
- Coarser grade descriptions (letter grades, pass/fail) trade away diagnostic information for simplicity — a trade that is not free even when convenient

### Target Learners
- All students; relative grading's peer-discouragement effect is specifically a concern in classes intended to foster collaboration or peer support
- Already-high-performing students are specifically disadvantaged by grading schemes that weight improvement heavily without a plan for their continued challenge

### Target Learning Objectives
- A grade that accurately and legibly communicates the intended thing (mastery, relative standing, growth, or some deliberate blend) rather than an unexamined default that happens to be convenient to compute

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Social Interdependence](social-interdependence.md) [-M] — relative grading's built-in incentive against helping classmates runs directly counter to cooperative-learning goals; a class using both should recognize the tension rather than assume they coexist without friction
