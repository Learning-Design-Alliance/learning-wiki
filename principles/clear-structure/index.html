Clear Structure¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 32 claims (22 for, 10 mixed) · 35 studies (12 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 7 review, 5 qualitative, 4 theoretical), q1–q4 · 8 of 35 report an effect size · 23 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner meeting unfamiliar material without an organization of their own for it (a novice for that material, not for the domain), making the material's organization explicit may help them select its key elements and build a representation of how it fits together. Organization can be made explicit by signals (headings, previews, summaries, cues to critical elements), a leaner organizer placed before instruction, or a graphic whose structure mirrors the content. The best-supported version is narrow. In multimedia materials, adding signals is associated with a small-to-moderate gain on immediate-performance learning tests, compared with the same material without signals. In one classroom experiment on teaching concepts, a lesson that announced what was coming, linked each part to the last, focused on the key attributes and gave examples beat an unclear lesson with the same content on every achievement measure when the concept structure was complex, while for a simpler structure the difference was not significant (A clear lesson beat an unclear one for a complex concept structure [+M]; Clarity matters less for simpler structures [~M]). In expository text, signals may change what readers recall more than how much they recall. Whether a structure helps depends on four things: whether its organization matches the content and the task the learner will later face, the learner's prior knowledge (added signals harmed high-knowledge learners in one field study), how complex the material is, and the outcome measured. The linked claims do not establish effects at delayed-retention, far-transfer or course-level navigation horizons. There is no pattern page that instantiates this principle yet. Chunking, Sequencing and Cognitive Load Management are its nearest neighbours.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: how much the learners already know about this material, what the learners must do with it afterwards (recall it, explain how it fits together, or use it in a task with its own structure), and whether the structure is for the content or for finding one's way through a course. The claims cover signals and organizers within materials; none tests course-level agendas or progress markers.

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record what the learner does with this material under stated conditions, not a trait such as "disorganized" or "needs structure". Asking where they are in a sequence, what comes next, which element matters most or how two sections relate are different observations, and they are different again from recall or problem-solving scores. An organized representation is an inferred state. Free recall order, cued recall, a learner-drawn map, navigation errors and time spent searching are observations, and none of them measures that state directly. Confusion about logistics (what to do, where things are) and confusion about the content's structure (how ideas relate) can look alike and need different responses.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Recalls many isolated details but not how they relate No organization offered; organization offered but not noticed; material too complex for the time given; the test rewards details Ask for a sketch or ordering of the main ideas before and after adding signals, on the same material and test. Follows the given outline or organizer but cannot use the content in a later task The organizer's structure does not match the task's structure; the outline was followed without being understood; the task needs a different organization Probe with tasks whose structure matches and does not match the organizer, as close in content as possible.
Asks repeatedly what to do next or misses steps Sequence not visible; instructions dense or inconsistent; a working-memory or attention constraint; low engagement Change one presentation feature at a time (a visible agenda, a consistent format) and record whether the questions change. These rows are proposals, untested with learners. A single contrast shifts confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause. Exposure to the probe can itself change what the learner knows, so record every probe given.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Clear structure improves learning [+M]: the umbrella claim. A meta-analysis of 29 experiments (44 effect sizes, 2,726 participants) compared multimedia materials with and without signals and found signals associated with better learning (d = 0.38, small). Benefits were clearer in higher-quality studies that used a pretest and controlled for prior knowledge. The page's second entry qualifies the claim: in two experiments with college students reading expository text, organizational signals often did not increase how much was freely recalled, but shifted what was recalled. Both entries were read from abstracts, and the claim's own Discussion calls it an umbrella over several distinct manipulations. Not yet checked against its sources.
  • Signaling improves learning [+M]: an overview of meta-analyses lists signaling among the multimedia principles with the largest benefits, with a best estimate of g = 0.43 (k = 209) from the largest signaling meta-analysis, and the meta-analysis above is its second entry. The third entry limits it. In a quasi-experimental field study, eighth graders used a digital textbook. Signals linking text to pictures helped those with low prior knowledge and harmed those with high prior knowledge, with higher extraneous load. The overview itself found no consistent prior-knowledge moderation across principles. These are syntheses of media-design studies, mostly on immediate learning measures, and they do not cover lesson or course navigation. The judge has not settled the entries, which were read from abstracts or a preprint.
  • Advance organizers improve learning [~M]: two older meta-analyses found small average benefits. One reports a mean effect of 0.21 on learning across 110 effect sizes, larger for high-ability (0.23) than low-ability (0.13) learners, from unweighted effect sizes with no significance tests. The other found no special benefit for low-ability or low-knowledge learners. A narrative review argues organizers help only in appropriate situations with suitable outcome measures. This cuts against assuming that more upfront structure helps weaker learners most. One of its three entries has been checked against its source.
  • Mismatched graphic organizers increase extraneous load [~M]: two lab experiments with German university students. Recall was more accurate when the format of the recall graph matched the format studied (η² = .03–.04), and cueing an incongruent perspective degraded recall (d = 0.52–0.54). Recall also favoured whichever perspective dominated the content's theme, whatever format had been studied. The claim's title says more than its single study shows: a format mismatch has a modest cost, which content relevance can override. Not yet checked against its sources.
  • A hierarchical organization helps only when matched to the task domain [~M]: 20 advanced high-school physics students were blocked by ability and randomly assigned to an organization with deductive or historical information at the higher levels. Two weeks later, each group did better on tasks matching its organization. It is one small study with no effect size printed, and it is not yet checked against its sources. It bears directly on the matching condition: no organization is clear in general, only for a task.
  • A clear variable coordinate lesson produced higher achievement than an unclear lesson with the same concept structure [+M]: a 2x3 factorial classroom experiment (Snyder et al., 1991) crossed lesson clarity with concept structure. For the variable coordinate structure the clear lesson beat the unclear one on definition, example identification, key words, nonexamples and application; the claim reports significance levels but no printed effect size. For the constant successive structure the clear lesson was descriptively better on every measure with no significant difference, and equivalence was not tested (Clarity matters less for simpler structures [~M]). In the same study the counts of clarity moves (keys, links, frames, focusing moves, examples) correlated with application and definition scores, from r(57) = .25 to .58 (Each positive clarity move correlates with every concept achievement measure [+W]); these are correlations and do not separate the moves from one another. It is one study, a test of whole-lesson clarity rather than of organization alone, and it bears on the complexity condition above.
  • The change-blindness claims from Mutlu-Bayraktar and Bayram (2018), the change on the verbally described object was the most detected [+W], central-object change was detected at a low rate without cueing [+W], the arrow-cued object drew attention from an equally signalled one [+W], fewer objects made changes easier to detect [+W] and signalling reduced detection elsewhere [~W], all rest on one eye-tracking study reporting how many of a dozen participants detected each change. They show where signals and cues draw attention, not what is learned, and the claims do not report a comparison between cued and uncued versions of the same scene.

Keep the treatment and comparator when carrying these findings over: signals versus the same material without them, and organizer versus no organizer or a different one. Also keep the outcome (mostly immediate learning or recall tests) and the learners (mostly university students, some school students). None of these claims tests course-level agendas, consistent interface conventions or visible progress markers. The relationship as this page used to state it, that visible organization of a lesson or course reduces confusion, has no direct claim in the wiki.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner is trying to get from the material, separately from what the designer wants them to retain. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. A structure that serves the designer's map of the content may not serve the route the learner needs through it. Choose the outcome before choosing the structure: finding one's way through a course, recalling the main ideas, explaining how they relate and using them in a task each call for different evidence.

For example, a returning adult learner in an online-self-paced course may value finding quickly what is due this week and what counts as done. The designer may want them to understand how the unit's concepts depend on one another. A module map with weekly checklists can serve the first aim while doing nothing for the second. A concept-level organizer can serve the second without answering the first. Record both aims, and test each against its own observation: navigation errors and questions about logistics for the first, a sketch or explanation of how the concepts relate for the second.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if signals or organizers on comparable material fail to improve aligned outcomes for learners without prior knowledge of it, in comparisons that control for prior knowledge. If the gains turn out to come from added time or repetition rather than organization, revise the explanation. It should be narrowed further if more studies like the field study show added structure harming learners who already know the material. If studies of course-level structure (agendas, consistent formats, progress markers) are added and show no effect on learning beyond reducing logistical questions, keep orientation and learning as separate claims. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as poor implementation, "too much structure" or low motivation.

A gain on an immediate test, a change in what is recalled, delayed retention, transfer to a task with a different structure, and ease of navigation are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal amount of structure, a fading rule as expertise grows, or effects at course scale.

Related Principles¶

  • Cognitive Load Management — clear structure is often one mechanism for managing extraneous load
  • Sequencing — structure and sequencing usually work together to make instruction legible
  • Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax — wording clarity and structural clarity are mutually reinforcing
  • Chunking — one of the main mechanisms for clear presentation
  • Scaffolding and Fading — early structure should support learners until they can navigate more independently
  • Multimodal Instruction — multiple modes can clarify structure when they are coherent rather than cluttered
  • Signaling Principle

Examples¶

  • Module overviews, lesson roadmaps, worked agendas, and slide decks with consistent signaling
  • Flipped Classroom — especially dependent on clear structure because pre-class and in-class phases must align visibly
  • State Objectives and Advance Organizers are concrete ways to implement this principle
  • Numbered procedures, exemplars, and checklists help make task structure inspectable for novices
  • Increase access to digital learning so materials are organized and accessible online
  • Use a three-part process-understanding-product rubric for formative and summative maker assessment
  • Use student-owned portfolios with digital and non-digital documentation to assess maker growth over time

Illustrative¶

Clear Structure & Presentation — A lesson or course makes goals, agenda, transitions, and expectations explicit so learners know how to move through it.

Consistent lesson architecture — Each session follows a stable pattern such as preview, model, guided practice, independent application, and reflection, reducing orientation overhead.

Visible unit maps and checklists — Learners can see how tasks connect and what completion looks like, which improves navigation and self-monitoring.
