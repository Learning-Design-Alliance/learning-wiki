# Minimize student frustration by making feedback clear, legible, focused, and transparent

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 18 claims (7 for, 11 mixed) · 19 studies (6 review, 4 causal, 3 qualitative, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 design, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 19 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **how teacher (or tool) comments on a learner's work are written and delivered so that the learner can read them, understand them and act on them**: their wording, legibility, number and focus, and whether the learner has been told how the teacher's feedback works. For a learner receiving comments on a draft or a performance (most often a student writer working on a `complex-skill` product, including second-language writers), comments written as complete sentences or detailed phrases that name the feature, where it occurs and what to try, that can be read without effort, that are limited to a few priorities for this draft, and that follow a known routine the learner has had explained, are expected to be understood and used in the next draft (`immediate-performance` on the revision) with less frustration and less loss of motivation (`motivation` as an outcome) than cryptic comments (bare symbols, abbreviations, single-word questions, vague remarks, grammar jargon), illegible handwriting, or commentary on every weakness at once.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's familiarity with the feedback routine and with the terms it uses, on the learner's writing level (detailed automated feedback is reported to overwhelm some students and to suit `advanced` writers best), on how many sources of feedback reach the learner at once, and on whether the next draft actually uses the comments. **No claim in the wiki tests this relationship directly**: none compares clear with cryptic comments, focused with exhaustive commentary, or explained with unexplained feedback routines, on learners' frustration or on their revisions. The nearest evidence is of three kinds: two meta-analyses showing that what feedback *says* matters (feedback about the task and the process outperforms praise or reinforcement, and more than a third of feedback interventions lower performance); one experiment in which learners rated a clearer, more structured feedback format as more specific, clearer and less effortful, without a learning advantage; and reports from one organisation's studies of automated writing-feedback tools that some students were overwhelmed by its feedback and needed guidance to use it. So the design below is a labelled default, not a tested model.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Explain your feedback routine before the first commented draft.** Give learners a one-page key (about 10 minutes in class, once): what you will comment on and what you will leave, what any symbol or code means, how comments are ordered, and what you expect learners to do with them. Walk through one sample paragraph with three comments on it. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (an information sheet explaining the teacher's method). A neighbouring package that trained feedback literacy raised a feedback-literacy questionnaire score ([Experimental-group students outperformed control-group students on post-test feedback literacy after CEA implementation](../claims/cea-improved-feedback-literacy.md) [+M], EFL speaking students, ChatGPT with e-portfolio assessment; carried to written comments).
2. **Write every comment as a full sentence or detailed phrase that names the feature, points to where it is, and says what to try.** "Your second paragraph gives two reasons but no example for either; add one example after each reason" rather than "?", "awk", "frag" or "vague". Use a grammar term only if it has been taught, and then gloss it once. Evidence for the *content*: feedback that carries task and process information has larger effects than reinforcement or praise ([Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S], two meta-analyses, the education one coding information content rather than levels). Evidence for the *wording* is perceptual only: learners rated a clearer, more specific and simpler feedback format higher, with lower mental effort and higher satisfaction ([AI multimodal feedback is rated higher than educator feedback on specificity, clarity, simplicity, motivation, satisfaction, and lower mental effort, with comparable correctness, trust, and acceptance](../claims/ai-feedback-higher-perceived-quality-value.md) [+M]).
3. **Limit each round to two or three priorities for this draft, plus one specific strength.** Choose them from what the task targets now; mark a recurring error once with its other locations ("the same tense shift appears in lines 4, 9 and 15") rather than marking every instance; leave lower-priority problems for a later draft. The numbers are untested proposals; from the earlier page (prioritize a few features rather than commenting on every weakness). Basis for the risk of volume: some students were overwhelmed by a tool's detailed feedback ([More than half of surveyed teachers agreed Ecree was useful for improving students' writing, but some students may be overwhelmed by the feedback](../claims/teachers-found-ecree-useful-but-some-students-overwhelmed.md) [~W]).
4. **Make every comment legible.** Type comments, or write in print large enough to read without guessing; put each comment next to the text it refers to. Untested proposal; from the earlier page.
5. **Lead with a short summary of what to do next.** Above the detailed comments, write one to three actions for the next draft, in order. When comments come from several sources (teacher, peers, a tool), reconcile them in that summary and say which to act on where they disagree. Untested proposal. A synthesis of peer comments is predicted, not shown, to reduce overload ([The Synthesizer is expected to reduce perceived overload and support affect relative to unstructured comment lists](../claims/synthesizer-expected-reduce-overload-support-affect.md) [+W], a theoretical expectation); disagreeing sources on comparable scales confused tutors and made them distrust the more critical one ([Discrepancies between lenient learner feedback and more critical automated feedback caused tutors confusion, distrust, and demands for rationale](../claims/feedback-source-discrepancies-cause-confusion.md) [~W], tutors on one platform, carried to students).
6. **Check that the comment was understood, in two minutes, before revision starts.** Ask each learner to write, under the summary, the one change they will make first, in their own words, and to circle any comment they cannot act on; answer circled comments before the revision session ends. Untested proposal. Whether the change then appears in the next draft is the loop that [Feedback Loops](feedback-loops.md) holds.
7. **Introduce an automated feedback tool with guided instruction, and filter it for less advanced writers.** Show learners which parts of the tool's output to read first and which to ignore for this draft, and for writers who are still developing, select the few items that match the current priorities. Basis: [Students need clear guidance so they are not overwhelmed by Ecree's feedback, which may be most useful for advanced writers](../claims/students-need-guidance-ecree-feedback-advanced-writers.md) [~W] and [Some students struggled to use Ecree's and MI Write's automated feedback](../claims/some-students-struggled-automated-feedback-use.md) [~W] (grade 8–11 English classes; the research organisation's studies of two tools). Do not add explanation layers learners do not need: some students found extra AI functionalities redundant when the feedback was already clear ([Student perceptions of GenAI feedback functionalities diverged, with some finding them redundant when feedback was perceived as clear](../claims/divergent-perceptions-genai-feedback-redundancy.md) [~W]).
8. **Hand over judgement across a term.** A default arc, untested in every quantity:
   - *First two or three commented drafts:* full-sentence comments only, two or three priorities, a written next-action summary from the teacher, the key from step 1 on the desk.
   - *Middle drafts:* introduce any shorthand code one at a time, only after it has been explained with an example and added to the key; the learner writes the next-action summary from the teacher's comments and the teacher checks it.
   - *Late drafts:* before receiving comments, the learner names the one or two features they want feedback on; the teacher answers those first and adds at most one more priority.
   - *Moving on:* a learner moves to the next stage when, on two consecutive drafts, their revision acts on the previous comments without asking what any of them meant. Train peer reviewers to write comments in the same form before they give feedback (a short criterion-referenced training unit is predicted to make peer comments more specific: [The Trainer is expected to make peer comments more specific, criterion-referenced, and evidence-based](../claims/trainer-expected-improve-comment-quality.md) [+W], a theoretical expectation).

When a condition is not met: if there is no time to explain the routine before the first draft, put the key at the top of the first commented draft and limit that draft to two comments; if comments cannot be typed, keep them fewer and in print.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the learner already knows the feedback routine and its terms.** A learner who has never seen your symbols, abbreviations or grammar terms cannot act on them, however accurate they are. If unstated, ask: have these learners had comments from this teacher or tool before, and could they say what each mark means?
- **The learner's writing level and confidence.** Detailed, comprehensive feedback is reported to overwhelm some students and to suit advanced writers best; a learner who is still developing needs fewer, ordered priorities. If unstated, ask: on the last piece of work, did this learner act on most comments, a few, or none, and why?
- **How many sources of feedback reach the learner at once, and in what form.** Teacher comments, peer comments and a tool's output on the same draft can disagree or simply pile up. If unstated, ask: who else comments on this draft, through what channel, and does anyone pull it together?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: which comments the learner acted on in the next draft, which they ignored, which they asked about or circled, the time between receiving comments and starting to revise, what they wrote as their first change, and what they said or wrote about the comments. "Frustrated", "overwhelmed", "does not understand the feedback" and "is not motivated to revise" are inferred states. An unrevised draft can come from any of them.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| The next draft changes nothing a comment pointed to | The comments were not understood; there were too many to choose from; the learner understood but did not know how to make the change; the learner disagreed | Ask the learner to say what one comment asks for, and to show the change on one sentence with you; compare a draft returned with two comments with one returned with all. |
| Fixes surface errors, ignores the content comments | Surface fixes are easier; the content comments were vague; the learner thinks the grade rests on surface errors | Give one content comment as a full sentence with an example and no surface marks; see whether it is acted on. |
| Asks "what does this mean?" about several marks | The code or term was never taught; handwriting is illegible; the comment is a single word or a "?" | Check the comment against the key; ask the learner to read the comment aloud. |
| Visibly upset or stops revising after reading comments | Volume; tone read as a judgement of the person; the learner's investment in the piece; a source disagreement left them unsure what to do | Ask which comment they would act on first; return a draft with the summary only and see whether they start. |
| Acts on peer or tool comments and ignores the teacher's, or the reverse | Trust in one source; the more lenient source felt fairer; one source was clearer | Ask which source they believed and why; reconcile the two in one summary and compare uptake. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Asking about a comment also explains it, so record each probe, and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares clear with cryptic comments, focused with exhaustive commentary, legible with illegible comments, or an explained with an unexplained feedback routine. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

- [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses. Across 435 educational studies, high-information feedback (task, process, sometimes self-regulation) had a very large effect and simple reinforcement or punishment a small one; across 607 effect sizes of feedback interventions in all settings, effectiveness declined as feedback drew attention toward the self. It supports writing comments that tell the learner what to change about the work. It says nothing about wording, legibility or the number of comments, and the education meta-analysis coded information content rather than the four levels.
- [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [~S]: the same two meta-analyses, read for their limit. Feedback does not help by default; more than a third of interventions lowered performance. It is the reason this page treats badly delivered feedback as a risk, not a lost opportunity. Its `## Studies bearing on this claim` table counts 7 distinct studies across it and 7 narrower claims.
- [AI multimodal feedback is rated higher than educator feedback on specificity, clarity, simplicity, motivation, satisfaction, and lower mental effort, with comparable correctness, trust, and acceptance](../claims/ai-feedback-higher-perceived-quality-value.md) [+M]: one experiment (81 baseline against 99 AI post-survey responses): learners rated AI-facilitated multimodal feedback clearer, more specific and simpler than the original educator feedback, reported lower mental effort and higher satisfaction and motivation, and rated correctness, trust and acceptance no differently. No effect sizes are printed. The comparison confounds format with source, the outcomes are ratings, and the source's title reports equivalent learning, so it supports "clearer feedback is experienced as easier", not "clearer feedback improves revisions".
- [More than half of surveyed teachers agreed Ecree was useful for improving students' writing, but some students may be overwhelmed by the feedback](../claims/teachers-found-ecree-useful-but-some-students-overwhelmed.md) [~W], [Some students may be overwhelmed by Ecree's feedback about their writing](../claims/teachers-found-ecree-useful-but-some-students-overwhelmed.md) [~W], [Students need clear guidance so they are not overwhelmed by Ecree's feedback, which may be most useful for advanced writers](../claims/students-need-guidance-ecree-feedback-advanced-writers.md) [~W] and [Some students struggled to use Ecree's and MI Write's automated feedback](../claims/some-students-struggled-automated-feedback-use.md) [~W]: briefs from one research organisation's 2021–22 study of Ecree in grade 8–11 English classes (the last synthesises that study with its study of MI Write). They report, with hedges and no proportions, that some students were overwhelmed by the volume of feedback, that clear guidance is needed, and that the tool may suit advanced writers best. Treat them as one or two studies reported several times, not four confirmations; two are flagged on their pages as possible duplicates of each other.
- [The Synthesizer is expected to reduce perceived overload and support affect relative to unstructured comment lists](../claims/synthesizer-expected-reduce-overload-support-affect.md) [+W]: a theoretical expectation in an article proposing a GenAI tool that condenses peer comments for EFL speaking classes; no test is reported. It is a prediction that a summary reduces overload, not evidence that it does.
- [Discrepancies between lenient learner feedback and more critical automated feedback caused tutors confusion, distrust, and demands for rationale](../claims/feedback-source-discrepancies-cause-confusion.md) [~W]: thematic analysis of open-ended responses from tutors on a language-tutoring platform. Disagreeing sources on comparable scales confused the recipients and led them to distrust the more critical source. The recipients were tutors, not students.
- [Student perceptions of GenAI feedback functionalities diverged, with some finding them redundant when feedback was perceived as clear](../claims/divergent-perceptions-genai-feedback-redundancy.md) [~W]: 18 university students; extra explanation features were redundant to some when the feedback was already clear. It limits the model: more scaffolding around clear feedback is not always wanted.
- [The Trainer is expected to make peer comments more specific, criterion-referenced, and evidence-based](../claims/trainer-expected-improve-comment-quality.md) [+W]: a theoretical expectation that short training units before peer feedback will make peer comments more specific; no test reported.

The limiting evidence, in one line: feedback can lower performance (more than a third of interventions), clarity has been shown to change learners' ratings rather than their learning, and extra layers around clear feedback can be redundant.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a better next draft: the learner understands the comments and uses them, with the frustration that stops revision kept low. Ask separately what the learner wants from comments: a grade explained, reassurance that the piece is good, to know the one thing to fix, to be left alone with their own text, or (for a working adult) a document their manager will accept. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants reassurance may read two priorities as "it's bad"; one who wants to fix everything before a deadline may want the full list a developing writer is spared. Record the learner's answer and the number of comments that worked for them, so that "fewer, clearer comments" is a decision made with the learner, not for them.

For example, a second-language university student may want every grammar error marked because they are judged on accuracy elsewhere, while the teacher's objective for this draft is the argument. Give the two argument priorities in full sentences, and offer the grammar marks as a separate, keyed list to use after the argument is revised, so both goals are served and neither buries the other.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of full-sentence with coded or cryptic comments, or of two or three priorities with comprehensive commentary, on comparable learners and drafts, show no difference in which comments are acted on in the next draft or in later writing quality. If clearer formats change learners' ratings and never their revisions, the model is about satisfaction, not learning, and should say so. If developing writers act on comprehensive feedback as well as on a short list once they have been taught how to use it, the cap on comments is a matter of training, not of number. If an explained routine (a key, a walk-through) makes no difference to uptake beyond clear wording alone, drop step 1. Do not protect the model by calling every unrevised draft "too many comments" after the fact.

Learners' ratings of feedback, their uptake in the next draft, later writing quality and their motivation to keep writing are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no number of comments, no wording rule and no effect of an explained feedback routine on revisions.

## Description
Teachers should avoid responding in ways that cause frustration: unclear or cryptic comments such as confusing symbols, abbreviations, single-word questions, vague remarks, and grammar jargon; illegible handwriting; and overwhelming commentary on every weakness. Feedback is most useful when understood, so teachers should comment in complete sentences or detailed phrases that are clear, specific, and concrete, prioritize a few features of the text, and articulate their feedback philosophies and expectations to learners.
