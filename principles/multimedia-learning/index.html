Multimedia Learning¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 114 claims (50 for, 60 mixed, 4 against) · 87 studies (44 causal, 17 review, 14 quant-synthesis, 5 associational, 4 theoretical, 2 design, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 21 of 87 report an effect size · 105 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner who cannot yet describe the structure, process or spatial relation an explanation is about (a novice on that task, whatever their wider experience), studying the words together with a relevant picture that depicts what the words describe may produce a better learning test than studying the words alone. The relationship is bounded by what the picture carries, where it sits relative to the words, whether the same words arrive twice through competing channels, whether the presentation is system-paced or learner-paced, what the learner is asked to do while studying, the learner's prior knowledge of this content, and the outcome and horizon being assessed. An image added without a job can change nothing: two recorded experiments found no gain from an added image or graph. The evidence below is mostly from short lab lessons with adult (postsecondary) learners on principle-type knowledge (how something works), assessed soon after study; it does not establish delayed retention, transfer to use, or benefit for every kind of content. The pattern specifies a reusable design policy; its local diagnostic branches remain proposals.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

No claim in the wiki sets how long a segment should run, how many pictures a lesson needs, or when to withdraw support. The steps follow a claim where one is named and are labelled proposals elsewhere; the pattern gives the full sequence and observation policy, and the modality principle the narration steps.

  1. Give the picture a job. Use a single static diagram for how parts are arranged and an animation for how they move or change; for a list of verbal facts, a picture may add nothing. Basis: the animation-or-static row below.
  2. Configure it. Write labels into the picture at the part they name; narrate a system-paced explanation and keep printed words beside the picture on learner-paced pages; leave out images unrelated to the content. Basis: the placement, modality and coherence rows below.
  3. Dose (proposal). Segments of one to three minutes of narration, or one diagram with its paragraph, each followed by a pause or "next" and one "what happens if this part changes" question about the picture. No claim sets the length.
  4. Leave time to study the picture with the words. In one experiment the diagram helped only when learners read the lesson twice, not when they answered questions while reading (diagrams helped only in the read-twice condition [~M]).
  5. Across sessions, withdraw support as the learner explains unaided (proposal). Early sessions: name the parts first, short segments, explicit cues on when to pause and replay. Once a learner answers a new changed-condition question correctly without supports on two occasions, lengthen the segments, drop the pretraining, and move to application tasks, without the picture if the target requires it. Basis for the direction: segmentation helps less as prior knowledge grows [~M]; the criterion and the number of sessions are untested.
  6. Judge it on the brief's own test and horizon, preferably an explanation or transfer item rather than recall of the words.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: does the picture depict what the words explain (a process, a structure, a spatial relation), or only decorate it; who controls the pace (system-paced video or narration, or learner-paced pages and pauses); and how much the learners already know about this content. Most rows below rest on Mayer (2008), a review that summarises a series of short lab experiments, largely from one research group, with transfer tested soon after study; read its median effect sizes as that programme's results, not as independent replications.

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record what the learner did with the material under stated conditions: which representation they had, whether they could pause, replay or re-read, how words and picture were placed, and what they were asked to do afterwards. Recalling a sentence, labelling a diagram, explaining how one part acts on another, and predicting what happens when something changes are different performances. "Visual learner" or "low attention" are not observations, and a learner's stated preference for a format does not show which format helps them learn.

Building an integrated model of words and picture, extraneous load from search or duplication, and attention drawn away by decoration are explanatory hypotheses. Test scores, reported effort, time on a screen and where a learner looked are observations; none directly measures the model. Lower reported effort could reflect easier processing or disengagement.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Recalls the words but cannot explain how the parts relate No integrated model built; the picture did not show the relation; the test asked for recall and the learner answered at that level Ask for a prediction or a "what if this part changed" explanation with the picture available and then withdrawn; compare a version where the picture depicts the causal relation with one where it only shows the object.
Describes the picture but not the explanation's key steps Attention went to the visual and the words were not processed; the words were too fast to follow (system-paced narration); language access to the words is limited Offer the same words as re-readable text or with pause control and the same picture; check comprehension of the words alone in an accessible form.
Reports high effort and does no better with the picture than without Picture and words are placed apart, forcing search; the same words appear as narration and on-screen text over a visualization; the content is too much for one unsegmented pass Re-present one segment with labels placed on the picture and no duplicated text, or with learner-controlled pauses, and compare effort and the explanation produced. Vary one feature at a time.
Does well with or without the picture The learner already has the model (prior knowledge); the test is too easy or close to the words; the picture adds nothing for this content Give a harder changed-condition question before deciding; record prior knowledge of this content. For a learner who already knows it, extra support may be unnecessary or a cost.
Engages more with an illustrated version but learns no more Decorative or interesting-but-irrelevant images raise mood or interest without informing; they may also draw attention from the informative picture; the image raised the learner's confidence rather than their understanding Remove the decorative image and keep the informative one; compare explanation quality, not liking. Ask the learner to judge their understanding and then explain: with an image, undergraduates rated their ability to draw the process higher with no better test score [~M] (d = 0.32), so a confident judgment beside a picture is not evidence of learning.

These are proposals and have not been tested with learners as diagnostic branches. A single contrast does not identify a cause, and probing can itself change what the learner knows. Match demands across versions, repeat where feasible, and record what remains unresolved.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Words with pictures against words alone [+M]: two randomized lab experiments (randomized, n=48 and n=47 German university students) found a text-with-picture group scored higher on a learning-outcomes test than a text-only group, η²=.14 and .09 as printed on the claim page; the timing of the test is not reported there. The same page records a randomized 2×2×2 experiment on youth interrogation rights (adults n=207; youth aged 12–17 n=193) in which, once words were present, adding an animation gave no reliable further comprehension gain (d=−0.07, 95% CI [−0.62, 0.48] for adults). That second entry is not settled against its abstract; read the page before relying on either direction. Neither study predicts delayed retention or transfer, and a non-significant difference is not equivalence.
  • Spatially integrating text and diagrams [+M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 58 comparisons (n=2426) found integrated placement of mutually referring text and diagrams beat separated placement, g=0.63 as printed. Learners, outcomes and horizons are not broken down on the page; the benefit is reported as holding across the moderators examined. Not yet checked against its source. It supports placement, not the decision to add a picture.
  • Duplicated words [~S]: an experiment found removing text that duplicated narration improved learning from a diagram lesson (no effect size recorded), while a meta-analysis of 57 studies, mostly with postsecondary students, found spoken–written presentations did not differ from written-only ones and outperformed spoken-only ones, for low prior knowledge learners, system-paced material and picture-free material; key-term text beat verbatim text. Checked by the judge on the abstract. It qualifies a blanket "never duplicate" rule: the harm is recorded where text competes with a visualization, not for words alone.
  • Interesting but irrelevant additions [~M]: a meta-analysis (abstract only) found seductive details can hinder learning depending on image type, delivery format and other features; three experiments with 7th and 8th graders found decorative pictures neither helped nor harmed overall but weakened the benefit of instructional pictures, most for lower prior knowledge; a within-subjects e-learning study found a small cost (η²=.04) and, in a harder follow-up, harm only for students with low attentional inhibition. Not settled against its sources. It limits "add a picture" to pictures that inform.
  • Segmenting and prior knowledge [~M]: a randomized experiment with 75 Dutch secondary students studying narrated animated worked examples in probability found segmented examples more efficient (equal transfer, lower effort) for lower prior knowledge, with the advantage gone at higher prior knowledge; no interaction on transfer performance alone, and no effect size beyond regression coefficients. Not yet checked against its source. It is one study of one support, not a general expertise-reversal estimate for pictures.
  • Diagrams helped only in the read-twice condition [~M]: a randomized factorial experiment (N = 198 undergraduates, a posterior probability lesson) found a diagram × questioning interaction: with diagrams, students who read the lesson twice did better on the posttest than without, while those answering elaborative questions as they read gained nothing from the diagram. No effect size is printed (B = 3.11 in a mixed-effects model). In the same study text duplicated inside the diagram [~M] made no difference. It bounds the relationship by what else the learner is asked to do while studying.
  • Instructional images and test scores [-M]: Jaeger and Fiorella's online experiments (163 and 48 undergraduates, biology texts) found adding an instructional image did not change test performance (d = 0.01, 0.06) or the accuracy of learners' judgments of their understanding; with the image, judgments of being able to draw the process were higher [~M] (d = 0.32). With graphs added to vector-calculus problems [-M], 36 engineering-physics students did not answer more correctly (56% against 52%). These are the recorded cases against the default; both used pictures whose job relative to the test is not stated on the claim pages.
  • Related decorative pictures [~M]: in Scherer et al.'s two randomized experiments, pictures related to the topic but carrying no needed information raised knowledge scores for university students (ηp² = .075) and monitoring accuracy on knowledge items for eighth-graders [~M], with no effect on transfer in either [~M]. It narrows the coherence rule: the harm is recorded for irrelevant additions, not for every picture without an explanatory job.

Retain learners, assignment, materials, pacing, comparator, instrument and horizon when transporting these observations. Do not rank them by effect label: an η² from a two-group lab study, a pooled g from a meta-analysis and a regression β answer different questions.

The evidence by question¶

Each question below links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it into one table, one row per distinct study, with each study's design and effect as recorded and no averaged verdict (eval/synthesis/multimedia-learning.json, scripts/synthesize_claim.py). The count is of distinct studies across the claim and the narrower claims that bear on it.

  • Do words with a relevant picture beat words alone? Multimedia Principle Improves Learning [+S]: 8 distinct studies.
  • How much do the design principles add, and when do they matter most? Multimedia design principles improve learning by g = 0.38 on average across the 11 largest reviews, an effect the authors call moderate, with large differences between principles [~M]: 2 distinct studies.
  • Should words sit next to, and arrive with, the picture they describe? Split Attention Effect Degrades Learning [+M]: 5 distinct studies.
  • Should the words be spoken or printed? Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning [~S]: 7 distinct studies.
  • Does presenting the same words twice (spoken and written) hurt or help, and for whom? On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions [~S]: 7 distinct studies.
  • Should interesting but unneeded material be cut? Coherence Principle Irrelevant Material Hurts Learning [+S]: 10 distinct studies.
  • Should continuous presentations be segmented, and does learner control of pace help? Segmenting multimedia instruction into learner-paced segments improves retention and transfer performance with small-to-medium effects [+M]: 4 distinct studies.
  • Animation or a static picture? Animation beats static pictures for identifying motions [+M] against a single picture beats animation for spatial arrangements [~M] (one experiment), with a small overall animation advantage [~M] (one meta-analysis). Not yet gathered into a synthesis table.
  • Does an added picture help when it has no stated job? Instructional visuals did not affect test performance [-M] and related decorative pictures did not affect transfer [~M]: two studies, not yet gathered into a synthesis table.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner wants to be able to do with the material and why, separately from the designer's objective. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations; do not infer that a learner values understanding because they watched to the end, or that they prefer video because they clicked it. Choose a representative task, criterion, permitted aids (can the diagram be consulted at test?) and horizon. Recalling the explanation, explaining a mechanism, troubleshooting a real device and passing a recognition quiz require different evidence.

For example, an apprentice may want to find out why a pump in their own workplace keeps failing, while the designer's objective is a module quiz on the pump cycle's stages. An immediate stage-naming quiz cannot show whether the apprentice can reason about a fault. The pattern shows how to keep both aims and test the capability that matters.

What would revise this model?¶

The bounded words-with-pictures expectation should weaken if comparable learners and aligned outcomes show no advantage of informative, integrated pictures over words alone under defensible comparisons, or if the advantage seen on an immediate test is absent at a delayed one. If placement, pacing or language access explain a learner's difficulty better than the presence of a picture, revise the local interpretation rather than adding media. If duplicated text helps the learners in front of you (for example where words alone are hard to follow), keep that observation rather than deleting text by rule. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as a decorative picture or low prior knowledge after the fact.

A learning-phase preference, immediate recall, explanation of a mechanism, delayed retention and use on a real task are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish a universal best format, an optimal segment length, or a forecast for an individual learner.

Claims¶

  • Representational pictures show consistently null effects for word problems with demanding characteristics such as rational numbers, probabilistic reasoning, or realistic considerations [~W]
  • Cognitive skills are the most probable student characteristic to modify picture effects: low math performers do better without representational pictures, low reading-comprehension students better with them [~W]
  • Affective variables and gender do not relate to picture effects on elementary schoolers' word problem-solving performance [~W]
  • Decorative pictures show consistently null effects on elementary schoolers' word problem-solving performance regardless of task characteristics [~W]
  • The mental integration task showed no general advantage of the dynamic over the static visual design in overall accuracy [~W]
  • All four original multimedia experiments showed a main effect favoring the dynamic visualization, especially for transfer tasks [+W]
  • Synced auditory and visual information in narrated presentations produces better learning than single-channel or out-of-sync presentation [+W]
  • Most EMME experiments that measured immediate learning scores reported at least one positive effect of gaze visualization on immediate learning [~W]
  • Meta-analytic effect sizes for cognitive-motivational techniques are substantial: multimedia d = 1.01 overall, elaborative feedback d = 0.71 recall, intrinsic integration d = 0.62 [+M]

Illustrative¶

Multimedia Learning — A lesson combines a labeled diagram, short narration, and a brief practice prompt so learners can connect process language to a visible structure.

Multimedia Learning — A reusable design pattern in which text, visuals, and interaction are coordinated intentionally rather than layered arbitrarily.

Flipped Learning — Pre-class micro-lessons often rely on multimedia principles: concise visuals, narrated explanation, and signaling that reduce learner confusion before live practice.
