Modality Principle¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 33 claims (9 for, 20 mixed, 4 against) · 26 studies (10 causal, 6 review, 5 theoretical, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q1–q3 · 8 of 26 report an effect size · 28 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision inside Multimedia Learning: once a lesson pairs words with a picture, should those words be spoken or printed? When the words explain a diagram, animation or scene that the learner has to look at while taking the words in, and the learner can follow the spoken language at the pace it is delivered, presenting the words as narration rather than as on-screen text is expected to produce better learning, typically measured on a transfer or comprehension test soon after the lesson. The usual explanation is that printed words and the picture compete for the eyes, while speech leaves the eyes free for the picture. That mechanism is a hypothesis; the evidence below measures outcomes and, in a few studies, reported load.

The relationship is tested, and the claims report six conditions on it:

  • Who controls the pace. In Ginns's meta-analysis the effect was large for system-paced material (d = 0.93) and absent, slightly negative, for self-paced material (d = −0.14). When learners can stop and reread, printed words beside the picture do as well. Mayer's (2010) summary of the literature says the same, and adds speed: the effect "tends to be strongest when the material is fast-paced … and under system control" (modality boundary conditions [~M], a narrative statement with no effect size).
  • How complex the material is. Ginns found the benefit moderated by element interactivity, and Noetel et al. found the multimedia design principles as a group mattered much more in complex media (g = 0.70) than in simple media (g = 0.20).
  • What field the content is in. Science (d = 1.20), engineering and mathematics lessons showed the effect; social-science lessons did not (d = −0.13).
  • How much the learner already knows. In the experiments reviewed by Kalyuga et al. (2003), diagram-plus-narration helped inexperienced trainees, and after further training a diagram-only version did better: the narration had become redundant. The same reversal is reported across worked examples, integrated text and isolated elements (guidance that helps novices can become counterproductive as expertise grows [~M]; for example, three experiments in which electrical trainees moved from needing text on a circuit diagram to doing better without it).
  • How big the effect is once the whole literature is pooled. Mayer's programme reports 17 of 17 experiments with a median d = 1.02; Ginns pooled d = 0.72 across 39 experiments; the largest review, as summarised by Noetel et al., found g = 0.38, and g = 0.20 after correcting for publication bias. Plan with the smaller figures. In Noetel et al.'s comparison of principles, where the average across all multimedia principles was g = 0.38, modality sat with captioning, contiguity and signalling among the moderate-to-large ones (multimedia design principles improve learning on average [+M]).
  • What medium carries the lesson. One meta-analytic contrast found the effect larger with head-mounted VR displays, but a later VR experiment found it reversed: printed text beat narration on recall and comprehension (see the VR row below). The direction in immersive VR is unsettled in the wiki.

How this sits beside its neighbours. The umbrella Multimedia Learning principle decides whether to add a picture at all, and its pattern carries the full lesson policy; this page assumes the picture is already there and is worth looking at. The redundancy decision (whether the spoken words should also appear as on-screen text) is the next question after this one, and the evidence on it is cited here only where it changes the modality choice. The contiguity decision (putting printed words next to the part of the picture they name) is what to do when the words stay printed. Cognitive Load Management is the general account that all of these draw on.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

The claims establish the direction and the conditions; none sets a segment length, a narration rate or a point at which to withdraw narration. The steps below follow the evidence where a claim is named and are labelled proposals elsewhere.

  1. Check the condition. Do the words explain something in a picture, animation or scene that must be watched at the same moment? Is the presentation system-paced (video, narrated animation, a lecture with slides), and fast for these learners? (Immersive VR is a separate case; see its row below.) Are the learners new to this content, and is the material complex (many parts acting on each other)? Can every learner hear and follow the spoken language? If the answers are yes, narrate. If the material is learner-paced pages, keep the words printed and placed beside the picture. Basis: pacing moderates the modality effect [~M].
  2. Narrate the explanation, and keep only labels or key terms on screen. Do not also print the narration word for word over the animation. Basis: Mayer's modality principle [+M]; Mayer's redundancy principle [+M] (5 of 5 experiments, median d = 0.72). The harm from verbatim duplicate text is less settled than this step makes it sound: Adesope and Nesbit's meta-analysis (57 effects) found spoken-plus-written words no worse than written alone on average, and better than speech alone for low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced and picture-free lessons; showing key terms drawn from the narration did better than verbatim text (redundant on-screen text can hurt, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average [~S]). With middle-school learners and animation, adding the written text to narration made no significant difference (redundancy not significant in middle school [~M]; not an equivalence test). So key terms are the default because they do at least as well, not because verbatim text is known to be costly.
  3. Time each sentence to the part of the picture it describes, so the learner hears about a part while it is on screen. Untested proposal on this page; the timing evidence sits with the umbrella's contiguity row.
  4. Segment length, 1–3 minutes of narration per segment, with a pause or a "continue" control at each conceptual break. Untested proposal: the narration studies recorded here are short lessons (a 15-minute immunology animation in Guttormsen Schär & Zimmermann), and no claim sets a length.
  5. Keep in print what learners must consult or copy: formulas, code, definitions, step lists they will follow later. Spoken words disappear. Untested proposal.
  6. Offer a transcript or captions on demand, off by default, for anyone who cannot or prefers not to listen. Untested proposal for content learning; captions do help second-language listening comprehension (captioning [~M]). Where a policy or platform turns captions on for everyone, keep them: the redundancy evidence in step 2 suggests a small cost at most; use condensed captions where the policy allows.
  7. Across sessions, withdraw the narration as expertise grows. Early sessions: narrated, system-paced segments. Once a learner can explain the diagram unaided on a short check (proposal: two consecutive correct explanations of a new "what happens if" question), offer the diagram alone with narration available on request. Basis for the direction: the modality effect reverses with expertise [~M] and the general expertise-reversal claim [~M]; the switching criterion is an untested proposal, and none of these studies says how many sessions the withdrawal takes.
  8. Judge it on the brief's own test, ideally a transfer or explanation question rather than recall of the words, since that is what most of the modality experiments measured.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: who controls the pace (does the video play on, or can learners pause, scroll and reread?); can every learner listen (hearing, language of instruction, a quiet place and headphones?); and how much do the learners already know about this content, and how complex is it?

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning [+S]: Ginns (2005) pooled 43 effects (39 between-subjects, 1,887 students), d = 0.72 [0.52, 0.92], moderated by element interactivity, pacing and field; Mayer & Moreno (1998), 146 college students learning lightning formation from an animation; and Noetel et al.'s (2022) overview, reporting the larger review's g = 0.38, g = 0.20 after publication-bias correction.
  • Modality principle: graphics with spoken text beat graphics with printed text for transfer [+M]: 17 of 17 experiments in six lesson domains, median d = 1.02, transfer tests. One research programme, short computer lessons.
  • Modality effect: explanatory text spoken rather than written with visual information [+M]: Sweller et al.'s (2019) review, recalling Mousavi et al.'s geometry experiment. The title's "increases effective working memory capacity" is the mechanism the review proposes, not a measurement.
  • Within-subjects designs also show a moderate-to-large modality effect [+M]: four effects from two articles, 40 students, d = 0.73, all system-paced.
  • Pacing moderates the modality effect [~M]: d = 0.93 system-paced, d = −0.14 self-paced (interval −0.32 to 0.04, so "reversed" on the claim page is stronger than the interval shows; read it as no benefit).
  • Modality effect sizes differ across fields of study [~M]: science 1.20, engineering 0.87, English comprehension 0.69, maths/logic 0.58, social science −0.13.
  • The modality effect is particularly strong in virtual reality [~M]: d = 1.34 with head-mounted displays, 0.64 audio-tape, 0.55 computer screen; a moderator contrast within one meta-analysis.
  • In immersive VR, visual-only text yields higher recall than audio-visual text [-M]: Albus and Seufert (2023), 61 learners randomised to printed or spoken text in a VR unit on HIV; printed text scored higher on recall (ηp² = 0.14) and comprehension [-M] (ηp² = 0.09), and Bayesian tests favoured the reversal [-M]. The authors ran them because the only earlier VR study they knew of had also found a reversal. This sits directly against the meta-analytic VR contrast above; the wiki holds no explanation of the difference (pacing, how the text was placed in the scene and the outcome measure are candidates, none tested).
  • The modality effect tends to be strongest when material is fast-paced and under system control [~M]: Mayer's (2010) narrative statement, agreeing with Ginns's pacing moderator and adding speed of presentation.
  • The modality effect reverses with expertise [~M]: Kalyuga et al.'s review of experiments with trainees on industrial machinery; no effect size printed.
  • Dual-mode presentation of worked examples helped except under high visual search without attention cues [~M]: when the learner cannot find what the narration refers to, the benefit disappears; it depends on the picture being easy to search or cued.
  • The modality effect held for middle-school students learning electricity [+M]: spoken text beat written text with both abstract and concrete animation, d = .35 each, in school settings. The study is described as quasi-experimental.
  • Spoken text did not differ from signalled written text [~M]: in the same study, written text with signals did as well as speech. Not an equivalence test, but it suggests well-signalled print narrows the gap.
  • Voice-based settings beat picture plus text [+M]: 36 adults, 15 minutes on immunology; animation+voice and picture+voice both beat picture+text (p = 0.004 each), with no difference in media preference. On the visual-knowledge outcome the same pattern held (means 10.18 and 9.94 against 6.79; p = 0.022 and 0.029), the result the authors read as avoiding a double load on the eyes. Mann-Whitney comparisons; no effect size printed.
  • Multimedia design interventions improve cognitive load management [+M]: modality showed g = 0.62 on cognitive load, from only k = 2 studies.
  • Design principle effects are moderated by pacing and element interactivity, but not by prior knowledge [~M]: across all the multimedia principles; the null on prior knowledge (p = 0.14) is not an equivalence test and sits against the expertise-reversal experiments above.
  • Multimedia design principles improve learning by g = 0.38 on average [+M]: Noetel et al.'s pooling of the 11 largest reviews (808 effects); the principle explained most of the variation, with modality among the moderate-to-large ones. The 0.38 here is the all-principles average, not the modality figure (which happens also to be 0.38 in the larger modality review, before bias correction).
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become counterproductive as expertise grows [~M]: Kalyuga et al.'s review across five cognitive-load paradigms, auditory narration among them, and their three circuit-diagram experiments; no effect sizes printed. Supports step 7's direction, not its timing.
  • On-screen text that duplicates the narration can hurt, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average [~S]: Kalyuga et al. (1999) found redundant on-screen text hurt in technical training; Adesope and Nesbit (2012; 57 effects, mostly postsecondary) found no average harm, an advantage over speech alone for low-prior-knowledge, system-paced and picture-free lessons, and key terms doing better than verbatim text. This is the next decision after modality; it changes step 2's reason, not its move.
  • Adding written text to narration made no significant difference for middle-school students [~M]: Kutbay and Akpınar (2020), t(184) = .35 and t(168) = .89 with abstract and concrete animation; equivalence not tested, and the source has no DOI.

Taken together: for system-paced lessons in which words explain a picture, narration beats printed text by a moderate amount, plausibly about 0.2 to 0.4 SD once publication bias is allowed for, and larger in science content and complex material. It is not expected for learner-paced material or social-science content, it fades or reverses as learners become expert, and in immersive VR the recorded studies point in opposite directions. Most studies are short computer lessons with adult or older-school learners tested soon after; the wiki holds no claim on delayed retention, on narration in long courses, or on learners who cannot hear or follow the spoken words.

The evidence by question¶

  • Should words that explain a picture be spoken rather than printed? Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning [+S]
  • When does the effect hold: pacing, complexity, field and medium? Presentation pacing moderates the modality effect [~M]
  • Does narration stop helping, or start to hurt, as learners gain expertise? The modality effect reverses with expertise [~M]
  • Does narration help in immersive VR? Unsettled: larger effect with head-mounted displays [~M] against a reversal in a VR experiment [-M]
  • If the words are spoken, does also printing them cost anything? Spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average; key terms beat verbatim text [~S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Read against the model on 2026-10-19; the eight bullets that changed it now sit in the conditions, the default design, the situation rows and the evidence list above. These three are relevant but change nothing:

  • Most surveyed Algerian university teachers of English report presenting words and graphics simultaneously [~W]: a survey of what teachers say they do (61.9%), with "printed and/or verbal words" run together, so it says nothing about which channel helps.
  • In one 37-student classroom experiment, recall and retention differ across media combinations [~W]: a conference paper with no effect size, which varied text and image combinations; it does not isolate spoken against printed words.
  • Screencast design findings [~W]: a review's report of a typology of screencast narration (explicit or implicit) and of a preference for handwriting; no learning outcome compared.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is that learners understand what a picture shows well enough to explain it and use it on a new case. Ask separately what the learner wants and how they will study: a learner who wants a reference to return to and copy from is served by print they can reread, and a learner who studies on a phone on a bus may not be able to listen. Agreement between the two makes narration an easy choice; divergence means offering both routes, with narration the default only where the lesson is system-paced.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if new system-paced comparisons, with comparable learners and the same words and pictures, find no advantage for narration on transfer, or if the advantage disappears on delayed tests. It should narrow if the field-of-study difference turns out to reflect the kind of picture (a process diagram rather than a photograph) rather than the discipline. It should be extended, not reversed, by studies in long online courses, with second-language learners and with learners who use captions as a matter of course.
