# Technology Integration Levels (SAMR / RAT / PIC-RAT)

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Several closely related models help teachers and designers judge the *effect* a specific use of technology is having on instruction, rather than whether technology is used at all (Kimmons, 2018):

- **RAT** (Hughes, Thomas, & Scharber, 2006) — technology use either **R**eplaces a prior practice with no discernible change in outcomes, **A**mplifies learning that was already occurring, or **T**ransforms learning in ways that were not previously possible.
- **SAMR** (Puentedura, 2003) — **S**ubstitution (a direct tool swap, no functional change), **A**ugmentation (a small functional improvement), **M**odification (a significant redesign of the task), or **R**edefinition (the task becomes possible only because of the technology). SAMR splits RAT's single "amplify" level into two (augmentation vs. modification), giving finer-grained distinction between small and large functional improvements.
- **PIC-RAT** (Kimmons, 2018) — crosses RAT's teacher-facing question (is the technology used to Replace, Amplify, or Transform prior practice?) with a second, learner-facing question: what is the *student's* relationship to the technology — **P**assive, **I**nteractive, or **C**reative? Plotting both questions on a grid distinguishes, for example, a teacher merely projecting notes instead of writing on a chalkboard (Passive-Replace) from students creating and publishing a collaborative writing blog (Creative-Transform).

The shared claim across all three models is that low-value technology integration substitutes for a prior practice with no functional change, while high-value integration uses the technology to do something that was previously impossible — and that most classroom technology use starts, and often stays, in the low-value quadrant unless teachers are deliberately pushed to reconsider it.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- An existing (or imaginable) non-technology version of the task, so that "replace" vs. "amplify/transform" has a clear point of comparison
- Willingness to redesign the task itself, not just swap tools, to reach the higher (transform/redefine) levels
#### Constraints
- These are heuristic, self-assessment frameworks, not empirically validated learning-outcome predictors — a "transformative" use of technology is not guaranteed to produce better learning outcomes than a well-designed "substitution" use; the framework describes the *kind* of change, not its educational value in a given context
- Provides no guidance on *which* content/pedagogy/technology combination to choose in the first place — see [TPACK](tpack.md) for that prior decision

### Target Learners
- Not learner-facing; a self-assessment tool for teachers and designers reflecting on their own technology choices

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not a learning objective — a design heuristic for evaluating whether a planned or existing use of technology is worth the investment relative to a non-technology alternative

## Examples
- A history teacher moving class notes from a chalkboard to a PowerPoint slide — Passive-Replace on the PIC-RAT grid
- Students producing and publishing a collaborative creative-writing blog that solicits peer, parent, and public feedback — Creative-Transform on the PIC-RAT grid
