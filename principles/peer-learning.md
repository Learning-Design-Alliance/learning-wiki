# Peer Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 17 claims (10 for, 7 mixed) · 26 studies (12 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review, 3 qualitative, 2 associational, 2 design, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 7 of 26 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the relationship between **learners of similar standing helping one another learn**: one learner explains, teaches, models or judges, and another asks, answers and responds. For learners who have already studied the material (they are not meeting it for the first time in the pair), and for goals that gain from explaining and checking ideas (`concept`, `principle` and `procedure` knowledge, reading comprehension, writing), a structured exchange in which each learner must explain to a peer and answer that peer's questions is expected to raise **individually measured** achievement (`immediate-performance`, course or test achievement) over studying the same material alone. The expectation runs in both directions: the learner who explains is expected to gain from preparing and explaining, and the learner who is helped from the explanation, the questions and the feedback.

Two kinds of claim in the wiki test parts of this. Meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes compare tutored learners, and the learners who served as tutors, with learners who had no tutoring ([Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]). A meta-analysis of 28 studies compares preparing to teach, and preparing and then teaching, with studying the same material with no expectation of teaching ([Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]). Several of their entries were read from abstracts only, and neither compares peer learning with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining alone. **No claim in the wiki tests the doses, the role rotation or the hand-over points in the default design below**; those are labelled proposals.

The relationship is conditional on four things the evidence points to: structure (defined roles, trained tutors, a script for questions and feedback; [structured tutoring beat unstructured](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M]), interaction (the gain was larger when the teaching responded to a learner than when it did not), the explainer knowing enough to explain correctly (peers can pass on errors; no claim measures how often), and an individual check of what each learner can now do. It is weakened where the exchange is co-presence or answer-swapping, and it does not forecast `delayed-retention` or `far-transfer`, which the recorded claims mostly do not measure.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) owns joint work on a shared task of any structure; [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) and [Social Interdependence](social-interdependence.md) own group goals with individual accountability. This page owns the narrower one-to-one exchange of explanation and response (tutoring, teaching a peer, reciprocal roles, peer assessment as a learning activity) and does not repeat their models. [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) owns commit-then-discuss on a conceptual question, specified as a routine in the [Peer Instruction pattern](../patterns/peer-instruction.md); the [Structured Peer Review pattern](../patterns/structured-peer-review.md) owns the comment-and-revise cycle on drafts; [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) and [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) own how support during practice is raised and withdrawn, which applies when the helper is a peer.

## Default design

The earlier page asked for tasks that require learners to explain and respond, norms for accountable interaction, monitoring of explanation quality, and structures so that some learners do not dominate while others hide. Below that guidance is made concrete. The relationship as a whole has claim support (peer tutoring against no tutoring; teaching against studying); **every dose, time and threshold here is an untested proposal**, and each step names its evidence status.

1. **Study first, then explain.** Give the pair material each learner has already met (a reading, a worked example, a lesson segment), not material the explainer sees for the first time. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's caution that peers can amplify misconceptions; the [self-explanation claim](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S] notes in its own discussion that a learner without the background to explain correctly may reinforce a misconception.
2. **Prepare to teach (5–10 minutes).** Each learner writes a short plan of what they will explain: the main idea, one example, and the question they expect to be asked. Preparing to teach alone gave a smaller benefit than preparing and then teaching, in one meta-analysis ([Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]); the minutes are an untested proposal.
3. **Explain to a partner who must respond (5–8 minutes per turn).** The explainer explains without reading from notes; the partner must restate the idea in their own words and ask at least two "why" or "how" questions; the explainer answers. Interactive teaching outperformed non-interactive teaching in the same meta-analysis; explaining is the interpersonal form of [prompted self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] (a moderate average gain on immediate tests, mostly individual and laboratory studies). The two-question minimum is an untested proposal.
4. **Swap roles.** Each learner explains a different part, or the same part to a new partner, so no one is fixed as the helper or the helped. Untested proposal: the recorded tutoring claims discuss reciprocal roles but do not test them.
5. **Give the roles a script, and train it once before relying on it (one 20–30 minute session).** A role card for each side: the explainer's moves (state, give an example, check the partner's restatement) and the partner's moves (restate, ask why, say what is still unclear). Model one exchange in front of the group, with one deliberate error the partner should catch. Basis: [structured peer tutoring](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M] (structured programmes produced larger achievement gains than unstructured ones; the second synthesis concerns social and behavioural outcomes); rater training was the strongest moderator of peer-assessment effects in one meta-analysis ([assessor-role claim](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [~M]). Session length is an untested proposal.
6. **Check explanation quality while pairs talk.** The teacher or facilitator listens to two or three pairs per round, notes any wrong explanation, and corrects it for everyone at the end of the round with a correct model; respond to what the learner said rather than a fixed reteach. Basis: [contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] (one-to-one tutoring studies; its classroom effect is not shown). How many pairs to sample is an untested proposal.
7. **End with an individual check.** Each learner answers one new item alone, or explains the idea in writing, and rates how sure they are before seeing the answer. Learners compare their answer with the criterion and note one thing to fix. Basis: [self-monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis; no effect size). The individual check is what separates a learner's own understanding from the pair's; [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) owns individual accountability in groups.
8. **Plan the exchange across sessions, not only one.** Untested proposal for a 4–8 week arc with two or three 20–30 minute peer sessions a week: early sessions (weeks 1–2) with full role cards, modelled exchanges and the teacher sampling pairs each round; middle sessions with the partner's prompts reduced to one card ("restate, ask why"), learners choosing which part to explain, and peer assessment against the course's own criteria; late sessions with learners running the exchange unprompted and the teacher sampling only. Programme duration and session timing were significant moderators in the mathematics peer-tutoring meta-analysis ([Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]); session length and frequency were not; the direction of the duration effect is not recorded on the claim page.
9. **Move on when the explanation is correct and the individual check succeeds.** A learner who explains correctly, answers the partner's questions and gets the individual check right on two consecutive sessions moves to harder material, or becomes the explainer for a learner meeting it for the first time; one who cannot explain after two rounds goes back to studying the material (or a worked example) with the teacher before explaining again. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can listen to pairs at all, give the partner a worked answer or criterion to check the explanation against, and keep the individual check; if the explainer does not yet have the content, replace step 3 with studying a worked example together and explaining it to each other line by line.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the explainer knows enough to explain correctly.** A learner meeting material for the first time can pass on errors; one who has studied it gains from explaining. If unstated, ask: have the learners studied this material, and can each answer one question on it alone?
- **Who can hear the exchange and correct it, and how often.** The tutoring and scaffolding evidence leans on trained tutors, scripts and monitoring; with one teacher and thirty learners, or no teacher, the structure has to be in the role cards and the check items. If unstated, ask: during peer work, who will hear an explanation, and what happens when it is wrong?
- **What each learner must be able to do alone, and when.** A pair can produce a correct answer neither learner could give alone. If unstated, ask: what will each learner do unaided after the peer work, with what aids, and how long after?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are `novice` and are meeting the material for the first time | Do not ask them to explain unstudied content; study a worked example together first, then explain it to each other step by step; have the teacher check one explanation per pair before swapping | untested proposal; the [self-explanation claim](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S] notes in its discussion that learners without background may reinforce misconceptions |
| Learners are `advanced`, or some are well ahead | Let the more knowledgeable explain to those meeting the material (cross-age or cross-level), with a role card so they question rather than tell; give them harder material to prepare | [Older peers as tutors support gains for older and younger students](../claims/tutoring-and-cross-age-peer-tutors-effective.md) [+W] (a narrative policy review, no data printed); [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S] for the explainer's side |
| Learners are `child` (primary age) | Fixed short scripts (one role card per side), sessions of 15–20 minutes, roles that swap each session, a group reward built from each child's own check | [Peer-assisted learning improves elementary achievement](../claims/peer-assisted-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] (meta-analysis read as abstract; larger effects for younger, urban, low-income and minority students, and with interdependent rewards, self-referenced evaluation and autonomy); session length is an untested proposal |
| Learners are `adult` professionals in a work setting | Peers explain a procedure or case from their own work to a colleague who must ask how they would handle a variant; the colleague restates; keep the individual check as a short case decision | untested proposal; the recorded claims are school and university studies |
| Learners are English learners, or instruction is in their second language | Use structured response groups and paired tutoring on the language target, with explanation allowed in the first language where the goal is content; check oral proficiency separately, as pairing alone may not move it | [Peer Tutoring and Response Groups positive effects on English language development](../claims/peer-tutoring-response-groups-positive-eld-effects.md) [+M] (WWC, three RCTs); limit: [cross-age tutoring showed no significant effect on oral proficiency](../claims/cross-level-peer-tutoring-null-oral-proficiency.md) [~M] (42 grade 3–5 students, daily 20-minute sessions over three months) |
| Goal is reading comprehension | Reciprocal roles on a text: each learner in turn leads predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarising, after the teacher has modelled all four | [Reciprocal Teaching Improves Reading Comprehension](../claims/reciprocal-teaching-improves-reading-comprehension.md) [+M] (16 studies; effect much larger on experimenter-made tests than standardised ones) |
| Goal is a written draft or a product judged against criteria | Peers assess each other's drafts against the criteria and each author revises; train raters on the criteria with one anchor example first; see the [Structured Peer Review pattern](../patterns/structured-peer-review.md) | [Peer assessment improves performance](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M] (54 control-group studies, small effect against no assessment); [assessor-role claim](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [~M] (rater training the strongest moderator; the assessor benefit itself is correlational) |
| The brief names its own routine, rubric, categories or criteria (its own check-in, prompt or template) | Keep it as given and run the peer exchange on it; change only one thing the evidence says matters: train peers on that rubric once, or add the partner's "restate and ask why" move, or add one individual check | untested proposal; rater training from the [assessor-role claim](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [~M] |
| `classroom` with one teacher and a large group, or scarce facilitator time (volunteers, a busy shift, one tutor checking in briefly) | Put the structure in the role cards and the check items, not in the facilitator: the facilitator samples two or three pairs per round, corrects the commonest error for everyone, and reads only the individual checks | [Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M]; sampling is an untested proposal, and contingent help is shown one-to-one only ([contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]) |
| Mixed modes: some learners remote, some in the room | Pair remote with remote and room with room, or give mixed pairs a shared document and an audio channel; schedule the peer exchange explicitly and time it, since it is the activity that tends to be dropped online | [Collaborative learning opportunities largely disappeared when courses shifted to remote instruction](../claims/remote-shift-reduced-collaborative-learning.md) [~W] (case studies of 29 courses; describes what instructors dropped, not learning); the pairing rule is an untested proposal |
| Online self-paced or phone-only, no live session | Asynchronous exchange: each learner records a 1–2 minute voice note or short text explaining one idea; an assigned partner must reply within a set window with a restatement and one question; the explainer answers | untested proposal; the [remote-shift claim](../claims/remote-shift-reduced-collaborative-learning.md) [~W] shows peer activity disappears unless designed in |
| Learners with low confidence, low literacy, or anxious about speaking in front of others | Pair with a trusted partner; let them rehearse the explanation privately first, explain orally or by showing rather than writing, use sentence starters, and keep the first rounds unassessed; the facilitator models that a wrong explanation is expected and fixable | [Social engagement in gateway math builds confidence, dependent on faculty fostering a supportive environment](../claims/social-engagement-builds-confidence-gateway-math.md) [~W] (interviews and surveys, 76 faculty and 50 students); the specific moves are untested proposals |
| Material is private or sensitive (personal histories, health, work incidents) | The learner controls what is shared and with whom; peers explain the concept on a neutral or invented example, and the learner applies it to their own case privately | untested proposal |
| Time is one session only, or a fixed short budget (for example 30 minutes) | One cycle: 3 minutes review, 5 minutes prepare, two 6-minute explain-and-question turns with roles swapped, 5 minutes individual check, 5 minutes teacher correction of the commonest error; report only immediate performance | untested proposal; the tested contrast (teaching against studying) does not set a duration |
| A whole-task performance the brief wants kept whole (a presentation, a clinical encounter, a lesson taught) | Peers watch the whole performance and debrief against the brief's criteria (one strength, one change, one question), rather than splitting it into parts to explain | untested proposal |
| Learners produce for a real audience (a performance, a product for clients) | Use peers as the rehearsal audience: each learner explains or performs to a peer who plays the audience and asks what that audience would ask, before the real event | untested proposal |
| Stakes are high, or grades depend on the work | Keep peer judgements formative; grade only the individual check or the individual product; record which explanations were corrected | untested proposal; individual accountability is modelled in [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) |
| A misconception is widespread in the group | Do not start with pair explanations; have every learner answer a diagnostic item first, address the misconception with the whole group, then use pairs to explain the corrected idea | untested proposal; contingent support evidence is one-to-one ([contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]) |

Twelve of the eighteen rows cite a claim, but only the advanced-learner, age, language, reading, writing and large-group rows rest on a claim that tests a peer arrangement against an alternative; the rest carry a neighbouring finding (self-explanation, contingent tutoring, descriptive case studies of remote teaching) or are untested proposals, and say so.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: what each learner said when explaining (record a sentence or two), which questions the partner asked, whether the partner's restatement matched, what the facilitator corrected, and each learner's individual check. "Understands it", "was carried by the partner" and "learned a wrong explanation from a peer" are inferred states. A correct pair answer shows what the pair can do with each other's help, not what either can do alone.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Pair answer correct, one learner's individual check wrong | The partner carried the reasoning; the learner understood in talk but cannot produce it alone; the check item differs more than intended | Ask that learner to explain the idea to the facilitator; give a matched item with the partner's explanation available, then without it. |
| Both learners give the same wrong explanation | The explainer passed on an error; both share a misconception from instruction; the material was not studied before the exchange | Check each learner's answer before the exchange next time; give an item where the misconception and the correct idea predict different answers. |
| One learner does all the explaining, the other only agrees | Uneven confidence or status; the partner has nothing to ask; the role card is not being used | Swap roles with a fresh part; require the partner's restatement before the explainer continues; ask the quieter learner privately what was unclear. |
| Explanations are fluent but repeat the text | Recitation rather than reorganising; the material is too easy for this learner; the partner asks no real questions | Ask a "what if" question the text does not answer; give the learner harder material to explain. |
| Peer work goes well in the room but stops online | No scheduled time; no shared surface; the activity was replaced by an individual quiz | Record whether the exchange happened at all before judging its effect; restore a timed, scripted exchange and compare. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Listening in changes the exchange it observes, so record when the facilitator joined a pair, and keep explanations open rather than choosing the one whose label fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

The claims below are the model's core. They test peer arrangements against no peer arrangement or against studying alone, mostly in school and university settings, mostly at the end of a programme or session, and several were read from abstracts only.

- [Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes. In 65 evaluations, tutored learners outperformed controls on examinations and the learners who served as tutors showed the same pattern (abstract only, no pooled effect size); in 50 mathematics studies the pooled effect was small (g = 0.333), moderated by educational stage, study design, programme duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size, but not by age, assigned roles, tutee ability, session length or frequency. Its `## Studies bearing on this claim` table counts **4 distinct studies**, all quantitative syntheses. The claim page says neither entry shows tutors gain more than tutees.
- [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]: across 28 studies, preparing and then teaching improved the teacher's own domain knowledge over studying without expecting to teach (g = 0.56), preparing alone less (g = 0.35), with interactive teaching better than non-interactive and the benefit reported for deep as well as surface learning and after a delay (abstract only). It also cites the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis for tutor gains. It does not compare teaching with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining or retrieval practice.
- [Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M]: in the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis (read in its conference version), structured programmes produced larger achievement effects than unstructured ones, though unstructured tutoring still helped; in 36 elementary peer-assisted-learning studies, structured roles were related to the size of social, self-concept and behavioural effects. Marked M here, below its cap, because only one synthesis bears on achievement.
- [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]: 54 control-group studies, primary to tertiary, g = 0.31 against no assessment and 0.28 against teacher assessment, not different from self-assessment. It does not separate the assessor's gain from the assessee's.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: explaining to oneself, g = .55 across 69 effect sizes, with classroom and delayed evidence much more limited. It is the mechanism this page's explaining step relies on, carried from individual prompting to explaining to a peer, and it is also the comparison the peer claims do not make: whether a partner adds anything over explaining alone is not shown in the wiki.

The claims that limit or qualify the model:

- [Cross-age peer tutoring showed no statistically significant effect on oral language proficiency](../claims/cross-level-peer-tutoring-null-oral-proficiency.md) [~M]: a randomised trial of 42 Spanish-speaking migrant students in grades 3–5, daily 20-minute sessions over three months, bilingual or English-only tutors against teacher-led whole-group teaching; effect sizes of −0.16 and 0.05, neither significant. Peer tutoring does not raise every outcome, and equivalence was not tested.
- [The quality of feedback students give peers predicts gains in their own work, but no recorded study isolates a causal benefit of the assessor role](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [~M]: 43 teacher-education undergraduates, correlational; the meta-analysis in the same claim (58 studies, d = 0.291) pools both roles and finds rater training the strongest moderator. The explainer's benefit in peer assessment is plausible, not shown.
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]: the evidence for support that responds to each attempt is mostly one-to-one tutoring by adults; its table counts 20 distinct studies across it and its narrower claims. Carried to peers as helpers, it supports training peers to question rather than tell, but nothing in the wiki shows peers can be contingent.
- [Collaborative learning opportunities largely disappeared when courses shifted to remote instruction](../claims/remote-shift-reduced-collaborative-learning.md) [~W] and [Social engagement in gateway math builds confidence](../claims/social-engagement-builds-confidence-gateway-math.md) [~W]: descriptive interview and case-study evidence that peer exchange depends on being designed in and on a climate where mistakes are safe; neither measures learning.
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]: a review and a theoretical synthesis that monitoring against a criterion lets learners notice gaps; it supports the individual check and the earlier page's point that interaction alone is not enough, without an effect size.

Learners, outcomes and comparisons differ across these claims (elementary peer tutoring, undergraduate teaching-expectancy studies, peer assessment across levels), so do not rank them by effect label. None compares peer explanation with an equal-time individual generative activity.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually what each learner can do alone afterwards: answer, explain or perform on a representative task, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what learners want from working with a peer: to be told the answer quickly, to avoid looking slow in front of a classmate, to help a friend, to practise saying things aloud before a real audience. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values getting the answer may push the partner to tell rather than explain; one who values not looking slow may agree with an explanation they did not follow.

For example, a nursing student may want to be able to explain a medication change to a patient's family, while the course objective is a correct written dosage calculation. Peer teaching of the calculation serves the objective; a rehearsal in which a peer plays the family member and asks what a family would ask serves the learner's goal. Record both, so that a correct calculation is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of peer explanation with an equal-time individual alternative (self-explaining, retrieval practice, a worked example) find no advantage for the peer arrangement; then the page should recommend explaining, with or without a partner, rather than peer learning as such. If the explainer's gain disappears when the partner asks no questions, the interactive element is the active ingredient and the role card should centre on questioning. If studies of untrained, unscripted peer pairs show gains as large as structured programmes, the training and script steps are a preference, not a condition. If delayed tests show the peer-tutoring gains fade, the model should say "immediate" throughout. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too little structure" after the fact.

Pair performance, each learner's immediate unaided performance, delayed retention, transfer and the learner's own valued use are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no session length, no number of rounds and no rule for when to swap roles.

## Description
Peer learning is the principle that learners can deepen understanding by explaining, questioning, modeling, and responding to one another. It is useful when learners can benefit from articulating ideas and encountering alternate explanations at a similar level of expertise.

## Implications

Peer learning is strongest when learners are asked to do real intellectual work with and for one another rather than merely sit in groups. Its value comes from explanation, comparison, and mutual response. Learners often make their own thinking clearer when they have to explain it to someone else [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and peer settings are stronger when learners monitor contribution, progress, and understanding rather than assuming interaction alone is enough [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]. The main instructional challenge is quality control: peers can also amplify misconceptions or normalize shallow reasoning if tasks and facilitation are weak, which is why peer learning often benefits from structures and prompts that respond to learner needs rather than leaving interaction fully unshaped [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Tasks that require learners to explain or respond to each other** — peer learning depends on articulation, not just co-presence
- **Norms for respectful and accountable interaction** — learners need expectations for listening, challenge, and evidence use
#### Constraints
- **Peers can reinforce misconceptions if explanation quality is not monitored**
- **Not all peer interaction is instructional** — the task has to require intellectual exchange, not only coordination
- **Uneven confidence can distort participation** — strong structures may be needed so some learners do not dominate while others hide

### Target Learners
- Learners who benefit from explanation, comparison, and rehearsal of ideas in dialogue
- Learners in large classes, discussion-heavy courses, or collaborative environments where peer response can multiply opportunities to think aloud
- Learners who may respond well to near-peer language, examples, and models

### Target Learning Objectives
- Improve understanding through explanation, comparison, and collaborative reasoning
- Strengthen learners’ ability to justify ideas and respond to alternatives
- Increase participation and responsibility for learning beyond teacher-only interaction

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Social Learning](../theories/sociocultural-theory.md) — peers can act as models, interpreters, and feedback sources
- [Social Constructivism](social-constructivism.md) — understanding is often co-constructed in interaction
- [Community of Inquiry](community-of-inquiry.md) — peer learning is stronger when discourse is both socially and cognitively supported

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — peer interaction can add coordination demands that need to be managed through structure and task design

### Claims

- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — explaining to peers can deepen conceptual understanding
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — peer contexts are stronger when learners monitor contribution, progress, and understanding
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] — peer learning often benefits from instructor or peer structures that respond to learner needs
- [Social engagement in gateway math courses builds confidence and deepens understanding, dependent on faculty fostering a supportive learning environment](../claims/social-engagement-builds-confidence-gateway-math.md) [~W]
- [Collaborative learning opportunities largely disappeared when courses shifted to remote instruction](../claims/remote-shift-reduced-collaborative-learning.md) [~W]

## Related Principles

- [Community of Inquiry](community-of-inquiry.md) — explains how peer interaction can become substantive inquiry rather than social activity alone

## Examples

### Illustrative

**[Peer Instruction](../patterns/peer-instruction.md)** — Learners answer a conceptual question individually, discuss reasoning with peers, then revise their answer.

**[Jigsaw Method](../patterns/fostering-communities-of-learning-fcl.md)** — Each learner becomes a temporary expert and teaches part of the whole to peers.

**Peer revision or peer explanation routines** — Learners respond to drafts, interpretations, or solutions and improve their own understanding through explaining what works and why.
