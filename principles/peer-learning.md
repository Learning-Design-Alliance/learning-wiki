# Peer Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 46 claims (22 for, 23 mixed, 1 against) · 51 studies (13 quant-synthesis, 12 causal, 7 review, 5 associational, 5 qualitative, 5 design, 4 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 13 of 51 report an effect size · 38 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the relationship between **learners of similar standing helping one another learn**: one learner explains, teaches, models or judges, and another asks, answers and responds. For learners who have already studied the material (they are not meeting it for the first time in the pair), and for goals that gain from explaining and checking ideas (`concept`, `principle` and `procedure` knowledge, reading comprehension, writing), a structured exchange in which each learner must explain to a peer and answer that peer's questions is expected to raise **individually measured** achievement (`immediate-performance`, course or test achievement) over studying the same material alone. The expectation runs in both directions: the learner who explains is expected to gain from preparing and explaining, and the learner who is helped from the explanation, the questions and the feedback.

Two kinds of claim in the wiki test parts of this. Meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes compare tutored learners, and the learners who served as tutors, with learners who had no tutoring ([Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]). A meta-analysis of 28 studies compares preparing to teach, and preparing and then teaching, with studying the same material with no expectation of teaching ([Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]). Several of their entries were read from abstracts only, and neither compares peer learning with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining alone; the only comparison in the wiki of the same task done with a peer and alone is a second-hand, one-sentence report that concept mapping with a peer beat concept mapping alone ([ICAP concept-mapping claim](../claims/interactive-beats-constructive-concept-mapping.md) [+W]), whose design is not described. **No claim in the wiki tests the doses, the role rotation or the hand-over points in the default design below**; those are labelled proposals.

The relationship is conditional on four things the evidence points to: structure (defined roles, trained tutors, a script for questions and feedback; [structured tutoring beat unstructured](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M]), interaction (the gain was larger when the teaching responded to a learner than when it did not), the explainer knowing enough to explain correctly (peers can pass on errors; no claim measures how often), and an individual check of what each learner can now do. It is weakened where the exchange is co-presence or answer-swapping, and it does not forecast `far-transfer`, which no recorded claim measures, or a large `delayed-retention` gain: the one randomised test of peer assessment with a delayed measure found a negligible benefit on unit quizzes that persisted at the same size to the final exam ([unit quizzes](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-unit-quiz-achievement.md) [+M], [final exam](../claims/peer-assessment-gains-persist-to-final-exam.md) [+M]).

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) owns joint work on a shared task of any structure; [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) and [Social Interdependence](social-interdependence.md) own group goals with individual accountability. This page owns the narrower one-to-one exchange of explanation and response (tutoring, teaching a peer, reciprocal roles, peer assessment as a learning activity) and does not repeat their models. [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) owns commit-then-discuss on a conceptual question, specified as a routine in the [Peer Instruction pattern](../patterns/peer-instruction.md); the [Structured Peer Review pattern](../patterns/structured-peer-review.md) owns the comment-and-revise cycle on drafts; [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) and [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) own how support during practice is raised and withdrawn, which applies when the helper is a peer.

## Default design

The earlier page asked for tasks that require learners to explain and respond, norms for accountable interaction, monitoring of explanation quality, and structures so that some learners do not dominate while others hide. Below that guidance is made concrete. The relationship as a whole has claim support (peer tutoring against no tutoring; teaching against studying); **every dose, time and threshold here is an untested proposal**, and each step names its evidence status.

1. **Study first, then explain.** Give the pair material each learner has already met (a reading, a worked example, a lesson segment), not material the explainer sees for the first time. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's caution that peers can amplify misconceptions; the [self-explanation claim](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S] notes in its own discussion that a learner without the background to explain correctly may reinforce a misconception.
2. **Prepare to teach (5–10 minutes).** Each learner writes a short plan of what they will explain: the main idea, one example, and the question they expect to be asked. Preparing to teach alone gave a smaller benefit than preparing and then teaching, in one meta-analysis ([Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]); the minutes are an untested proposal.
3. **Explain to a partner who must respond (5–8 minutes per turn).** The explainer explains without reading from notes; the partner must restate the idea in their own words and ask at least two "why" or "how" questions; the explainer answers. Interactive teaching outperformed non-interactive teaching in the same meta-analysis; explaining is the interpersonal form of [prompted self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] (a moderate average gain on immediate tests, mostly individual and laboratory studies). The two-question minimum is an untested proposal.
4. **Swap roles.** Each learner explains a different part, or the same part to a new partner, so no one is fixed as the helper or the helped. Untested proposal: the recorded tutoring claims discuss reciprocal roles but do not test them. The reason for swapping is an argument, not a result: in the ICAP framework [the dominant speaker in a pair may learn more than a partner who only agrees](../claims/individual-dialogue-dominant-speaker-learns-more.md) [~W], offered by its authors as one explanation for collaboration studies that found no advantage over working alone; no study in the wiki measures it.
5. **Give the roles a script, and train it once before relying on it (one 20–30 minute session).** A role card for each side: the explainer's moves (state, give an example, check the partner's restatement) and the partner's moves (restate, ask why, say what is still unclear). Model one exchange in front of the group, with one deliberate error the partner should catch. Basis: [structured peer tutoring](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M] (structured programmes produced larger achievement gains than unstructured ones; the second synthesis concerns social and behavioural outcomes); rater training was the strongest moderator of peer-assessment effects in one meta-analysis ([assessor-role claim](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [~M]). Session length is an untested proposal.
6. **Check explanation quality while pairs talk.** The teacher or facilitator listens to two or three pairs per round, notes any wrong explanation, and corrects it for everyone at the end of the round with a correct model; respond to what the learner said rather than a fixed reteach. Basis: [contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] (one-to-one tutoring studies; its classroom effect is not shown). How many pairs to sample is an untested proposal.
7. **End with an individual check.** Each learner answers one new item alone, or explains the idea in writing, and rates how sure they are before seeing the answer. Learners compare their answer with the criterion and note one thing to fix. Basis: [self-monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis; no effect size). The individual check is what separates a learner's own understanding from the pair's; [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) owns individual accountability in groups.
8. **Plan the exchange across sessions, not only one.** Untested proposal for a 4–8 week arc with two or three 20–30 minute peer sessions a week: early sessions (weeks 1–2) with full role cards, modelled exchanges and the teacher sampling pairs each round; middle sessions with the partner's prompts reduced to one card ("restate, ask why"), learners choosing which part to explain, and peer assessment against the course's own criteria, added to the explaining rather than replacing it, since peer assessment's average gain is small (g = 0.31 across 54 studies; d = .12 in one large randomised college course, [Peer assessment improves performance](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M], [unit-quiz claim](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-unit-quiz-achievement.md) [+M]); late sessions with learners running the exchange unprompted and the teacher sampling only. Programme duration and session timing were significant moderators in the mathematics peer-tutoring meta-analysis ([Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]); session length and frequency were not; the direction of the duration effect is not recorded on the claim page.
9. **Move on when the explanation is correct and the individual check succeeds.** A learner who explains correctly, answers the partner's questions and gets the individual check right on two consecutive sessions moves to harder material, or becomes the explainer for a learner meeting it for the first time; one who cannot explain after two rounds goes back to studying the material (or a worked example) with the teacher before explaining again. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can listen to pairs at all, give the partner a worked answer or criterion to check the explanation against, and keep the individual check; if the explainer does not yet have the content, replace step 3 with studying a worked example together and explaining it to each other line by line.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the explainer knows enough to explain correctly.** A learner meeting material for the first time can pass on errors; one who has studied it gains from explaining. If unstated, ask: have the learners studied this material, and can each answer one question on it alone?
- **Who can hear the exchange and correct it, and how often.** The tutoring and scaffolding evidence leans on trained tutors, scripts and monitoring; with one teacher and thirty learners, or no teacher, the structure has to be in the role cards and the check items. If unstated, ask: during peer work, who will hear an explanation, and what happens when it is wrong?
- **What each learner must be able to do alone, and when.** A pair can produce a correct answer neither learner could give alone. If unstated, ask: what will each learner do unaided after the peer work, with what aids, and how long after?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: what each learner said when explaining (record a sentence or two), which questions the partner asked, whether the partner's restatement matched, what the facilitator corrected, and each learner's individual check. "Understands it", "was carried by the partner" and "learned a wrong explanation from a peer" are inferred states. A correct pair answer shows what the pair can do with each other's help, not what either can do alone. Nor is a learner's report that peer work helped a measure of what it did: in the randomised college statistics course, [students' perceived benefit from peer assessment was uncorrelated with their estimated actual benefit](../claims/perception-of-peer-assessment-benefit-uncorrelated-with-actual-benefit.md) [~M] (r = .01), so judge the step by the individual check, not by a satisfaction survey.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Pair answer correct, one learner's individual check wrong | The partner carried the reasoning; the learner understood in talk but cannot produce it alone; the check item differs more than intended | Ask that learner to explain the idea to the facilitator; give a matched item with the partner's explanation available, then without it. |
| Both learners give the same wrong explanation | The explainer passed on an error; both share a misconception from instruction; the material was not studied before the exchange; the less sure learner deferred to the more confident one | Check each learner's answer, and how sure each is, before the exchange next time; give an item where the misconception and the correct idea predict different answers. In one experiment, [the more confident a learning partner's answers, the more competent learners judged that partner](../claims/partner-confidence-raises-perceived-competence.md) [~W], so a confidently wrong explainer is likely to be believed. |
| One learner does all the explaining, the other only agrees | Uneven confidence or status; the partner has nothing to ask; the role card is not being used | Swap roles with a fresh part; require the partner's restatement before the explainer continues; ask the quieter learner privately what was unclear. Agreeing without contributing is the pattern ICAP argues leaves the listener [learning less than the speaker](../claims/individual-dialogue-dominant-speaker-learns-more.md) [~W] (a theoretical interpretation, untested here), so compare the two learners' individual checks. |
| Explanations are fluent but repeat the text | Recitation rather than reorganising; the material is too easy for this learner; the partner asks no real questions | Ask a "what if" question the text does not answer; give the learner harder material to explain. |
| Peer comments on drafts are generic, or only about surface errors | The reviewer was not trained on the criteria; the reviewer lacks the content to judge argument; an AI tool in the loop is steering comments toward technical detail | Have the reviewer apply the criteria to one anchor example and compare with the teacher's reading; count content versus surface comments with and without the tool (in one quasi-experiment AI-assisted peer comments carried fewer content suggestions than teacher feedback, [feedback-focus claim](../claims/feedback-focus-shift-across-tasks.md) [~M]). |
| Peer work goes well in the room but stops online | No scheduled time; no shared surface; the activity was replaced by an individual quiz | Record whether the exchange happened at all before judging its effect; restore a timed, scripted exchange and compare. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Listening in changes the exchange it observes, so record when the facilitator joined a pair, and keep explanations open rather than choosing the one whose label fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

The claims below are the model's core. They test peer arrangements against no peer arrangement or against studying alone, mostly in school and university settings, mostly at the end of a programme or session, and several were read from abstracts only.

- [Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement](../claims/peer-tutoring-improves-achievement.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes. In 65 evaluations, tutored learners outperformed controls on examinations and the learners who served as tutors showed the same pattern (abstract only, no pooled effect size); in 50 mathematics studies the pooled effect was small (g = 0.333), moderated by educational stage, study design, programme duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size, but not by age, assigned roles, tutee ability, session length or frequency. Its `## Studies bearing on this claim` table counts **4 distinct studies**, all quantitative syntheses. The claim page says neither entry shows tutors gain more than tutees.
- [Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+S]: across 28 studies, preparing and then teaching improved the teacher's own domain knowledge over studying without expecting to teach (g = 0.56), preparing alone less (g = 0.35), with interactive teaching better than non-interactive and the benefit reported for deep as well as surface learning and after a delay (abstract only). It also cites the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis for tutor gains. It does not compare teaching with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining or retrieval practice.
- [Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M]: in the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis (read in its conference version), structured programmes produced larger achievement effects than unstructured ones, though unstructured tutoring still helped; in 36 elementary peer-assisted-learning studies, structured roles were related to the size of social, self-concept and behavioural effects. Marked M here, below its cap, because only one synthesis bears on achievement.
- [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]: 54 control-group studies, primary to tertiary, g = 0.31 against no assessment and 0.28 against teacher assessment, not different from self-assessment. - [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: explaining to oneself, g = .55 across 69 effect sizes, with classroom and delayed evidence much more limited. ## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually what each learner can do alone afterwards: answer, explain or perform on a representative task, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what learners want from working with a peer: to be told the answer quickly, to avoid looking slow in front of a classmate, to help a friend, to practise saying things aloud before a real audience. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values getting the answer may push the partner to tell rather than explain; one who values not looking slow may agree with an explanation they did not follow.

For example, a nursing student may want to be able to explain a medication change to a patient's family, while the course objective is a correct written dosage calculation. Peer teaching of the calculation serves the objective; a rehearsal in which a peer plays the family member and asks what a family would ask serves the learner's goal. Record both, so that a correct calculation is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of peer explanation with an equal-time individual alternative (self-explaining, retrieval practice, a worked example) find no advantage for the peer arrangement; then the page should recommend explaining, with or without a partner, rather than peer learning as such. If a study measuring both partners finds the learner who only listens and agrees gains as much as the one who explains, the role swap (step 4) is a fairness rule rather than a learning one. If the explainer's gain disappears when the partner asks no questions, the interactive element is the active ingredient and the role card should centre on questioning. If studies of untrained, unscripted peer pairs show gains as large as structured programmes, the training and script steps are a preference, not a condition. If delayed tests show the peer-tutoring gains fade, the model should say "immediate" throughout. If further randomised tests of peer assessment keep finding effects near d = .1, the peer-assessment step should be framed as practice in judging quality rather than as an achievement intervention; if adding AI feedback to peer review keeps outperforming peer review alone, the AI-feedback row should move into the default design for writing. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too little structure" after the fact.

Pair performance, each learner's immediate unaided performance, delayed retention, transfer and the learner's own valued use are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no session length, no number of rounds and no rule for when to swap roles.

# Peer Feedback/Peer Review

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (7 for, 9 mixed) · 22 studies (8 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 3 qualitative, 2 review, 2 theoretical, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 22 report an effect size · 12 claims rest on one study

## Description
Peer feedback and peer review are instructional structures in which learners evaluate one another's work against shared criteria in order to improve the work and deepen their own understanding. The learning benefit is reciprocal: reviewers practice noticing quality, naming evidence, and proposing revisions, while recipients receive more perspectives and revision cues than instructor-only feedback can usually provide. Peer review is not automatically effective, however; learners need preparation, clear criteria, and protocols that keep the feedback specific, respectful, and usable.

## Implications
Peer feedback improves learning when it teaches learners to recognize quality and act on revision cues, not just receive more comments. Reviewing a peer’s work can sharpen evaluative judgment because learners must explain what is working, what is weak, and why [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], while recipients gain additional information they can use to monitor and revise their own work [Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. The design challenge is credibility and specificity: peer review collapses quickly into vague praise, surface correction, or distrust unless learners share criteria, practice giving feedback, and have time to revise in response. In practice, peer review often needs modeled prompts and structured protocols before it becomes reliably useful [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Shared criteria or rubric**: Learners need a common standard for what to look for and comment on.
- **Preparation for giving feedback**: Strong peer review usually requires modeling, examples, and sentence frames or protocols.
- **Time for revision**: Feedback matters most when the recipient can act on it.
- **A psychologically safe climate**: Learners need norms that make critique about the work rather than the person.
#### Constraints
- **Vague praise or vague criticism**: Feedback that is non-specific is rarely useful.
- **Reviewer inexperience**: Learners may miss important issues or focus only on surface errors unless coached.
- **Trust and status issues**: Learners may discount peer comments if the process lacks credibility or safety.
- **Misalignment with the task**: Peer review is weaker when learners do not understand the genre, content, or criteria well enough to respond meaningfully.

### Target Learners
- **Learners engaged in drafting and revision**: Especially useful for writing, design, presentations, and project work.
- **Learners building evaluative judgment**: Reviewing peer work helps learners notice quality and criteria more explicitly.
- **Learners who benefit from multiple perspectives**: Peer review can diversify the feedback a learner receives.
- **Learners developing communication and critique skills**: The process builds both giving and receiving feedback productively.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Improved revision quality**: Using external feedback to strengthen a draft, product, or performance.
- **Metacognitive monitoring**: Learning to recognize quality, gaps, and next steps in one's own work.
- **Constructive critique skills**: Practicing evidence-based, actionable feedback.
- **Agency and participation in assessment**: Involving learners more actively in the improvement process.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Social constructivist perspectives — learners build understanding through interaction and shared criteria.
- Self-regulated learning — peer feedback supports monitoring, revision, and strategic adjustment.
- Reflective and evaluative learning traditions — reviewing others' work sharpens one's own sense of quality.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Peer review should complement, not automatically replace, expert feedback where technical accuracy or domain nuance matters.
- Anonymous peer review can reduce some social friction, but it can also reduce accountability or relational learning depending on the context.

## Related Principles
- [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) — peer review is a more criteria-driven form of peer discussion centered on improvement.
- [Formative Assessment](formative-assessment.md) — peer feedback is often a low-stakes formative mechanism before final evaluation.
- [Process-Based Writing](process-based-writing.md) — drafting and revision make peer review especially useful.
- [Building Empathy](building-empathy.md) — constructive peer review depends on understanding audience, tone, and the impact of feedback.

## Examples
- **Structured draft review**: Learners use a rubric and sentence stems to comment on a peer's draft before revision.
- **Gallery walk critique**: Learners review several products and leave targeted feedback linked to criteria.
- **Real-time digital peer response**: Learners respond to classmates' work in a shared platform while the task is still revisable.
- **Two-stage review**: Learners first identify strengths and evidence, then suggest one or two specific next moves.
