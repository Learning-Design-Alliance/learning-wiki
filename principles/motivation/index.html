Motivation¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 52 claims (31 for, 17 mixed, 4 against) · 57 studies (18 review, 13 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 7 qualitative, 7 theoretical, 3 design, 2 associational), q1–q4 · 6 of 57 report an effect size · 39 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner whose effort on a learning task is low or fragile (does not start, gives up after the first difficulty, does only what is checked, does not come back to optional work), motivation is not one quantity to be raised. It is expected to rise, observed as starting, effort and voluntary persistence (persistence, motivation-affect, measured as behavioural observation or self-report), when the design repairs the component that is low for this learner on this task:

  • Expectancy: the learner does not believe they can succeed at this task. Repaired by early success they can attribute to their own strategy, near goals about the process, and feedback that names what worked.
  • Value: the learner sees no reason the task is worth doing, or the cost (time, effort, risk of public failure) outweighs the reason. Repaired by a visible reason the learner connects to their own goals, and by lowering the cost.
  • Ownership: the learner works only under pressure, threat or a promised reward. Repaired by autonomy support: choices that matter within a structure, a reason for required work, informational rather than controlling language and incentives.
  • Belonging: the learner reads ordinary difficulty as a sign they do not belong in this class, subject or programme. Repaired by personal contact and, at transitions, by framing difficulty as common and temporary.

A move aimed at the wrong component is expected to do little: a reason for the task does not help a learner who believes they cannot do it, and early success does not help one who sees no point. Generic boosts (rewards for taking part, entertaining additions, a one-off message that ability can grow) are not expected to carry motivation on their own, and some can lower later voluntary engagement. Raised motivation is expected to raise learning of the target (immediate-performance, general-achievement) only when the extra effort goes into a task that requires thinking with the target, and only on the horizon the effort lasts.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as a whole. None diagnoses which component is low and compares a matched move with an unmatched one. The claims below test or describe single components: self-efficacy and task value as predictors (reviews, no source text read), the undermining effect of expected tangible rewards (two competing meta-analyses), process goals for novices (one experiment), belonging exercises at college entry (two randomized trials), growth-mindset messages (a pre-registered trial and a meta-analysis that find small average effects), a personal message about a student's potential before an enrolment decision (one regression-discontinuity study), the quality of a learner's reasons as a predictor of intended effort (one survey), and teacher support as the strongest correlate of school belonging (one meta-analysis). The four components come from Expectancy-Value Theory, Self-Efficacy Theory and Self-Determination Theory; the matching rule is a design proposal built on them. A practitioner review groups the same four (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy, value) and asserts that each raises perseverance (A CCSR research review concludes that four academic mindsets (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy and value) increase perseverance and improve academic behaviors [+W], held below its M cap because the review prints no effect sizes and does not report the studies it cites), and an ERIC digest states the expectancy and value prerequisites as an argument from theory (Instruction should present the learning task as engaging and meaningful and promote positive expectations of success, because effort requires that learners value the task and believe they can succeed [+W], no data). Neither diagnoses which component is low or compares matched with unmatched moves.

How the converted neighbours sit inside this. This page owns the general relationship and the diagnostic step: which component is low, and which move matches it. Autonomy owns the ownership component in full (when choice helps, the controlling side of rewards and praise); read it before offering choice. Engagement owns the observable conditions around a task (a personal contact, a visible reason, visible progress) and the second link, that engagement raises learning only through a task that demands thinking; this page does not repeat it. Goal Setting and Monitoring owns what a goal should be and how progress against it is made visible. Self-Regulated Learning owns what a motivated learner does next: planning, monitoring and choosing a strategy. No pattern page instantiates this principle.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (value, achievable challenge, autonomy, visible progress, caution about novelty and external reward), turned into a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal, with its evidence status beside it.

  1. Find which component is low before changing anything. Over the first two or three sessions, record one behaviour (starts within the first few minutes; finishes; returns to an optional task) and ask four short questions, in private, once: "Before you try this one, how sure are you that you can do it?" (then compare with the attempt); "What, if anything, would this be useful for, for you?"; "If this were not required, would you do it? Why or why not?"; "Who here would you ask if you got stuck?". Untested proposal; the questions are illustrative. Compare the confidence answer with the attempt rather than taking it at face value: self-efficacy judgments matched behaviour less well in arithmetic skill learning than in performance settings (Self-efficacy judgments correspond less well to behavior in learning settings than in performance settings [~M], one review reporting earlier studies).
  2. If expectancy is low, arrange success the learner can own. Start with an item the learner can complete with the help available; set a near goal about the steps to carry out ("check each sentence for a verb") rather than a score; after success, name the strategy that worked, and do not praise the learner as clever, especially before harder work. Move to outcome goals once the steps are under control. Evidence: Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M] (two reviews, predictive, not a test of a move); Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] (high-school writing revision and a review chapter); Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [+M] (second-hand); Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [~M] (fifth graders); A personalized ability signal increased the probability of participating in AP classes by 49 percentage points among surveyed students [+M] (one regression-discontinuity study in one district, a personal message sent before course choice; the 49-point difference is among surveyed students only and no standardized effect size is printed). A message about potential before a choice is not praise for cleverness after success, which is what the praise claim warns against. Dose: the first two or three sessions of a new topic, and again after a failed assessment.
  3. If value is low, make the reason visible, let the learner make the connection, and lower the cost. At the start of each unit, say what the work is for and who it matters to; then ask each learner to write two or three sentences on how it connects to something they want (about 5 minutes). Lower cost where you can: a private first attempt before any public one, smaller pieces, time inside the session rather than at home. Evidence: Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M] (two reviews read without source text; the writing exercise and the cost point are reported second-hand). Dose: once per unit, about every two to three weeks (untested proposal).
  4. If ownership is low, support autonomy rather than adding pressure or prizes. Offer two or three options for topic, product or order, all aimed at the same objective; give a reason for anything required; use "here is what would help" rather than "you must". Do not promise tangible rewards for doing a task the learner already finds interesting; feedback about competence is fine. Evidence: Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M] (its experimental basis is the reward meta-analysis; the direct case for autonomy support is review-level); Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]; Within SDT, intrinsic motivation predicts intended effort most strongly, followed by identified regulation, with introjected regulation much weaker [+M] (one survey of 511 second-language learners; intended effort is self-reported, and the design is associational, so it says which reasons go with effort, not that a move changes the reasons). How to bound and widen choice is in Autonomy.
  5. If belonging is low, start with contact, and at a transition normalise difficulty. Greet or message each learner by name each session. At the start of a course or programme, or after a move to a new school or level, give a short exercise in which learners read that early difficulty is common and passes, and write about their own experience in that light (under 30 minutes, once); check that the setting gives them real ways to take part. Evidence: Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes [+M] (first-year college students; the effect appeared only where the institution afforded belonging); Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement [+M] (correlational); Teacher support is the strongest predictor of school belonging [+M] (one meta-analysis of 51 studies, associational; no effect size in the text read). The contact routine is modelled in Engagement.
  6. Once expectancy and value are in place, set a specific, challenging goal. A goal the learner accepts and believes reachable, with progress they can read against it. Evidence: Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [~M] (one narrative review; conditional on commitment and ability). What the goal should be is modelled in Goal Setting and Monitoring.
  7. Make progress visible and let the learner record it. A short end-of-session record (three minutes) of what was done against the goal, or a checklist of steps completed. Evidence: Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis; motivation is not measured). From the earlier page: learners continue when they can see improvement.
  8. Do not let novelty, rewards or a motivational message carry the design. A surprising hook is fine if it shows the idea to be learned; a points system is a small, uncertain addition, tied to progress rather than to taking part; a message that ability can grow helps, if at all, alongside strategy instruction and a setting where effort changes results. Evidence: Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~S] (a brief online intervention raised lower-achieving ninth-graders' grades by about a tenth of a grade point; two meta-analyses found weak effects overall); the hook and points evidence is on Engagement.
  9. Judge the design on two outcomes, not one. Record the behaviour from step 1 and a short probe of the target. Enjoyment ratings at the end of a session are not evidence that either moved: in one study, pupils' ratings of introductory activities correlated weakly with near tests and not with later exams (Positive motivational assessments of introductory activities correlate weakly with learning outcomes on near-in-time tests but not with distant exam results [~M]).

Across sessions (an untested arc; adjust from the behaviour and the probe):

  • Weeks 1–2: the teacher arranges early success, states the reason for each task, sets process goals, makes personal contact each session, and asks the four questions once.
  • Weeks 3–6: learners write their own process goals and their own relevance sentences at each new unit, keep their own progress record, and are offered their first choices. Teacher checks drop to once per work block.
  • After that: outcome goals, wider choices, fewer teacher prompts. Ask the four questions again at each transition (a new unit, a failed assessment, a move to harder material), because the low component can change.
  • Moving on: withdraw a support when the behaviour has held for three consecutive sessions and the probe has not fallen; reinstate it if the behaviour drops for two sessions running. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can ask the four questions, put them in the first activity of a self-paced course as a short form, and route learners to the matching first activity (an easier first item; a relevance prompt; a choice of topic; a welcome naming who to contact). If the probe shows no learning while the behaviour has risen, change the task, not the motivation supports.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which component is low for these learners. The same withdrawal can come from doubt about success, no reason to care, resentment of control, or not belonging, and each calls for a different move. If unstated, ask: what does the learner do when the task starts, and what do they say when asked why they stopped or why they did it at all?
  • Whether taking part is voluntary. In a required course, motivation shows in effort and quality; in a voluntary one (an open online course, an optional programme, adult learning in spare time) it shows first as dropout, and anything that discourages early can cost the learner entirely. If unstated, ask: can the learner leave, and what happens if they do?
  • Who can see a learner's effort and respond, and how often. A tutor with one learner can diagnose and adjust each session; a teacher with thirty, a volunteer with ten minutes, or an unattended platform cannot. If unstated, ask: how often does anyone look at each learner's work or attendance, and for how long?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: whether and when a learner starts, how long they continue after a difficulty, whether they finish, whether they return to optional work, what they choose when given options, what they say about why they are doing it, and their confidence rating before an item against their result on it. "Motivated", "lazy", "has low self-efficacy" and "doesn't care" are inferences. A survey of motivation is a self-report, and a learner who complies under pressure can look motivated.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Starts, then stops at the first difficulty Low expectancy (reads the difficulty as inability); the task is too hard; no reason to push through Give an easier next item and see whether the learner continues; ask what they think the difficulty means; ask what the task is for.
Does the work only when it is graded or rewarded Low ownership (the incentive is the reason); low value (no other reason); cannot do the task without the incentive's structure Remove the incentive for one optional task but keep the structure and a stated reason, and record voluntary return; give a short unaided probe.
Says the work is pointless, but succeeds when made to do it Low value, with expectancy intact; the stated reason does not connect to anything the learner wants; cost (time, embarrassment) is high Ask what the learner wants to be able to do; offer a choice of topic tied to it; make the first attempt private and compare.
Confident before the item, wrong on it, and unbothered Overconfidence (self-efficacy judgments match behaviour poorly while learning); low value, so the error does not matter; misread the task Ask for a confidence rating on several items and compare with results; ask what would happen if this were wrong at work or in an exam.

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking about motivation is itself an intervention and can change it, so record each question asked, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim tests the matching relationship: none diagnoses which component is low and compares a matched move with an unmatched one. The claims below each bear on one component or one move.

  • Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]: two narrative reviews (Bandura's book and Pajares 1996), coded q2, read without source text and with no effect size. They report self-efficacy as a strong predictor of academic performance and persistence, task-specific rather than global, and built most by mastery experiences. Predictive and review-level: it supports treating expectancy as a component to assess; it does not show that a move raising self-efficacy raises persistence. The earlier page's [+S] was above the claim's recorded evidence and is lowered to M.
  • Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M]: two reviews of expectancy-value research (Eccles & Wigfield 2002; Wigfield & Cambria 2010), read without source text. Expectancy and value predict academic choices and effort beyond prior performance; brief utility-value writing exercises are reported to raise engagement and grades, especially for students from underrepresented groups; the cost of a task is named as underused. The writing exercise is reported second-hand and no effect size is printed. Held at M, below its S cap, for that reason.
  • Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M]: a narrative review of the self-determination framework, the 128-experiment reward meta-analysis (d = −0.4 for expected tangible rewards on already-interesting tasks) and a conceptual framework of three kinds of classroom autonomy support. Its one experimental basis is about rewards as a controlling condition, so the direct test of autonomy support is review-level; held at M below its cap, as on Autonomy. It does not predict learning gains.
  • Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]: two competing meta-analyses. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (128 experiments) found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced free-choice engagement after the reward was withdrawn (d = −0.28 to −0.40), more for children than college students, while positive verbal feedback raised it. Cameron and Pierce (96 experiments) found no overall undermining, with a minimal negative effect for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Both agree on that case and on praise. The outcome is free-choice persistence and interest in mostly laboratory tasks, not learning.
  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: one experiment with high-school students learning writing revision (process goals beat outcome goals; shifting to outcome goals once the skill was mastered could help) and a review chapter. No effect size or sample size recorded. It supports near, process-focused goals for learners whose expectancy is low.
  • Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes [+M]: two randomized experiments at college entry, both read as abstracts. A brief exercise framing adversity as common and transient raised African American students' grades over three years (92 students), and an online version under 30 minutes raised first-year full-time completion across 26,911 students at 22 institutions, concentrated in groups that had progressed at lower rates and only where the institution afforded them opportunities to belong. No effect size recorded. Held at M, below its cap, because neither effect size was read.
  • Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~S]: the limiting claim. A pre-registered national experiment (12,490 ninth-graders) found a brief online growth-mindset intervention raised lower-achieving students' core-course grades by 0.10 grade points (standardized difference 0.11, coded i0), larger where peer norms supported the message; two meta-analyses (abstract) found the mindset–achievement relation and intervention effects weak overall, with somewhat more support for low-SES or at-risk students. A message about motivation, on its own, moves achievement little, and the claim's title is stronger than its entries.
  • Positive motivational assessments of introductory activities correlate weakly with learning outcomes on near-in-time tests but not with distant exam results [~M]: a second limit, on measurement. Elementary pupils' ratings of how interesting, instructive and fun introductory activities were correlated weakly with near tests (for example r = .36) and not with later exams (N = 172). Reported enjoyment is not a stand-in for learning.
  • A personalized ability signal increased the probability of participating in AP classes by 49 percentage points among surveyed students [+M]: a regression-discontinuity analysis of a PSAT-based personal message in Oakland. Among surveyed students the message raised AP participation by 49 percentage points, and they enrolled in and passed about one more AP course the next year; no standardized effect size is printed, and the estimate is for the surveyed subgroup. The one causal claim here on a move aimed at expectancy, with a choice (enrolment) as outcome, not persistence or learning. A separate issue-brief write-up of the same study is kept on the claim page.
  • Within SDT, intrinsic motivation predicts intended effort most strongly, followed by identified regulation, with introjected regulation much weaker [+M]: an SEM on 511 second-language learners; intrinsic motivation predicted intended effort most strongly (standardized coefficient .58), identified regulation next (.33), introjected regulation least (.13). It supports the ownership component's direction (reasons the learner holds go with more intended effort than pressure does) and is associational and self-reported.
  • Teacher support is the strongest predictor of school belonging [+M]: a meta-analysis of 51 studies (N = 67,378) in which teacher support and positive personal characteristics were the strongest of ten correlates of school belonging; no effect size in the text read. It supports making teacher contact the first belonging move for school-age learners; it does not show that raising teacher support raises belonging.
  • Girls show less confidence and interest in math and science from early adolescence, and a strong math/science self-concept predicts course choice and performance for both genders [+M]: an IES practice guide's review of correlational and longitudinal studies: from early adolescence girls report less confidence and interest in math and science, and a strong self-concept predicts course choice and performance for both genders. Predictive, like the self-efficacy claim; no effect sizes printed.
  • A CCSR research review concludes that four academic mindsets (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy and value) increase perseverance and improve academic behaviors [+W] and Instruction should present the learning task as engaging and meaningful and promote positive expectations of success, because effort requires that learners value the task and believe they can succeed [+W]: a practitioner review and an ERIC digest that state the components this page uses; neither reports data.
  • Teacher adoption of AI, one theoretical paper (Yurt 2024) read through expectancy-value theory: expectancy [+W]; attainment value [+W]; utility value [+W]; interest value [+W]; cost [+W]. No data; it carries the components to teachers as learners and names cost (time, resources, doubts about reliability) as the barrier to cut.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (laboratory tasks, high-school writers, ninth-graders, college entrants, elementary pupils), and most report no standardized effect size, so do not rank them. None measures whether motivation raised by these moves lasts beyond a term, and none tests the matching rule.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do rewards and praise undermine voluntary engagement? Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]
  • Does a learner's belief that they can succeed at a task predict whether they start and persist, and which goals and feedback raise it? Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]
  • Does seeing a task as interesting, important or useful raise the effort learners invest in it? Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a learning outcome (a capability on a stated measure at a stated time), with motivation as the route to it; sometimes motivation is itself the objective (continued study, voluntary practice, choosing the next course). Say which, because they are measured differently. Then ask the learner, separately, what they want from the work: to pass, to get a job, to be able to help their children, to be left alone, to avoid looking foolish. The answer is also the material for the value component: a reason the learner does not share is one more instruction. Agreement, divergence and "I don't know" are all observations.

For example, an adult learner in an evening English class may want to talk with their child's teacher at a parents' evening, while the designer's objective for the term is accurate use of past tenses on a written test. A relevance exercise about "English for your career" misses the learner's goal; a role-play of the parents' evening that needs past tenses ("what did she do this week?") serves both, and the written test still checks the designer's objective. Record attendance and the test separately, so that a full room is not read as the accuracy the course set out to build, or a good test score as the conversation the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The matching rule should be dropped if comparisons show that a move aimed at the component a learner reports as low does no better than the same move given to everyone, or than the move aimed at another component. It should weaken if self-reports of expectancy, value, ownership and belonging cannot be told apart in practice, or do not predict which move helps. The cautions about rewards and generic messages should change if expected tangible rewards for taking part turn out not to lower later voluntary engagement in real courses, or if brief mindset messages show effects well beyond a tenth of a grade point without strategy support. If motivation raised by these moves raises learning on aligned measures whatever the task, the condition "only through a task that demands thinking" should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong component" after the fact.

Starting, effort, voluntary persistence, enjoyment, reported motivation and learning of the target are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how to diagnose the low component reliably, how long a raised level lasts, or how much motivation is enough.

Related Principles¶

  • Engagement — motivation and engagement are closely linked but not identical
  • Self-Regulated Learning — what a motivated learner does next: planning, monitoring and choosing a strategy
  • Authentic Audiences and Purposes — a real audience as a source of task value

  • Cognitive Activation — challenging, effortful tasks give learners authentic evidence that strategy and effort produce improvement
  • Active Learning — creates the productive struggle that growth framing helps learners interpret adaptively
  • Collaborative Learning — peer norms that normalize struggle sustain mindset messages beyond the initial intervention
  • Error Analysis — growth mindset depends on treating mistakes as useful evidence rather than failure.
  • Check-ins — regular low-stakes check-ins normalize partial understanding and continued improvement.
  • Positive Self-Talk — internal language can reinforce or undermine growth-oriented interpretations of effort and difficulty.

Examples¶

  • Build students' intrinsic motivation and share learning sciences principles with students to support buy-in and ownership
  • Reduce cheating holistically by enhancing perceived assessment purpose, fostering self-efficacy, and increasing perceived costs of cheating
  • Classroom strategy set for supporting students in foster care: buddies, helper tasks, structure, earned rewards, small successes, sincere praise, kept promises, high expectations, clean slates, and assumption-free assignments
  • Process-oriented instructor feedback: Replacing "You're good at this" with specific feedback about what strategy worked and what to try next.
  • Revision routines: Asking learners to resubmit work after feedback so improvement is expected, not exceptional.
  • Public modeling of struggle: Instructors narrate how they handle confusion, dead ends, and correction while learning a new tool or skill.
  • Reflection prompts after mistakes: Short prompts such as "What did this attempt teach you?" or "What strategy will you change next time?"
  • Cultivate positive academic mindsets through instructional practices aligned with students' psychological needs
  • Growth-mindset-informed math teaching practices: norms, no fixed labels, mixed-ability grouping, process praise, and formative assessment with retakes
  • Reinforce growth mindsets through explicit messages plus implicit classroom practices
  • Chatbot support should reinforce that learning ability develops through effort, strategy use, feedback, and reflection
  • Introduce student teachers to mindset theory early and foster a learning orientation through classroom language, feedback and attitude to challenge

Illustrative¶

Learner Choice — Meaningful options can increase ownership when they remain aligned to shared goals.

Game-Based Learning — Carefully designed challenge, progression, and feedback can increase willingness to persist.

Visible progress routines — Learners are more likely to continue when they can see improvement, checkpoint completion, or movement toward a meaningful target.
