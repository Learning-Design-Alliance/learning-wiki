Motivation¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 79 claims (48 for, 30 mixed, 1 against) · 79 studies (22 review, 18 causal, 10 associational, 9 qualitative, 9 theoretical, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 design), q1–q4 · 9 of 79 report an effect size · 65 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner whose effort on a learning task is low or fragile (does not start, gives up after the first difficulty, does only what is checked, does not come back to optional work), motivation is not one quantity to be raised. It is expected to rise, observed as starting, effort and voluntary persistence (persistence, motivation-affect, measured as behavioural observation or self-report), when the design repairs the component that is low for this learner on this task:

  • Expectancy: the learner does not believe they can succeed at this task. Repaired by early success they can attribute to their own strategy, near goals about the process, and feedback that names what worked.
  • Value: the learner sees no reason the task is worth doing, or the cost (time, effort, risk of public failure) outweighs the reason. Repaired by a visible reason the learner connects to their own goals, and by lowering the cost.
  • Ownership: the learner works only under pressure, threat or a promised reward. Repaired by autonomy support: choices that matter within a structure, a reason for required work, informational rather than controlling language and incentives.
  • Belonging: the learner reads ordinary difficulty as a sign they do not belong in this class, subject or programme. Repaired by personal contact and, at transitions, by framing difficulty as common and temporary.

A move aimed at the wrong component is expected to do little: a reason for the task does not help a learner who believes they cannot do it, and early success does not help one who sees no point. Generic boosts (rewards for taking part, entertaining additions, a one-off message that ability can grow) are not expected to carry motivation on their own, and some can lower later voluntary engagement. Raised motivation is expected to raise learning of the target (immediate-performance, general-achievement) only when the extra effort goes into a task that requires thinking with the target, and only on the horizon the effort lasts.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as a whole. None diagnoses which component is low and compares a matched move with an unmatched one. The claims below test or describe single components: self-efficacy and task value as predictors (reviews, no source text read), the undermining effect of expected tangible rewards (two competing meta-analyses), process goals for novices (one experiment), belonging exercises at college entry (two randomized trials), growth-mindset messages (a pre-registered trial and a meta-analysis that find small average effects), a personal message about a student's potential before an enrolment decision (one regression-discontinuity study), the quality of a learner's reasons as a predictor of intended effort (one survey), teacher support as the strongest correlate of school belonging (one meta-analysis), praise given before a task (one experiment), the peer and teacher conditions under which a mindset message raised grades (moderation within national trials), and generative AI's pull on the value of effort (interviews and one experiment in programming courses). The four components come from Expectancy-Value Theory, Self-Efficacy Theory and Self-Determination Theory; the matching rule is a design proposal built on them. A practitioner review groups the same four (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy, value) and asserts that each raises perseverance (A CCSR research review concludes that four academic mindsets (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy and value) increase perseverance and improve academic behaviors [+W], held below its M cap because the review prints no effect sizes and does not report the studies it cites), and an ERIC digest states the expectancy and value prerequisites as an argument from theory (Instruction should present the learning task as engaging and meaningful and promote positive expectations of success, because effort requires that learners value the task and believe they can succeed [+W], no data). Neither diagnoses which component is low or compares matched with unmatched moves.

How the converted neighbours sit inside this. This page owns the general relationship and the diagnostic step: which component is low, and which move matches it. Autonomy owns the ownership component in full (when choice helps, the controlling side of rewards and praise); read it before offering choice. Engagement owns the observable conditions around a task (a personal contact, a visible reason, visible progress) and the second link, that engagement raises learning only through a task that demands thinking; this page does not repeat it. Goal Setting and Monitoring owns what a goal should be and how progress against it is made visible. Self-Regulated Learning owns what a motivated learner does next: planning, monitoring and choosing a strategy. No pattern page instantiates this principle.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (value, achievable challenge, autonomy, visible progress, caution about novelty and external reward), turned into a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal, with its evidence status beside it.

  1. Find which component is low before changing anything. Over the first two or three sessions, record one behaviour (starts within the first few minutes; finishes; returns to an optional task) and ask four short questions, in private, once: "Before you try this one, how sure are you that you can do it?" (then compare with the attempt); "What, if anything, would this be useful for, for you?"; "If this were not required, would you do it? Why or why not?"; "Who here would you ask if you got stuck?". Untested proposal; the questions are illustrative. Compare the confidence answer with the attempt rather than taking it at face value: self-efficacy judgments matched behaviour less well in arithmetic skill learning than in performance settings (Self-efficacy judgments correspond less well to behavior in learning settings than in performance settings [~M], one review reporting earlier studies).
  2. If expectancy is low, arrange success the learner can own. Start with an item the learner can complete with the help available; set a near goal about the steps to carry out ("check each sentence for a verb") rather than a score; after success, name the strategy that worked, and do not praise the learner as clever, especially before harder work. Move to outcome goals once the steps are under control. Evidence: Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M] (two reviews, predictive, not a test of a move); Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] (high-school writing revision and a review chapter); Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [+M] (second-hand); Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [~M] (fifth graders); Under task-involving feedback, pre-task person praise reduces motivation and indirectly lowers task performance; under ego-involving feedback it indirectly raises performance [~M] (one experiment with 146 students: praising the person before a task lowered the number of attempts, and through it performance, when feedback was about mastering the task, and raised them when feedback was a public comparison; this page's default is mastery-focused feedback, so it does not praise the person before the task either); Process praise to toddlers predicts fourth grade math and reading comprehension achievement via children's incremental motivational frameworks [+M] (53 children followed from parents' praise at 14–38 months to fourth grade; associational, no standardized effect size); Dispositional learning goals positively predict memory self-efficacy in younger and older adults [+M] and Memory self-efficacy positively predicts memory performance, mediating the indirect effects of both goal orientations [+M] (one survey-and-recall study of 216 younger and older adults; β = .28 and .16, associational: learners oriented to learning rather than to performing report more confidence, and confidence goes with better recall); A personalized ability signal increased the probability of participating in AP classes by 49 percentage points among surveyed students [+M] (one regression-discontinuity study in one district, a personal message sent before course choice; the 49-point difference is among surveyed students only and no standardized effect size is printed). A message about potential before a choice is not praise for cleverness after success, which is what the praise claim warns against. Frequent low-stakes checks with immediate marking are one way to give that early, owned success: Intervention students reported significantly higher exam confidence than controls (M=4.3 vs 3.4 on a 5-point scale, d=0.76) [+M] (one A-level sciences study, t(140), high-frequency automatically marked formative assessments against a control; confidence self-reported after the intervention only). Harder work is not in itself the threat to expectancy: Task complexity and desirable difficulties do not significantly affect intrinsic interest or expectancies for success [~M] (one experiment, multivariate F(2, 126); η² ≤ .02 and equivalence not tested), so keep tasks demanding and use this step for the learner who is already withdrawing, not to make every task easy. Dose: the first two or three sessions of a new topic, and again after a failed assessment.
  3. If value is low, make the reason visible, let the learner make the connection, and lower the cost. At the start of each unit, say what the work is for and who it matters to; then ask each learner to write two or three sentences on how it connects to something they want (about 5 minutes). Lower cost where you can: a private first attempt before any public one, smaller pieces, time inside the session rather than at home. Evidence: Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M] (two reviews read without source text; the writing exercise and the cost point are reported second-hand); A general chemistry utility-value and growth mindset intervention showed statistically significant positive outcomes overall but statistically nonsignificant outcomes for underrepresented students [~M] (one randomized course study, reported in a case-study collection with no effect sizes: the reviews' "especially for underrepresented students" did not hold in that course, so do not count on the exercise to close a gap); Students' perception of science instruction as interesting is positively associated with their emotional engagement in science [+M] (6,465 students in 25 schools, b = 0.32, associational). In a second language, the experience of the lessons themselves went with intended effort far more than a picture of a future self did (L2 Learning Experience Beats Ideal L2 Self [+M], standardized paths .71 against .25, one survey of 511 learners), so the reason a learner can see in this week's lesson counts for more than a vision exercise about where the language will take them. A scaffolding review reports the effort and achievement link second-hand and adds that task value predicts expectancy of success (Students perceiving high task value exert greater effort and achieve more than students perceiving low value [+W], no study read), one reason to address value before outcome goals. Lowering the cost of failure can itself sustain persistence: video gamers interviewed did not report failure as costly because a life could be regenerated, and kept trying new approaches (Gaming participants did not report the negative consequences of task failure; low-stakes experimentation and regeneration supported perseverance [+W], one mixed-methods study of gaming, not of coursework), which is the argument for a private first attempt and resubmission. Dose: once per unit, about every two to three weeks (untested proposal).
  4. If ownership is low, support autonomy rather than adding pressure or prizes. Offer two or three options for topic, product or order, all aimed at the same objective; give a reason for anything required; use "here is what would help" rather than "you must". Do not promise tangible rewards for doing a task the learner already finds interesting; feedback about competence is fine. Evidence: Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M] (its experimental basis is the reward meta-analysis; the direct case for autonomy support is review-level); Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]; Within SDT, intrinsic motivation predicts intended effort most strongly, followed by identified regulation, with introjected regulation much weaker [+M] (one survey of 511 second-language learners; intended effort is self-reported, and the design is associational, so it says which reasons go with effort, not that a move changes the reasons). How to bound and widen choice is in Autonomy.
  5. If belonging is low, start with contact, and at a transition normalise difficulty. Greet or message each learner by name each session. At the start of a course or programme, or after a move to a new school or level, give a short exercise in which learners read that early difficulty is common and passes, and write about their own experience in that light (under 30 minutes, once); check that the setting gives them real ways to take part. Evidence: Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes [+M] (first-year college students; the effect appeared only where the institution afforded belonging); Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement [+M] (correlational); Teacher support is the strongest predictor of school belonging [+M] (one meta-analysis of 51 studies, associational; no effect size in the text read); First-year students' sense of belonging to their university predicts intentions to persist, controlling for background variables and other persistence predictors [+M] and A belonging-enhancing intervention produced a smaller decline in sense of belonging over the first year than the control groups [+M] (one study of African American and White first-year students: belonging fell over the year in every group, less in the randomly assigned intervention group, and belonging predicted intention to persist; no effect size in the text read, and the outcome is intention, not enrolment); School belonging mediates the higher intrinsic motivation of Dominican children relative to European-American peers [+W] (one study in middle childhood: school belonging, not the ethnic category, carried the difference in intrinsic motivation between two groups; associational, held at W for its rigour); in secondary science classrooms whose teachers took part in a motivation programme, Instructional practices aligned to the Belonging MDP were associated with significant declines in students' perceptions that science required too much effort or threatened self-esteem [+W] and Students' perceptions of motivationally supportive instruction were associated with higher positive motivational beliefs [+W] (one development-phase report, correlational survey data, no effect size printed): belonging practice went with a lower perceived cost, the value component's other half. Expect belonging to slip over a first year even when the exercise works, so repeat the contact rather than treating the exercise as done. The contact routine is modelled in Engagement.
  6. Once expectancy and value are in place, set a specific, challenging goal. A goal the learner accepts and believes reachable, with progress they can read against it. Evidence: Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [~M] (one narrative review; conditional on commitment and ability). What the goal should be is modelled in Goal Setting and Monitoring.
  7. Make progress visible and let the learner record it. A short end-of-session record (three minutes) of what was done against the goal, or a checklist of steps completed. Evidence: Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis; motivation is not measured). From the earlier page: learners continue when they can see improvement.
  8. Do not let novelty, rewards or a motivational message carry the design. A surprising hook is fine if it shows the idea to be learned; a points system is a small, uncertain addition, tied to progress rather than to taking part; a message that ability can grow helps, if at all, alongside strategy instruction and a setting where effort changes results, and only where the teacher and the peer group act on it: if the teacher treats ability as fixed, change the teacher's practice first or leave the message out. Evidence: Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~S] (a brief online intervention raised lower-achieving ninth-graders' grades by about a tenth of a grade point; two meta-analyses found weak effects overall); GPA treatment effects among lower-achieving students were larger in schools whose peer norms supported challenge-seeking [~M] (same trial: 0.14–0.18 grade points where peers sought challenge, a moderation analysis rather than a randomized contrast); Growth mindset interventions improve math outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged and high-risk students, but effects depend on a supportive teacher-mindset context [~M] (a research overview reporting a later national trial in which only students of teachers who held a growth mindset improved their math grades, and no difference for students of fixed-mindset teachers); The growth mindset intervention was estimated to prevent about 5.3% of US lower-achieving ninth-graders per year from being off track for graduation [+M] (pre-registered secondary outcome: the share of lower achievers below a 2.0 GPA fell from 46% to 41%; small per student, which is why it is cheap and worth pairing, not why it can carry a design); the hook and points evidence is on Engagement.
  9. Judge the design on two outcomes, not one. Record the behaviour from step 1 and a short probe of the target. Enjoyment ratings at the end of a session are not evidence that either moved: in one study, pupils' ratings of introductory activities correlated weakly with near tests and not with later exams (Positive motivational assessments of introductory activities correlate weakly with learning outcomes on near-in-time tests but not with distant exam results [~M]).

Across sessions (an untested arc; adjust from the behaviour and the probe):

  • Weeks 1–2: the teacher arranges early success, states the reason for each task, sets process goals, makes personal contact each session, and asks the four questions once.
  • Weeks 3–6: learners write their own process goals and their own relevance sentences at each new unit, keep their own progress record, and are offered their first choices. Teacher checks drop to once per work block.
  • After that: outcome goals, wider choices, fewer teacher prompts. Ask the four questions again at each transition (a new unit, a failed assessment, a move to harder material), because the low component can change. Expect a dip some weeks into a block of repeated assessments even when nothing in the design changed: across 71,405 law exams, grades fell by 0.24 grading points in the eighth week after a learner's first exam in an area, more on fine-weather days, which the authors read as a motivational cycle (A pronounced drop in performance occurs at week 8 after the first exam in an area of law, which is attributed to cyclic drops in motivation [~M], associational; the motivational reading is the authors' inference). Plan a check-in and a fresh near goal before that point rather than reading the dip as a lasting change.
  • Moving on: withdraw a support when the behaviour has held for three consecutive sessions and the probe has not fallen; reinstate it if the behaviour drops for two sessions running. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can ask the four questions, put them in the first activity of a self-paced course as a short form, and route learners to the matching first activity (an easier first item; a relevance prompt; a choice of topic; a welcome naming who to contact). If the probe shows no learning while the behaviour has risen, change the task, not the motivation supports.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which component is low for these learners. The same withdrawal can come from doubt about success, no reason to care, resentment of control, or not belonging, and each calls for a different move. If unstated, ask: what does the learner do when the task starts, and what do they say when asked why they stopped or why they did it at all?
  • Whether taking part is voluntary. In a required course, motivation shows in effort and quality; in a voluntary one (an open online course, an optional programme, adult learning in spare time) it shows first as dropout, and anything that discourages early can cost the learner entirely. If unstated, ask: can the learner leave, and what happens if they do?
  • Who can see a learner's effort and respond, and how often. A tutor with one learner can diagnose and adjust each session; a teacher with thirty, a volunteer with ten minutes, or an unattended platform cannot. If unstated, ask: how often does anyone look at each learner's work or attendance, and for how long?

Most rows carry a claim to learners or settings it did not test (belonging evidence is from college entry, the praise evidence from fifth graders and toddlers, the pretest evidence from one open online course, task value from reviews of school and college, the ability signal from one district's AP enrolment, the AI evidence from programming courses, the attention evidence from laboratory tasks rather than lessons, the remote-learning evidence from focus groups in one emergency term), and each row says so.

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: whether and when a learner starts, how long they continue after a difficulty, whether they finish, whether they return to optional work, what they choose when given options, what they say about why they are doing it, and their confidence rating before an item against their result on it. "Motivated", "lazy", "has low self-efficacy" and "doesn't care" are inferences. A survey of motivation is a self-report, and a learner who complies under pressure can look motivated.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Starts, then stops at the first difficulty Low expectancy (reads the difficulty as inability, more likely after a long history of failure in the subject); the task is too hard; no reason to push through; frustration aimed at the teacher or the course rather than at the problem Give an easier next item and see whether the learner continues; ask what they think the difficulty means and, after a success, what they think made it work (luck or "it was easy" points to expectancy); ask what the task is for; ask what the frustration is about: adult learners interviewed described frustration at not understanding a concept as driving them on, and frustration blamed on teachers or the curriculum as stopping them (Frustration at not understanding a concept is perceived to motivate exploration and persistence, while frustration attributed to teachers or curriculum impairs motivation [~W], one interview study, the authors' interpretation).
Does the work only when it is graded or rewarded Low ownership (the incentive is the reason); low value (no other reason); cannot do the task without the incentive's structure Remove the incentive for one optional task but keep the structure and a stated reason, and record voluntary return; give a short unaided probe.
Says the work is pointless, but succeeds when made to do it Low value, with expectancy intact; the stated reason does not connect to anything the learner wants; cost (time, embarrassment) is high Ask what the learner wants to be able to do; offer a choice of topic tied to it; make the first attempt private and compare.
Confident before the item, wrong on it, and unbothered Overconfidence (self-efficacy judgments match behaviour poorly while learning); low value, so the error does not matter; misread the task Ask for a confidence rating on several items and compare with results; ask what would happen if this were wrong at work or in an exam.
Withdraws in class, works at home or online Low belonging or fear of public failure; a relationship difficulty; the class pace Offer a private channel for answers in class; ask who they would ask for help; compare participation in pairs with whole-class participation.

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking about motivation is itself an intervention and can change it, so record each question asked, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim tests the matching relationship: none diagnoses which component is low and compares a matched move with an unmatched one. The claims below each bear on one component or one move.

  • Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]: two narrative reviews (Bandura's book and Pajares 1996), coded q2, read without source text and with no effect size. They report self-efficacy as a strong predictor of academic performance and persistence, task-specific rather than global, and built most by mastery experiences. Predictive and review-level: it supports treating expectancy as a component to assess; it does not show that a move raising self-efficacy raises persistence. The earlier page's [+S] was above the claim's recorded evidence and is lowered to M.
  • Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M]: two reviews of expectancy-value research (Eccles & Wigfield 2002; Wigfield & Cambria 2010), read without source text. Expectancy and value predict academic choices and effort beyond prior performance; brief utility-value writing exercises are reported to raise engagement and grades, especially for students from underrepresented groups; the cost of a task is named as underused. The writing exercise is reported second-hand and no effect size is printed. Held at M, below its S cap, for that reason.
  • Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M]: a narrative review of the self-determination framework, the 128-experiment reward meta-analysis (d = −0.4 for expected tangible rewards on already-interesting tasks) and a conceptual framework of three kinds of classroom autonomy support. Its one experimental basis is about rewards as a controlling condition, so the direct test of autonomy support is review-level; held at M below its cap, as on Autonomy. It does not predict learning gains.
  • Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]: two competing meta-analyses. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (128 experiments) found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced free-choice engagement after the reward was withdrawn (d = −0.28 to −0.40), more for children than college students, while positive verbal feedback raised it. Cameron and Pierce (96 experiments) found no overall undermining, with a minimal negative effect for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Both agree on that case and on praise. The outcome is free-choice persistence and interest in mostly laboratory tasks, not learning.
  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: one experiment with high-school students learning writing revision (process goals beat outcome goals; shifting to outcome goals once the skill was mastered could help) and a review chapter. No effect size or sample size recorded. It supports near, process-focused goals for learners whose expectancy is low.
  • Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes [+M]: two randomized experiments at college entry, both read as abstracts. A brief exercise framing adversity as common and transient raised African American students' grades over three years (92 students), and an online version under 30 minutes raised first-year full-time completion across 26,911 students at 22 institutions, concentrated in groups that had progressed at lower rates and only where the institution afforded them opportunities to belong. No effect size recorded. Held at M, below its cap, because neither effect size was read.
  • Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~S]: the limiting claim. A pre-registered national experiment (12,490 ninth-graders) found a brief online growth-mindset intervention raised lower-achieving students' core-course grades by 0.10 grade points (standardized difference 0.11, coded i0), larger where peer norms supported the message; two meta-analyses (abstract) found the mindset–achievement relation and intervention effects weak overall, with somewhat more support for low-SES or at-risk students. A message about motivation, on its own, moves achievement little, and the claim's title is stronger than its entries.
  • Positive motivational assessments of introductory activities correlate weakly with learning outcomes on near-in-time tests but not with distant exam results [~M]: a second limit, on measurement. Elementary pupils' ratings of how interesting, instructive and fun introductory activities were correlated weakly with near tests (for example r = .36) and not with later exams (N = 172). Reported enjoyment is not a stand-in for learning.
  • A personalized ability signal increased the probability of participating in AP classes by 49 percentage points among surveyed students [+M]: a regression-discontinuity analysis of a PSAT-based personal message in Oakland. Among surveyed students the message raised AP participation by 49 percentage points, and they enrolled in and passed about one more AP course the next year (Students who received the ability signal enrolled in and passed about one more AP course the following year [+M]); no standardized effect size is printed, and the estimate is for the surveyed subgroup. The one causal claim here on a move aimed at expectancy, with a choice (enrolment) as outcome, not persistence or learning. A separate issue-brief write-up of the same study is kept on the claim page.
  • Within SDT, intrinsic motivation predicts intended effort most strongly, followed by identified regulation, with introjected regulation much weaker [+M]: an SEM on 511 second-language learners; intrinsic motivation predicted intended effort most strongly (standardized coefficient .58), identified regulation next (.33), introjected regulation least (.13). It supports the ownership component's direction (reasons the learner holds go with more intended effort than pressure does) and is associational and self-reported.
  • Teacher support is the strongest predictor of school belonging [+M]: a meta-analysis of 51 studies (N = 67,378) in which teacher support and positive personal characteristics were the strongest of ten correlates of school belonging; no effect size in the text read. It supports making teacher contact the first belonging move for school-age learners; it does not show that raising teacher support raises belonging.
  • Girls show less confidence and interest in math and science from early adolescence, and a strong math/science self-concept predicts course choice and performance for both genders [+M]: an IES practice guide's review of correlational and longitudinal studies: from early adolescence girls report less confidence and interest in math and science, and a strong self-concept predicts course choice and performance for both genders. Predictive, like the self-efficacy claim; no effect sizes printed.
  • A CCSR research review concludes that four academic mindsets (belonging, growth beliefs, self-efficacy and value) increase perseverance and improve academic behaviors [+W] and Instruction should present the learning task as engaging and meaningful and promote positive expectations of success, because effort requires that learners value the task and believe they can succeed [+W]: a practitioner review and an ERIC digest that state the components this page uses; neither reports data.
  • Praise before a task: Under task-involving feedback, pre-task person praise reduces motivation and indirectly lowers task performance; under ego-involving feedback it indirectly raises performance [~M]: one experiment (146 students) in which praising the person before the task changed how many attempts students made, and through that their performance, in opposite directions depending on whether feedback was about mastering the task or a public comparison. It extends the praise caution from after success to before the task, for the mastery-focused feedback this page recommends; it does not recommend person praise where feedback is competitive, because public comparison is itself a cost the value step tries to lower. The same experiment's main effect is the stronger point for this page: Students generally attempted fewer items under ego-involving than task-involving feedback, but this gap disappeared among praised students [+M] (b = −1.5 attempted items for comparison feedback; −2.78 without praise, −0.2 and not significant with it). Feedback about mastering the task kept students attempting more items than feedback ranking them. Process praise to toddlers predicts fourth grade math and reading comprehension achievement via children's incremental motivational frameworks [+M] is the long-horizon, associational counterpart (53 children).
  • Goals and confidence in adults: Dispositional learning goals positively predict memory self-efficacy in younger and older adults [+M] and Memory self-efficacy positively predicts memory performance, mediating the indirect effects of both goal orientations [+M]: one SEM of 216 younger and older adults, β = .28 (learning goals to self-efficacy) and .16 (self-efficacy to recall). Associational and dispositional; it carries the expectancy component to adult and older learners but does not test a move that sets learning goals.
  • Where a mindset message works: GPA treatment effects among lower-achieving students were larger in schools whose peer norms supported challenge-seeking [~M], Growth mindset interventions improve math outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged and high-risk students, but effects depend on a supportive teacher-mindset context [~M] and The growth mindset intervention was estimated to prevent about 5.3% of US lower-achieving ninth-graders per year from being off track for graduation [+M]: the national trial's moderation by peer norms (β = 0.23 on the moderation, 0.14–0.18 grade points in supportive schools), a policy review's report of a later trial in which only students of growth-mindset teachers improved, and the trial's pre-registered off-track outcome (46% to 41% of lower achievers below a 2.0 GPA). Together they turn step 8's caution into a condition: the message is worth adding only where teachers and peers act on it. The moderators were measured, not assigned. A general chemistry utility-value and growth mindset intervention showed statistically significant positive outcomes overall but statistically nonsignificant outcomes for underrepresented students [~M] adds one randomized course in which combined value and mindset prompts helped overall and did not reach significance for underrepresented students (no effect sizes printed).
  • Belonging over the first year: First-year students' sense of belonging to their university predicts intentions to persist, controlling for background variables and other persistence predictors [+M] and A belonging-enhancing intervention produced a smaller decline in sense of belonging over the first year than the control groups [+M]: one study with three survey waves and random assignment to an intervention or two controls; no effect size in the text read. School belonging mediates the higher intrinsic motivation of Dominican children relative to European-American peers [+W]: one associational study in middle childhood; the group difference is carried by belonging, which is the variable a design can act on, not the group. Instructional practices aligned to the Belonging MDP were associated with significant declines in students' perceptions that science required too much effort or threatened self-esteem [+W] and Students' perceptions of motivationally supportive instruction were associated with higher positive motivational beliefs [+W]: one WestEd development report on a programme for secondary science teachers, correlational, no effect sizes; it links belonging practice to lower perceived cost, not to learning.
  • Whether the low component holds still: A majority of adolescents (about 63%) followed stable trajectories of physics and chemistry motivation across middle and high school [~M] (growth mixture model, grades 7 to 12: Stable High 26.6% and Stable Moderate 36.2% in ability self-concept and task value; the other groups changed) and During a year-long AI curriculum, most students maintained or moved upward in motivational profile, with substantial upward transitions out of the Disengaged profile [~M] (2,086 students over one year; of the Disengaged, 43% moved to Developing and 21% to Self-Determined; no comparison group, so the curriculum is not shown to have caused it). Together they argue against assuming a general adolescent decline and for asking each learner again at transitions: most profiles hold, and a sizeable minority move in either direction. A pronounced drop in performance occurs at week 8 after the first exam in an area of law, which is attributed to cyclic drops in motivation [~M] adds a short-horizon dip within a run of exams (0.24 grading points across 71,405 law exams; associational, motivation inferred).
  • Attention difficulties: Children with ADHD show higher external regulation, introjected regulation and amotivation, and lower intrinsic motivation and identified regulation, toward learning than TD children [~M] (questionnaire, r = 0.59 for external regulation, 0.29 for amotivation) and Increased motivation eliminated ADHD–TD differences on most attention measures but differences persisted in complex multitasking and commission errors [~M] (reward and competition during laboratory attention tasks; the multitasking difference shrank to r = 0.28 and stayed): one study of 60 children. It qualifies the reward caution for in-task performance in this group; it says nothing about later voluntary engagement, which is what the reward meta-analyses measured.
  • Value from interest and the lesson itself: Students' perception of science instruction as interesting is positively associated with their emotional engagement in science [+M] and The association between perceiving science as interesting and emotional engagement is stronger in grades 4, 6, 7, 8, and 12 than in grade 3, with the steepest increase in the grade-6 transition year [~M] (6,465 students, b = 0.32, steepest at grade 6); L2 Learning Experience Beats Ideal L2 Self [+M] (511 second-language learners, paths .71 against .25). All associational and self-reported.
  • Expectancy after long failure: Prolonged reading failure leads students to attribute failure to fixed low ability and define reading as a failure situation [+W]: a 1989 ERIC paper reporting an earlier experiment with fifth-grade readers, in which poor readers credited success to luck or task ease rather than ability. Second-hand; it supports asking how a learner explains a success, not a particular repair.
  • Generative AI and effort: Nearly all students valued learning through effort while feeling drawn toward AI, and split into those who limited use and those whose use conflicted with stated values [~W], Peer AI use diminished some students' sense of their own effort's value through social comparison, independent of their own AI use [~W], ChatGPT-assisted students reported substantially lower ownership of their submitted code across self-attributed ownership, psychological ownership, and authorship attribution [~M], Students rated the LLM-based assignments as useful, challenging, engaging, and enjoyable at both the first and fifth iterations, with no significant change across the semester [+W] and Students' self-efficacy for using generative AI increased over the semester, with current self-efficacy significantly higher after the fifth assignment than after the first [+W]: interview, experimental and single-course survey evidence, all from computing or university courses. A second interview study adds career fear (Students express fear and apathy about AI's impact on their careers, shifting focus toward outcomes over process and questioning effort investment in learning computer science [~W]), and a systematic review repeats the deep-engagement concern second-hand and hedged (Easy access to AI-generated answers may reduce learners' motivation to engage deeply with material [~W]). They point at value (effort that peers can bypass looks worthless) rather than expectancy, and the positive ratings carry no comparison group or learning measure.
  • Expectancy from frequent checks, and difficulty: Intervention students reported significantly higher exam confidence than controls (M=4.3 vs 3.4 on a 5-point scale, d=0.76) [+M] (one A-level sciences study of high-frequency, automatically marked formative assessments; confidence measured once, after the intervention, by self-report) and Task complexity and desirable difficulties do not significantly affect intrinsic interest or expectancies for success [~M] (one experiment; non-significant, equivalence untested). The first supports early success the learner can see; the second says harder tasks did not detectably lower expectancy, so the expectancy step is not a reason to make tasks easy for everyone.
  • Value, cost and persistence: Students perceiving high task value exert greater effort and achieve more than students perceiving low value [+W] (a review reporting one study second-hand); Gaming participants did not report the negative consequences of task failure; low-stakes experimentation and regeneration supported perseverance [+W] (interviews with video gamers); Participants identified assessment redesign approaches for helping all students feel successful, centering relevance, lowered stakes, choice, emotional support, and preparation [+W] (one convening, qualitative); Students in courses with guided note-taking and other active learning activities reported less difficulty with motivation and engagement after the shift online [+W] (focus groups, 2020). Weak and mostly reported, they agree with lowering the cost of failure and giving learners something to do.
  • What a learner's frustration is about: Frustration at not understanding a concept is perceived to motivate exploration and persistence, while frustration attributed to teachers or curriculum impairs motivation [~W]: adult learners' interviews; it adds an explanation to the "stops at the first difficulty" row.
  • Persistence on platforms: Teachers treat resilience under challenge as a teachable skill that current routines and dashboards do little to support [~W]: a teacher workshop; it describes the abandon-after-failure response this page diagnoses as low expectancy, and the absence of tools that show it.
  • Teacher adoption of AI, one theoretical paper (Yurt 2024) read through expectancy-value theory: expectancy [+W]; attainment value [+W]; utility value [+W]; interest value [+W]; cost [+W]. No data; it carries the components to teachers as learners and names cost (time, resources, doubts about reliability) as the barrier to cut.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (laboratory tasks, high-school writers, ninth-graders, college entrants, elementary pupils, toddlers followed to fourth grade, older adults, second-language and programming students), and most report no standardized effect size, so do not rank them. None measures whether motivation raised by these moves lasts beyond a term, and none tests the matching rule.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do rewards and praise undermine voluntary engagement? Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [~S]
  • Does a learner's belief that they can succeed at a task predict whether they start and persist, and which goals and feedback raise it? Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]
  • Does seeing a task as interesting, important or useful raise the effort learners invest in it? Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals. [+M]
  • Where does a brief growth-mindset message raise grades? GPA treatment effects among lower-achieving students were larger in schools whose peer norms supported challenge-seeking [~M]; Growth mindset interventions improve math outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged and high-risk students, but effects depend on a supportive teacher-mindset context [~M]
  • Does praising the person, before or after a task, change effort? Under task-involving feedback, pre-task person praise reduces motivation and indirectly lowers task performance; under ego-involving feedback it indirectly raises performance [~M]
  • Does feedback that ranks learners against each other reduce effort compared with feedback about mastering the task? Students generally attempted fewer items under ego-involving than task-involving feedback, but this gap disappeared among praised students [+M]
  • Does motivation in a subject hold steady across adolescence, or move? A majority of adolescents (about 63%) followed stable trajectories of physics and chemistry motivation across middle and high school [~M]
  • Does a belonging exercise at entry hold belonging up over a first year? A belonging-enhancing intervention produced a smaller decline in sense of belonging over the first year than the control groups [+M]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a learning outcome (a capability on a stated measure at a stated time), with motivation as the route to it; sometimes motivation is itself the objective (continued study, voluntary practice, choosing the next course). Say which, because they are measured differently. Then ask the learner, separately, what they want from the work: to pass, to get a job, to be able to help their children, to be left alone, to avoid looking foolish. The answer is also the material for the value component: a reason the learner does not share is one more instruction. Agreement, divergence and "I don't know" are all observations.

For example, an adult learner in an evening English class may want to talk with their child's teacher at a parents' evening, while the designer's objective for the term is accurate use of past tenses on a written test. A relevance exercise about "English for your career" misses the learner's goal; a role-play of the parents' evening that needs past tenses ("what did she do this week?") serves both, and the written test still checks the designer's objective. Record attendance and the test separately, so that a full room is not read as the accuracy the course set out to build, or a good test score as the conversation the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The matching rule should be dropped if comparisons show that a move aimed at the component a learner reports as low does no better than the same move given to everyone, or than the move aimed at another component. It should weaken if self-reports of expectancy, value, ownership and belonging cannot be told apart in practice, or do not predict which move helps. The cautions about rewards and generic messages should change if expected tangible rewards for taking part turn out not to lower later voluntary engagement in real courses, or if brief mindset messages show effects well beyond a tenth of a grade point without strategy support. If motivation raised by these moves raises learning on aligned measures whatever the task, the condition "only through a task that demands thinking" should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong component" after the fact.

Starting, effort, voluntary persistence, enjoyment, reported motivation and learning of the target are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how to diagnose the low component reliably, how long a raised level lasts, or how much motivation is enough.

Related Principles¶

  • Learner Choice — one route to stronger ownership and autonomy
  • Goal Setting & Monitoring — motivation often strengthens when learners can see progress toward meaningful goals
  • Engagement — motivation and engagement are closely linked but not identical
  • Self-Regulated Learning — what a motivated learner does next: planning, monitoring and choosing a strategy
  • Authentic Audiences and Purposes — a real audience as a source of task value
  • Supports To Raise Teacher Expectancy And Value For Ai Use
  • Assessment for Learning — formative assessment frames errors as information, the structural condition under which growth framing works
  • Cognitive Activation — challenging, effortful tasks give learners authentic evidence that strategy and effort produce improvement
  • Active Learning — creates the productive struggle that growth framing helps learners interpret adaptively
  • Collaborative Learning — peer norms that normalize struggle sustain mindset messages beyond the initial intervention
  • Error Analysis — growth mindset depends on treating mistakes as useful evidence rather than failure.
  • Check-ins — regular low-stakes check-ins normalize partial understanding and continued improvement.
  • Positive Self-Talk — internal language can reinforce or undermine growth-oriented interpretations of effort and difficulty.

Examples¶

  • Build students' intrinsic motivation and share learning sciences principles with students to support buy-in and ownership
  • Reduce cheating holistically by enhancing perceived assessment purpose, fostering self-efficacy, and increasing perceived costs of cheating
  • Classroom strategy set for supporting students in foster care: buddies, helper tasks, structure, earned rewards, small successes, sincere praise, kept promises, high expectations, clean slates, and assumption-free assignments
  • Process-oriented instructor feedback: Replacing "You're good at this" with specific feedback about what strategy worked and what to try next.
  • Revision routines: Asking learners to resubmit work after feedback so improvement is expected, not exceptional.
  • Public modeling of struggle: Instructors narrate how they handle confusion, dead ends, and correction while learning a new tool or skill.
  • Reflection prompts after mistakes: Short prompts such as "What did this attempt teach you?" or "What strategy will you change next time?"
  • Cultivate positive academic mindsets through instructional practices aligned with students' psychological needs
  • Growth-mindset-informed math teaching practices: norms, no fixed labels, mixed-ability grouping, process praise, and formative assessment with retakes
  • Reinforce growth mindsets through explicit messages plus implicit classroom practices
  • Chatbot support should reinforce that learning ability develops through effort, strategy use, feedback, and reflection
  • Introduce student teachers to mindset theory early and foster a learning orientation through classroom language, feedback and attitude to challenge
  • Combine metacognitive strategy information with motivational interventions that increase task value, and reduce need frustration
  • Raise serotonin through morning sunlight, timed tryptophan intake and exercise, and gratitude practices to support tolerance for failure
  • Build autonomy-supportive learning contexts through perspective-taking, choice, self-initiation, and non-controlling language
  • Need-supportive 4C/ID learning environment with 12 integrated autonomy, competence and relatedness implementations
  • Design assessment as information, not reward and punishment, and provide an engaging curriculum with choice and community

Illustrative¶

Learner Choice — Meaningful options can increase ownership when they remain aligned to shared goals.

Game-Based Learning — Carefully designed challenge, progression, and feedback can increase willingness to persist.

Visible progress routines — Learners are more likely to continue when they can see improvement, checkpoint completion, or movement toward a meaningful target.
