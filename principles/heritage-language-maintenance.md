# Preserve Heritage Language While Acquiring an Additional Language

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Most children worldwide grow up bilingual or multilingual; the United States is unusual in its relative monolingualism and in schooling practices that sometimes implicitly treat a student's home language as an obstacle to English acquisition rather than an asset. Bilingual proficiency ranges from balanced fluency in both languages to more uneven competence across domains, and many bilingual students in U.S. schools are in the process of losing proficiency in a heritage language while still developing English — a pattern of language attrition that research links to *slower*, not faster, English development: a strong vocabulary base in a first language facilitates rather than competes with acquiring a second (Hansen, Umeda, & McKinney, 2002). Fully proficient bilingual learners also show cognitive advantages in metalinguistic awareness — the ability to reflect on language as an object, not just use it — and in flexibility of concept expression (Jimenez, Garcia, & Pearson, 1995; Francis, 2006).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Meaningful, ongoing opportunities to actually use the heritage language, not just passive exposure at home
- Adult and peer models fluent in the heritage language
- Family and school support for heritage-language maintenance rather than pressure toward English-only use
- Assessment conducted in both languages where possible, so that language loss (attrition) is not mistaken for a language disorder
#### Constraints
- Negative attitudes toward a heritage language, or pressure to assimilate, can accelerate language loss and its downstream costs to English development
- Unbalanced bilingualism (strong in one language, weak in the other) limits the cognitive and academic advantages associated with full bilingual proficiency
- Speech-language intervention conducted only in English is less effective for multilingual children with language impairments than intervention that uses both of the child's languages (Kohnert, Yim, Nett, Kan, & Duran, 2005)

### Target Learners
- Students from multilingual homes, which represents a global majority of children
- English Language Learners in U.S. and other Anglophone school settings
- Children of immigrant or refugee families, where heritage-language loss risk is often highest

### Target Learning Objectives
- Continued development of heritage-language proficiency alongside the school/majority language, not instead of it
- Metalinguistic awareness and the cognitive flexibility associated with full bilingual competence
- Accurate identification of language-development needs (distinguishing typical attrition from an actual disorder)

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Language Acquisition Theory](../theories/language-acquisition-theory.md) [+M] — the social-interactionist account's emphasis on responsive interaction applies to whichever language(s) a child is developing
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) [+M] — bilingual learners construct meaning across, and connect concepts between, multiple language systems
