# Chunking

## Description
Chunking is the instructional design strategy of breaking complex information or tasks into smaller, more manageable units (chunks) to align with the limited capacity of human working memory. By organizing discrete items into familiar, meaningful groups, chunking reduces intrinsic cognitive load and enhances learning retention.

## Implications
Chunking matters because learners cannot process unlimited novelty at once. When information is grouped into meaningful units, working memory can handle it more efficiently and learners are more likely to retain the underlying structure rather than just the surface details [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S]. The design challenge is that chunks are not universal: novices usually need smaller, more explicit units, while more experienced learners can handle larger integrated structures [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [~M]. Good chunking therefore reduces overload without hiding how the parts connect into a coherent whole.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Logical Grouping**: Information must be organized into units that share a conceptual, functional, or structural relationship.
- **Hierarchical Structure**: Effective chunking often requires clear headings, bulleted lists, and a visual hierarchy to signal the boundaries of each chunk.
- **Fading Support**: For complex procedures, chunks should gradually be integrated into larger holistic units as learner expertise grows.

#### Constraints
- **Prior Knowledge Dependence**: What constitutes a "chunk" depends on the learner's existing schemas; an expert can process larger, more complex chunks than a novice [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [~M].
- **Over-segmentation**: Breaking information into too many tiny pieces can disrupt the learner's understanding of the global task structure or the relationships between parts.

### Target Learners
- **Novice Learners**: Benefit most from smaller, more frequent chunks to prevent cognitive overload [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S].
- **Learners with Low Working Memory Capacity**: Essential for managing the processing burden during acquisition of new concepts.
- **Learners with Attention Deficits**: Helps maintain focus by providing natural stopping points and "bite-sized" goals.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Knowledge Retention**: Storing complex conceptual information in long-term memory.
- **Procedural Fluency**: Learning step-by-step processes by mastering individual stages before integration.
- **Reading Comprehension**: Improving the processing of dense or technical texts by segmenting them into thematic blocks.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [[theories/information-processing-theory|Information Processing Theory]] — identifies chunking as a key mechanism for transferring info from working memory to long-term memory.
- [[theories/cognitive-load-theory|Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller)]] — chunking is the primary method for reducing intrinsic cognitive load [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S].
- **Miller's Law (Miller 1956)** — establishes the average working memory limit of 7 (±2) units.

### Claims
- [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S] — organizing information into meaningful units lowers working-memory burden and improves manageability

## Related Principles
- [[principles/scaffolding-and-fading|Scaffolding and Fading]] — chunking is often the first step in a scaffolded instructional sequence.
- [[principles/worked-examples|Worked Examples]] — can be chunked to show the sub-steps of a problem solution.
- [[principles/graphic-organizers|Graphic Organizers]] — provide a visual framework for how chunks relate to each other.

## Examples
- A chemistry lesson breaks stoichiometry into interpreting formulas, balancing equations, and converting quantities before asking learners to coordinate the full procedure.
- A professional training module presents one decision rule per screen with a recap diagram after every three steps so learners can integrate the parts into the larger workflow.
- A coding tutorial introduces one new construct at a time, then combines earlier chunks into a small working program once each part is stable.
