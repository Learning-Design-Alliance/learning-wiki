Redundancy Principle¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 22 claims (6 for, 16 mixed) · 17 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 17 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision: whether to present the same information twice, most often the same words both spoken and written, and more broadly a second source (a caption, an explanatory note, a transcript) that repeats what another source already carries. The answer depends on what else is on the screen and who is learning.

When a picture, diagram or animation is explained by narration, adding on-screen text that repeats the narration word for word is expected to lower transfer for learners new to the content. In Mayer's programme, learners given animation and narration did better on a transfer test than learners given the same animation, narration and on-screen text in 5 of 5 experiments, median d = 0.72. A 63-study review places the harm in the same place: written text competing with a visualization. When no picture competes for the eyes, the same words spoken and written do not hurt: across a meta-analysis of verbal redundancy they did not differ from written-only presentations and beat spoken-only ones, with the advantage largest for second-language and less fluent readers. And as expertise grows, explanatory text that novices need becomes redundant: experienced trainees did better with a diagram alone than with the text integrated into it.

The relationship is conditional on six things, each from the evidence below:

  • Whether a visualization is present. Spoken plus written words beat spoken-only words with no image (g = 0.45) but not when images or animation were included (g = 0.06 in both cases) (Adesope & Nesbit 2012 [~M]). The Mayer harm was measured with animations.
  • Who the readers are. The spoken–written advantage was g = 0.82 for nonfluent first-language readers, 0.58 for second-language readers and 0.24 for fluent readers; for low prior knowledge 0.29, for high prior knowledge −0.05 (a marginal difference).
  • Pacing. The spoken–written advantage appeared for system-paced material (g = 0.34) and not for learner-paced material (g = 0.12); multimedia design principles in general mattered more in system-paced media (g = 0.41 against 0.27 learner-paced; Noetel et al. 2022).
  • Verbatim or abridged. Key terms extracted from the narration went with better outcomes than verbatim duplicated text; the review reports abridged text as less harmful than verbatim.
  • The outcome measured. The spoken–written advantage was g = 0.53 on retention and 0.01 on transfer; 0.63 on multiple-choice tests against 0.11 on mixed formats. Mayer's harm from duplicated text was measured on transfer.
  • Age. In a middle-school quasi-experiment on electricity, adding written text to narrated animation made no detectable difference; the review lists learner age among the moderators that reverse the effect.

How this sits inside its umbrella: Multimedia Learning states the general relationship (a relevant picture with the words that explain it) and names redundancy as one of its qualifications; this page is that qualification in depth and does not repeat the umbrella's model. It borders three neighbours. The modality question (speak the words or print them, when a picture is present) is the umbrella's; this page starts after it is answered, and asks whether to add the printed copy back. Split attention concerns two sources that each need the other (Spatially integrating text and diagrams [+M]); redundancy concerns a source that is intelligible alone, so the remedy is removal, not integration. Coherence concerns material that is irrelevant; redundant material is relevant but repeated. Behind all three sits Cognitive Load Management: redundancy is one source of avoidable load, and as with the other supports, what helps a novice can cost an expert.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

The claims establish when duplication hurts and when it helps; none sets a dose (how many on-screen words, how long a segment, when to withdraw text). The rules below follow the evidence; the numbers and the progression are untested proposals.

  1. Inventory each segment's channels. For every screen, slide or page, list what is spoken, what is written and what is pictured, and mark each written item as verbatim (repeats the narration), abridged (key terms of it) or complementary (says something the narration does not). (Untested proposal: the audit itself.)
  2. Narrated picture or animation: remove the verbatim on-screen text. Keep narration plus picture, and at most a few key terms (untested proposal: about one to five words per screen) placed on the parts of the picture they name. Basis: Redundancy principle: animation plus narration beats animation, narration, and on-screen text for transfer [+M]; Redundant on-screen text impairs learning when it competes with a visualization [~M]; key terms over verbatim text from On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions [~S].
  3. Words with no picture (an audio explanation, a read-aloud, a lecture recording without slides): give the words both spoken and written for second-language, less fluent or low-prior-knowledge readers, and especially for system-paced, unsegmented audio. Basis: Spoken–written advantages are larger for second-language and nonfluent first-language readers and for low prior knowledge learners [+M]; Spoken–written presentations do not outperform written-only presentations but do outperform spoken-only presentations [~M].
  4. Self-paced reading pages: written text alone is enough for fluent readers; offer the audio on demand rather than by default. Basis: spoken–written did not beat written-only (g = −0.04), and learner-paced material showed no detectable advantage (system-paced only [~M]).
  5. Video captions: on by default for second-language learners and for anyone who needs them for access; a toggle for everyone else. Basis: Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+M]; the toggle for fluent viewers carries step 2 to video, an untested proposal.
  6. Across sessions, withdraw explanatory text as expertise grows. Early sessions (learners new to the diagrams or notation): explanatory text integrated into the diagram. Once a learner can explain a diagram from the diagram alone (untested proposal: on two consecutive sessions, on a diagram not seen before), switch that learner to diagram-only material and keep the text available on request. If the unaided explanation fails, return the text. Basis: The split-attention benefit for novices reverses into a redundancy effect for more experienced learners [~M]; Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [~M]; the switching rule is an untested proposal.
  7. Segment length is not set by these claims. Keep each narrated segment to one step of the explanation (untested proposal: two to four minutes), and note that the spoken–written advantage was smaller for segmented presentations (g = 0.15) than unsegmented ones (g = 0.53) (segmentation moderates verbal redundancy [~M]): once audio is segmented, written duplicates matter less.
  8. Judge the configuration on a transfer item, not on a retention or multiple-choice test alone, where duplication looks better than it is. Basis: Spoken–written advantages are larger for retention outcomes and multiple-choice test formats [~M]. Use the brief's own assessment when it gives one; add no test it did not ask for, and instead read its transfer items, if it has any, separately.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: is a picture, diagram or animation on screen while the words are spoken? (if yes, remove verbatim text; if no, spoken plus written can help); how fluent are the learners as readers of this language, and how much do they already know of this content?; and who controls the pace (a video or lecture that runs on, or pages and pause controls the learner uses)?

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Redundancy principle: animation plus narration beats animation, narration, and on-screen text for transfer [+M]: Mayer's (2008) review of his group's computer-based lessons on lightning and environmental science; 5 of 5 experiments, median d = 0.72 on transfer. One research programme, short lessons, immediate tests.
  • Redundant on-screen text impairs learning when it competes with a visualization, though written text duplicating narration alone can help [~M]: Trypke et al.'s (2023) review of 63 studies (44 adding written text to narrated visualizations). Negative effects of written text competing with a visualization; positive effects of written text added to narration with no visualization; reversals for older learners, learner-paced delivery and abridged text. No pooled effect size.
  • On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions [~S]: Kalyuga et al. (1999), where removing text that duplicated narration improved learning from a diagram lesson (no effect size recorded), and Adesope & Nesbit's (2012) meta-analysis, which qualifies the title: verbal redundancy was not harmful in any configuration it reports, and key terms beat verbatim text.
  • Verbal redundancy (spoken–written presentation) yields a small overall learning benefit with large between-study heterogeneity [~M]: 57 effect sizes from 33 reports (N = 3,452), g = 0.15 [0.08, 0.22], I² = .82. The average hides the moderators below.
  • Spoken–written presentations do not outperform written-only presentations but do outperform spoken-only presentations [~M]: g = −0.04 against written-only, 0.29 against spoken-only.
  • Verbal redundancy does not facilitate learning when materials include images or animation [~M]: g = 0.45 without images, 0.06 with images or animation.
  • Spoken–written advantages are larger for second-language and nonfluent first-language readers and for low prior knowledge learners [+M]: fluency levels differed significantly; the prior-knowledge difference was marginal.
  • The spoken–written advantage over spoken-only presentation appears for system-paced but not learner-paced materials [~M]: g = 0.34 (k = 24) against 0.12 (k = 10); the authors' explanation is that a learner who misses something in a system-paced presentation cannot go back.
  • Nonsegmented spoken–written presentations show a larger advantage over spoken-only presentation than segmented ones [~M] and Spoken–written advantages are larger for retention outcomes and multiple-choice test formats [~M]: further Adesope & Nesbit moderators.
  • Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+M]: Noetel et al. (2022) reporting Montero Perez et al. (2013), g = 0.99 (k = 15) and 0.87 (k = 10). Captions are written words duplicating speech; for second-language viewers they help.
  • Multimedia design principles improve learning by g = 0.38 on average across the 11 largest reviews, an effect the authors call moderate, with large differences between principles [~M]: in Noetel et al.'s comparison of principles, verbal redundancy was among the weakest. Design principle effects are larger in system-paced and in complex media, and learner prior knowledge was not a significant moderator (p = 0.14) [~M]: across principles, not for redundancy alone.
  • The redundancy effect does not hold for middle school students [~M]: Kutbay & Akpınar (2020), a quasi-experiment; no significant difference with abstract or concrete animation. A non-significant difference is not equivalence.
  • Redundant text in the diagram did not affect posttest accuracy or difficulty ratings [~M]: Clinton et al. (2016), N = 198, a print lesson on posterior probability; content redundancy between text and diagram made no detectable difference.
  • The split-attention benefit for novices reverses into a redundancy effect for more experienced learners [~M] and Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [~M]: Kalyuga et al. (2003) reporting Kalyuga et al. (1998) with electrical trainees and Yeung et al. (1998) with explanatory notes in text; no effect sizes recorded.

Taken together: with a picture on screen, verbatim narration-plus-text costs transfer for novices in short system-paced lessons (one programme's experiments, supported by the review); without a picture, spoken plus written words help less fluent and low-knowledge readers and do no harm, with the gains concentrated on retention and multiple-choice tests; for experienced learners, explanatory text becomes the redundant source. No claim in the wiki sets a dose, tests the withdrawal schedule, or tests redundancy in live lectures, VR, or with beginning readers.

The evidence by question¶

Each question links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it (this page's synthesis spec, built by scripts/synthesize_claim.py; one row per distinct study, no averaged verdict).

  • When a picture or animation is narrated, does adding on-screen text that repeats the narration hurt learning? Redundant on-screen text impairs learning when it competes with a visualization [~M].
  • Without a competing picture, do the same words spoken and written help or hurt, and for whom? On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions [~S].
  • Does text that helps novices become redundant for experienced learners? Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [~M].

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Redundancy Effect Impairs Learning [~M]: the same Trypke et al. (2023) entry as redundancy-principle, cited above; its title states the harm without the review's reversals.
  • Multimedia Principles Benefit Novices [~M]: the same Kalyuga et al. (2003) and (1998) entries as the expertise-reversal claim cited above.
  • Cognitive load effects including integration benefits may reverse for more experienced learners [~M]: Ginns (2006) restating Kalyuga et al. (1998).
  • Bimodal captioned input improved L2 listening skills [+W]: a pronunciation article reporting Charles & Trenkic (2015).
  • Avoiding double load on the visual channel improves visual knowledge [+W]: picture plus text against picture plus audio; a modality comparison that bears on why text over a picture costs.
  • Spoken text modality does not significantly differ from signaled written text modality, or from signaled written plus spoken text [~M]: Kutbay & Akpınar (2020); signalling the written text did not change written-plus-spoken results.
  • Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning [~M]: the modality neighbour.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually understanding that transfers: explaining how the system in the animation works, using the idea on a new problem. Ask separately what the learner wants. A second-language learner may value the written words for learning the language itself, which the captioning evidence supports; a learner who will take a multiple-choice recognition test next week may prefer the transcript, and on that test it may well help. Where the learner's goal and the designer's diverge, say so and offer the text on request rather than deleting it by rule or imposing it.

What would revise this model?¶

The picture rule should weaken if comparisons outside Mayer's programme, with comparable novices and transfer tests, find no cost of verbatim text over narrated animation; the review's reversals for older learners and learner-paced delivery point to where that might happen. The no-picture rule should weaken if spoken plus written words hurt fluent readers on transfer. The expertise rule should be extended, not reversed, by a test of when to withdraw text (the switching rule in the default is a proposal). Studies of live lectures, VR and phone-only video would extend the model to settings it does not yet cover.
