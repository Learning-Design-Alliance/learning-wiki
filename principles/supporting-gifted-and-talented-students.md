# Supporting Gifted and Talented Students

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
**Giftedness** traditionally refers to an IQ of 130 or higher (Lally & Valentine-French, 2015) — the upper roughly 2% of the population, given a standard deviation of about 15 points on most IQ scales. It is a common assumption that unusually high intelligence brings adjustment problems that make social relationships harder. Terman and Oden's (1959) landmark longitudinal study directly tested this: roughly 1,500 high school students scoring in the top 1% on the Stanford-Binet and similar tests (IQ ≈ 135+) were tracked for more than seven decades (subjects became known as the "Termites" and are still studied today). The study found these students were, if anything, *above* average in physical health, taller and heavier than the general population, had above-average social relationships, and were less likely to divorce. They also achieved disproportionately in prestigious professions: 7% earned doctoral degrees, 4% medical degrees, and 6% law degrees — all far above general-population base rates. A separate study of adolescents with even higher IQs found they attended graduate school at a rate more than 50 times the general population (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). The stereotype of the socially awkward, narrowly-focused genius is not supported by this evidence (Steiner & Carr, 2003); gifted and talented students come from all economic and cultural backgrounds.

The meaning of "gifted" has broadened over time beyond a pure IQ threshold to include unusual talent in music, creative writing, the arts, and other domains not fully captured by traditional intelligence tests (G. Davis & Rimm, 2004) — reflected in the now-common dual term "gifted and talented." Typical qualities include learning more quickly and independently than same-age peers, well-developed vocabulary and advanced reading/writing skill, high motivation especially on genuinely challenging tasks, and self-imposed standards higher than what's usually expected. Ironically, these very strengths often go unrewarded: without accommodation to their actual skill level, gifted students can make little further progress (a precociously advanced kindergarten reader may simply stop advancing while peers catch up), languish with boredom, and in some cases develop behavior problems as a result.

Support for gifted and talented students has historically been treated as a special-education responsibility alongside disability categories (Friend, 2008), but many educators now argue this framing is misleading: the defining difference is in *potential*, not deficit — gifted students are, by definition, already capable of adult-level creative, committed work, where students with disabilities may reach comparable levels eventually but not as soon or as often. This reframes gifted/talented support as a matter of differentiating instruction for diversity broadly, a responsibility of every teacher, rather than a narrow special-education specialty.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- The ability to observe and get to know a gifted or talented student as an individual, since the real design challenge is choosing and combining supports based on that student's specific profile, not applying a single template
#### Constraints
- Acceleration (grade-skipping, or covering more material faster within a grade) works, but not indefinitely — repeated grade-skipping creates large maturity gaps within a classroom, and redesigning a curriculum to move faster is impractical to sustain broadly without leaving other students behind
- Enrichment (additional or different material beyond the standard curriculum) risks being perceived as busywork rather than genuine stimulation, particularly if the teacher is unfamiliar with the enrichment material or unable to engage with it fully
- Labeling students "gifted and talented" and giving them exclusive accelerated programs is genuinely contested: it may help the identified students (Colangelo & Assouline, 2009) while isolating them from peers and denying similar opportunities to students not formally classified

### Target Learners
- Students scoring in the traditional gifted range (IQ ≥ 130) or showing exceptional, unusual talent in a non-academic domain
- Students already showing boredom or emerging behavior problems that may stem from an unaccommodated skill mismatch rather than a motivation or conduct issue

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained academic progress at a pace and depth that matches actual current skill, rather than stalling at grade-level material already mastered
- Continued engagement and motivation, protected from the boredom that unaccommodated giftedness commonly produces

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Multiple Intelligences Theory](../theories/multiple-intelligences-theory.md) [+M] — the broadened "gifted and talented" definition (beyond pure IQ, into music, art, creative writing) parallels MI theory's argument for more than one way to be "smart"
