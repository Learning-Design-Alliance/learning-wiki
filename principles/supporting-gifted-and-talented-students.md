# Supporting Gifted and Talented Students

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · no claims cited

## Conditional relationship

This page owns how a teacher or designer plans **ordinary class instruction for a student who already does, or quickly learns, what the class is about to be taught**: a student identified as gifted or talented, or one who simply shows it on the current unit. For such a student, whose response to the unit's own work before or early in teaching shows the target already done unaided, replacing the practice they do not need with work at their level, either moving on to the next content sooner (**acceleration**) or going deeper and wider on the same content (**enrichment**), chosen from that response rather than from the label alone, is expected to produce continued progress on the subject (`general-achievement`, `near-transfer`) and continued engagement (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`) compared with keeping the student on grade-level practice they have already shown. The earlier page's warning stands beside it: without that accommodation, a student who started ahead may make little further progress and grow bored.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship.** None compares acceleration, enrichment, a blend of the two, or compacting the curriculum with keeping gifted students on grade-level work, and none follows a gifted student's progress or boredom under either. The claims the wiki holds bear on neighbouring parts: guidance that helps novices becoming redundant for learners who already hold the schema (cognitive-load experiments and a narrative review, not with students identified as gifted); adaptive tutoring systems that adjust difficulty to performance (meta-analyses, general populations); mastery pacing (positive on examinations, but apparently strongest for the weaker students in a class); cooperative learning with high-ability and gifted students (a digest reporting other researchers' work, which also records that no long-term research exists); praise for intelligence rather than effort (fifth graders); and, on identification, one issue brief, one narrative review and one witness's testimony on under-identification. The design below is therefore the earlier page's guidance, made concrete and labelled step by step.

The relationship is conditional on four things. **What the student actually shows on this unit's work**: a gifted label or a test score from another year does not say whether the student already does this unit's target; the response does, and expertise is domain-specific, so a student advanced in mathematics may be a novice in the next writing unit. **Whether the replacement work is harder or only more**: extra worksheets at the same level, or enrichment the teacher cannot engage with, is what the earlier page calls busywork, not accommodation. **The kind of target**: acceleration suits cumulative subjects where the next unit builds on this one (`procedure`, `principle`); enrichment suits targets that open outwards (`complex-skill`, a talent in music, art or writing). **Who is identified at all**: under-identification of culturally and linguistically diverse students, and of girls where a test score alone gates entry, is reported in the wiki's identification claims, so a plan that serves only labelled students may miss students who show the same response.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Mastery Learning](mastery-learning.md) holds the general gate-correct-recheck relationship; this page applies the same gate at the top, letting a student who already meets the criterion move on rather than wait. [Adaptive Learning](adaptive-learning.md) holds adjusting task difficulty and support to measured performance, which is how acceleration within a class is usually run. [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md) holds the fairness of an identification decision made from a test score, including gifted identification; this page does not repeat its model, and follows it for who gets into a programme. [Supporting Students with Intellectual Disabilities](supporting-students-with-intellectual-disabilities.md) sits at the other tail of the same distribution and shares the rule that a classification sets neither a ceiling nor a floor. [Provide a continuum of gifted services](continuum-of-gifted-services.md) is the school-level version of the same idea.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it.

1. **Find out what the student already does, on this unit, before teaching it.** At the start of each unit give every student the same short pre-check built from the unit's end-of-unit task (10–15 minutes, the hardest items included), and record which parts each student does unaided. Do not plan from the label, an IQ score or last year's test. Untested proposal; it follows the earlier page's requirement to know the student "as an individual" and the domain-specific reading of expertise in [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M] (cognitive-load experiments with trainees and a narrative review; not with students identified as gifted).
2. **Compact what the student has shown.** As a labelled proposal: where a student does at least four in five of the pre-check's items unaided, excuse them from the practice on those parts (keep any part they missed, and keep the unit's end-of-unit check). Expect this to free roughly a third to a half of the unit's class time for that student. Untested numbers. The move rests on the redundancy of guidance for learners who already hold the schema ([expertise reversal](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M]; [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [+M]), carried from lab and training studies to classes.
3. **Choose acceleration, enrichment or both from the response and the subject.** If the student also does the *next* unit's pre-check well and the subject is cumulative, move them on in that subject (subject acceleration: work from the next unit or the next year's course, in class or online). If not, or the target opens outwards, give enrichment that is harder, not more: a task with an open problem, a real audience or a professional's standard (a proof of a general case, an original composition, a source analysis). Blend the two in most cases, as the earlier page advises. Untested proposal. [Adaptive learning](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M] (tutoring systems that adjust difficulty to performance beat teacher-led large-group instruction in meta-analyses; general populations, not gifted students) supports running in-class acceleration through work that adjusts to the student's responses.
4. **Make the replacement work something the teacher can follow.** Choose enrichment the teacher (or a named mentor) can read, question and give feedback on; set a contract with the student naming the task, the standard, the check-in dates and what the student hands in. The earlier page's constraint is that enrichment the teacher cannot engage with reads as busywork. Untested proposal.
5. **Keep the student in the class's shared work for part of each week.** Plan whole-class discussions, labs and group tasks where the student takes a role that stretches them (the hardest sub-problem, the reviewer's role, the first draft of a general rule), not the role of explaining to others for most of the lesson. Evidence: [cooperative learning with gifted and high-ability students](../claims/cooperative-learning-gifted-students.md) [+W] (one 1993 digest reporting Johnson and Johnson's studies: higher mastery and more higher-level reasoning in cooperative than competitive or individual work; it also records that no long-term research exists). A position essay argues that all-day mixed-ability grouping asks gifted students to help slower learners ([homogeneous grouping for reading, heterogeneous for discussion](../claims/mixed-homogeneous-heterogeneous-grouping.md) [~W]: the author's experience, no data).
6. **Name what the student did, not that they are gifted.** Feedback names the strategy, effort or choice ("you tested the general case before trusting it"), and work is set hard enough that real struggle, and real effort to name, happens. Evidence: [praise for intelligence rather than effort](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [+M] (fifth graders and a review of children's praise studies: ability praise left children more fragile after a later failure; general population, not tested with gifted students).
7. **Watch for boredom and underachievement as signals of a mismatch.** When a student who did well starts handing in little or behaving badly, check first whether the work is below their level, before treating it as a conduct or motivation problem (the earlier page's point). Offer an adult who takes an interest (a mentor, a club, an extracurricular in the area of talent). Evidence: [extracurricular involvement and adult mentorship are associated with reversal of gifted underachievement](../claims/extracurriculars-mentoring-reverse-underachievement.md) [+W] (one issue brief reporting earlier studies; associational).
8. **Open the door by response, not only by label.** Give the pre-check and the enrichment option to every student, and let any student who shows the response take the replacement work, labelled or not. Each year compare who is in gifted provision with the school's enrolment by group. Evidence: [culturally and linguistically diverse students are underrepresented in gifted identification nationwide](../claims/gifted-underrepresentation-diverse-students.md) [~W] and [teachers under-refer them](../claims/teacher-underreferral-diverse-gifted.md) [~W] (one 2008 issue brief); for decisions from a test score see [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md).
9. **Plan an arc across a term, not one lesson.** A default arc, with untested quantities. In weeks 1–2 of a unit: pre-check, compact, and teacher-set extension work with a short check-in (5 minutes) twice a week. Weeks 3–6: the student chooses an enrichment or acceleration task from two or three the teacher can follow, works on it in the time freed by compacting (two to three class periods a week as a proposal), and meets the teacher or mentor weekly for 10 minutes with a draft. End of the unit: the student sits the same end-of-unit check as the class, and presents or hands in the extension product, to the class or a real audience where there is one. At the next unit, start again from the pre-check: a student who compacted one unit does not automatically compact the next. If after two weeks the student is still finishing everything easily, raise the level (the next unit's content, or a harder version of the task), not the amount.

When a condition is not met: if no time is freed because the student did not pass the pre-check, keep them in the class's teaching on the parts they missed and offer enrichment on the parts they showed; if the teacher cannot follow any enrichment the student wants, use subject acceleration through existing course material instead, or find a mentor before starting.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the student does now on this unit's own work.** A student who does the end-of-unit task before teaching needs compacting and replacement work; one who learns faster but does not yet do it needs faster pacing within the unit, not removal from it. If a brief omits it, ask: what does the student do, unaided, on this unit's end-of-unit task before it is taught?
- **Whether the subject is cumulative and whether harder work is available.** Acceleration needs a next unit, a next course or adaptive material to move into; enrichment needs a task the teacher or a mentor can follow. If omitted, ask: what comes after this unit, and who can read and judge the student's harder work?
- **How the student came to be identified, and who else shows the same response.** Provision tied to a label and a test cut-off can miss students who show the response. If omitted, ask: how are students chosen for this provision, and could a student who is not labelled take it on the same evidence?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| The student is a `child` in primary school, well ahead in reading or number | Compact by pre-check within the class; give harder texts or problems in the same topic for the freed time; keep the student in class routines; review the arrangement every unit | untested proposal; the earlier page's example of the advanced kindergarten reader who stops progressing |
| The student is an `adolescent` in secondary school, ahead in one subject | Subject acceleration in that subject (the next course, online or with an older class), with the student staying with their year group for other subjects | untested proposal; the earlier page's constraint that repeated grade-skipping opens large maturity gaps |
| Whole-grade acceleration (grade-skipping) is being considered | Decide with the student, family and next teacher, on the student's work across subjects and their social readiness, for one grade at a time; set a review after the first term | untested proposal; the earlier page records that acceleration works but not indefinitely, citing a chapter not held as a claim |
| The target is cumulative (`procedure`, `principle`, as in mathematics or a language's grammar) | Prefer acceleration: move to the next unit's content once the pre-check is passed, through adaptive or self-paced material with a teacher check each week | [Adaptive learning improves outcomes](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M] (general populations, not gifted students); [expertise reversal](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M] carried from training studies |
| The target opens outwards (`complex-skill`, writing, art, music, a research project) | Prefer enrichment: an open task with a professional's standard and a real audience, with a mentor in the field where one can be found | untested proposal; long expert development is described in [Even the most talented performers need around 10 years of intense involvement](../claims/ten-year-rule-expert-performance.md) [~W] (a review, chess, music, sport) |
| The talent is in music, art, sport or another field outside the academic subjects | Count it: give access to training, a teacher or coach in the field, and practice time, and judge it by performance, not by an IQ score | [Accumulated deliberate practice distinguishes more from less accomplished musicians](../claims/deliberate-practice-hours-differentiate-musicians.md) [~W] (a review reporting one retrospective study); untested as a school design |
| One teacher, a full mixed class, several students ahead at different points | Use one pre-check and two or three tiers of replacement work the teacher can run at once; check in with the ahead group together for 5 minutes twice a week rather than individually | untested proposal |
| Scarce teacher time (a busy class, a gifted coordinator who visits weekly, volunteer mentors) | Give the visiting adult one job each visit: read the latest draft, ask one question that raises the standard, and agree the next step and date; the contract carries the rest between visits | untested proposal |
| Some students join remotely, or work from a phone or tablet at home | Put the compacted student's replacement work and contract online; hold the weekly check-in as a short call; keep the student's role in the class's shared discussion when present | untested proposal |
| Only a single session is available (a one-off enrichment day, a visiting class) | Give one hard, open task with a model of the standard, time to attempt it, and feedback naming what the student did; do not claim it as provision | untested proposal |
| The student is bored, handing in little or behaving badly | Check the work's level first (a pre-check on the next unit); if it is below the student's level, change the work before treating the behaviour; offer a mentor or an extracurricular in the area of talent | [Extracurricular involvement and adult mentorship are associated with reversal of gifted underachievement](../claims/extracurriculars-mentoring-reverse-underachievement.md) [+W] (one issue brief, associational) |
| The student has low confidence, avoids hard work, or fears being wrong | Set work hard enough that mistakes happen; name the student's strategy and effort, never "you're so smart"; show the student the improvement over drafts | [Praise for intelligence rather than effort](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [+M] (fifth graders, general population; not gifted students) |
| Identification is by a test score cut-off, or by teacher referral | Screen every student, use the unit pre-check as a second route in, and compare provision with enrolment by group each year | [Underrepresentation of culturally and linguistically diverse students](../claims/gifted-underrepresentation-diverse-students.md) [~W], [teacher under-referral](../claims/teacher-underreferral-diverse-gifted.md) [~W] (one issue brief); [a talent search admitting by SAT score invited over 2,500 boys and 1,081 girls in one summer](../claims/test-scores-gate-gifted-program-entry.md) [~W] (one witness's testimony); see [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md) |
| The brief names its own routine (a school's gifted programme model, an extension menu, a published enrichment curriculum, its own rubric for advanced work) | Keep it. Change only what the evidence and the earlier page say matters: a pre-check on the unit's own work before deciding, replacement work that is harder rather than more, feedback that names what the student did, and the student's own interests in choosing the task | untested proposal |
| The brief's own rubric or criteria for the advanced work | Use them, and raise the level by the task, not by substituting another rubric | untested proposal |
| Adults in a work setting who are far ahead of a training course (an experienced hire in onboarding) | Let them test out of modules by doing the module's task to its standard; give them the hardest cases or a role reviewing others' work for the freed time | [expertise reversal](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M] (electrical trainees, among others); untested as a test-out design |
| The product is for a real audience (a competition, an exhibition, a published piece, a presentation to experts) | Plan the extension work towards it from the start, with the audience's standard as the criterion and at least one rehearsal or review by someone from that field | untested proposal |

Nine of the eighteen rows cite a claim, and none of those claims tests the move in its row with gifted students. The cumulative-target and work-setting rows carry cognitive-load and tutoring evidence from general populations and trainees; the identification rows carry an issue brief and a testimony that report under-identification, not a tested remedy; the talent rows carry reviews of expert performance; the boredom and confidence rows carry an associational brief and praise experiments with children in general. ## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a task under stated conditions: the pre-check items done and missed, the time taken, the quality of the extension work against its standard, what the student did when the work became hard, and the end-of-unit check. "Gifted", "bored", "unmotivated" and "underachieving" are inferred states, and so is "has mastered the unit": a pre-check shows what the student did on those items that day.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Passes the pre-check easily, every unit | Ahead in this subject; the pre-check is too easy or does not sample the hardest part of the unit; the student learned the material outside school | Add items from the next unit and a transfer item in a new form; ask the student to explain one answer. |
| Finishes class work fast, then disrupts or switches off | Work below the student's level; work at the right level but without meaning to the student; a social or attention difficulty unrelated to level | Give a harder version of the same task for a week and record behaviour; ask the student what they would rather be working on. |
| Grades fall in a student who did well | The work became harder and the student has never had to struggle; boredom with work below level; trouble outside school; avoidance to stay with peers | Look at which tasks are not done (the hard or the easy); talk with the student; check whether a mentor or extracurricular changes it. |
| Avoids extension work or chooses the easy option | Fear of failing at something hard after always succeeding; the extension task is extra work, not replacement; the student values being with friends | Make the extension replace, not add to, class work; let the student work on it with a peer; note what is said about mistakes. |
| Does well in one subject, average in another | Domain-specific advancement (the usual case); the identification rested on one area | Pre-check in each subject separately; do not carry compacting across subjects. |
| A student not identified as gifted does the pre-check as well as identified students | Identification missed the student (referral, a test, language); the student developed since identification | Offer the same replacement work; record it, and review the identification route. |

These rows are proposals, untested with students as diagnostics. A pre-check is also teaching and practice, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain open rather than settling on the one that fits the label.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares acceleration, enrichment or compacting with grade-level instruction for gifted students. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M]: three entries (a narrative review of cognitive-load experiments across five paradigms, three experiments with electrical trainees reading circuit diagrams, and a conceptual paper restating the review). Integrated explanations and worked examples helped less experienced learners and lost their advantage, or reversed, for learners who already held the schema; no effect sizes are printed. It supports removing practice and guidance a student has shown they do not need. It was not run with students identified as gifted, and it defines expertise per topic, not per student. Held at M, below the claim's cap, because it is carried to a different population.
- [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [+M]: two entries (the same review and a synthesis on the transition from example study to problem solving); advanced learners benefit less from full worked examples, and example-heavy guidance should be reduced as learners move to independent problem solving. Same scope as above.
- [Adaptive learning improves outcomes](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring systems: higher achievement than teacher-led large-group instruction (g = 0.42), other computer-based instruction (g = 0.57) and textbooks or workbooks (g = 0.35), but no advantage over individual human tutoring or small-group instruction; a median of 0.66 SD over conventional instruction in 50 evaluations, depending heavily on whether tests were locally developed. It supports running acceleration through work that adjusts to performance; it is not a test with gifted students, and held at M for that reason.
- [Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [+M]: experiments with fifth graders and a narrative review of children's praise studies; ability praise led to performance goals and worse performance after a later failure than effort praise, moderated by age and gender. It bears directly on how a teacher talks to a student labelled gifted; general population.
- [Cooperative learning shows benefits for gifted and high-ability students, though long-term evidence is lacking](../claims/cooperative-learning-gifted-students.md) [+W]: one 1993 ERIC digest (`q2`, review) reporting Johnson and Johnson's nine studies of high-ability and gifted students over 15 years (higher mastery and retention, more higher-level reasoning when working cooperatively), and Slavin's statement that there is no long-term research on ordinary cooperative learning with the gifted. No effect sizes; second-hand. It supports keeping gifted students in shared work for part of the week, and limits it to the short term.
- [Extracurricular involvement and adult mentorship are associated with reversal of gifted underachievement](../claims/extracurriculars-mentoring-reverse-underachievement.md) [+W]: one 2008 issue brief (`q2`, review `r1`) reporting earlier studies that gifted students in extracurricular activities are typically not underachievers and that students who reversed underachievement often cite an adult. Associational; not a test of offering a mentor.

**The limiting claims.** [Algebra ability level differences appear on both criteria with no treatment-by-ability interaction](../claims/ability-level-differences-verbal-problem-solving.md) [~M]: in one 1972 comparison of two strategies for teaching verbal problems in algebra, high-ability students outscored intermediate and low-ability students on both criteria, but neither strategy worked better for one ability group than another. It is a reminder that "gifted students need a different method" is not established by the wiki's evidence; what differs here is the level and pace of the content, not a method. And [Mastery learning improves outcomes](../claims/mastery-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations found positive effects of mastery programmes on examinations, apparently stronger for the weaker students in a class, with more student time on instructional tasks. A group-paced gate helps weaker students; it says nothing about the students who already meet the criterion, and a class that waits for everyone to pass holds them where they are, which is the problem this page addresses.

Learners, settings, outcomes and comparisons differ across these claims (trainees in a lab, tutoring-system users, fifth graders, students in 1970s algebra classes, digests reporting other studies), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures progress or boredom for gifted students under acceleration or enrichment.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

The earlier page cited no claim pages. These claims bear on single rows or on the earlier page's description, and have not been read against the whole model.

- [Teachers underrefer culturally and linguistically diverse students for gifted identification](../claims/teacher-underreferral-diverse-gifted.md) [~W] and [Culturally and linguistically diverse students are underrepresented in gifted identification nationwide](../claims/gifted-underrepresentation-diverse-students.md) [~W]: one 2008 issue brief (`q2`, review `r1`) reporting NCES enrolment and identification shares and summarising referral research. They bear on who reaches gifted provision, not on what it does.
- [A narrative review, citing Ford (2003), attributes the underrepresentation of culturally and linguistically diverse students in gifted education to identification measures biased toward another cultural background](../claims/culturally-diverse-underrepresentation-biased-measures.md) [~W]: a narrative review presenting no data on representation rates or on bias in any measure; the attribution is the review's position, reported from Ford, not a tested cause.
- [Traditional gifted identification tests cannot fully determine learning potential and omit classroom-learning components](../claims/traditional-identification-tests-fail-learning-potential.md) [~W]: an argument in the same review (`q2`, theoretical), citing earlier authors. It supports adding classroom evidence to identification; it is not a test of any measure.
- [Testimony at a 1987 congressional hearing reported that the Johns Hopkins talent search, which admitted by SAT score, invited over 2,500 boys but 1,081 girls in one summer although equal numbers took the test](../claims/test-scores-gate-gifted-program-entry.md) [~W]: one witness's written testimony reporting invitation counts for one programme in one summer; it does not compare admission routes. Related testimony on the SAT and girls is held on [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md).
- [Even the most talented performers need around 10 years of intense involvement to reach international level](../claims/ten-year-rule-expert-performance.md) [~W], [Amount of accumulated deliberate practice distinguishes more from less accomplished musicians](../claims/deliberate-practice-hours-differentiate-musicians.md) [~W], [Relative-age effects, not innate talent, bias selection into elite youth sports](../claims/relative-age-effect-talent-selection-bias.md) [~W] and [Early training produces neural and physiological adaptations that can be misattributed to innate talent](../claims/early-training-adaptations-misread-as-talent.md) [~W]: one review from the expert-performance perspective (Ericsson et al., 2005), reporting other studies. They qualify the earlier page's account of talent as fixed: early selection and training produce part of what is read as talent. They bear on talent development and selection, not on classroom provision. See also [perceived giftedness as access to training resources](perceived-giftedness-confers-training-resources.md).
- [Children scoring highly on one intelligence tend to score highly on others, consistent with general intelligence](../claims/high-scorers-across-intelligences-support-g.md) [~W] and [The Theory of Multiple Intelligences is weak in stressing science and social sciences as fields of talent](../claims/multiple-intelligences-weak-on-science-talent.md) [~W]: a review reporting a finding second-hand, and a position essay. They qualify the earlier page's link to [Multiple Intelligences Theory](../theories/multiple-intelligences-theory.md) as support for the broadened definition of giftedness; neither is evidence about provision.
- [Homogeneous grouping for reading instruction and heterogeneous grouping for discussion groups each serve distinct purposes](../claims/mixed-homogeneous-heterogeneous-grouping.md) [~W]: a position essay (`q1`) arguing against all-day mixed-ability grouping because gifted students are asked to help slower learners; the author's experience, no data.
- [Teacher expectation bias predicted Dutch secondary students' performance up to five years later, after controls for prior achievement, IQ and motivation, in one large correlational cohort study](../claims/teacher-expectation-effects-on-achievement.md) [~M]: associational, about 11,000 Dutch secondary students; the effect was larger for students with higher prior achievement. It bears on the risk that a label, or its absence, shapes what a teacher offers; it is not a test of gifted provision.
- [Differentiated instruction shows a medium positive pooled effect on academic achievement (REM ES = 0.729) with a confidence interval spanning negative values](../claims/di-medium-positive-effect-academic-achievement.md) [~W]: a pooled estimate over three studies whose confidence interval runs from −3.659 to 5.117; it does not establish an effect of differentiation, for gifted students or anyone.
- [No significant impact of frustration or boredom (or anxiousness, discouragement, distractedness) on practice performance was found](../claims/frustration-boredom-no-significant-impact.md) [~W]: an exploratory affect model in one retrieval-practice study; self-rated boredom did not predict practice performance there. It is a caution against reading boredom as a measure of mismatch on its own; it says nothing about gifted students.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually continued progress in a subject at the student's level (the next unit's content, a harder standard), or a talent developed towards a performance, measured on a stated task at a stated time. Elicit separately what the student wants: to go faster, to go deeper into one interest, to stay with friends, not to be singled out, not to be given more work for finishing early. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A student who values being with friends may refuse a pull-out group or a grade-skip that serves the objective well; one who wants to pursue an interest may work hard on an enrichment project and little on accelerated content in the same subject.

For example, a 12-year-old who finishes every mathematics unit early may want to build games, while the designer's objective is the next year's algebra. Algebra applied in a game the student designs serves both; an accelerated textbook chapter serves only the objective. Record the student's own goal in their contract in their words, and check progress on it as well as on the objective.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with students who already meet a unit's criterion show that compacting and replacement work produce no more progress on later units or on a delayed check than staying with grade-level practice, or that the gain shows only on the extension work itself. If enrichment that is harder rather than more does no better than more work at the same level, the distinction this page leans on is not real. If the boredom and underachievement the earlier page describes do not fall when the work's level changes, they are not signals of mismatch and the diagnostic advice is wrong. If opening replacement work to any student who shows the response does not change who takes it up, the identification rows matter less than the label suggests.

Progress on the next unit, progress on enrichment work, retention over time, engagement and social adjustment are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no pre-check criterion, no amount of time to free and no rule for choosing acceleration over enrichment.

## Description
**Giftedness** traditionally refers to an IQ of 130 or higher (Lally & Valentine-French, 2015) — the upper roughly 2% of the population, given a standard deviation of about 15 points on most IQ scales. It is a common assumption that unusually high intelligence brings adjustment problems that make social relationships harder. Terman and Oden's (1959) landmark longitudinal study directly tested this: roughly 1,500 high school students scoring in the top 1% on the Stanford-Binet and similar tests (IQ ≈ 135+) were tracked for more than seven decades (subjects became known as the "Termites" and are still studied today). The study found these students were, if anything, *above* average in physical health, taller and heavier than the general population, had above-average social relationships, and were less likely to divorce. They also achieved disproportionately in prestigious professions: 7% earned doctoral degrees, 4% medical degrees, and 6% law degrees — all far above general-population base rates. A separate study of adolescents with even higher IQs found they attended graduate school at a rate more than 50 times the general population (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). The stereotype of the socially awkward, narrowly-focused genius is not supported by this evidence (Steiner & Carr, 2003); gifted and talented students come from all economic and cultural backgrounds.

The meaning of "gifted" has broadened over time beyond a pure IQ threshold to include unusual talent in music, creative writing, the arts, and other domains not fully captured by traditional intelligence tests (G. Davis & Rimm, 2004) — reflected in the now-common dual term "gifted and talented." Typical qualities include learning more quickly and independently than same-age peers, well-developed vocabulary and advanced reading/writing skill, high motivation especially on genuinely challenging tasks, and self-imposed standards higher than what's usually expected. Ironically, these very strengths often go unrewarded: without accommodation to their actual skill level, gifted students can make little further progress (a precociously advanced kindergarten reader may simply stop advancing while peers catch up), languish with boredom, and in some cases develop behavior problems as a result.

Support for gifted and talented students has historically been treated as a special-education responsibility alongside disability categories (Friend, 2008), but many educators now argue this framing is misleading: the defining difference is in *potential*, not deficit — gifted students are, by definition, already capable of adult-level creative, committed work, where students with disabilities may reach comparable levels eventually but not as soon or as often. This reframes gifted/talented support as a matter of differentiating instruction for diversity broadly, a responsibility of every teacher, rather than a narrow special-education specialty.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- The ability to observe and get to know a gifted or talented student as an individual, since the real design challenge is choosing and combining supports based on that student's specific profile, not applying a single template
#### Constraints
- Acceleration (grade-skipping, or covering more material faster within a grade) works, but not indefinitely — repeated grade-skipping creates large maturity gaps within a classroom, and redesigning a curriculum to move faster is impractical to sustain broadly without leaving other students behind
- Enrichment (additional or different material beyond the standard curriculum) risks being perceived as busywork rather than genuine stimulation, particularly if the teacher is unfamiliar with the enrichment material or unable to engage with it fully
- Labeling students "gifted and talented" and giving them exclusive accelerated programs is genuinely contested: it may help the identified students (Colangelo & Assouline, 2009) while isolating them from peers and denying similar opportunities to students not formally classified

### Target Learners
- Students scoring in the traditional gifted range (IQ ≥ 130) or showing exceptional, unusual talent in a non-academic domain
- Students already showing boredom or emerging behavior problems that may stem from an unaccommodated skill mismatch rather than a motivation or conduct issue

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained academic progress at a pace and depth that matches actual current skill, rather than stalling at grade-level material already mastered
- Continued engagement and motivation, protected from the boredom that unaccommodated giftedness commonly produces

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Multiple Intelligences Theory](../theories/multiple-intelligences-theory.md) [+M] — the broadened "gifted and talented" definition (beyond pure IQ, into music, art, creative writing) parallels MI theory's argument for more than one way to be "smart"
