# Gamification

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 13 claims (8 for, 5 mixed) · 24 studies (11 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 7 of 24 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page is about **adding a game layer to ordinary instruction**: points or XP, levels, progress bars, badges, streaks, team scores, a theme or story, around practice the learner would otherwise do without them. For learners whose bottleneck is keeping going through practice they must repeat many times (vocabulary and other `verbal-association` targets, fact fluency, a `procedure` drilled to accuracy, coding exercises), a game layer added to instruction that already works is expected to produce a **small gain in what learners can do afterwards** (`immediate-performance`, `general-achievement`) over the same instruction without it. The expectation is conditional on three things: the layer rewards progress on the target (accuracy on new or harder items, improvement on the learner's own past) rather than mere activity or completion; it leaves learners meaningful choice; and its theme and decoration are tied to the content rather than added to entertain.

**What gamification is usually adopted for is the least supported part.** Gains in interest and enjoyment (`motivation-affect`) and in participation (`persistence`) were smaller in the one meta-analysis here and were not significant once the analysis was restricted to the more rigorous studies; the cognitive gain was. And two conditions can reverse the expectation: an expected tangible reward for doing or finishing a task that learners already find interesting can lower their later, unrewarded engagement with it; and interesting but irrelevant decoration can cost comprehension, most for learners with little prior knowledge or who find irrelevant material hard to ignore.

**What tests this, and what does not.** One claim tests gamified against non-gamified instruction on average, across 40 experiments in school, university, work and informal settings ([Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]). **No claim tests the conditional part**: whether mechanics that reward mastery beat those that reward activity, whether leaderboards help or harm, or which element (a streak, a badge, a story) carries an effect. Those conditions are carried from neighbouring evidence that was not gamified: laboratory reward experiments, cooperative-learning studies in schools, and studies of decorative material in texts. Each is labelled where it is used.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step says what supports it.

1. **Start from practice that works without the game.** Name the repeated practice to be gamified (items, attempts, exercises), check that it is sound instruction on its own (feedback after each attempt, items matched to the target), and add the layer only to that. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("a motivational layer on top of sound instruction, not a replacement for it").
2. **Give points for accuracy and improvement, not for activity.** Award points or XP for correct attempts on new or harder items and for beating the learner's own previous score; give none for logging in, time spent, or items completed regardless of accuracy. Evidence: in laboratory reward experiments, tangible rewards contingent on engaging in, completing, or performing a task lowered later free-choice engagement with it (d = −0.28 to −0.40), with children more affected than college students ([Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]); undermining was weaker where the task had a clear, well-defined standard of quality ([Rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivating activity can reduce future engagement with it](../claims/overjustification-effect-reduces-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]). Neither tests points or badges in a course, so treat this as carried evidence.
3. **Pair every point with an informational sentence.** "Correct: you used the past tense because the action is finished" says what was done well; a bare "+10" does not. Word badges and messages about effort and strategy ("ten days of practice", "solved without hints"), not ability ("genius"). Evidence: positive verbal feedback *raised* later free-choice engagement (d = 0.33) in the same meta-analysis ([rewards claim](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], lab tasks); praise for intelligence after success left fifth graders less persistent and lower-scoring after a later failure than praise for effort ([Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [~M], children, carried to badge wording).
4. **Make a level mean a skill passed.** A level or badge unlocks when the learner meets a criterion on the target without hints, for example 8 of 10 correct on a short check (an untested proposal; set the criterion locally and record it). Show progress against the learner's own past (a personal best, a mastery map) as the default comparison. Untested; from the earlier page (self-referenced progress, as in Khan Academy's mastery levels).
5. **No public individual leaderboard by default; if scores are shared, share team scores built from each member's learning.** Form small mixed teams; each member takes an individual quiz; the team score is the sum of members' improvement over their own previous scores, as in Teams-Games-Tournament or Student Teams-Achievement Divisions. Evidence: in school studies of four weeks or more, cooperative methods whose group rewards rested on every member's individual learning had a median effect of +.32 on achievement against +.07 without ([In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]); a review of Johnson and Johnson's meta-analysis reports cooperative learning ahead of competition and individual work ([Meta-analyses by Johnson and Johnson find cooperative learning promotes higher achievement than competition or individual work across ages, subjects, and tasks](../claims/johnson-meta-analysis-cooperative-achievement.md) [+M]). None of it was gamified, and no claim here tests leaderboards. If a leaderboard is required, show each learner a small bracket of similar scorers or a team table, and let them opt out of display (untested proposal).
6. **Offer bounded choice.** Two or three options at a time: which topic next, which of two challenge levels, which avatar or route through the material; hold the target fixed. Evidence: autonomy support with non-controlling feedback goes with higher intrinsic motivation ([Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], narrative review and the rewards meta-analysis, not gamified); in one 21-student study of a role-playing vocabulary app, perceived choice and interest/enjoyment were the lowest-rated motivation dimensions ([Interest/enjoyment and perceived choice were the weakest motivation dimensions, suggesting Mangomon's RPG mechanics did not yet fully serve learner autonomy](../claims/rpg-mechanics-not-yet-serve-autonomy.md) [~W]).
7. **Keep theme and story out of the moment of practice.** Tie any theme to the content (the story's problems are the target problems); put narrative, animation and celebration between items or at the end of a level, not on the screen while the learner works an item. Evidence: interesting but irrelevant additions can impair comprehension and transfer, with effects that vary by material and learner; decorative pictures weakened the benefit of instructional pictures for low-prior-knowledge 7th and 8th graders, and harmed comprehension only in students with low attentional inhibition on harder texts ([Interesting but irrelevant details can impair comprehension and transfer, with effects that vary with the material and the learner](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [~M]); added load matters most for complex material ([Cognitive Overload Degrades Learning](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]). Neither was tested on a gamified course.
8. **Plan the dose and the arc across sessions.** All untested proposals; the only tested durations on the claim pages are a four-week app study and the meta-analysis's finding that duration did not moderate cognitive or behavioural outcomes.
   - *Session length:* 10–20 minutes of gamified practice, three to five times a week, for at least four weeks.
   - *Week 1:* short levels (about five items), points and an informational message after every item, an easy first level so every learner has an early success.
   - *Weeks 2–3:* longer levels with mixed and harder items; points weighted toward harder and previously missed items; a level unlocks only after a check on a later day, not the same session.
   - *Week 4 onward:* move points from every item to every level; shift attention to personal records and goals the learner sets; reduce celebratory effects.
   - *Ending the layer:* before the course ends, run at least a week with the points hidden or removed and see whether practice continues; a drop then is the undermining or novelty risk showing itself, and a reason to change what points were given for.
   - *Moving on:* a learner moves to the next level on the step 4 criterion, not on points accumulated.
9. **Judge the layer outside the game.** Measure the capability on a task with no game around it, at the horizon the objective sets, and record enjoyment and participation separately. If possible, compare with the same practice ungamified for one group or one unit. Untested proposal; the meta-analysis's effects were heterogeneous, so a local check is what tells whether this layer worked.

When a condition is not met: if the practice underneath is not sound (no feedback, misaligned items), fix that first, because nothing here suggests a game layer compensates for it; if learners already find the task interesting and practise freely, use step 3 and step 4 only, without points or rewards.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether learners already find the task interesting.** Rewards for doing a task risk lowering later engagement most when interest is already there; a dull, necessary drill carries less of that risk. If unstated, ask: do learners do this task now without being made to, and do they say they enjoy it?
- **Which outcome must change.** A capability on a later test, continued practice over weeks, and enjoyment are different outcomes, and the evidence is firmest for the first. If unstated, ask: what should learners be able to do afterwards, and is the real problem that they stop practising?
- **Who sees the scores.** Private progress, team scores and a public individual ranking are three different designs with different risks for those ranked low. If unstated, ask: will anyone other than the learner see points, ranks or badges, and will they affect a grade?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: points and levels earned, accuracy per item, response times, sessions started and completed, practice after the layer is removed, a learner's choices, and performance on a task outside the game. "Is motivated", "has mastered it" and "is gaming the system" are inferred states. Points are an observation about the game; they say something about the capability only if they are earned for accuracy on items the learner has not already mastered.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Points and levels rise; the outside test does not | Points can be earned without the target (repeating easy items, guessing); the items are misaligned with the test; the gain is in the app's format only | Correlate points with accuracy on new items; check what share of points came from already-mastered items; give the test once in the app's format and once outside it. |
| Practice drops when points are removed, or after a few weeks | Rewards displaced interest the learner had; novelty wore off; the material became too hard or too easy | Compare practice before, during and after the layer; check whether the drop began before removal; ask what the learner did instead. |
| Low-ranked learners stop practising | Public comparison discourages them; the items are too hard; they never valued the task | Offer a private, self-referenced view and an easier entry level, separately, and see which changes practice; note where they stop. |
| Fast answers with falling accuracy | Speed or volume is what earns points; the learner is chasing a streak; the items are too easy to need thought | Compare response time with accuracy; remove the speed element for one round and watch accuracy. |
| A learner ignores the game layer but practises steadily | Values the task, not the game; finds the theme childish; the layer adds nothing for them | Ask; offer a plain mode; check the outside test, which is what counts. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Removing or changing a mechanic to test an explanation is itself an intervention, so record each change and its date.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 38 publications (40 experiments, 2013–2017) comparing gamified with non-gamified instruction in school, higher education, work-related and informal settings (`synthesis-mixed`). Cognitive g = .49 (k = 19, N = 1686), motivational g = .36, behavioural g = .25, all heterogeneous; restricted to experiments and quasi-experiments with pre- and posttests, only the cognitive effect held (g = .42). Game fiction, and competition combined with collaboration, moderated behavioural outcomes (direction not given on the claim page); duration did not moderate cognitive or behavioural outcomes. It tests gamification on average, over many combinations of elements, and does not show that any one element works. Read in full.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]: two meta-analyses of laboratory experiments on free-choice persistence and interest, mostly with puzzle-like tasks. Deci et al. (1999, 128 studies) found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards undermined later free-choice engagement (d = −0.28 to −0.40), verbal positive feedback raised it (d = 0.33), and tangible rewards were more detrimental for children. Cameron & Pierce (1994, 96 studies, read as an abstract) found no overall undermining and a minimal negative effect only for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. It limits this page; neither tests points or badges in a course.
- [Rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivating activity can reduce future engagement with it](../claims/overjustification-effect-reduces-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]: Deci's (1971) paid university students spent less free time on puzzles afterwards than unpaid students; two syntheses report the effect weaker or absent for flat-rate pay and for well-defined tasks with a clear quality standard; a 2001 review argues rewards should be used selectively. No effect sizes are recorded. It supplies the moderators used in step 2.
- [In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]: a best-evidence review of 99 school studies of four weeks or more (median +.32 with group rewards on individual learning against +.07 without), an elementary peer-assisted-learning meta-analysis (d = 0.33; larger with interdependent rewards and self-referenced evaluation) and a randomised component analysis with 64 at-risk 4th and 5th graders. It is the basis for team scores built from individual learning; no study was gamified, and the claim notes structured interaction can work without rewards.
- [Meta-analyses by Johnson and Johnson find cooperative learning promotes higher achievement than competition or individual work across ages, subjects, and tasks](../claims/johnson-meta-analysis-cooperative-achievement.md) [+M]: a 1993 digest reporting a meta-analysis of 122 studies (1924–1981) that found cooperation ahead of competition and individual work. Second-hand, no pooled effect size; it bears on competition as a structure, not on leaderboards.
- [Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]: a narrative review of self-determination theory, the rewards meta-analysis, and a conceptual framework; its Discussion says autonomy works best with structure and may unsettle novices, with no study recorded. Basis for bounded choice.
- [Interesting but irrelevant details can impair comprehension and transfer, with effects that vary with the material and the learner](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [~M]: six studies of texts and e-learning materials, a meta-analysis read as an abstract and five experiments; only one prints an effect size (η² = .04, a "very small" comprehension cost with decorative images, and on harder texts a cost only for students with low attentional inhibition). Decorative pictures neither helped nor harmed 7th and 8th graders overall but weakened instructional pictures for low-prior-knowledge learners. None tests a game theme.
- [Interest/enjoyment and perceived choice were the weakest motivation dimensions, suggesting Mangomon's RPG mechanics did not yet fully serve learner autonomy](../claims/rpg-mechanics-not-yet-serve-autonomy.md) [~W]: the authors' interpretation of questionnaire means (M = 3.52 for both) from 21 Thai undergraduates; an interpretation, not a tested result.

Learners, tasks, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (gamified courses pooled across settings, laboratory puzzles, school cooperative learning, reading texts), so do not rank them by effect label. None follows a gamified course after the layer is removed.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a capability (vocabulary recalled a week later, a procedure done accurately) and, often, continued practice. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the layer and from the practice: to win, to keep a streak, to see progress, to avoid being ranked, to get the practice over with, or to be able to use the skill. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the streak may log in daily for one easy item; one who values not being ranked may stop when a leaderboard appears; one who values the skill may find points childish and still practise well.

For example, an adult learning Italian before a move may want to hold a conversation with neighbours, while the designer's objective is 300 high-frequency words at 80% recall after a week. Points for correct recall serve the designer's objective; a weekly level that ends with a short spoken exchange using the week's words serves the learner's. Record both, so that a long streak is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if controlled comparisons of the same practice with and without a game layer, on outcomes measured outside the game, show no cognitive gain, or a gain that disappears once the layer is removed. The alignment condition should be dropped if mechanics rewarding activity do as well as those rewarding accuracy and improvement. The caution about rewards should weaken if gamified courses, unlike the laboratory tasks, show no fall in practice after points are removed, and strengthen if they do. If public leaderboards are tested and do not harm low-ranked learners, the default against them is a preference, not a finding. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "poorly aligned mechanics" after the fact; record which mechanics were used before the outcome is known.

### What should a gamification layer be expected to change?
- **Default:** expect a small-to-medium gain in cognitive outcomes and do not count on a motivational one; across 40 experiments the cognitive effect (g = .49) held in the more rigorous designs (g = .42), while the motivational (g = .36) and behavioural (g = .25) effects did not — [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]
- **Changes when:** the evaluation asks which element produced the gain → the pooled effect covers many combinations of elements and does not show that any single one (a streak, a leaderboard) works — [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [~M]
- **Tested with:** studies from 2013–2017 in school, higher education, work-related and informal settings; all effects heterogeneous.
- **Not settled:** which elements matter for cognitive outcomes; the authors call this "still somewhat unresolved".

### What should points, badges and rewards be given for?
- **Default:** avoid expected, tangible rewards for merely doing or completing an activity learners already find interesting; engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards undermined later free-choice engagement (d = −0.28 to −0.40), while positive verbal feedback raised it (d = 0.33) — [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S], [Rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivating activity can reduce future engagement with it](../claims/overjustification-effect-reduces-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]
- **Changes when:** the reward is a flat rate for taking part, or the task has a clear, well-defined standard of quality → undermining was weaker or absent in that meta-analytic work — [Rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivating activity can reduce future engagement with it](../claims/overjustification-effect-reduces-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]
- **Changes when:** learners are children rather than college students → tangible rewards were more detrimental for children — [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-S]
- **Tested with:** lab experiments on free-choice persistence and interest in novel tasks, mostly puzzle-like.
- **Not settled:** the two meta-analyses disagree (Cameron & Pierce 1994 found no overall undermining), and neither tests points or badges in a course; how far the effect reaches sustained engagement in academic settings is debated on the claim page.

### Individual competition or team-based mechanics?

## Description
Gamification is the use of game design elements in non-game contexts (Deterding et al., 2011). In learning design, it means structuring learning activities with mechanics such as points, badges, levels, progress indicators, narratives, and leaderboards. The recommendation is not to decorate learning with rewards, but to align game mechanics with genuine learning behaviors — effortful practice, mastery, collaboration — so that motivational dynamics support rather than substitute for learning.

## Implications

Gamification's effects on learning are real but conditional. Meta-analytic evidence shows small-to-medium positive effects on cognitive, motivational, and behavioral outcomes, with the largest gains when gamification includes collaboration and when it is applied in short-term or skill-based settings [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]. The mechanism is primarily motivational: well-designed mechanics satisfy needs for competence (visible progress, achievable challenges) and autonomy (meaningful choice), consistent with [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. But mechanics that reward mere activity rather than mastery, or that introduce social comparison through leaderboards, can backfire — undermining intrinsic motivation or demotivating lower-performing learners [Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]. Effective designs treat gamification as a motivational layer on top of sound instruction ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)), not as a replacement for it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear alignment between game mechanics and target learning behaviors — points and badges should mark mastery or productive effort, not completion volume
- Rapid, informative [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) — game-like progress indicators only help if they reflect actual performance
- Progressively structured challenge ([Adaptive Difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md)) — levels and difficulty curves keep learners in a productive challenge zone
- Meaningful choice or agency ([Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md)) — mechanics imposed without learner control tend to feel coercive rather than playful

#### Constraints
- Leaderboards can demotivate learners who consistently rank low; team-based or self-referenced comparison (progress vs. one's own past performance) is safer [~M]
- Extrinsic rewards for tasks learners already find interesting can reduce intrinsic motivation once rewards are removed [Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]
- Novelty effects inflate short-term results; gains often attenuate over long deployments [~W]
- Rewarding speed or volume can encourage shallow, game-the-system behavior at the expense of deep processing
- Poorly integrated mechanics add cognitive and attentional overhead, competing with learning content [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, who respond strongly to narrative, levels, and immediate feedback
- Learners in repetitive or effortful skill-building (language learning, math fact fluency, coding practice) where sustained engagement is the bottleneck
- Low-stakes and formative contexts; effects are weaker in high-stakes or long-duration settings [~M]
- Adult learners respond better to progress visualization and mastery framing than to overt game elements like badges [~W]

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained engagement in deliberate practice and fluency building
- Formative skill development with frequent low-stakes attempts
- Motivation and persistence in self-paced or online learning
- Collaborative problem-solving (when team mechanics are used)

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) — well-designed mechanics support competence, autonomy, and relatedness, the needs that fuel intrinsic motivation
- [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) — points, badges, and immediate feedback function as contingent reinforcement shaping practice behavior
- [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) — leaderboards and team mechanics leverage social comparison and modeling, though with the risks noted above

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) — the same theory warns that controlling extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation, especially for tasks learners already value
