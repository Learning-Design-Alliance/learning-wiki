# Active Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 34 claims (16 for, 17 mixed, 1 against) · 32 studies (8 causal, 6 associational, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 4 theoretical, 3 design, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 5 of 32 report an effect size · 27 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For learners meeting new material in a course, replacing part of the time they would spend only listening with tasks in which they generate, explain, predict, compare or apply the content, with feedback on what they produce, is expected to raise later course performance. The best-recorded case is undergraduate STEM courses (`postsecondary`, `classroom`, `term-plus`), with active-learning sections compared against traditional lecturing on course exams and concept inventories (`course-assessment`, `general-achievement`, `conceptual-understanding`). The relationship is about **what learners are asked to do with the content**, not about visible activity: a task that can be completed without thinking with the content is not expected to help.

Four conditions bound it. Learners with little prior knowledge of the target (`novice`) are not expected to benefit from activity that leaves them to discover the essential content unaided. The kind of engagement matters: generating beyond the material is predicted to help more than merely manipulating it, though that ordering has not held in every setting. The comparisons behind the average usually changed several course features at once, so the average belongs to "an active-learning course", not to any single activity within it. And the horizon recorded is the end of a course; durable retention and far transfer are not established here. This is a bounded expectation for groups under stated conditions, not a forecast for one learner.

Two converted pages hold narrower relationships inside this one. [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) is one active task, recalling from memory, with its own delayed-retention evidence; [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) is another, discussing reasoning after an individual commitment, with an immediate isomorphic-question outcome. This page owns the general claim that changing what learners do in class changes what they learn, and the qualifications that apply to every such task. The wiki holds no converted pattern for active learning as a whole.

## Observation, state and explanation

Record what a learner does **with a stated task under stated conditions**: whether they produce an explanation, a prediction, a solution or a comparison, what support was available, and what they could do afterwards on an independent probe. "Engaged" and "passive" are inferences, not observations. Talking, writing, clicking a response or moving materials are behaviours; whether the learner is generating inferences beyond the material is a hypothesis about processing that none of these behaviours establishes on its own. Learner expertise is task-specific, as elsewhere: a student new to this topic is a novice for it whatever their course level.

The rows below are proposals for reading responses during an active task. They have not been tested with learners.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Completes the activity sheet or group task, but cannot explain or apply the idea on an individual probe | The task could be completed without thinking with the content (busywork); a peer did the work; the learner understood during the task but did not retain it | Ask for an individual explanation on a matched item immediately after the task, and record who produced each part of the group output. If immediate explanation succeeds and later recall fails, the problem is retention, not the task. |
| Stalls or produces mostly wrong output on a generative task | Missing prior knowledge needed to generate anything useful (`novice`); task demand too high for working memory; unclear task instructions | Give a short worked example or explanation of the same relation, then a matched generative task. Success after the example points to missing knowledge or load; continued failure with clear instructions points elsewhere. |
| Generates confidently, but the output is wrong | A misconception the task has surfaced; a correct idea poorly expressed; generation aimed at the wrong features of the task | Ask the learner to predict an outcome the misconception and the correct idea disagree on, then give corrective feedback and re-probe on a new case. |
| Section scores rise after a switch to an active-learning format | The activities; extra quizzes, homework or preparation time that came with the new format; a different instructor or exam | Record every feature that changed with the format, including home time and assessments. Only a comparison that holds the other features constant can attribute the gain to the activities. |
| Learner reports the active session as less useful than lecture | Dislike of effort or exposure to peers; a real mismatch with what the learner values; feeling of fluency from lecture that is not matched by performance | Compare self-report with an independent probe on the same content, and ask what the learner wanted from the session. |

These probes can shift confidence among explanations; one contrast does not identify a cause, and a probe is itself a learning event. Record what remains unresolved.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Active learning improves exam performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]: the central comparison. A meta-analysis of undergraduate STEM courses found exam and concept-inventory scores 0.47 SD higher under active learning than under traditional lecturing (158 studies), and failure under lecturing 1.5 times as likely (odds ratio 1.95, 67 studies); the effect held across disciplines and class sizes and was largest in small classes and on concept inventories. A second meta-analysis, of comparisons in which the same instructor taught both ways, found the exam gap for underrepresented and low-income students narrowed from −0.62 to −0.42 SD, varying widely between studies, and only in classes using high-intensity active learning. So the expected benefit depends on how much class time is active. Course-end outcomes only; one of the two entries passes the source check (abstract), the other could not be confirmed.
- [Most active learning comparison studies differ on more than one course feature](../claims/most-comparisons-differ-on-multiple-features.md) [~M]: a limit on attribution. A review of 59 college science comparison studies found only 11.9% in which exactly one feature differed between conditions; active-learning conditions averaged 5 of 14 course features. The average effect above therefore describes course redesigns, not a single activity. Descriptive counts from one review, not yet checked against its source. Its neighbours, recorded from the same review, say most active-learning conditions still contained a substantial lecture component and that active conditions often asked for more home time (listed under Further evidence below).
- [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]: a qualification on guidance. Across 580 comparisons, unassisted discovery did worse than explicit instruction (d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), while discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations did better than other instruction. The claim page says plainly that the deficit belongs to unguided discovery, not to active learning as a whole. For novices, "active" has to mean guided activity. Partly checked (1 of 3 entries pass on abstract).
- [Constructive learning beats active and passive learning](../claims/constructive-learning-beats-active-passive.md) [~M]: a qualification on the kind of engagement. A theoretical review with an embedded lab study reports learning rising from passive to active to constructive to interactive engagement; a large multilevel field study in university classrooms supported only the end points (passive lowest, interactive highest) and did not confirm that constructive activities outperform active ones, on perceived learning and affective outcomes. Neither reports a standardised effect size. The direction is a working hypothesis, not a settled ordering.
- [Discussion promotes more active thinking than lecture, though lecture is no worse for transmitting information](../claims/discussion-promotes-more-active-thought-than-lecture.md) [~M]: a qualification on the objective. Reviews summarised on the claim page find lecture about as effective as alternatives for acquiring information, while an observation study of students' reported thoughts found more problem-solving and synthesis during discussion (8.3% against 1% of class time). Where the objective is information acquisition alone, the expected advantage of active formats is not established by this evidence. Not yet checked against its sources; two of its three entries have no source text available.

Retain the learners, setting, comparator, which course features changed, the instrument and its alignment, and the horizon when transporting these observations. A course-level average does not forecast an individual's response, and a nonsignificant difference between engagement modes does not establish that they are equivalent.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

Ask what the learner wants from the session and why, separately from the designer's objective. A learner may value hearing an expert explain the material clearly, or may value being able to use it on a problem they care about; a designer may want conceptual understanding that shows on a concept inventory. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. Do not infer understanding from participation, and do not treat a learner's preference for lecture as a deficit to be corrected; it may be a real goal, or a feeling of fluency that an independent probe can test.

For example, a first-year chemistry student may value passing the end-of-term exam and see in-class problem sets as time taken from "covering the material", while the instructor's objective is that students can explain why a reaction rate changes with temperature on an unfamiliar case. Attendance and completion of the in-class sheets cannot establish either aim. An individual explanation on a new case, the exam the student is aiming at, and the student's own account of what the problem sets were for can.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons that hold course features other than the activities constant (time, assessments, quizzes, instructor) show no advantage for active-learning sections on aligned outcomes. If gains are explained by the extra quizzes, homework or home time that came with the format, credit those features rather than activity. If constructive and interactive tasks keep failing to outperform merely active ones in authentic classrooms, drop the engagement ordering and keep only the end points. If novices given guided activity do no better than novices given clear explanation followed by practice, the claim reduces to the value of practice. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as "not really active learning" after the fact.

A course-end exam advantage, a narrowed achievement gap, durable retention, transfer to unfamiliar problems and changes in reasoning ability are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal share of active class time, a best activity type, or an effect for any individual learner, and it rests largely on undergraduate STEM courses.

## Description
Active learning is the principle that learners should do meaningful cognitive work with content rather than only receive it. The core move is not activity for its own sake, but requiring learners to explain, apply, compare, predict, generate, or revise so that understanding is constructed through use.

## Implications
Active learning improves learning when the activity requires learners to think with the content rather than merely touch it. Explanation, prediction, retrieval, and comparison are especially valuable because they force learners to surface assumptions, test understanding, and revise mental models [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. That is why active learning typically works best when tasks are focused, accountable, and supported with feedback: poorly structured activity can still become busywork, while well-designed activity can even turn confidently expressed mistakes into opportunities for stronger retention when those errors are corrected and discussed [High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~S].

The effect size for active learning in STEM is unusually large and consistent for an education intervention: Freeman et al.'s (2014) meta-analysis of 225 studies comparing constructivist/active class designs against traditional lecturing found students in straight lecture courses were 1.5 times more likely to fail (odds ratio 1.95) than students in courses with some active learning, and that assessment performance (exams, concept inventories) improved by about half a standard deviation on average (d = 0.47) — with no significant difference in this effect across biology, chemistry, computer science, engineering, geology, math, physics, or psychology. Beyond raising performance broadly, active learning has also been shown to disproportionately help already-disadvantaged students specifically [Active learning narrows achievement gaps for disadvantaged and underrepresented students](../claims/active-learning-narrows-achievement-gaps.md) [+S], functioning as an equity intervention in gateway STEM courses, not merely an average-performance one.

## Description
In the reconstructed lectures Mead contrasts old education, which he argues neglected and even wiped out the attitude of the child, with an approach centered on the learner's response. The article quotes Mead's claim that "the whole of the technique of the process...does not turn on the material provided by elders, but falls around the response of the child to this material". This follows from Mead's goal-seeking account of action, in which the stimulus does not force the reaction but is a stimulus for it, and in which children are trained to choose the stimuli for their own acts.
