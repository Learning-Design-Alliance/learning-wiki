# Assessment for Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 8 claims (4 for, 4 mixed) · 18 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 5 causal, 3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 5 of 18 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

Assessment for learning concerns **who uses assessment evidence during instruction, against what criteria, and under what stakes**. For learners working towards a capability they have not yet reached (in `pre-k-12` or `postsecondary` settings; the evidence below does not report expertise), a response elicited before the outcome is judged is expected to support later performance on that capability only when three conditions hold: the learner and the teacher share the criteria the response is read against; the response is produced under conditions that make revealing partial understanding safe; and a revision, regrouping or reteaching step can follow before the summative judgement. The expected change is in `general-achievement` and, where learners judge their own work, in self-regulated strategy use and `assessment-accuracy`, at the horizon of the next aligned task or unit; the wiki holds no evidence for a longer horizon.

The converted [formative assessment principle](formative-assessment.md) and its [pattern](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) model the narrower teacher-side loop inside this relationship: a response is read against rival hypotheses and a contingent next action is chosen, then reobserved. This page owns what surrounds that loop: whether learners themselves can read the evidence (self-assessment against criteria, and how well calibrated it is), and whether the purpose and stakes of the checkpoint let the evidence be honest. Use the formative assessment pattern as the reusable policy; no separate assessment-for-learning pattern exists.

## Observation, state and explanation

Record what was observed (a response to a named task, a self-rating, a revision, a silence) with its conditions: stakes, who sees the result, whether criteria were shown, response mode and help. Understanding, calibration and willingness to reveal uncertainty are inferred states; a correct answer, an accurate self-rating and a candid "I don't know" are each consistent with more than one of them. The rows below are proposals, untested with learners.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Learner rates own work much higher than the teacher does | Missing task knowledge to see the errors; criteria not understood; incentive to inflate under competitive or graded norms | Ask the learner to point to evidence in the work against each criterion; repeat the rating privately and ungraded; compare with a peer's rating of the same work. |
| Few errors or questions appear on a checkpoint that carries marks | Learners have reached the target; learners avoid revealing uncertainty because the mark counts; the task is too easy to discriminate | Re-elicit the same capability on an ungraded, private item of comparable difficulty, and check whether the item separates learners at all. |
| Self-ratings become accurate but the work does not improve | Calibration has improved without a revision opportunity; criteria are understood but strategy is missing; the rated task is not the target capability | Check whether a revision step followed the rating, and whether a new unassisted task shows the same gain. |
| Results are collected but the next lesson is unchanged | No usable next action was planned; the evidence did not discriminate between options; time or pacing constraints override it | Record the decision that each checkpoint was meant to inform before it is given; compare what was planned with what was done. |

A single contrast can shift confidence between explanations; it does not identify a cause. Do not read compliance, a grade or a confident self-rating as a learning state.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]: syntheses of US K–12 (Lee et al.) and K–12 (Kingston and Nash) formative assessment studies with control conditions report small positive overall effects on achievement (d = .29; about .20). In Lee et al.'s meta-regression, interventions supporting student-initiated self-assessment show larger estimates (d = .61), as do formal formative evidence such as written feedback on quizzes (d = .40) and a medium cycle within or between units (d = .52). These feature estimates compare study configurations within a synthesis (`synthesis-mixed` grain, assignment per study unknown); the claim page says they are not randomised comparisons, not additive and not a forecast for a new course, and no common outcome horizon is reported. Partly checked against its sources.
- [Self Assessment Improves Self Regulated Learning](../claims/self-assessment-improves-self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 19 experimental and quasi-experimental self-assessment interventions in K–12 and higher education reports a small effect on self-regulated strategy use (d = 0.23, 95% CI 0.08–0.39, 12 effect sizes) and a larger one on self-efficacy (d = 0.73); the other two SRL analyses have intervals crossing zero. It bears on the learner-side route of this model, not on achievement directly, and its page notes that gains in scores are thought to be mediated by strategy use. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]: in a `randomized` 2×2 experiment with 80 Dutch secondary pre-university students solving genetics problems, those who studied modelling examples of self-assessment rated their own posttest performance more accurately than those who did not (ηp² = .10); a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies found self–teacher mark correspondence inconsistent, closer in advanced courses and better-designed studies. This limits the model: a learner's self-assessment is evidence a designer can act on only once its accuracy has been taught or checked. Not settled from the abstracts available.
- [Prior Knowledge Needed For Accurate Self Assessment](../claims/prior-knowledge-needed-for-accurate-self-assessment.md) [~M]: a synthesis of 22 meta-analyses reports moderate self–performance agreement (mean r = .29), higher for domain-specific judgements and familiar tasks; a semester-long classroom study of 96 undergraduates found the lowest performers grossly overconfident before and after exams. The `associational` evidence qualifies the learner-side route most for exactly the learners who most need revision. Not settled from the abstracts available.
- [A revised low-stress TBL format produces significantly higher individual summative assessment (iSAT) scores than the original high-stakes format](../claims/low-stress-tbl-higher-isat-scores.md) [~W]: the only claim found that contrasts stakes directly compares 119 dental students in a revised low-stress format with 161 medical students in the original, reporting a 5.2% higher iSAT (p < .05) and no effect size. Cohort, programme and format differ together (`controlled-nonrandom`), so it cannot separate stakes from population. Not yet checked against its sources.

**No claim in the wiki tests the stakes and goal-structure part of this model as stated**: that grading pressure or competitive classroom norms make learners hide uncertainty or inflate self-assessments, or that a teacher's beliefs about ability change how assessment evidence is used. The previous body argued this from Dweck, Harlen and Patrick et al., which are listed under Key Sources but have no claim pages here. Treat that branch as a hypothesis.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is a capability measured by a named instrument at a stated horizon; the learner's valued goal is what they want the work to achieve and why. Under assessment for learning the two often diverge on the checkpoint itself: a learner may value the mark, the designer the information. Ask, record both, and treat agreement as an observation.

For example, in a first-year writing course the designer's objective may be an argued essay scored on an analytic rubric at the final submission, while a learner's valued goal is "not to lose marks on the draft". If the draft is graded, that goal rewards hiding weak sections; if the draft is ungraded and the learner rates it against the rubric before feedback, the same goal can be served by revision. Which arrangement the learner experiences as safe, and whether their self-rating matches the teacher's, are things to elicit, not assume.

## What would revise this model?

Revise it if comparable learners using criteria-based self-assessment with a revision step do not outperform comparable learners without one on an aligned delayed task under a defensible comparison; if removing marks from checkpoints does not change how much uncertainty learners reveal, measured by a private re-elicitation; or if trained self-assessment remains as miscalibrated as untrained for the learners in question, in which case the learner-side route should rely on external evidence. A synthesis-level average, a feature estimate from meta-regression, or a confounded cohort contrast does not settle any of these. Do not protect the model by relabelling every null result as poor fidelity.

## Examples
- Using exit tickets to decide whether the next lesson should reteach, extend, or regroup.
- Giving draft feedback before a final submission so learners revise against criteria.
- Asking learners to self-rate confidence before checking answers and then discussing mismatches.

## Description
Assessment for learning is the principle that assessment should be used during instruction to support learning, not only after instruction to certify it. The central question is not "What grade does this produce?" but "What does this evidence suggest we should do next?" It overlaps strongly with formative assessment, but highlights the instructional purpose of assessment evidence: clarifying goals, surfacing current understanding, and guiding revision.

## Implications
A teacher's own beliefs about the purpose of assessment shape whether assessment for learning actually happens in practice, not just whether it's nominally intended. Teachers holding an **incremental view of ability** (Dweck, 2000) — that ability grows with learning — communicate that the goal is mastering material and figuring things out, and use assessment results to decide whether to move on, re-teach, or provide targeted remediation ("We are going to practice over and over again. That's how you get good. And you're going to make mistakes. That's how you learn." — Patrick, Anderman, Ryan, Edelin, & Midgley, 2001, p. 45). Teachers holding a **fixed view of ability** instead communicate that the goal is doing well on tests, especially outperforming others, which pushes assessment toward ranking rather than diagnosis (Harlen, 2006) — see [Goal-Orientation Theory](../theories/goal-orientation-theory.md) for the fuller mastery/performance-orientation framework this maps onto. This matters directly for self- and peer-assessment specifically: those practices only function as intended in a classroom genuinely built around incremental, learning-goal-oriented norms — in a classroom emphasizing interpersonal competition instead, students have a real incentive to inflate their own (and their friends') self-assessments, since accurate reporting costs them relative standing.

Assessment for learning works when evidence changes what happens next for the learner or the instructor. Midstream checks are most useful when they clarify the target, expose current misunderstandings, and create an immediate next move such as regrouping, reteaching, revising, or choosing a new strategy [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. This is also why low-stakes design matters: learners are more likely to reveal uncertainty when the checkpoint is framed as information for improvement rather than judgment, and that makes the evidence more instructionally useful for both self-monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M] and process-focused revision rather than mere answer chasing [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Transparent goals and criteria**: Learners need to know what the evidence is about.
- **Evidence that informs a next step**: Assessment prompts should reveal something instructionally useful.
- **Revision pathways**: Learners need a chance to act on the result.
- **Instructional responsiveness**: Teachers or systems must be able to adapt based on what the evidence shows.
#### Constraints
- **Summative contamination**: Heavy grading pressure can make learners hide uncertainty instead of exposing it.
- **Weak task alignment**: Poorly designed prompts create misleading signals.
- **Performative checking**: Frequent assessment without instructional change turns the practice into compliance.

### Target Learners
- **Learners building foundational competence**: Benefit when misunderstandings are identified before they harden.
- **Learners who need more confidence to reveal uncertainty**: Low-stakes assessment can normalize partial understanding.
- **Instructors serving mixed-readiness groups**: Assessment evidence supports grouping, pacing, and differentiation.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Clarifying the goal state**: Helping learners understand what quality looks like.
- **Guiding improvement**: Turning evidence into revision, re-teaching, or support.
- **Developing self-assessment**: Helping learners judge progress against criteria.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Formative assessment theory emphasizes using evidence to adapt teaching and learning.
- Self-regulated learning supports using criteria and feedback for learner monitoring.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Assessment does not automatically become "for learning" just because it happens midstream.
- High-stakes accountability systems can distort the learning function of assessment.
