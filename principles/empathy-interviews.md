# Empathy Interviews

## Description
Empathy interviews are structured, open-ended conversations designed to help educators or designers understand how learners experience school, work, systems, and support. Unlike a survey or diagnostic checklist, the goal is not only to collect facts but to hear how learners interpret challenges, strengths, motivations, and belonging in their own words. Used well, empathy interviews improve the instructor's model of the learner and can lead to more responsive supports, stronger relationships, and better alignment between instruction and lived reality.

## Implications
Empathy interviews are valuable because they replace assumption with direct evidence about how learners experience the system. Open-ended listening can reveal barriers, strengths, and motivations that standard diagnostics miss, and that usually leads to better instructional fit when the information is actually used to change supports or communication. These conversations depend heavily on learners explaining their experience in their own words [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](/claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~M], and they are most useful when what is learned feeds into more authentic, context-sensitive design rather than abstract learner profiles [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](/claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~M]. The design risk is extractive listening: if interviews gather stories without altering practice, they produce the appearance of care without the instructional value.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Clear listening purpose**: Interviewers need to know what they are trying to understand, such as barriers, motivations, strengths, or belonging.
- **Open-ended prompts**: Questions should invite story, explanation, and concrete examples rather than short confirmatory answers.
- **Trust and confidentiality**: Learners need clear boundaries around what will be recorded, shared, or acted on.
- **Instructional follow-through**: Interviews matter most when what is learned shapes support, curriculum, communication, or design decisions.
#### Constraints
- **Extractive use**: Interviews can become performative if learner stories are collected but never used to improve the experience.
- **Power dynamics**: Learners may censor themselves if they fear consequences from honesty.
- **Boundary risk**: Not every challenge disclosed in an empathy interview can or should be handled by the instructor alone.
- **Indirect evidence base in this repo**: Current linked claims support explanation, transfer, and reflection mechanisms more directly than empathy interviews as an independent intervention.

### Target Learners
- **Learners whose needs are not well captured by standard diagnostics**: Especially useful when context, identity, or history strongly shape engagement.
- **Learners in redesign or improvement efforts**: Empathy interviews are strong for courses, services, or supports that need to be adapted around learner experience.
- **Educators building relationships early**: Useful at the beginning of a course, mentoring relationship, or intervention.
- **Programs serving heterogeneous populations**: Interviews can surface variation in barriers and strengths that one-size-fits-all design misses.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Improved instructional fit**: Helping educators tailor examples, supports, expectations, and communication.
- **Belonging and trust**: Giving learners a structured opportunity to feel heard and taken seriously.
- **Context-aware support**: Identifying what barriers or assets shape participation, persistence, and performance.
- **Reflective interpretation by educators**: Moving from assumptions about learners to evidence-informed decisions.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Humanistic and belonging-oriented views of learning — learners engage differently when they feel recognized, safe, and respected.
- Self-determination theory — understanding learner experience can help instructors better support autonomy, competence, and relatedness.
- Transformative and reflective practice traditions — hearing learner narratives can unsettle instructor assumptions and shift design choices.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Interviews do not automatically produce empathy or better design; interpretation quality matters.
- Listening to learners is not the same as agreeing with every preference or turning every interview into an individualized curriculum.

### Claims
- [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](/claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~M] — empathy interviews depend on learners narrating and interpreting their own experience in ways that reveal real barriers and strengths
- [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](/claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~M] — interviews are most instructionally valuable when they reshape authentic tasks, supports, and contexts rather than staying as detached listening exercises

## Examples
- **Beginning-of-course learner interviews**: Short structured conversations about goals, barriers, prior experience, and support needs.
- **Design interviews before program revision**: Staff interview learners about friction points before changing curriculum or services.
- **Mentoring check-in interviews**: A mentor periodically revisits learner priorities, constraints, and sense of progress.
- **Affinity-informed listening rounds**: Learners choose whether to share in individual, paired, or small-group interview formats.
