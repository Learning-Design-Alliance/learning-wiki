# Relationships as Foundation for Argumentation

## Description
Argumentation instruction is often taught as a set of discourse moves (making claims, citing evidence, responding to counterarguments) without addressing the relational conditions that make those moves possible for students to risk in the first place. This principle holds that trust and emotional safety — knowing and being known by the teacher and peers, feeling it is acceptable to be wrong and revise publicly, having space to bring one's own cultural and linguistic resources into the conversation — are a foundational layer beneath argumentation, not a separate, optional classroom-climate concern. Teachers who center this view also tend to reframe "rigor" itself: away from difficulty and volume of work, toward the depth of discourse and peer engagement a trusting classroom makes possible.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Deliberate, sustained relationship-building activities (daily talk routines, group-cohesion activities, teachers learning about students' backgrounds and interests) that precede and continue alongside argumentation instruction, not a one-time icebreaker
- A teacher willing to model vulnerability (admitting uncertainty or error) to normalize public revision of ideas
#### Constraints
- Evidence for this principle comes from teachers' own collaborative reflection on their practice, not from a controlled study measuring argumentation quality with and without relationship-building; the mechanism is plausible and consistently reported but not causally isolated
- Reframing "rigor" away from a traditional, difficulty-based conception can create friction with parents, administrators, or colleagues who hold the traditional view

### Target Learners
- K-12 science students, with particular salience for students of color and other groups who may feel less safe sharing or challenging ideas in a given classroom's default norms

### Target Learning Objectives
- Supporting equitable, sustained participation in scientific argumentation, and expanding what counts as academic "rigor" to include collaborative sensemaking

## Examples
- A teacher instituted a daily talk routine ("every day we talk... every day it's the expectation") specifically to build the interpersonal familiarity needed before asking students to argue with each other productively.
- A teacher reframed her own definition of rigor from "how much homework are you giving my kids" to designing tasks that "hooked it to the kid... their experiences and their cultural [background]," reasoning that this makes rigorous discourse more equitably accessible than a traditional difficulty-based standard.
