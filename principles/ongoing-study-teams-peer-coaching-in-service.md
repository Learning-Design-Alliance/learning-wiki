# Teacher professional development should include ongoing activities like study teams and peer coaching rather than only occasional large-group sessions

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 51 claims (31 for, 17 mixed, 3 against) · 33 studies (9 design, 8 review, 5 associational, 4 causal, 4 qualitative, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q3` · 2 of 33 report an effect size · 49 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one structural choice in teacher professional development: whether teachers' learning is carried by **ongoing, school-based structures** (a study team of colleagues that meets regularly to examine its own practice and students' work, peer coaching in which colleagues observe and debrief each other's lessons, or a school-based coach such as a literacy coach) **in addition to** occasional large-group sessions, or by the large-group sessions alone. For in-service teachers (`adult`, working in `pre-k-12` schools) whose practice does not change in their own `classroom` after a workshop or an in-service day, adding such structures over a `term-plus` span is expected to produce more change in observed classroom practice (`skill-performance`, observed in lessons) and, less reliably and by less, in students' `general-achievement`, than occasional sessions alone. The change is expected only where the ongoing time is protected from other duties, the structure focuses on instruction, and participation is not tied to evaluation.

**No claim in this wiki compares study teams or peer coaching with occasional large-group sessions alone.** The nearest evidence is a meta-analysis of causal coaching studies, reported here on several claim pages: coaching (mostly by a dedicated coach, not by peers) against comparison conditions raised observed instructional practice by a pooled 0.49 SD and student test scores by a pooled 0.18 SD. The same meta-analysis found no dosage relationship and found that coaching **paired with** group training went with larger effects, which turns the old title's "rather than" into "as well as": the large-group session is not the problem, using it alone is. Peer coaching as such rests on one assertion with no studies behind it.

How this sits beside the converted pages: the [Professional Development pattern](../patterns/professional-development.md) holds the full cycle for one practice (diagnose, model, rehearse, enact with feedback, fade, check maintenance) and its doses; this page does not repeat it, and says which ongoing structure carries that cycle and how it is kept going. The [Communities of Practice principle](communities-of-practice.md) owns the model of sustained participation in a standing professional group, which a study team is one instance of. The [Reflection principle](reflection.md) owns the relationship between structured reflection and learning; a study team's examination of assumptions is reflection done with colleagues.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page (and the literacy-coach page folded into it this week) gave usable guidance; it is kept here as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it. Doses are untested proposals unless a claim is named.

1. **Open with a short large-group session that sets a shared language, then keep group sessions as one part of the year.** Use one day (about 3–6 hours, a proposal) at the start of the year to introduce the practice or framework all teams will work on, and one shorter group session each term to add the next piece. For a literacy initiative, this is where the shared language of evidence-based reading instruction is established for teachers and administrators before coaches or teams build on it (from the folded literacy-coach page). Evidence status: [pairing coaching with group trainings is associated with larger effects](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [+M] (an exploratory regression across the meta-analysis's studies; the authors say it is descriptive, not a causal effect of the feature).
2. **Form study teams of 3–6 teachers who share a grade or subject, meeting every one to two weeks for 45–60 minutes.** Each meeting has a facilitator and a written agenda in which instruction is a formally led item, not left to spare time: one teacher brings a piece of student work or a short lesson video, the team names what the students did, asks what assumption about teaching it reflects, and agrees one change each member will try before the next meeting. Evidence status: team size, frequency and agenda are untested proposals. [Daily teaching demands competed with and distracted from a sustained developmental focus](../claims/daily-demands-compete-with-developmental-focus.md) [~M] (interviews in seven small Chicago high schools: teachers valued instructional discussion but rarely initiated it) is the reason to lead the item formally rather than hope it happens. [Collaboration with colleagues around instruction has consistently positive relationships with math and science instructional practices](../claims/colleague-collaboration-consistently-positive-practices.md) [+M] (a survey association in one district, not a comparison). The schedule is feasible where teams already exist: in one large district, [around half of teachers reported meeting in grade-level teams at least weekly and observing colleagues' classrooms at least quarterly](../claims/ccss-teacher-collaboration-grade-level-teams-observations.md) [+W] (a description of how common the structures were, not of what they did). Treat each agreed change as a small test: the teacher plans it, tries it, brings back what students did, and the team decides to keep, adapt or drop it. In one five-year network of high-school mathematics teachers, [teachers credited the focus on instructional routines and plan–do–study–act testing cycles with improving their instruction](../claims/pdsa-testing-routines-structure-teacher-reflection.md) [+W] (interviews with 24 teachers, all crediting routines and 11 the cycles; self-attribution, no comparison group).
3. **Run peer coaching in pairs or trios, one cycle every two to three weeks.** A cycle: a 10–15 minute planning conversation in which the observed teacher names one thing they want watched; a 20–30 minute observation (live or on video) recording only that; a 20–30 minute debrief within two school days that starts from what students did, names one thing to keep and one to try, and is kept between the two teachers. Evidence status: cycle shape and timings are untested proposals. [Peer coaching is asserted, citing no studies, to be a highly effective form of teacher professional development](../claims/peer-coaching-supports-teacher-professional-development.md) [+W] is an assertion only. [Peer reflective groups encouraged student teachers to challenge existing theories](../claims/peer-reflective-groups-challenge-student-teacher-views.md) [+W] (one study of student teachers' writing and interviews, reported in a digest).
4. **Keep participation voluntary and separate from appraisal.** Say in writing that what is seen in a peer observation or study-team meeting is not used in evaluation; let teachers choose their own focus within the shared practice. Evidence status: [coaching framed as a partnership, with voluntary non-evaluative participation](../claims/coaching-partnership-voluntary-non-evaluative.md) [+M] (correlations from one program's teacher survey); [a district culture of coaching makes teachers more comfortable participating](../claims/coaching-culture-increases-teacher-participation.md) [+W] (the authors' own qualitative finding).
5. **Where the school has a coach, use the coach to run training, demonstrate, co-teach and observe with immediate feedback, and protect that time.** The folded literacy-coach page gave the coach's role: facilitate teacher training on evidence-based reading instruction and data-based decision making; demonstrate lessons; co-teach or observe and give immediate feedback. Spend most coaching time on instructional delivery rather than on behaviour management alone, and keep the coach's caseload and other duties small enough that weekly contact happens. Evidence status: [coaching time focused on instructional delivery improved practice, while time focused on behavioural management was associated with a decrease](../claims/coaching-focus-instructional-delivery-versus-behavior-management.md) [~M] (one study, reported second-hand); [time spent between teachers and coaches is one of the most important factors](../claims/coach-teacher-time-key-success-factor.md) [+W] (qualitative, from the authors of one three-year study); [the complexity of coaching responsibilities may fragment coaching and reduce its impact](../claims/coaching-complexity-may-fragment-impact.md) [-W] (an author's rationale from the literacy-coaching literature, with no data) is the reason to keep the coach's role narrow rather than add programme administration to it, as the district-programme row of the [Professional Development pattern](../patterns/professional-development.md) also says. A literacy-coaching randomised trial with early childhood educators, [reported in a review](../claims/pd-coaching-expert-support-gains.md) [+M], found larger classroom-quality and child language and literacy gains than controls, with 16 hours of workshops and seven coaching sessions over a semester.
6. **Pair the ongoing work with the instructional materials teachers will use.** The study team and the coach work on lessons from the curriculum the school has adopted, not on generic examples. Evidence status: [pairing coaching with instructional resources and materials is associated with larger gains](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [+M] (same exploratory regression; a video library alone was associated with smaller effects). The [high-quality instructional materials principle](../patterns/professional-development.md) holds the materials side.
7. **Plan the year as an arc in which the source of support moves from coach or facilitator to the teachers.** Proposed default, untested:
   - *Weeks 1–10:* launch session; the coach or a trained facilitator leads every study-team meeting with a fixed protocol; the coach models or co-teaches once in each teacher's class; peer coaching starts in week 4 with the facilitator present at the first debrief.
   - *Weeks 11–25:* teachers take turns leading the study-team protocol; peer coaching carries most observation; the coach observes each teacher once a month and joins a study-team meeting once a month.
   - *Weeks 26 onward:* the team chooses its own next focus from what its student work shows; peer observation every three to four weeks; the coach is on call.
   A teacher or team moves to the next practice, or to less coach support, when the practice is seen at the agreed level in two consecutive observed lessons and the teacher can say why they made the choices they did (a proposal). Total coach contact under this arc is roughly 15–20 hours per teacher across the year. Evidence status: dose untested; [no specific coaching dosage is established as necessary, and quality appears to matter more than dosage, with sustained duration favouring comprehensive coaching](../claims/coaching-dosage-quality-over-quantity.md) [~M] (a review: studied dosages ranged from 3 to 32 sessions; early-learning coaching ran 5 to 7 months on average); [coaching dosage shows precisely estimated null effects](../claims/coaching-dosage-null-effect.md) [~M]. In one program, [more than 81% of coached teachers received at least 30 minutes a week and averaged more than 16 hours over the year](../claims/sustained-coaching-contact-hours.md) [+W]; that is a description of one program's dose, not a tested minimum.
8. **Check practice again after support ends, and give later adopters the same start.** Observe each teacher with the same look-for list at least six weeks after coaching stops and fold the practice into the study team's standing routine. When the practice spreads beyond the first teachers, give each later group the same launch, facilitated team meetings and coached first weeks rather than a short orientation. Evidence status: untested here; [most coaching studies did not measure maintenance, and where it was measured it was not always kept](../claims/coaching-maintenance-rarely-measured.md) [~M]. In one university, [student gains from a pilot taught by faculty who had intensive professional development were not consistently sustained when later adopters did not get it](../claims/epdp-pilot-gpa-retention-gains-not-sustained.md) [+W] (a campus report, no statistics for the later years, higher education).

When a condition is not met: if no time can be protected for teams or coaching, the ongoing structures will be the first thing daily demands push out, so shrink the design (one focus, one cycle a month) rather than keep a schedule that will not run; if participation is tied to evaluation, separate the peer structures from appraisal before starting.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **How much protected time exists for ongoing work, and whose time it is.** A study team or peer-coaching cycle that competes with meetings about attendance and test preparation rarely survives. If unstated, ask: how many minutes per fortnight are set aside for instructional work, inside the timetable, and is there a coach whose time is protected from other duties?
- **Whether the ongoing work is tied to evaluation.** Voluntary, non-evaluative participation goes with more reported change; observation that feeds appraisal changes what teachers show. If unstated, ask: will anything seen in a peer observation or team meeting reach a teacher's appraisal?
- **What the target is: observed practice, or student achievement, on what horizon.** The pooled effect on practice is much larger than on achievement, and the link between them is weak across studies. If unstated, ask: what will an observer see in a lesson when the practice is in place, and which student measure, if any, is expected to move, and when?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: attendance at team meetings and peer cycles; what a teacher brings and what the team agrees; the practice as seen in an observed lesson against the look-for list; students' work. "Has changed practice", "is reflecting on assumptions" and "has bought in" are inferred states. Talk in a study team is not change in a lesson.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Study-team meetings happen, but talk is about logistics, attendance or test dates | Daily demands crowd out instruction; no one leads the instructional item; the team does not trust each other enough to show work | Read the last three agendas and notes; ask who led the instructional item; run one meeting with a facilitator and a protocol and compare what is discussed. |
| Teachers describe the practice well in meetings, but it does not appear in observed lessons | They do not yet know how to do it; materials or pacing block it; the observation came too soon | Ask the teacher to plan the next use of it aloud; if they can, find the constraint; if not, the coach models it in their class. |
| Peer debriefs are uniformly positive and nothing changes | Peers avoid criticism of colleagues; the look-for list is vague; the observation is felt as evaluation | Ask each pair what one change was agreed last time and whether it happened; check whether anything reaches appraisal. |
| Practice changes, but student work does not | The practice is enacted in form but not purpose; the student measure is not aligned; the horizon is too short | Look at what students did in a filmed segment; check whether the student measure tests what the practice targets. |
| Practice in place at the end of the year, gone the next term | The team structure stopped; staff turnover; the practice was tied to one coach | Ask when it stopped and what replaced it; check whether the team still meets. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Record each probe, because asking a team about its agenda changes the next agenda.

## Evidence and qualifications

**In this wiki no claim compares ongoing study teams or peer coaching with occasional large-group sessions alone, and none tests peer coaching.** Seven of the claims below come from one meta-analysis (Kraft, Blazar and Hogan 2018), six directly and one through a practitioner review, so they are one study, not seven.

- [Teacher coaching programs improve teachers' instructional practice by a pooled 0.49 SD across 43 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-instruction-effect-0-49-sd.md) [+M]: random-effects meta-analysis of causal coaching studies, with substantial variation across programs (random-effect SD 0.33). Coaching here is mostly by a coach, compared with conditions that may include usual PD; it is the nearest evidence that ongoing classroom-based support changes practice, and it is carried to peer structures untested.
- [Teacher coaching raises student achievement on standardized tests by a pooled 0.18 SD across 31 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-achievement-effect-0-18-sd.md) [+M]: same meta-analysis; 0.12 SD on state tests, which assess broad domains. Many achievement measures were aligned with the programs. One later single study sits at that size: [students of teachers coached by a commercial provider outperformed matched comparison schools on the state mathematics test](../claims/e2l-coaching-staar-math-outperformed.md) [+W] (propensity-matched, 1,060 students in grades 6–8, effect size +0.13; the provider's other study had conflicting state-test results). A practitioner review [restates the meta-analysis and adds one contrasting study in which coached teachers felt more confident but were not assessed as more effective](../claims/coaching-meta-analysis-positive-effects-instruction-achievement.md) [~M]; it is the same meta-analysis, not independent support, and its contrasting study is a reason not to read teachers' confidence as changed practice.
- [Frequent feedback and coaching for teachers is positively associated with charter school achievement impacts](../claims/teacher-feedback-coaching-associated-charter-success.md) [+W]: a review's association across charter schools, school-level and confounded with everything else those schools do; it bears on coaching as a standing school practice, not on peer structures.
- [Pairing coaching with group trainings is associated with larger effects on instruction and achievement; instructional materials with larger, and video libraries with smaller, effects on instruction](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [+M]: an exploratory regression across the same studies; associations between studies, not effects of the features. It is why the default keeps group sessions rather than replacing them.
- [Coaching dosage shows precisely estimated null effects](../claims/coaching-dosage-null-effect.md) [~M]: no evidence that more coaching hours, or more PD hours alongside coaching, go with larger effects; exploratory, because hours were reported inconsistently. It limits the page: "ongoing" is not shown to work through more hours. With [no specific coaching dosage is established as necessary](../claims/coaching-dosage-quality-over-quantity.md) [~M] (a review that says quality appears to matter more, while comprehensive coaching does better sustained), it qualifies the old page's preference for continuous activity: duration and quality, not hours, are what the evidence points to, and neither is tested against occasional sessions. ## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a named practice observed in teachers' lessons, sometimes with an aligned student outcome. Teachers value other things: time with colleagues, help with a hard class, materials that work, not being judged, or meeting a mandate. Ask each teacher, and record where it differs from the objective. Teachers report more satisfaction with informal learning than with in-service days ([informal PD more satisfying than in-service days](../claims/informal-pd-more-satisfying-than-in-service.md) [+W], a survey); in a nationally representative survey of 856 teachers, [almost all took part in formal PD, averaging 30 required hours a year, but far fewer were satisfied with it](../claims/formal-pd-high-participation-low-satisfaction.md) [+M] (84 percent attended in-service days, 20 percent were satisfied with them). Satisfaction is an observation about what they value, not evidence that practice changed. A study team that teachers enjoy and that never discusses instruction serves the valued goal and not the objective; one that reviews instruction under appraisal pressure may serve neither.

For example, a K–3 teacher may want a calmer reading block, while the school's objective is consistent phonics instruction. A literacy coach who co-teaches the phonics segment and helps with transitions in the same visit serves both; record both outcomes.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing ongoing school-based structures plus group sessions with group sessions alone, with observed practice as the outcome, show no difference, or if peer coaching without a trained coach shows no effect on observed practice. If peer coaching works only with a coach training the peers, the default should make the coach the carrier and peers the supplement. If the meta-analytic association with group training reverses in direct comparisons, drop step 1's emphasis. If maintenance studies show practice holding without a standing team, the sustain step is a preference, not a requirement. Do not protect the model by explaining every null result as "not enough time" or "not enough buy-in" after the fact.

Observed practice, student achievement, teacher satisfaction and maintenance after support are separate claims. The present evidence in this wiki establishes no dose, no team size and no peer-coaching cycle.

## Description
The report recommends that administrators, instructional leaders, and teachers designing professional development "might consider a NIC or incorporating features of NICs." Interview analysis indicated NICs may be useful PD because "the work was directly connected to their classrooms, included opportunities to share and brainstorm new ideas with other teachers, and created a sense of accountability for addressing challenges and making incremental improvements." The BMTN embodied long-identified effective PD features: content focused, job embedded, and collaborative.
