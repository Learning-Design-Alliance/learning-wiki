Coherence Principle¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 37 claims (15 for, 20 mixed, 2 against) · 37 studies (17 causal, 6 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 theoretical, 2 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), q1–q4 · 10 of 37 report an effect size · 30 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision inside Multimedia Learning: what to leave out. When learners are studying an explanation of a process, system or idea they do not yet understand, and the lesson carries material that is interesting but not needed for that understanding (a vivid anecdote, a fun fact, a decorative photograph, a tangential video clip, background music), leaving that material out is expected to produce better comprehension and transfer than the same lesson with it in. The added material is called a seductive detail because learners usually like it: it can raise mood or interest while drawing attention, and working memory, away from the explanation.

The relationship is tested in the wiki's claims, and the claims bound it in five ways:

  • How large the effect is depends on who studied it. In Mayer's own programme of short lab lessons, a concise lesson beat an expanded one on a transfer test in 13 of 14 experiments, median d = 0.97 (Mayer 2008). Rey's (2012) meta-analysis confirmed the direction on retention and transfer, with a smaller magnitude than the headline experiments, and Sundararajan and Adesope's (2020) meta-analysis found the effect depends on design features. An independent e-learning study measured a cost of η² = .04, which its authors call very small (González et al. 2019). Pooled across all multimedia principles the average was g = 0.38, and which principle was applied explained most of the variance, so that figure is not coherence's own size either (Multimedia design principles improve learning overall [+M]). Plan for a reliable but modest effect, not d = 0.97.
  • What is added. Interesting but irrelevant text and pictures cost transfer in science lessons (Harp & Mayer 1998); readers recalled the seductive details themselves better and the main ideas worse (Garner et al. 1992). Purely decorative pictures, in three experiments with 7th and 8th graders, neither helped nor harmed learning overall, but weakened the benefit of the instructional pictures beside them (Lenzner et al. 2013). The type of image, the delivery format, language, subject, pacing and the kind of recall question all moderated the effect in the meta-analysis.
  • Who the learner is. The cost was larger for readers with lower working-memory capacity (Sanchez & Wiley 2006), appeared on harder texts only in students with low attentional inhibition (González et al. 2019), and the loss of the instructional-picture benefit was largest for lower prior knowledge (Lenzner et al. 2013). A combined revision of a middle-school mathematics lesson for signalling, contiguity and coherence lowered processing load for low prior knowledge students and raised it for middle prior knowledge students, with no difference in test scores (Clinton et al. 2017). Pooled over all multimedia principles, prior knowledge did not significantly moderate effects (Noetel et al. 2022).
  • Pacing and complexity. Across multimedia design principles, not coherence alone, design mattered more in system-paced media (g = 0.41) than in learner-paced media (g = 0.27), and more for complex material (g = 0.70) than simple material (g = 0.20) (Noetel et al. 2022).
  • The outcome. The evidence is on comprehension, recall of main ideas and transfer, mostly soon after a short lesson. Liking, mood and situational interest are separate outcomes and can move the other way.

How this sits beside its neighbours: Multimedia Learning holds the umbrella model (words with a relevant picture) and its other principles; this page is its "cut what the explanation does not need" principle in depth, and does not repeat that model. Redundancy (the same words twice, spoken and printed) is a separate decision on the umbrella page: redundant words are needed content delivered twice, while a seductive detail is unneeded content delivered once. Cognitive Load Management gives the general account of why extraneous processing costs learning; the overview that pooled load-reducing principles, removing seductive details among them, found the benefit largest for complex material, as the overload account predicts (Cognitive overload degrades learning [+M]). Signalling is the constructive counterpart: cue what matters rather than add what does not.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

The claims establish the direction and its moderators; none sets a lesson length or a schedule. The steps follow the evidence where a claim is named; the timings and the progression are labelled proposals, untested.

  1. Name what the explanation is for. Write one sentence saying what the learner should be able to explain or do afterwards, and list the parts of the content that sentence needs. (untested proposal)
  2. Audit every element against that list. For each block of text, picture, sound, clip and animation, ask whether the goal needs it. Cut background music, decorative photographs, tangential anecdotes and fun facts, and extra clips that do not carry the explanation. Check emphasis too: bold, underlining or highlighting on incidental material is incoherence of its own, since in one experiment underlining low-relevance text raised scores on that material (Coherence principle [+S]; Relevancy of emphasis directs attention [+M])
  3. Replace rather than only delete where a picture is wanted. Swap a decorative image for one that shows the relation being explained, such as a sketch of the problem's structure in place of a photograph of its setting. (Decorative photographs distract from the mathematics [+W]; Seductive details effect [~S], where decorative pictures weakened the benefit of instructional ones)
  4. Put interest material outside the explanation. If a story or hook is wanted, place it before or after the core explanation, not interleaved with it. (untested proposal)
  5. Keep each core explanation short. One idea per segment, about 3–6 minutes of narration or one screen sequence, with segmenting handled as on Multimedia Learning. (untested proposal for the length)
  6. Progression across sessions. (untested proposal, except where named)
  7. Early sessions on a new topic: the strict version above, for every learner. Lower prior knowledge, lower working memory and low attentional control are where the claims record the cost (Seductive details effect [~S]).
  8. Moving on: when a learner can explain the core mechanism on a changed case without the materials, add elaborations that carry content (applications, worked contexts, a story that is itself an instance of the idea) after the core explanation.
  9. Later sessions, as learners become experienced: support they no longer need, including explanatory text added to a diagram, becomes extraneous in its turn; cut it (Multimedia principles benefit novices [~M], where experienced trainees did better with the explanatory text removed; Split attention reverses into redundancy for experienced learners [~M]; Expertise reversal [~M], where effects that help novices shrink, vanish and can reverse with expertise).
  10. Judge it on the brief's own assessment of understanding, comparing explanation quality, not on how much learners liked the version. Add no test the brief did not ask for.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: does the added material carry anything the learning goal needs, or is it there to engage (ask: "if this were removed, what could the learner no longer explain?"); how much do the learners already know of this content, and how complex is it (ask what they have studied before and how many interacting parts the explanation has); and who controls the pace (video, live lecture or narration, or pages the learner reads and replays at will).

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning [+S]: Mayer's (2008) review of his programme, concise lessons beat expanded ones on transfer in 13 of 14 experiments, median d = 0.97; Harp and Mayer (1998) and Mayer, Heiser and Lonn (2001), experiments in which added interesting material reduced transfer; and Rey's (2012) meta-analysis, which confirmed the direction with a smaller average effect. The d = 0.97 is one research group's short lab lessons with immediate transfer tests; read it as that programme's result.
  • Seductive details effect [~S]: Sundararajan and Adesope's (2020) meta-analysis, read as an abstract, found seductive details can hinder learning with the effect depending on image type, delivery format, language, subject, pacing and question type; Garner et al. (1992), poorer recall of main ideas; Sanchez and Wiley (2006), a larger cost for lower working-memory readers; Lenzner et al. (2013), no overall harm from decorative pictures for 7th and 8th graders but a weaker benefit from instructional pictures, most for lower prior knowledge; González et al. (2019), a cost of η² = .04 in Study 1 and, on harder texts, harm only for low attentional inhibition (interaction η² = .15).
  • Design principle effects moderated by pacing and complexity [~M]: Noetel et al. (2022), across multimedia principles pooled, not coherence alone. System-paced g = 0.41 against learner-paced 0.27; complex 0.70 against simple 0.20; prior knowledge not a significant moderator (p = 0.14, which does not establish equivalence). The same overview's pooled average across principles, g = 0.38, with the principle itself explaining most of the variance (Multimedia design principles improve learning overall [+M]), and its reading that complex material is where poor design overloads learners (Cognitive overload degrades learning [+M], which also cites Sweller 1988, read as an abstract only).
  • Multimedia principles benefit novices [~M]: Kalyuga et al. (2003, review) and (1998, three experiments): support that helps novices loses its advantage, and can become a cost, as learners gain experience; the most experienced trainees did better with explanatory text removed from a diagram. It is evidence about redundancy and split attention, carried here to the claim that what counts as extraneous changes with expertise. The same reversal, as reviews report it: integrated text that novice electrical trainees needed became redundant for experienced ones, and integrated explanatory notes helped low language-competence readers and hindered more knowledgeable ones (Split attention reverses into redundancy for experienced learners [~M], Kalyuga et al. 2003 and Ginns 2006 citing Kalyuga et al. 1998 and Yeung et al. 1998); and a 2019 narrative review's statement that such effects first decrease, then disappear and can reverse (Expertise reversal [~M]). None of these tests seductive details.
  • Revised visuals raise processing for middle prior knowledge students [~M] and Revisions had no effect on test scores [~M]: Clinton et al. (2017), 62 middle-school students, one lesson revised for signalling, contiguity and coherence together. Eye-tracking load fell for low prior knowledge students and rose for middle prior knowledge students, and test scores did not differ by condition. The same pattern held for total fixation time on the lesson text (Revised visuals reduce processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students [~M]) and on the mathematics visuals (Revised visuals reduce processing of math visuals for low prior knowledge [~M]); average fixation length on the visuals was also shorter in the revised version for high prior knowledge students (Revisions to math-relevant visuals affect load by prior knowledge [~M]); and condition made no reliable difference to time with the lesson (Revisions made no reliable difference to time [~M]; a nonsignificant difference does not establish equal time). Because three principles were changed at once, it cannot say what coherence alone did.
  • Relevancy of emphasis directs attention [+M]: Nist and Hogrebe (1985), 67 college freshmen; underlining high-relevance or low-relevance text raised scores on whichever material was underlined. One experiment, F values only, immediate test; carried here to emphasis on incidental material as a form of incoherence.
  • Hypermedia usability problems [~M]: Scheiter and Gerjets (2007, review) name distraction, disorientation and cognitive overload as reasons learner-controlled hypermedia is hard to show effective; it bears on the learner-paced row, not on seductive details as such.
  • Classroom design affects learning progress [~M]: associational; moderately decorated primary classrooms outperformed bare and heavily decorated ones. The physical room, carried over to the wall-display row.
  • Decorative photographs distract from the mathematics [+W]: a curriculum developer's application of the principle, with no new data; it shows the replace-not-only-delete move.

Taken together: leaving out interesting but irrelevant material reliably does not hurt and usually helps comprehension and transfer, most for novices, for complex system-paced material and for learners with lower working memory or attentional control. The size is large in one programme's lab lessons and small in independent work. No claim in the wiki tests coherence alone with delayed outcomes, in VR, with young children or with second-language learners specifically.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Cognitive load reduction improves learning [+M]: its entries are all on split attention and contiguity; it names coherence as a lever only in its discussion, so it does not bear on this page's relationship.
  • Different media combinations affect recall and retention [~W]: a dual-coding experiment on which media carry the content, not on adding irrelevant material; it bears on the umbrella Multimedia Learning page more than here.
  • Adding seductive details to multimedia instruction significantly worsens recall of pertinent content compared with instruction without seductive details [+M] — attached 2026-10-16 from Kienitz et al. (2023), which proposed "Prompt learners that seductive details are irrelevant to the learning objective to protect recall of pertinent content"; tests this page's relationship.
  • In Study 1, a greater seductive detail amount linearly worsened recall performance (b = −0.11, p = .030) [+W] — attached 2026-10-18 from Lukas Wesenberg et al. (2026), which proposed "Avoid large amounts of seductive details in learning materials; small amounts may have negligible effects"; tests this page's relationship.
  • In Study 2 with a longer text and five SD amounts, greater seductive detail amount again worsened recall (b = −0.33, p = .005) [+W] — attached 2026-10-18 from Lukas Wesenberg et al. (2026), which proposed "Avoid large amounts of seductive details in learning materials; small amounts may have negligible effects"; tests this page's relationship.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is understanding that transfers: explaining a mechanism, solving a changed problem. Ask separately what the learner values. Learners often prefer the decorated version, and decorative pictures improved mood, alertness and perceived difficulty in one set of experiments (Seductive details effect [~S]); a learner who will not open a bare lesson learns nothing from it. Learners may also value seeing people like themselves in materials (culturally relevant visual design [~W]). Where liking and learning diverge, keep what learners value outside the core explanation or make it carry content, and say which outcome each choice serves.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if independent comparisons with comparable learners, holding the essential content equal, find no comprehension or transfer cost from added interesting material, or if a cost on immediate tests disappears at a delay. It should be qualified, not reversed, if added material that raises interest is shown to improve persistence or later learning in self-paced settings enough to offset its cost. A finding that middle or high prior knowledge learners do worse with stripped materials on learning outcomes, not only on processing measures, would move the boundary in the table. Do not protect the model by relabelling any addition that helped as "relevant" after the fact.

Claims¶

  • Related decorative pictures do not enhance transfer performance in either experiment (null results for correctness and metacognitive monitoring) [+W]
  • Extraneous cognitive load did not mediate the detrimental effect of seductive details in this online study [+W]
  • Low-WMC readers recall fewer correct causes when text contains seductive illustrations compared with nonillustrated text [+W]
  • No seductive details effect on transfer was observed, and no definitive conclusions about comprehension effects can be drawn from the multiple-choice transfer data [-W]
  • Seductive detail amount did not significantly affect transfer performance in Study 1 [-W]
  • Weeding extraneous information such as music and complex backgrounds improves retention and transfer from video [+W]
  • Working memory capacity interacts with illustration type: seductive illustrations impair comprehension only for low-WMC readers [~W]
  • Coherence principle: people learn better when extraneous material is excluded rather than included (d = 0.86 across 19 tests) [+W]
  • Weeding seductive details fostered learning only in a mandatory (high-reward) learning situation, not a voluntary one [~W]
  • Disrupting text coherence lowered both practice-test and retention-test performance [+W]
