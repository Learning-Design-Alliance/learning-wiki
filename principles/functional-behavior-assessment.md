# Functional Behavior Assessment

## Description
Disruptive behavior is handled more effectively by identifying the specific circumstance or event that triggers it than by focusing on the personality or character of the student doing the disrupting. Triggers generally fall into three categories (Heineman, Dunlap, & Kincaid, 2005): **physiological** (illness, fatigue, hunger, medication side effects); **physical/environmental** (a classroom too warm or cold, uncomfortable seating, a seating arrangement that interferes with hearing or seeing); and **instructional** (unduly restricted student choice, unclear instructions, activities that are too difficult or too long, or preventing students from asking for help when they need it). Once the specific trigger variables are identified, prevention becomes far more targeted — either by avoiding the trigger directly where possible, or by explicitly teaching the student a specific alternative response to the triggering circumstance, rather than applying a generic behavior-management approach that doesn't address the actual cause.

Fair, consistent discipline compounds this approach's effectiveness over time. When a student's behavior plan is part of a formal individual educational plan, departures from it should be rare and cautious, since an IEP functions as a legal agreement among teacher, other professionals, student, and parents, and any significant change requires due process consultation with everyone involved. Even without a formal plan, escalating punishment in response to frustration ("increasing the volume") tends to backfire; keeping careful, documented records of both the student's behavior and the teacher's own responses — demonstrating the reasonableness of rules and reactions — makes collaboration with parents and other professionals more productive, builds others' confidence in the teacher's judgment, and supports better long-term outcomes for the student.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Systematic observation aimed at identifying the specific triggering circumstance behind a disruptive pattern, rather than attributing the behavior to fixed personality traits
- Documentation practices robust enough to demonstrate the reasonableness of rules and responses to parents and other professionals over time
#### Constraints
- A formal IEP-governed behavior plan should not be departed from unilaterally, however tempting a stronger response may feel in the moment — due process consultation is both a legal requirement and a practical safeguard against decisions made in frustration
- Escalating punishment intensity in response to a student's continued disruption is a less effective strategy than better-targeted trigger identification and documented, collaborative adjustment

### Target Learners
- Students exhibiting recurring disruptive behavior where the underlying trigger has not yet been clearly identified
- Students under a formal behavior plan, where documentation and due process directly affect how support can be adjusted

### Target Learning Objectives
- Reduction in disruptive behavior through trigger avoidance or explicit alternative-response instruction, rather than through increased punishment alone
- Sustained collaborative support built on documented, defensible teacher judgment

### Theory
