# Functional Behavior Assessment

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (10 for, 6 mixed) · 18 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 4 review, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 18 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **finding out what sets off and keeps up one learner's recurring problem behavior, and choosing the response from that**. For a learner whose disruptive or interfering behavior keeps recurring although ordinary classroom management is in place, recording when it happens, what comes just before it (the trigger) and what follows it (what the behavior gets the learner, such as attention or escape from a task), then changing the trigger and teaching a replacement response that gets the learner the same thing, is expected to reduce the behavior (`behavioural` outcome) more than a generic response chosen without that information, and more than escalating punishment. Triggers fall into three groups (Heineman, Dunlap & Kincaid, 2005, as the earlier page summarised them): **physiological** (illness, fatigue, hunger, medication), **physical or environmental** (heat, cold, seating, not being able to see or hear), and **instructional** (too little choice, unclear instructions, work too hard or too long, no way to ask for help). The second half of the relationship, from the earlier page: responses that are fair, consistent, documented and agreed with the family and other professionals make long-term support workable, and escalating punishment out of frustration tends to backfire.

The relationship is conditional on the behavior **recurring often enough to observe** (a rare or dangerous behavior cannot wait for a pattern), on the trigger being **specific** (behavior that occurs everywhere and all day points to a skill gap or a wider need, not a trigger), on someone having **time to record and review**, and on the adults **carrying out the plan as written**. Whether gains last after support is faded is a separate question the evidence here does not settle.

**No claim in the wiki tests a function-based plan against a generic one, or tests documentation and consistency as such.** The nearest evidence is: one functional analysis that identified escape and attention as what kept up one child's behavior (a single case, in a feeding programme); a meta-analysis showing that universal classroom management, chosen without functional information, reduces disruptive behavior substantially; single-case studies of self-monitoring; a randomised study of an antecedent change (greeting students at the door); and randomised studies of an empathic, non-punitive discipline mindset for teachers. Each is described below with its limits.

How this page sits beside its neighbours: the [Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports pattern](../patterns/positive-behavioral-interventions-and-supports.md) places this assessment as the individual, most intensive tier of a schoolwide system, after universal practices. The strategy pages [Functional Behavior Assessment](../strategies/functional-behavior-assessment.md) and [Functional Behavioral Assessment](../strategies/functional_behavioral_assessment.md) give the step-by-step procedure (interviews, ABC recording, experimental analysis). [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md), a converted principle, holds learners checking their own on-task behavior; this page uses it late in a plan, as a way of handing control to the learner. The reinforcement ideas used here (reinforce the replacement, fade the reinforcer, distinguish a consequence from a reinforcer that measurably changes behavior) are set out on [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), which now holds the former reinforcement-theory principle.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete as a plan a teacher or support team can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it. Quantities are untested proposals unless a claim is cited.

1. **Check universal practice first.** Before an individual assessment, confirm the class already has clear routines, taught expectations and frequent positive contact; universal classroom management reduces disruptive behavior for most students without any functional information ([universal classroom management](../claims/universal-classroom-management-reduces-problem-behavior.md) [~M]). Start an individual assessment when one learner's behavior persists for about two to three weeks despite it (untested threshold).
2. **Define one behavior in observable terms.** "Calls out during independent work", not "is disrespectful". One behavior at a time, with a count, duration or intensity that two adults would record the same way. Untested here; from the strategy pages.
3. **Record trigger, behavior and what follows, across settings (week 1 to 2).** Use a short antecedent–behavior–consequence (ABC) sheet: time, activity, what happened just before, the behavior, what adults and peers did just after, and what the learner got or avoided. Aim for about 10 to 15 recorded incidents across at least three activities or times of day, and also note times the behavior did *not* occur. Use the three trigger groups as a checklist: physiological (was the learner tired, hungry, unwell, newly on medication?), environmental (temperature, seat, noise, sightline), instructional (task length and difficulty, clarity of instructions, any choice offered, any way to ask for help). Ask the learner and the family the same questions. Untested proposal; the trigger groups are from the earlier page.
4. **Write a hypothesis with two parts:** "When [trigger], [learner] does [behavior], and gets [attention / escape from the task / an object / sensory relief]." The evidence that functional analysis can separate these: one child's mealtime behavior was shown to be kept up by both escape and attention, and that result set the treatment ([escape and attention](../claims/inappropriate-behavior-maintained-by-escape-and-attention.md) [+W]; one child, a feeding programme, not a classroom). Expect more than one function.
5. **Test the hypothesis cheaply (week 2 to 3).** For three to five sessions, change the suspected trigger (shorter task, instructions given one step at a time, a choice of two tasks, a seat nearer the board, a snack before the lesson) and keep recording. If the behavior drops, the hypothesis gains support; if not, return to step 3 rather than adding consequences. Untested proposal.
6. **Prevent: change the trigger where you can.** Keep the change that worked. Antecedent changes that cost little can be built into the day: a brief, personal greeting at the door raised engaged time and reduced disruptive behavior in middle-school classes ([greetings at the door](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]; a randomised study of teachers and a three-student single-case study).
7. **Teach a replacement response that gets the same thing (weeks 3 to 6).** If the behavior gets help or escape from a hard task, teach a help card or a "break, then back" request; if it gets attention, teach an appropriate way to get it and give attention for that. Model it, rehearse it twice in a calm moment, and prompt it just before the known trigger. Reinforce the replacement every time at first (specific praise, the help or break it asks for), and keep reinforcement for the old behavior as low as safety allows. Untested here; from the earlier page and the strategy pages ([Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) sets out reinforcement and fading). Prefer praise and the natural payoff over tangible prizes for activities the learner already likes: expected tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement in laboratory experiments, while verbal praise did not ([expected tangible rewards](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]).
8. **Respond to the incidents that still happen consistently, without escalating.** Decide in advance what the adult does, keep it the same each time, and do not raise the "volume" of punishment when frustrated. A related finding: a short exercise encouraging teachers to treat misbehavior with an empathic rather than punitive mindset reduced suspensions, most for students previously suspended, though the preregistered overall effect was small and marginal ([empathic discipline](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]; middle-school math teachers; suspension is the outcome, not classroom behavior).
9. **Document both sides, every day, briefly.** Record the behavior count and the adult's own response, so that the reasonableness of rules and reactions can be shown to the family and colleagues. Share a one-page summary weekly with the family and the team. Untested; from the earlier page. Where the plan is part of an individualised education programme (IEP), do not depart from it alone: a significant change needs the team and the family, which is a legal requirement in that setting, not an evidence claim.
10. **Hand control to the learner (weeks 6 to 10).** Thin reinforcement for the replacement from every time to roughly every second or third time, then to the end of the lesson. For learners in about grade 4 and above, add self-recording: at set prompts the learner marks whether they were on task ([self-monitoring of on-task behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M]; single-case studies of students with or at risk for disabilities; on-task behavior, not learning). Untested as a schedule.
11. **Review against the baseline every two weeks.** Compare counts with the first two weeks. Move to the next behavior, or to lighter monitoring, once the behavior has stayed at or below the agreed level for two consecutive weeks (untested threshold). If there is no change after about three weeks of the plan as written, check first whether it was carried out as written (low-fidelity implementation of positive behavior support went with smaller improvements: [fidelity](../claims/low-fidelity-pbis-smaller-student-improvements.md) [~W]), then revisit the hypothesis. Do not respond to no change by escalating consequences.

**Dose and progression, in summary (untested proposal):** weeks 1 to 2, record and interview, no new plan; weeks 2 to 3, test one trigger change for three to five sessions; weeks 3 to 6, trigger change kept, replacement taught and reinforced every time, adult prompts before each known trigger; weeks 6 to 10, reinforcement thinned, adult prompts replaced by the learner's own record and a daily check with one adult; from then on, a weekly check. Each ABC entry takes under a minute; each review about ten minutes.

When a condition is not met: if the behavior is dangerous, do not wait two weeks to observe; put a safety plan in place and involve a behavior specialist at once. If the behavior occurs in every setting and activity, look for a skill the learner lacks (they do not know how to join a group, ask for help, or wait), and teach it directly (see [Explicit Interpersonal Skills Instruction](../strategies/explicit-interpersonal-skills-instruction.md)), or for a wider need that calls for referral.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **How often, where and how dangerously the behavior occurs.** Frequent behavior in some settings and not others suits recording and a trigger hypothesis; rare or dangerous behavior needs a safety plan and specialist help first; behavior everywhere suggests a skill gap or a wider need. If unstated, ask: how many times a day or week, in which lessons or places, and has anyone been hurt?
- **Who can observe and record, and for how long.** The plan above assumes an adult can make a one-minute note after each incident for two weeks and meet for ten minutes every two weeks. If unstated, ask: who sees the learner during the problem times, and how much time a week can they give to recording and review?
- **What the plan is for, and who must agree to it.** Fewer disruptions in one lesson, staying in school (avoiding exclusion), or the learner managing their own behavior in a new setting need different targets and different checks; a formal plan (IEP or behavior plan) sets who must agree to changes. If unstated, ask: what would count as success, by when, and is there an existing written plan?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a behavior in a setting: its time, the activity, what happened just before, what adults and peers did just after, what the learner got or avoided, and how often it occurs. "Attention-seeking", "avoidant", "defiant" and "lacks the skill" are inferred states, and the same behavior can serve different functions for different learners, or for one learner at different times.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Calls out or disrupts during independent work | Gets adult attention; the work is too hard and the disruption gets help or escape; the learner does not know how to ask for help | Give the same work at an easier level for two sessions; separately, give attention every few minutes regardless of behavior. Which one lowers the rate? |
| Refuses or leaves when a task is set | Escape from task difficulty or length; tired, hungry or unwell at that time; no choice offered | Compare the same task in the morning and afternoon, a shorter version, and a choice of two tasks; ask the learner what makes it hard. |
| Behavior clusters at one time of day or in one room | A physiological pattern (before lunch, after medication); an environmental one (heat, noise, seat); the subject or the adult in that lesson | Move the activity to another time or room for a week; check medication and meal times with the family. |
| Behavior falls, then returns | Reinforcement thinned too fast; a new trigger; adults have drifted from the plan; the replacement stopped working for the learner | Check the record of adult responses against the plan, then restore the earlier reinforcement level for a week. |
| Behavior occurs in every setting, with every adult | A skill the learner lacks; a wider need (anxiety, trauma, an unmet learning need) rather than a specific trigger | Teach the missing skill directly and see whether the behavior falls; if not, refer for a wider assessment. |
| Behavior gets worse when a consequence is applied | The consequence removes the learner from the task (escape) or gives attention, so it reinforces the behavior | Record what the learner gets from each consequence for a week; replace consequences that give escape or attention. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Changing a condition to test a hypothesis also changes the behavior, so record each test and its dates, and keep two explanations open while both still fit the record.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares a function-based plan with a plan chosen without functional information, or tests trigger identification, documentation or consistency of response as such. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

- [Inappropriate mealtime behavior was maintained by escape and attention](../claims/inappropriate-behavior-maintained-by-escape-and-attention.md) [+W]: in a day-treatment feeding programme, a functional analysis with escape, attention and control conditions showed that one child's inappropriate mealtime behavior was kept up by both escape and attention, which set the baseline and the extinction treatment (Groff et al. 2011; one participant; coded `q2`, causal `r1`; no effect size). It shows a functional analysis separating two functions in one case, and that one behavior can serve two. It is not a classroom study and does not compare function-based with other treatment.
- [Teachers' universal classroom management practices significantly reduce problem classroom behavior](../claims/universal-classroom-management-reduces-problem-behavior.md) [~M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 12 studies of whole-classroom management interventions in K–12 classrooms (Oliver, Wehby & Reschly 2011) reports a mean classroom effect of 0.80 (ICC = .05) or .71 (ICC = .10), ranging from −0.05 to 1.74, coded `q3 i2`. **It qualifies this page**: classroom-wide practice, chosen with no functional information about any one student, reduces disruption substantially, so the earlier page's contrast with "a generic behavior-management approach" holds only for the learner whose behavior persists despite it. Two sibling claims from the same review: [students in treatment classrooms were less disruptive in all 12 studies](../claims/treatment-classrooms-less-disruptive-than-control.md) [+M], and [effect sizes across the studies were homogeneous, so no moderator analyses were justified](../claims/homogeneous-effect-sizes-classroom-management.md) [~M], which means the review cannot say for which students or settings universal practice is enough.
- [Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: middle-school teachers randomly assigned to greet students individually at the door, against an attention control, produced more academic engaged time and less disruptive behavior (Cook et al. 2018, `q3`); in a three-student multiple-baseline study, on-task behavior rose from a mean of 45% to 72% (Allday & Pakurar 2007, `q1`). Neither reports a standardised effect size. It supports changing what comes before a behavior as a cheap preventive move; it is a class-wide antecedent, not one chosen from an assessment.
- [Self Monitoring Improves On Task Behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of single-case studies with students with or at risk for disabilities (grades 4–12 in one) found on-task behavior rose when students recorded their own behavior at prompted intervals; in one, effects were similar with or without added reinforcement. Effects are reported as response ratios and overlap statistics, not standardised effect sizes, and the outcome is on-task behavior, not learning. It supports self-recording as the later stage of a plan.
- [Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]: a brief online exercise encouraging middle-school math teachers to take an empathic rather than punitive approach to discipline halved year-long suspension rates (9.6% to 4.8%, 31 teachers; abstract read); a preregistered, active-controlled replication (66 randomised teachers, 5,822 students) found a small, marginal overall effect and larger reductions for Black and Hispanic students and previously suspended students. It bears on the earlier page's warning against escalating punishment; the outcome is suspension, and the intervention targets teachers' mindset, not an individual plan. The claim's evidence would allow a stronger marker; it is held at M because it is carried from a teacher-mindset intervention to this page's individual plans.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]: two meta-analyses of experiments (128 and 96 studies) disagree on the size of the undermining effect but agree that verbal praise does not undermine intrinsic motivation; one found tangible rewards more detrimental for children than for college students. It qualifies how a replacement response is reinforced; the studies are mostly laboratory tasks, not behavior plans.
- [Teachers implementing PBIS with low fidelity tended not to experience large student behavior improvements](../claims/low-fidelity-pbis-smaller-student-improvements.md) [~W] and [teacher fidelity shows a significant interaction with pre-to-post change in problem behavior](../claims/tkss-fidelity-ancova-interaction-problem-behavior.md) [~M]: one pre–post study of 37 students with emotional disturbance in self-contained classrooms after a year of teacher training in positive behavior support (Benner et al. 2008), with no control group; the low-fidelity claim is the authors' interpretation (`q1`). It supports checking whether a plan was carried out before judging it.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (one child in a feeding programme, whole K–12 classrooms, students with disabilities in single-case designs, middle-school suspension records), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures how long a reduction in behavior lasts after a function-based plan is faded.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's or school's objective is usually fewer incidents of one behavior in stated settings, measured against a baseline, by a stated date, and sometimes the learner staying in class rather than being excluded. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to avoid looking unable in front of peers, to get help without asking out loud, to have a break when overwhelmed, to be treated fairly, to stay with friends. The function of the behavior often *is* a goal the learner values, met in a way that costs them, so agreement can be built in: a replacement response that meets the same need (a private help signal for a learner who values not looking stuck) serves both. Divergence is an observation too: a learner who values leaving the room will not keep a plan whose only payoff is staying.

For example, a Year 8 student who walks out of mathematics may value not being seen to fail; the teacher's objective is that the student completes the lesson. A plan that removes public answering, adds a help card, and starts each lesson with an item the student can do serves both. Record the student's goal and the objective separately, so that "stayed in the room" is not read as "learned the mathematics".

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing function-based plans with plans chosen without functional information, in ordinary classrooms, show no difference in behavior at follow-up; or if brief classroom recording by teachers turns out to agree poorly with what an experimental analysis finds, in which case the default design needs a specialist step rather than a teacher-run one. If universal classroom management, done well, leaves almost no learners with persisting behavior, individual assessment is a rare specialist tool rather than a routine one. If consistent, documented responses make no difference to family collaboration or outcomes, the second half of the earlier page's relationship should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong function" after the fact; record the hypothesis and the test before the plan starts.

A reduction in behavior during the plan, its maintenance after fading, its spread to other settings, and any effect on learning are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no recording period, no number of observations, no reinforcement schedule and no criterion for ending a plan.

## Description
Disruptive behavior is handled more effectively by identifying the specific circumstance or event that triggers it than by focusing on the personality or character of the student doing the disrupting. Triggers generally fall into three categories (Heineman, Dunlap, & Kincaid, 2005): **physiological** (illness, fatigue, hunger, medication side effects); **physical/environmental** (a classroom too warm or cold, uncomfortable seating, a seating arrangement that interferes with hearing or seeing); and **instructional** (unduly restricted student choice, unclear instructions, activities that are too difficult or too long, or preventing students from asking for help when they need it). Once the specific trigger variables are identified, prevention becomes far more targeted — either by avoiding the trigger directly where possible, or by explicitly teaching the student a specific alternative response to the triggering circumstance, rather than applying a generic behavior-management approach that doesn't address the actual cause.

Fair, consistent discipline compounds this approach's effectiveness over time. When a student's behavior plan is part of a formal individual educational plan, departures from it should be rare and cautious, since an IEP functions as a legal agreement among teacher, other professionals, student, and parents, and any significant change requires due process consultation with everyone involved. Even without a formal plan, escalating punishment in response to frustration ("increasing the volume") tends to backfire; keeping careful, documented records of both the student's behavior and the teacher's own responses — demonstrating the reasonableness of rules and reactions — makes collaboration with parents and other professionals more productive, builds others' confidence in the teacher's judgment, and supports better long-term outcomes for the student.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Systematic observation aimed at identifying the specific triggering circumstance behind a disruptive pattern, rather than attributing the behavior to fixed personality traits
- Documentation practices robust enough to demonstrate the reasonableness of rules and responses to parents and other professionals over time
#### Constraints
- A formal IEP-governed behavior plan should not be departed from unilaterally, however tempting a stronger response may feel in the moment — due process consultation is both a legal requirement and a practical safeguard against decisions made in frustration
- Escalating punishment intensity in response to a student's continued disruption is a less effective strategy than better-targeted trigger identification and documented, collaborative adjustment

### Target Learners
- Students exhibiting recurring disruptive behavior where the underlying trigger has not yet been clearly identified
- Students under a formal behavior plan, where documentation and due process directly affect how support can be adjusted

### Target Learning Objectives
- Reduction in disruptive behavior through trigger avoidance or explicit alternative-response instruction, rather than through increased punishment alone
- Sustained collaborative support built on documented, defensible teacher judgment

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Reinforcement Theory](../theories/behaviorism.md) [+M] — once a trigger and desired alternative response are identified, reinforcing that alternative response follows directly from established reinforcement practice
