# Summative Assessment

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 71 claims (15 for, 43 mixed, 13 against) · 59 studies (17 associational, 15 theoretical, 8 review, 6 causal, 6 qualitative, 4 design, 3 quant-synthesis), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 59 report an effect size · 69 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **end-point judgment itself**: when it is made, what it samples, under what stakes, and what comes before it. For learners at the end of a unit or course who have had instruction and practice on a stated capability (any `knowledge_type`, from a `procedure` or `concept` to a `complex-skill`), an end-of-sequence judgment is expected to report that capability fairly, and to steer study towards it beforehand, when four conditions hold:

- **It samples the capability at the level the outcome names**, with several tasks, not one, and in the proportions the outcome implies (recall, application, analysis, whole performance).
- **The criteria and the cut are set before the work is judged**, against the capability (criterion-referenced), not against the rank of other learners.
- **Learners have met the kind of thinking it asks for in low-stakes practice first**: retrieval of the content during the course, and items or tasks at the same cognitive level, with feedback.
- **The stakes and conditions do not add a demand the capability does not include**, such as speed, test anxiety or a single unrepeatable sitting, unless that demand is part of the capability.

The outcomes are `general-achievement` and `skill-performance` on the end-point measure, and how far a learner's result reflects their capability rather than the format (`assessment-accuracy`). The horizon is the end of the unit or course; nothing here forecasts retention beyond it.

**No claim in the wiki tests summative assessment as a design** against another way of reaching an end-point judgment, or compares summative designs built this way with designs that are not. The claims below test neighbouring parts: that low-stakes classroom quizzing during a course raised later classroom exam scores; that higher-order practice questions helped on lower-order exam questions; that collected university exam questions sat mostly at the lower levels of Bloom's taxonomy; that learners score lower on higher-order exam questions; that a lower-stress team-based format coincided with higher individual end-of-module scores in a cohort comparison; that test anxiety is associated with lower mathematics scores; and that one class's scorers disagreed using a rubric. The rest are arguments about grading, with no data.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier pages' guidance (the principle and the folded pattern, at unit or course grain, using a [summative assessment](../elements/summative-assessment.md), a [final exam](../elements/final-exam.md) or a [performance-based assessment](../elements/performance-based-assessment.md)), kept as a concrete default. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Write the end-point first, from the outcomes.** Before teaching, list each outcome with the performance that would show it (recall a fact, apply a procedure to a new case, analyse, produce a whole piece of work) and draft the end-point tasks from that list. From the earlier page ("clear target outcomes", "defined outcomes"); untested as a sequence here; alignment itself is held by [Constructive Alignment](../patterns/constructive-alignment.md).
2. **Blueprint the sample before writing items.** Make a two-way table of outcomes by level and decide what share of marks each cell carries. Check the draft against it: collected university exams were found to sit mostly at comprehension and knowledge level ([University examination questions concentrate at the lower cognitive levels of Bloom's Taxonomy, dominated by comprehension and knowledge](../claims/exam-questions-concentrate-lower-bloom-levels.md) [~M]; [Collected exam questions required no cognitive processes above apply and no originality in analysis or high-level interdependence](../claims/exam-questions-capped-at-apply-and-standardized-analysis.md) [~M]); a RAND evaluation reported in a review found that only 2 percent of mathematics items and about 20 percent of English language arts items on NCLB-era state tests asked for higher-order thinking ([State tests under NCLB measured little higher-order thinking, with only 2 percent of math items qualifying](../claims/nclb-tests-low-higher-order-content.md) [~M], second-hand, descriptive). For a criterion-referenced end-point, choose items that represent the skills essential to the outcome and order them as the teaching hierarchy does, so a result can be read skill by skill ([Criterion-referenced test items should represent skills essential to learning to read and be arranged in the hierarchy used for teaching](../claims/criterion-referenced-items-should-represent-essential-skills-in-a-teaching-hierarchy.md) [+W], a recommendation about reading tests, carried here to other outcomes). A proposed default: no outcome rests on one item; at least a quarter of marks at the level above "apply" where the outcomes ask for it; untested proposal.
3. **For a complex goal, include at least one whole task.** Where the outcome is a `complex-skill`, include an integrated performance (a case, a project, a practical) alongside shorter items that check components, so a failure can be traced. From the earlier page; the whole-task argument is a design argument, not a tested comparison ([A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~W]).
4. **Set criteria and the cut in advance, against the capability.** Write the criteria (a rubric for performances, a mark scheme for items) and the standard for each grade before marking, and share them with learners at the start of the unit. Criterion-referenced reporting says whether a capability was reached rather than where a learner ranks ([Criterion-referenced tests indicate whether a particular skill or objective has been achieved rather than comparing performance to other test takers](../claims/criterion-referenced-tests-measure-mastery-not-relative-standing.md) [+W], a definitional account). Treat scores just either side of a cut as not reliably different, and review them before deciding ([Grade cutoffs are arbitrary because adjacent scores receiving different grades are not significantly different, per the author's statistical argument](../claims/grade-cutoffs-arbitrary-adjacent-scores.md) [~W], an argument). Where the same end-point is used with later groups, keep the cut and its meaning fixed, or say plainly that results are not comparable: in one district's state test, the number of correct answers needed to meet a grade-five mathematics standard fell from 36 to 32 over four years, and students matched on other tests scored higher on the state test in later years ([ISAT scores are not comparable over time: students with identical earlier and later test scores earned different seventh-grade ISAT scores depending on the year tested](../claims/isat-scores-noncomparable-over-time.md) [~W], a district cohort analysis); a promotion cut raised each year pushed eighth-grade retention from under 2 to 11.1 percent ([Eighth-grade retention rates surged from under 2 percent prepolicy to 8.3 percent in the first policy year, peaking at 11.1 percent](../claims/chicago-gate-retention-surge.md) [~W], descriptive counts). Where a later outcome exists, check a cut against it: students who met one state test's college-ready mathematics standard were less likely to need remedial courses than those who met another test's proficient standard, while the two English standards could not be told apart ([In mathematics, students meeting the PARCC college-ready standard are less likely to need remediation than students meeting the MCAS proficient standard; in English language arts the standards are not statistically distinguishable](../claims/parcc-math-standard-less-remediation-than-mcas-proficient.md) [~W], a policy brief). A cut's label ("proficient", "pass") says nothing until it is checked against what it is meant to predict.
5. **Run low-stakes retrieval in every session before the end-point.** Five to ten minutes of quiz questions (clickers, paper or software) on earlier and current material each session, with answers given, counting for little or nothing. Classroom quizzing raised later classroom exam scores in middle-school and college courses, as reported in a review ([Classroom quizzing delivered by clickers, computer software, or paper improves student performance on classroom exams in middle school and college courses](../claims/classroom-quizzing-improves-exam-performance-across-grades-and-content.md) [+M]); the dose of five to ten minutes is an untested proposal. In a survey reported in the same review, most middle and high school students who had had such programmes said frequent retrieval practice helped them feel less nervous about exams ([Most middle and high school students surveyed after classroom retrieval practice programs viewed them positively and said frequent retrieval practice helped them feel less nervous about exams](../claims/students-report-classroom-retrieval-practice-helps-learning-and-reduces-exam-nervousness.md) [+W], student beliefs, not measured anxiety).
6. **Practise at the level of the end-point.** Include in those quizzes and in class tasks questions at the higher levels the end-point will ask for; one course found higher-order practice questions helped even on lower-order exam questions ([HOCS clicker questions during learning improve performance on LOCS exam questions more than LOCS clicker questions do](../claims/hocs-clickers-improve-locs-exam-performance.md) [+M]). Practice need not copy the end-point's item format exactly ([The benefits of retrieval practice do not depend on an exact match between initial retrieval practice conditions and the final test format](../claims/retrieval-practice-benefits-do-not-require-matching-initial-and-final-test-formats.md) [+M], lab and classroom studies reported in a review), but give one full practice run in the end-point's format about a week before it, so the format itself is not new; untested proposal.
7. **Keep the end-point from being the only assessment, and lower the stakes of any single sitting.** Spread the summative weight over two or more occasions or tasks (for example a mid-unit task and a final, or a portfolio plus an exam), and offer a reassessment route for a learner who fails one. From the earlier page ("one-shot stakes can distort performance"; "summative designs should not be the only assessment structure"); a lower-stress team-based format coincided with higher individual end-of-module scores in one cohort comparison ([Dental students in a revised low-stress TBL format scored higher on the individual summative assessment (iSAT) than medical students in the original high-stakes format, in a non-randomised comparison of two cohorts](../claims/low-stress-tbl-higher-isat-scores.md) [+W], two different cohorts, so the format is confounded with the programme; the same cohorts' surveys favoured the lower-stress format on stress, frustration and the perceived fairness of questions: [Dental students in a revised low-stress TBL format reported lower stress and frustration than medical students in the original format, in a non-randomised comparison of two cohorts](../claims/low-stress-tbl-lower-stress-frustration.md) [~W]; [Medical students in the high-stakes TBL format perceive questions as more difficult and less fair than dental students in the revised format](../claims/high-stakes-tbl-questions-seen-harder-less-fair.md) [~W]). Make at least one of the occasions a different kind of demand (a written paper and an observed performance, or a product and an oral check of it), not two records of the same performance: a conceptual framework proposes that distinct demands support a capability claim better ([Proposition 4: evidence gathered through distinct demands supports capability claims better than multiple records of the same performance](../claims/proposition-4-distinct-evidence-demands.md) [+W], a proposition, untested), and an essay argues that some check of whether each learner reached the objectives is needed, though tests are not the only way to make it ([Abandoning assessment entirely is not an acceptable alternative to competitive grading, per the author's argument](../claims/nonassessment-unacceptable-alternative.md) [+W], an argument).
8. **Remove demands the capability does not include.** Allow enough time unless speed is part of the outcome; keep reading load and language below the level being assessed; give a calm, predictable setting. Test anxiety is associated with lower mathematics scores, most in timed and evaluative conditions ([Math anxiety has a small-to-moderate negative correlation with math achievement (r = −.28) in a meta-analysis of correlations from 223 studies](../claims/math-anxiety-degrades-performance.md) [~M], a meta-analysis of correlations). Untested as a design move here.
9. **Check the scorers before releasing results.** For any task marked by judgment, have two scorers mark a sample of the same scripts against the rubric, compare, and settle differences before marking the rest; one class's six scorers using one rubric differed by more than a point in nine ([In one community-college lab class, six scorers rating six teams with an engineering rubric differed by a mean of 1.2 of 9 points, which the authors read as a sign the rubric may not be reliable](../claims/engineering-rubric-low-scoring-reliability.md) [-W]). Sample size: about one script in ten, at least five, is an untested proposal. How agreement is measured belongs to [Validity, Reliability and Bias in Classroom Assessment](validity-reliability-and-bias-in-classroom-assessment.md).
10. **Report by outcome, not only as one grade.** Return a result per outcome (reached, nearly, not yet) beside any overall grade, so the learner and the next teacher can see what the grade stands for. Untested proposal; the earlier page's "standards or criteria for judging performance". A faculty memo argues that transcript grades are a "blurred" record of performance, through inflation and changing grading conventions ([Transcript grades are a blurred currency of performance information, partly due to grade inflation driven by student pressure](../claims/transcript-grades-blurred-currency.md) [~W], an argument with no data), which is the reason for a per-outcome report beside the grade. Where learners start far below the cut, report their growth on the same measure beside whether they reached it: in one district's English-learner records, learners who had not reached the language-proficiency cut by grade eight had started grade one much lower, gained every year, and still never met a cut that rose with each grade ([ELs who did not reach proficiency by eighth grade showed continued growth but started first grade with much lower ACCESS scores, and rising grade-level thresholds meant they never met the proficiency cut](../claims/non-proficient-els-low-first-grade-access-growth.md) [~W], descriptive), and districts assessing broad "future-ready" skills reported more success when they focused on growth than on proficiency ([Districts report more success with future-ready skill assessment focused on student growth than on proficiency](../claims/growth-over-proficiency-pog-assessment.md) [+W], district interviews, no learner outcome). Do not use learners' end-point scores to judge their teacher: a policy brief reports that test-based measures of individual teacher effectiveness proved too unreliable for that use ([Standardized test data proved too statistically unreliable for determining individual teacher effectiveness, and support for its use is waning](../claims/test-based-teacher-effectiveness-unreliable.md) [-W], a review of evaluation research).

**Across a unit or course (dose and progression).** A proposed arc, untested: in the first session, show learners the outcomes, the criteria and one example end-point task; in every session after it, five to ten minutes of low-stakes retrieval, rising from mostly recall in early sessions to mostly application and analysis in late ones; at mid-unit, one graded task worth no more than a third of the total, returned with feedback within a week; about a week before the end-point, one full practice run in its format with worked answers; then the end-point, with a reassessment opportunity two to three weeks later for any outcome not reached. A learner moves on to the next unit when every outcome is reached or has a recorded plan for reassessment; for a course-level final, include earlier units in proportion to their weight in the outcomes rather than only the last unit.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time for low-stakes practice, at least make the end-point's format and criteria known at the start; if only one sitting is possible, widen its sample (more, shorter items and one whole task) rather than raise its difficulty; if no second scorer exists, re-mark a sample of your own scripts a few days later, blind to your first marks, and compare.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What decision rides on the result, and for whom.** A unit grade a teacher can revisit, a course grade on a transcript, and a licence or progression decision call for different breadth, scorer checks and reassessment routes. If unstated, ask: what happens to a learner who passes, and to one who fails, and can it be undone?
- **What the outcome asks the learner to do.** Recalling facts, applying a procedure to a new case, analysing, and carrying out a whole performance need different tasks, and a test of one does not report another. If unstated, ask: what would a learner who has reached the outcome be able to do that one who has not cannot, and with what aids?
- **What the learners have met before the end-point.** Learners who have practised retrieval and higher-level questions during the course, and seen the format, are judged on the capability; learners meeting the format or the level for the first time are partly judged on that. If unstated, ask: what low-stakes practice of this kind and this format did learners have, and how recently?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a task under stated conditions: the item or task and its level, the time and aids allowed, the answer, the score each scorer gave, and how this compares with the same learner's coursework and low-stakes quizzes. "Has reached the outcome", "panicked" and "was taught to the test" are inferred states. A summative score shows what a learner did on these tasks on this occasion; it is a sample of the capability, not the capability.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| High scores on recall items, low on application or analysis items | The capability at that level was not reached; higher-level questions were not practised; the higher-level items were badly worded or longer to read | Compare with class tasks at the same level; give one higher-level item orally and ask for reasoning; check item wording with a colleague. |
| Much lower on the final than on coursework and quizzes | Anxiety or time pressure on the day; disengaged answering (rapid guessing) on a test that carries little for the learner; coursework was easier or helped; the final sampled different outcomes; forgetting since the unit | Compare item by item with coursework on the same outcomes; look at response times on a computer-delivered test for runs of implausibly fast answers; offer a reassessment under lower stakes; ask the learner what happened in the sitting. |
| Two scorers give the same script different grades | Criteria are vague; scorers read a criterion differently; the script is genuinely between levels | Have both scorers explain their mark against each criterion; score three anchor scripts together and re-mark. |
| Most of the group fails one item | The item is flawed or ambiguous; that outcome was not taught or practised; it sits above the level taught | Read the item with a colleague; check the teaching record for that outcome; look at wrong answers for a common pattern. |
| A learner just below the cut on overall score | Below the standard; within measurement error of it | Review the script against the criteria outcome by outcome, and look at other evidence on the same outcomes, before confirming. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. The disengagement explanation rests on an analysis of a state summative test, which found disengagement had a material impact on individual scores and possibly a minor one on aggregates ([Disengagement has a material impact on individual state summative test scores, though its impact on score aggregations may be relatively minor](../claims/disengagement-material-individual-summative-scores-minor-aggregations.md) [~M], no statistic printed in the source text read); a second analysis of the same kind found school means, proficiency rates and rankings only minimally affected ([Disengagement had only minimal impact on school mean performance, proficiency rates, and relative ranking on the state accountability test](../claims/disengagement-minimal-impact-school-aggregations.md) [~W]), while another found that even a small amount of rapid guessing could change institutional rankings ([Even a small amount of rapid-guessing behavior can impact institutional rankings](../claims/small-rapid-guessing-impacts-institutional-rankings.md) [~W]). ## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective at the end-point is usually a defensible judgment that a capability was reached, at a stated level, for a record others will read. Elicit separately what the learner wants from it: a pass, a grade for a scholarship or progression, a licence to practise, proof to themselves that they can do it, or to be able to do the thing in their own life or work. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the grade may study the format and the likely questions; one who values the capability may resent a paper that tests recall of what they can already do.

For example, a nurse in a medication-safety course may want to calculate doses confidently on a busy ward, while the course's end-point is a timed written paper of calculation items. The paper serves the course's record; an observed calculation during a simulated ward round, judged against the same criteria, tests what the nurse came for. Record both, and do not read a pass on the paper as the capability on the ward.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if end-point judgments built with broad sampling, advance criteria, prior low-stakes practice and lowered stakes turn out to report capability no better, by later performance or by agreement with other evidence on the same outcomes, than a single conventional final. If randomised comparisons show that spreading summative weight over several occasions does not raise scores or lower anxiety-linked gaps, step 7 is a preference, not a recommendation. If blueprinting papers towards higher levels lowers scores without improving how well they predict later performance, the level shares should be revisited. If calibrated double-marking adds little agreement over a single trained scorer with a clear rubric, the marking plan can be lighter. Do not protect the model by calling every poor result "not enough practice" or "the wrong format" after the fact.

A summative score at the end of a course, retention months later, transfer to a new setting and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of items or occasions, no share of higher-order marks and no scorer-agreement threshold.

# High-Stakes Testing and Accountability Effects

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 32 claims (2 for, 22 mixed, 7 against, 1 unmarked) · 28 studies (11 associational, 6 review, 5 qualitative, 3 causal, 3 theoretical), `q1`–`q3` · 1 of 28 report an effect size · 32 claims rest on one study

## Description
Standardized testing is "high-stakes" when performance carries real consequences — for students (a graduation test required for a diploma, a licensure exam required for a career), or for schools (under the No Child Left Behind Act, or NCLB, failing to reach targeted proficiency levels can trigger reduced funding or restructuring). Accountability, standardized testing, academic content standards, and teacher quality are the four components of the broader Standards-Based Reform movement in U.S. education, dating to the 1980s.

**Content standards must be neither too broad nor too narrow.** A standard as broad as "students should be able to construct meaning through experiences with literature, cultural events and philosophical discussion" (with no grade level indicated) gives teachers nothing to prepare students for; a standard as narrow as "students can define, compare, and contrast synonyms and antonyms" produces a restricted curriculum. A 2005-06 American Federation of Teachers study found standards in several states weak, particularly in reading, with one-fifth of reading standards redundant across grade levels — the same standard repeated word-for-word at least half the time across different grades. Compounding the difficulty, even a handful of broad standards typically expands into dozens of specific goals, benchmarks, and objectives underneath — one state's first-grade math standard, judged high quality, contained 5 broad standards, 10 goals, and 29 total objectives — leaving more content than any single school year can realistically cover.

**Tests must actually align with the standards they're meant to assess**, or the whole premise of accountability collapses: a mismatch between what's tested and what the standards specify means test results simply cannot speak to students' standards-based proficiency, undermining the "theory of action" NCLB depends on. The 2006 American Federation of Teachers study found only 11 states had all their tests aligned with their own state standards.

**When there are too many standards to test them all, tests must sample, which creates genuine unpredictability.** Reliably assessing any one content standard requires more than one or two items, so testing all standards every year is not feasible — and when a significant fraction of standards go untested in a given year, teachers are put in the position of guessing which ones will actually appear, with real instructional cost when the guess is wrong (studying content that isn't tested, and not studying content that is). One prominent testing expert, Popham (2004), called this "a muddleheaded way to run a testing program" (p. 79).

**Absolute proficiency and student growth are not the same thing, and rewarding only the former creates a real distortion.** NCLB's Adequate Yearly Progress (AYP) framework is based on the absolute level of performance reached at one point in time, not on how much a student improved to get there — which means a school whose students improve substantially but still fall short of the proficiency line gets no credit for that improvement, while a school whose already-proficient students stagnate can still meet AYP. Because AYP timetables require every school to reach the same proficiency target by a fixed date, schools starting from a lower baseline must show much larger year-over-year gains than schools starting closer to the target already. Growth (or value-added) models correct for this by tracking how much an individual student's score changed year to year rather than only where it landed — and the evidence for why this matters is substantial: in one study, low-achieving fourth-grade students in Dallas, Texas were followed for three years, and 90 percent of those with effective teachers passed the seventh-grade math test, versus only 42 percent of those with ineffective teachers (cited in Bracey, 2004) — see [Effective Teachers Compound Their Advantage Over Consecutive Years](../claims/teacher-effectiveness-compounds-over-consecutive-years.md). The same study found low-achieving students were *more* likely than high-achieving students to be assigned an ineffective teacher three years running, compounding the disadvantage rather than correcting for it.

**States define "proficient" very differently from each other, which undermines any comparison across state lines.** Because NCLB lets each state set its own content standards, assessments, and proficiency thresholds, apparent proficiency rates can diverge sharply from a common external yardstick: in 2003, Colorado and Missouri reported 67 percent and 21 percent of fourth-graders proficient in reading on their own state tests respectively, but the same students scored 34 percent and 28 percent proficient on the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) — a well-regarded, nationally-standardized test (Linn, 2005). The gap shows there is no common meaning behind "proficient achievement" as states currently define it.

**The predictable downstream effect of high-stakes testing is curriculum narrowing and teaching to the test.** Surveys of elementary teachers found more instructional time devoted to math and reading, and less to social studies and science, in 2004 than in 1990 (Jerald, 2006); principals in high-minority-enrollment schools in several states reported reduced arts instruction. This has a real cost beyond the narrowing itself: reading comprehension in a subject like science or social studies depends on prior vocabulary and background knowledge in that subject (Recht & Leslie, 1988), so students can develop strong general reading skills yet still struggle with subjects they were given little instructional time to build background knowledge in. Teachers surveyed nationally (over 4,000 respondents) largely reported adapting instruction to the tests — elementary teachers reported the heaviest influence (56 percent said the tests shaped their daily or near-daily teaching, versus 46 percent of middle school and 28 percent of high school teachers) — while also expressing real skepticism about the tests themselves: 40 percent reported teachers had found ways to raise scores without improving actual learning, and over 70 percent said the scores didn't accurately reflect what minority students know and can do (Pedulla, Abrams, Madaus, Russell, Ramos, & Miao, 2003).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content standards specific enough to guide instruction but few enough to actually be taught and assessed within a school year
- Genuine, verifiable alignment between published standards and the content actually tested — not merely asserted alignment
- A growth or value-added component in accountability calculations, not absolute-proficiency measurement alone, if the system is meant to fairly credit schools serving initially lower-performing students
#### Constraints
- Standards too broad give teachers nothing concrete to teach toward; standards too narrow restrict the curriculum to trivial specifics — neither failure mode is safer than the other
- When only a sample of many standards can be tested each year, some amount of teacher guessing about what will appear is structurally unavoidable, not merely a matter of insufficient effort
- Comparing "proficiency rates" across states (or even across a state's own tests from different years) without a common external reference like NAEP risks comparing numbers that don't mean the same thing
- Heavy accountability pressure predictably narrows instructional time away from untested subjects, with a genuine downstream cost to comprehension in exactly those subjects once they are addressed

### Target Learners
- All students in accountability-tested grades and subjects; students in initially lower-performing schools bear a disproportionate share of the pressure created by absolute (non-growth) proficiency targets
- Students in untested subjects (art, and often social studies/science at some grade bands) are indirectly affected by instructional-time reallocation even though they are not themselves being tested

### Target Learning Objectives
- Accurate, comparable information about whether students are meeting content standards, without the curriculum-narrowing and teaching-to-the-test side effects that undermine the deeper goal of actual learning

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Assessment for Learning](assessment-for-learning.md) [-M] — high-stakes accountability testing is explicitly assessment *of* learning for external certification, and the pressure it creates can actively distort assessment *for* learning's low-stakes, revision-friendly design goals
