# Inquiry-based Learning

## Description
Inquiry-based learning organizes learning around questions, investigation, evidence, and explanation rather than around direct transmission alone. Learners are asked to pose or pursue questions, gather and interpret information, test ideas, and communicate what they have found. The core promise of inquiry is that learners become more active sense-makers, but strong inquiry design is not the same as leaving learners alone. It usually depends on well-scaffolded tasks, structured prompts, and explicit support for evidence use and explanation.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A meaningful question or problem**: Inquiry needs a genuine issue to investigate, not just loosely disguised recall.
- **Access to evidence or materials**: Learners need sources, data, phenomena, cases, or experiences they can examine.
- **Support for investigation**: Prompts, tools, and feedback help learners search, compare, and interpret information productively.
- **A synthesis task**: Inquiry is strongest when learners explain, argue, present, or revise based on what they found.
#### Constraints
- **Novice overload**: Open inquiry can overtax learners who lack background knowledge or inquiry routines.
- **Weak evidence use**: Inquiry can become opinion-sharing unless standards for evidence and reasoning are explicit.
- **Time intensity**: Good inquiry often requires more time for exploration, feedback, and revision than direct explanation.
- **False autonomy**: Simply making tasks open-ended does not guarantee meaningful agency or good learning.

### Target Learners
- **Learners developing research and reasoning habits**: Strong fit for tasks involving question formulation, evidence use, and synthesis.
- **Learners who benefit from autonomy with structure**: Inquiry can increase ownership when enough scaffolding is present.
- **Groups tackling open-ended problems**: Collaborative inquiry supports discussion, comparison, and joint meaning-making.
- **Learners with enough initial grounding to investigate productively**: Inquiry often works best after some framing, modeling, or background-building.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Question-driven investigation**: Learning to pursue a question through evidence rather than guesswork alone.
- **Critical thinking and reasoning**: Evaluating sources, patterns, and explanations.
- **Self-regulation in learning**: Planning, monitoring, and revising an investigative process.
- **Communication of findings**: Presenting, defending, or refining conclusions based on evidence.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Constructivist views of learning — learners build understanding by engaging actively with problems and evidence.
- Self-regulated learning — inquiry can strengthen planning, monitoring, and adaptation when learners have enough support.
- Experiential and sociocultural perspectives — investigation is often strongest when tied to interaction, tools, and authentic contexts.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- For novices, unguided inquiry can create excessive search and confusion; instruction often needs to begin with more support [[claims/we-1]] [~M].
- Inquiry quality depends heavily on scaffolding. Without it, learners may gather information without building sound explanations.

## Examples
- **Source-based investigation**: Learners pursue a question using multiple texts, data, or cases and justify their conclusion.
- **Guided science or social inquiry**: Learners investigate a phenomenon or issue using structured prompts and staged evidence gathering.
- **Problem-framing workshops**: Learners refine a broad topic into investigable questions before researching.
- **Digital inquiry tasks**: Learners navigate simulations, databases, or online sources to test and revise explanations.
