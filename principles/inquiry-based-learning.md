# Inquiry-based Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 61 claims (34 for, 20 mixed, 7 against) · 68 studies (19 causal, 15 quant-synthesis, 10 review, 9 theoretical, 6 qualitative, 6 design, 3 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 16 of 68 report an effect size · 48 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

Inquiry-based learning organizes learning around a question that learners investigate: they gather or examine evidence, test ideas and explain what they found. This page owns the general relationship. Read it in two layers.

**Guided inquiry, in school science, is associated with better achievement than the comparison instruction, and guidance is what carries it.** In school science, two syntheses recorded here report (one states its studies were `pre-k-12`; the claim page does not report the other's learners or settings) that inquiry-based teaching improved achievement and conceptual understanding, and that teacher-led inquiry showed larger effects than student-led inquiry. Most of the underlying studies are quasi-experimental, and the claim pages do not report the comparator, learners' prior knowledge or the horizon for each study.

**Unguided inquiry is the condition under which the relationship fails.** For a learner who is a `novice` on the target relation (for example, how to design a fair test), leaving essential content or procedures to be discovered without support learns less than explicit instruction, while discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations does better than other instruction (`guided-discovery`). Investigating *before* instruction (`problem-first`) is a narrower configuration with support for conceptual knowledge and transfer only when instruction follows, no procedural advantage, and a reversal for young children.

So the expected change is in conceptual understanding and evidence-based explanation (`conceptual-understanding`, `near-transfer`), conditional on: the learner's task-specific knowledge of the target relation and of the inquiry moves themselves; how much guidance is given and when (prompts, feedback, modelling, teacher-led explanation); whether a consolidation or synthesis phase connects findings to the canonical idea; the outcome chosen (conceptual, procedural, inquiry skill, far transfer); and the horizon. No claim in the wiki reports a delayed-retention outcome for inquiry, and none tests inquiry against another method outside science or with adults beyond one pre-service-teacher study; for undergraduate research courses, one-to-one mentored research, remote inquiry and gamified scenarios the wiki holds only descriptive case studies and learner ratings. The guidance itself should change with expertise: supports that help novices can become redundant for learners who already know the relation and the moves (expertise reversal, recorded outside inquiry).

**Siblings.** [Problem-based learning](problem-based-learning.md) is one inquiry form, centred on an authentic problem; its conditional model (guidance for novices leads, problem-first is a narrower configuration, the curriculum-level average is small and variable) sits inside this one and should be read for problem-centred designs. [Scaffolding and fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) models how support is raised, lowered and withdrawn; inside inquiry it supplies the policy for the guidance this page says is needed, though its evidence comes mostly from one-to-one tutoring rather than inquiry tasks. The [inquiry-based learning pattern](inquiry-based-learning.md) and the [5E learning cycle](inquiry-based-learning.md) are reusable designs built on this relationship; neither has yet been converted to this format, and their sequences are proposals.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The page's earlier guidance, kept here as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and no claim here tests the sequence as a whole.

1. **Name the target before the question.** Decide whether the inquiry is meant to teach a content relation (why moths change colour), an inquiry practice (designing a fair test, judging evidence) or both, and how each will be assessed. Untested; from the earlier page's learning objectives.
2. **Pose a meaningful, investigable question** that needs the target idea to answer, not recall in disguise, and give learners access to the evidence they will examine (data, phenomena, a simulation, sources, cases). Untested; from the earlier page's requirements.
3. **Check what each learner can already do, and teach what is missing.** Probe the key relation and the key inquiry move (for example, ask the learner to judge whether a given comparison is fair). Where it is absent, explain or model it before or alongside the investigation. Evidence: unassisted discovery learned less than explicit instruction, and children who learned the control-of-variables procedure directly did as well on a later transfer task as the few who discovered it ([Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S], below). Treat this as diagnosis, not as an "Engage" activity expected to raise learning by itself: advance organizers helped learning in a 1980 meta-analysis, but activating prior topic knowledge did not improve primary pupils' text comprehension in one randomised experiment, and two amounts of activation did not differ in another ([Activating prior knowledge](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]; none of these is an inquiry study).
4. **Guide the investigation**: staged prompts, structured procedures, feedback on moves and teacher-led explanation phases, keeping the question with the learners. Evidence: teacher-led inquiry showed larger effects than student-led, and enhanced discovery beat other instruction (below). Asking learners to explain what each result shows is one form of guidance with its own evidence: self-explanation prompts improved learning by a moderate average amount (g = .55 across 69 effect sizes), mostly on immediate tests and outside inquiry ([Self-explanation prompts](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]). Make help contingent on what the learner just did (more support after a failed move, less after a success): in one-to-one tutoring, fully contingent support beat fixed, high or no support at immediate and one-month tests, though in one dynamic-assessment synthesis explicit strategy training did better still ([Contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]; untested in classroom inquiry). Which forms of guidance matter most inside inquiry is not settled in the wiki.
5. **Require a synthesis with explicit standards of evidence**: learners explain, argue or present a conclusion and say what in the data supports it, then revise. Partly supported: in the 37-study meta-analysis, studies emphasizing epistemic activities, or procedural, epistemic and social activities together, showed the highest effects. Structuring the synthesis as argument (claim, evidence, rebuttal) has support of its own: across 46 studies of online science argumentation, both argument skill and content learning rose by a medium amount (g ≈ 0.6), more in K-12 than in higher education ([Argumentation improves reasoning](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]; read from the abstract). The standards themselves are untested here; from the earlier page.
6. **Connect findings to the canonical idea.** If learners investigate before instruction, follow the investigation with instruction that builds on what they produced. Evidence: the problem-first advantage on conceptual knowledge is recorded only when instruction follows ([Productive Failure](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~M], below).
7. **Withdraw guidance as learners show they can make the moves unaided**, checking on an unassisted task before removing each support. From [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md); untested within inquiry here, and one large scaffolding synthesis found no difference by whether scaffolds were faded. The reason to withdraw is expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can become redundant, or depress performance, once learners have the schemas ([Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as expertise increases](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M], reviews of worked-example research, not inquiry). Across a unit, a workable progression is heavy guidance and modelled moves in the first investigations, prompts only in the middle ones, and an unprompted investigation of a new question at the end; this dose is a proposal.
8. **Judge the design on separate outcomes**: conceptual understanding, near and far transfer, procedural fluency and inquiry skill, at a stated horizon, not on fact recall alone and not on engagement. Check during the investigation, not only at the end: formative assessment raised K-12 achievement by a small amount in two meta-analyses (d ≈ .20–.29), more where students self-assessed ([Assessment for learning](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]; not inquiry-specific). Evidence: one study of guided simulation inquiry found a near-transfer gain and no far transfer (below).

Constraints from the earlier page, still untested: inquiry usually takes more time than explanation; open tasks can overtax learners without background knowledge or inquiry routines; inquiry can become opinion-sharing unless evidence standards are explicit; and making a task open-ended does not by itself give learners meaningful agency.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: **can the learners already make the key inquiry move and do they know the target relation?**, **how old are they?**, **is the outcome conceptual understanding, inquiry skill or a procedure?**, and **will instruction come before the investigation or after it?**

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what a learner does with a question under stated conditions: the question they pursue, the comparison or source they choose, the data they record, the conclusion they draw and the reason they give, the prompts and help they receive, and what they produce. "Can inquire", "owns the question" and "understands the concept" are inferred states. Activity, talk and enthusiasm are observations of participation; none measures whether the target relation was learned. A correct conclusion reached by following a worksheet does not show that the learner could reach it without the worksheet. In a group, a shared report does not show what each member can do.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Designs a comparison that changes several things at once | Missing the control-of-variables procedure; holds the idea but is overloaded by the apparatus or the setting; does not see the question as needing a comparison | Ask the learner to judge a ready-made fair and unfair comparison (evaluation, not production); if they judge correctly, reduce the apparatus demand rather than reteaching the idea. |
| Records data accurately, but the conclusion restates a prior belief | No working standard for what counts as evidence; a misconception resistant to one investigation; the data did not discriminate between the ideas | Ask what result would have changed their mind; check whether the data, as collected, could distinguish their belief from the canonical one. |
| Completes every guided step correctly, cannot explain or apply the idea to a new case | The prompts carried the reasoning (compliance, not inquiry); the idea is held but the learner lacks the language to explain it; the new case differs in more than surface features | Pose a changed-context question without prompts, allow an accessible response mode, and score near and far transfer separately. |
| Busy and engaged, little use of the target idea in the conclusion | Floundering search without the needed knowledge; consolidation not yet given; the question as posed does not require the target idea | Ask the learner to name the idea their conclusion rests on; check whether a correct answer to the question as written needs it; record whether any instruction connected the findings to the canonical idea. |
| Group report strong, individual explanation weak | Work carried by one or two members; learning distributed but not individually consolidated; the individual probe is harder than the group task | Ask each learner for an individual explanation under matched conditions, and record who did what during the investigation. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners. One design study gives a partial observation for the second row: when Year 8 students' data contradicted their hypothesis and they were prompted to re-observe, most generated a plausible explanation of the discrepancy rather than dismissing the result, though the explanations were not judged against a scientific criterion ([Scripted personally meaningful inquiry](../claims/scripted-personal-inquiry-associated-with-inquiry-knowledge-gains.md) [~W]). In an undergraduate design inquiry, many students still could not identify engineering tradeoffs, and the designers judged the budget constraint meant to surface them too complicated ([Students found tradeoffs difficult](../claims/students-found-tradeoffs-difficult-in-inquiry.md) [-W], an instructor's report): a constraint built to make reasoning visible can add load of its own. A probe can shift confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause on its own. Each probe is itself a learning exposure and should be recorded as one.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Inquiry Based Teaching Improves Science Achievement](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 37 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of inquiry-based science teaching (1996–2006) reports an overall mean effect on student learning of g = .50; a mixed-methods synthesis of 138 studies (1984–2002) of `pre-k-12` science reports a positive trend favouring inquiry, especially instruction emphasizing active thinking and drawing conclusions from data, over more passive instruction for conceptual understanding, with no pooled effect size. The claim page frames the comparator as transmission-style instruction, but the entries do not state comparators, learners' prior knowledge or horizons by study. Not settled: the abstracts could not confirm the entries, and one judge failure was reviewed and dismissed. It does not predict the effect for one class, for unguided inquiry, outside science, or at a delayed horizon.
- [Teacher Guided Inquiry Outperforms Student Led](../claims/teacher-guided-inquiry-outperforms-student-led.md) [~M]: in the same 37-study meta-analysis, studies with teacher-led activities had mean effects about .40 larger than those with student-led conditions. It rests on the same two syntheses as the claim above and is listed there as a merge candidate, so it is not independent support. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries passes. It is marked `~` because it limits the relationship to guided forms; the claim page reads it as a claim about novices, but neither entry breaks results down by prior knowledge.
- [Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 164 studies found explicit instruction outperformed unassisted discovery across 580 comparisons (d = 0.38 in favour of explicit instruction), while enhanced or assisted discovery (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) outperformed other instruction across 360 comparisons (d = 0.30). An experiment with 112 third- and fourth-grade `child` learners, `novice` at the control-of-variables strategy, found many more mastered it under direct instruction than discovery, and those taught directly did as well on a later transfer task as the few who discovered it; that experiment compares direct instruction with discovery and does not test guided inquiry itself. A narrative review argues the same direction. Partly checked: 1 of 3 entries passes; one judge failure was reviewed and dismissed. This is the boundary of the principle: it predicts poor learning when inquiry means unsupported discovery of essential content, not that guided inquiry fails. The abstract does not break results down by prior knowledge.
- [PAIR-C scaffolding shows mixed evidence for deep understanding and reduced misconceptions in emergent-phenomena instruction](../claims/pair-c-scaffolding-shows-mixed-evidence-for-emergent-phenomena-instruction.md) [~W]: one quasi-experiment (`adult` pre-service teachers, n = 50, no random individual assignment) in which both groups learned natural selection with the same agent-based `simulation`s; adding prompts, annotations and feedback around one causal framework produced a significant near-transfer gain, trends that did not reach p < .05 on deep understanding and misconceptions, and no far transfer in either group, at an immediate posttest only. Not yet checked against its sources. It shows that added guidance inside simulation inquiry can help near transfer and that far transfer did not follow; it does not test inquiry against another method.
- [Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~M]: a three-level meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons) set problem solving followed by instruction against the same instruction first; problem-first favoured conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), not procedural knowledge (g = −0.03), with larger effects when learners generated multiple solutions, worked in groups and received instruction building on their solutions, and a reversal for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries passes. Carry it only to designs where investigation precedes instruction and instruction follows; it is reported most reliably in well-structured domains (mathematics, physics) and does not support investigation with no subsequent instruction.

How far each may be carried: the two inquiry syntheses are school science and mostly quasi-experimental; the discovery meta-analysis spans domains but is read from its abstract; the PAIR-C study is one domain, one adult population and one immediate test. Retain learner age and starting knowledge, the exact comparator (transmission, explicit instruction, unassisted discovery, instruction-first), the amount and timing of guidance, time on task, outcome type and horizon when transporting these results. A pooled effect over unlike implementations is not a forecast for one class or one learner, and a nonsignificant difference does not establish equivalence.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

Ask what the learner wants from the investigation, and why, separately from the designer's objective. A question chosen as authentic by the designer may not be one the learner cares about, and a learner who cares about the answer may not care about the method the designer wants practised. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations; engagement during the investigation does not establish that the learner values the target capability, and choosing one's own question does not by itself establish ownership. Choose a representative instrument, criterion, permitted resources and horizon for each outcome that matters: conceptual understanding, transfer, inquiry skill and the quality of the final explanation are different claims.

For example, a secondary student testing water from a local creek may want to know whether it is safe to swim in, while the designer's objective is that the student can design a fair comparison and justify a conclusion from data. A correct answer about the creek, reached by following the teacher's sampling plan, establishes neither. Record both aims, let the student's question drive the investigation, and check the designer's objective separately with an unprompted fair-test task on a new question.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners receiving guided inquiry fail to outperform the comparison instruction on aligned conceptual measures in randomized or well-matched comparisons with stated comparators, or if the advantage disappears at a delayed horizon. If guided inquiry and explicit instruction of matched time produce the same conceptual and transfer outcomes, the case for inquiry rests on other aims (inquiry skill, the learner's valued goals) and should say so. If the teacher-led advantage turns out to be an artefact of study quality or outcome alignment rather than guidance, revise the guidance claim. If unguided inquiry is found to work for learners with stated prior knowledge, record that boundary rather than generalizing it. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as poor facilitation or insufficient authenticity after the fact.

An engaging investigation, an immediate conceptual gain, near transfer, far transfer, delayed retention and the learner's valued use are separate claims. The evidence recorded here establishes no optimal amount or form of guidance, no readiness threshold for open inquiry, and no effect of inquiry outside school science beyond one adult simulation study.

## Description
Inquiry-based learning organizes learning around questions, investigation, evidence, and explanation rather than around direct transmission alone. Learners are asked to pose or pursue questions, gather and interpret information, test ideas, and communicate what they have found. The core promise of inquiry is that learners become more active sense-makers, but strong inquiry design is not the same as leaving learners alone. It usually depends on well-scaffolded tasks, structured prompts, and explicit support for evidence use and explanation.

## Implications
Inquiry-based learning is powerful when learners investigate genuine questions and build explanations from evidence rather than only consume finished answers. That can strengthen reasoning, ownership, and transfer [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+M], but only when inquiry is scaffolded enough that learners know how to search, compare, interpret, and synthesize [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The central design tradeoff is openness: too little structure turns inquiry into unguided search [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [~M], while too much structure collapses it back into compliance. Strong inquiry keeps the question authentic while making the investigative moves teachable.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A meaningful question or problem**: Inquiry needs a genuine issue to investigate, not just loosely disguised recall.
- **Access to evidence or materials**: Learners need sources, data, phenomena, cases, or experiences they can examine.
- **Support for investigation**: Prompts, tools, and feedback help learners search, compare, and interpret information productively.
- **A synthesis task**: Inquiry is strongest when learners explain, argue, present, or revise based on what they found.
#### Constraints
- **Novice overload**: Open inquiry can overtax learners who lack background knowledge or inquiry routines.
- **Weak evidence use**: Inquiry can become opinion-sharing unless standards for evidence and reasoning are explicit.
- **Time intensity**: Good inquiry often requires more time for exploration, feedback, and revision than direct explanation.
- **False autonomy**: Simply making tasks open-ended does not guarantee meaningful agency or good learning.

## Description
Collaborative inquiry is a pattern in which learners pose questions together, investigate possible answers, critique emerging explanations, and revise their understanding in public. # 5E Learning Cycle

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (7 for, 2 mixed, 1 against) · 25 studies (10 quant-synthesis, 7 causal, 5 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 10 of 25 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The 5E Learning Cycle is an instructional sequence for science (and other inquiry-oriented) teaching built on the earlier Karplus learning cycle. Learners first **Engage** with a phenomenon that surfaces prior ideas and creates curiosity, then **Explore** it through hands-on or data-based investigation, then receive formal **Explain** instruction that connects their experience to canonical concepts, then **Elaborate** by applying concepts to new situations, and finally **Evaluate** their own and others' understanding. Its core design move is delaying direct explanation until after concrete experience, so that new concepts answer questions learners have already begun to ask.

## Implications

The pattern rests on the constructivist premise that learners build new understanding on existing conceptions, including misconceptions that must be surfaced before they can be revised. The Engage phase activates prior knowledge [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M] and the Explore phase creates a need for explanation, so the formal instruction in Explain lands on prepared ground rather than cold. Exploration before explanation also gives abstract concepts a concrete referent, reducing the working-memory burden of purely verbal instruction [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Evidence for inquiry-oriented sequences is broadly positive but conditional: guided inquiry approaches like 5E outperform unguided discovery, and their benefits depend on the quality of the guidance provided during Explore [Inquiry-based teaching with guidance improves science achievement.](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A anchoring phenomenon or problem rich enough to sustain exploration and raise genuine questions
- Materials, datasets, or simulations that let learners gather evidence before receiving explanations
- Instructor skill in facilitating investigation without prematurely giving away the explanation ([Eliciting student thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md))
- Time — the full cycle typically spans multiple sessions, not a single lecture
- Time for genuine student-led exploration before formal vocabulary or explanation is introduced
- A teacher willing to act primarily as facilitator during Explore, providing materials and guidance rather than direct instruction

#### Constraints
- Time-intensive relative to direct instruction; difficult to complete within a single class period
- Weakly guided Explore phases can leave novices floundering and reinforce misconceptions [Minimal guidance during exploration is less effective for novices than explicit instruction.](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~M]
- Requires learners to have enough prior knowledge to make productive observations; true novices may extract little from open exploration
- Assessment is often squeezed to the end, weakening the formative role of the Evaluate phase [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-W] when treated as summative-only
- Direct instruction should be minimal during Explore — introducing formal explanation too early undercuts the model's premise that terminology is more meaningful once a learner already has a concrete experience to attach it to

#### Grain Size
Unit or multi-day lesson sequence — one full 5E arc typically spans one to two weeks; individual phases map to single lessons.

### Target Goals
- Conceptual understanding of science phenomena, especially where misconceptions are common
- Science practices: observing, predicting, collecting and interpreting evidence, constructing explanations [Argumentation improves reasoning.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Transfer of concepts to novel contexts during Elaborate
- Learner curiosity and engagement with phenomena
- Inquiry-driven concept formation, where formal terms and explanations are introduced after (not before) direct experience with the phenomenon

### Target Learners
- K–12 and undergraduate science learners, the population for which the model was developed
- Learners holding common misconceptions that need to be surfaced and confronted
- Less effective for complete novices in highly technical domains, who may need more structured Explore activities or earlier explanation
- Learners for whom hands-on exploration and peer discussion, rather than lecture-first instruction, is the intended path to understanding

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — knowledge is built through experience; the Explore-before-Explain sequence lets learners construct concepts from evidence
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) (Sweller) — concrete experience before formal explanation provides a schema anchor, but insufficient guidance during Explore can overload novices
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) (Zimmerman) — the Evaluate phase positions learners as assessors of their own understanding
- [Sociocultural Theory](../theories/sociocultural-theory.md) — the Explain stage's introduction of formal terminology by the teacher-as-expert, after the student already has an experiential referent, mirrors scaffolded vocabulary acquisition within a zone of proximal development
- [Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [+M] — (also cited [~M]: unguided Explore phases underperform; the model works only with strong guidance)
- [Teachers' reported weekly use of the 5E instructional model for lesson planning rose from 13 percent to 42 percent over the Initiative](../claims/ngss-early-implementers-5e-use-increased.md) [+W]

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — the expertise-reversal literature qualifies the model: learners with high prior knowledge often benefit more from explanation-first sequences [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Claims
#### Supporting
- [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M] — the Engage phase activates and surfaces prior conceptions
- [Inquiry-based teaching with guidance improves science achievement.](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+M] — guided inquiry sequences like 5E outperform traditional instruction when guidance is meaningful
- [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M] — structured comparison during Explore prepares learners to benefit from explanation
- [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S] — the Explore and Elaborate phases enact active learning

#### Contradicting

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Engage** — Short activity, discrepant event, or question that surfaces prior ideas and creates cognitive disequilibrium ([Activation](../elements/activation.md), [Eliciting student thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md))
2. **Explore** — Learners investigate the phenomenon in small groups, gathering observations and data with structured prompts ([Guided inquiry](../elements/guided-inquiry.md), [Collaborative learning](../elements/collaborative-learning.md))
3. **Explain** — Instructor and learners jointly construct the canonical explanation, connecting it to the Explore experience ([Direct instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md), [Clear structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
4. **Elaborate** — Learners apply the concept to a new context or problem, extending understanding ([Application of knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md), [Transfer tasks](../elements/transfer-tasks.md))
5. **Evaluate** — Learners demonstrate understanding and assess their own conceptual change ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Reflection](../elements/reflection.md))

### Affordances
- [Activation](../principles/activation.md) — the Engage phase makes activating prior knowledge a mandatory first step rather than an optional warm-up, surfacing misconceptions before instruction begins
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — Explore and Elaborate are built around learners doing investigative work rather than receiving information, enacting the principle structurally rather than as an add-on
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — the Evaluate phase is designed to reveal conceptual change and feed forward into revision, not merely to grade
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — Explore-phase prompts, data tables, and guiding questions provide the structured guidance that distinguishes 5E from unguided discovery
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — sequences discovery before formal explanation, consistent with a constructivist view of concept formation
- [Bloom's Taxonomy](../theories/blooms-taxonomy.md) — the stages naturally progress from lower to higher cognitive levels, from initial engagement (Remembering/Understanding) through Elaborate and Evaluate (Applying, Analyzing, Evaluating, Creating)

### Personalization

**Novices with no prior knowledge:** Heavily structure the Explore phase with step-by-step protocols, guiding questions, and data organizers. Consider a brief pre-explanation of vocabulary so observations are productive.

**Learners with some background knowledge:** Open the Explore phase with more autonomy — let learners design their own investigations. Shorten Explain and expand Elaborate into application or extension problems.

**Learners with anxiety or low confidence:** Use structured group roles during Explore so every learner has a defined contribution. Normalize the confusion of the Engage phase explicitly — the point is that no one knows yet.

**Learners with diverse prior knowledge in the same cohort:** Use tiered Explore tasks with a common phenomenon but different levels of scaffolding; regroup for Explain so all learners arrive with observations to contribute.

**Learners with language or learning differences:** Pre-teach key vocabulary with visuals before Engage; provide written Explore protocols and sentence frames for constructing explanations during Explain and Elaborate.
- Explore-stage group size and scaffolding can be adjusted for learners needing more structure, without changing the overall five-stage sequence

### Sequence

2. **Explore** — Students directly explore the topic and materials, individually or (preferably) in groups, building a common understanding and practicing communication about it. 3. **Explain** — Students put their experience into communicable form: articulating process, sequence, and results to themselves, peers, or the teacher. ## Description
Guided Discovery Learning is a pattern in which learners investigate examples, data, or problems and are led by prompts, questions, and scaffolds toward important concepts or principles. The key word is guided. Learners are not left to discover ideas entirely on their own; the instructor shapes the sequence, materials, and questions so that exploration becomes productive rather than random.

The pattern is useful when the goal is to build conceptual understanding through active sense-making. It becomes weaker when the task is too open, the domain is too unfamiliar, or the guidance is too thin for novice learners.

# Interdisciplinary Course-Based Research Experience

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 unmarked) · 2 studies (1 qualitative, 1 design), `q2` · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
A Course-Based Research Experience (CBRE) has a whole class work on authentic, open research instead of individually supervised capstone projects. This pattern adapts CBRE for interdisciplinary contexts by deliberately recruiting students across disciplines and levels (undergraduate and graduate together) and organizing the course around a **shared scientific paper as a boundary object** that different students engage differently depending on their own discipline, while the class collectively builds a shared understanding of what counts as a strong research paper across disciplines. It extends [Productive Disciplinary Engagement](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md) (Engle & Conant's four supports: problematizing, granting authority, holding accountable, providing resources) into what the source study calls **Productive Syncretic Engagement (PSE)**: instead of committing learners to one discipline's problem-framing, authority structure, accountability norms, and resources, the course explicitly asks them to explore, weigh, and negotiate *among* different disciplines' versions of each.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Intentional recruitment across disciplines, career stages, and experience levels (the source study mixed undergraduate and graduate students across natural and social sciences)
- A shared object — here, "writing a scientific paper" — broad enough to be recognizable and valuable across all represented disciplines
- Iterative, low-stakes, frequent feedback cycles (peer review, drafts) rather than a single high-stakes final product
- Access to distributed mentorship: peers, the instructor, and — critically — students' own external disciplinary advisors/labs
#### Constraints
- Disciplinary "noncoherence" (different fields disagreeing on what a good paper looks like, when a hypothesis should be formed, what a reasonable data-collection sequence is) does not resolve on its own; learners cope with it via distinct tactics (separation — writing different papers for different audiences; domestication — reframing differences as minor variations; care — treating rubric-building as collaborative, provisional tinkering) and a course should expect and support this variability rather than assume convergence
- Works best over a full semester or longer; the source study's iterative four-moment structure took 13 weekly modules
#### Grain Size
- Course (semester-length; the source study iterated the design over 6 years)

### Target Goals
- Building genuine cross-disciplinary integration of research practice, not just co-located parallel research projects
- [Course-based research experience boundary objects support disciplinary integration and authentic research networks](../claims/course-based-research-experience-boundary-objects-support-disciplinary-integration-and-authentic-research-networks.md)

### Target Learners

#### Supporting

## Design

### Sequence
The shared object (a scientific paper) is engaged in four moments across the term, each shifting its function:
1. **Compelling object**: the instructor introduces the paper-writing goal as broadly urgent and recognizable, drawing on her own cross-field publishing experience to recruit students across disciplines who might not otherwise see a shared stake in the same activity.
2. **Concrete objects**: students bring their own discipline-specific papers into class; the group examines them side by side to articulate what a paper "looks like" and "does" differently across fields, connecting the abstract genre-goal to concrete disciplinary instances.
3. **Normative object**: the class collaboratively "live-blogs" the structure of example papers and builds a shared rubric — surfacing which paper attributes are common across disciplines and which are locally specific.
4. **Novel objects**: students draft their own papers through iterative, low-stakes peer review cycles, with mentorship from both classmates and their own external disciplinary advisors/labs; papers are revised repeatedly rather than produced once.

## Design
