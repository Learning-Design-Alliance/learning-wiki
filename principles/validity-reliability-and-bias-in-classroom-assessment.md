# Validity, Reliability, and Bias in Classroom Assessment

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 205 claims (88 for, 103 mixed, 14 against) · 159 studies (55 associational, 42 design, 22 review, 19 theoretical, 16 causal, 3 qualitative, 2 quant-synthesis), `q1`–`q3` · 13 of 159 report an effect size · 199 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **quality of the inference a teacher or designer draws from a classroom assessment score**, whatever the format. A score on a quiz, test, performance task or rating is expected to support a decision about a learner (reteach, regroup, grade, place, certify) only to the extent that three distinct conditions hold for that learner and that use:

- **Validity of the interpretation.** The tasks sample the intended capability in the intended proportions (content), performance depends on that capability and not on something entangled with it, such as reading load, English proficiency, speed or anxiety (construct), and, where the score is used to predict, it does predict the later outcome (criterion). Validity belongs to an interpretation and a use, not to the test: the same fractions test can support a conclusion about a fluent-English learner's understanding of fractions and not about a recent immigrant's.
- **Reliability.** The score would be similar on another occasion, with other items from the same domain, and from another rater. It rises with more items or tasks, clearer wording and clearer scoring criteria. A reliable score can still be invalid; an invalid one gains nothing from being consistent.
- **Freedom from bias.** Neither the content nor its administration changes measured performance because of group membership: no offensive stereotypes, and no item that rewards background experience some learners had outside class and others did not.

The outcome this relationship is about is `assessment-accuracy`: whether the decision made from the score is the one the learner's actual capability warrants. It is not a learning outcome. Learning follows only if a more accurate decision leads to a better next step, which is the subject of the converted pages beside this one.

**No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as a whole**: none compares decisions made from classroom assessments built with a table of specifications, more items, calibrated scoring and a bias review against decisions made without them, or measures whether those moves change what happens to learners. The claims below test parts of it in other settings: that raters differ in severity, that rubric guidance improved peer raters' placement of writing samples on complex criteria, that ignoring passage-based item dependence overstates reliability, that item position changes difficulty, that bias-detection methods disagree, that a pass/fail decision is least consistent where scores bunch, that anxiety is associated with lower mathematics scores, that rapid guessing and low effort distort individual scores (and that alerting a proctor to it was followed by higher performance and better convergent validity), that AI and LLM scorers agree with teachers only moderately and least well on mid-range and linguistically weak responses, and that high school grades predicted later attainment better than single test scores did, that state-test word problems were disproportionately hard for English-language learners because of their language, and that peer marks tracked a teacher's on exams with a clear marking scheme. Most come from large-scale testing, research instruments or single classes, and are carried to classroom assessment by extrapolation.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Competency-Based Assessment](competency-based-assessment.md) applies these properties to one kind of judgement, a criterion-referenced "demonstrated / not yet" decision against a threshold, and owns its threshold-setting and assessor-agreement model; this page holds the general measurement properties any classroom score needs and does not repeat that model. [Assessment for Learning](assessment-for-learning.md) owns how evidence is used during instruction; it presupposes evidence trustworthy enough to act on, which is this page's subject. The [Constructive Alignment](../patterns/constructive-alignment.md) pattern owns whether outcome, activities and assessment call for the same capability at the same level, which is the content-validity question at course scale; the [Authentic Assessment](../patterns/authentic-assessment.md) pattern is one design that raises construct match for performance goals at a cost to scoring consistency. [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md) holds large-scale cases.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher or designer can act on and revise. It is a procedure for building and using an assessment, run over a term, not a lesson. Each step's evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name the decision before writing an item.** Write down what the score will be used for (decide whom to reteach tomorrow, award a unit grade, place in a set, predict who needs help before a spring test) and for whom. Every later check is made against that use. Untested; from the earlier page's definition of validity as the justification of an interpretation and use, and close to one article's argument that a classroom assessment should be judged by whether it serves the decision its user has to make ([shift from validity to effectiveness](../claims/shift-from-validity-to-effectiveness.md) [~W], argument, no data).
2. **Plan the sample with a table of specifications.** Cross content areas against objectives, and set a planned item count in each cell before writing, in proportion to teaching time and importance, so over- and under-sampled content shows in advance. As a proposal, no objective that the decision rests on gets fewer than three items or one full performance task. Untested; from the earlier page (Linn & Miller).
3. **Ask for the performance the objective names.** If the objective is to conduct an experiment, have learners conduct one; do not ask questions about conducting one. Untested here; the course-scale version is [Constructive Alignment](../patterns/constructive-alignment.md).
4. **Use enough items or tasks for the decision.** One confusing item moves a 5-item score by 20 per cent and a 50-item score by 2 per cent (the earlier page's arithmetic). As proposals: a quick check that steers tomorrow's lesson can rest on 3 to 5 items; a grade or placement for an individual should rest on at least 20 selected-response items or three or more performance tasks, across more than one occasion. Where several items hang on one passage or scenario, count the passage, not its items, when judging how much evidence there is: in a simulation of passage-based reading data, treating dependent items as independent overstated reliability, by 4.8–7.4 per cent under a small passage effect and 17.0–21.8 per cent under a larger one ([ignoring local dependence overestimates reliability](../claims/ignored-local-dependence-overestimates-reliability-simulation.md) [~M], a simulation of a large-scale English reading test; untested for classroom quizzes). Do not aim for the highest possible internal consistency on a test that deliberately samples separate topics: where items cover distinct pieces of knowledge, a very high alpha may mean the items are measuring something general (reading, diligence, general ability) rather than each topic ([reflective modelling can misrepresent domain knowledge](../claims/reflective-model-misrepresents-domain-knowledge.md) [~W]; [a high alpha can signal a knowledge test failing its design](../claims/high-alpha-indicates-domain-knowledge-test-failure.md) [~W]; one theoretical article, no data). Judge such a test by its coverage of the table of specifications and by consistency across occasions and markers, not by alpha alone.
5. **Strip demands that are not the construct.** Have a colleague read each item for wording that could be misread, vocabulary and sentence length beyond the objective, unfamiliar formats and unneeded time pressure. Keep the reading load of a mathematics or science item below the learners' reading level; give a glossary of non-target words, or read items aloud, where reading is not what is being assessed. Basis: on a state fourth-grade mathematics test, some word problems were disproportionately hard for English-language learners compared with non-ELLs of the same overall mathematics score, and a textual analysis traced the difficulty to those items' linguistic features ([MCAS word problems showed DIF for ELLs](../claims/mcas-math-word-problems-dif-for-ells.md) [+M]; [linguistic features of the flagged problems](../claims/linguistic-features-of-dif-mcas-word-problems.md) [+M]; one large-scale study, abstract only on the claim pages, no effect size), and the same article summarises earlier work finding that linguistic complexity raises item difficulty more for ELLs of equal proficiency ([linguistic complexity raises ELL item difficulty](../claims/linguistic-complexity-increases-ell-math-item-difficulty.md) [+W], second-hand). It is the nearest the wiki comes to testing this step's premise, on a state test rather than a classroom one; it shows the language demand distorts the score, not that the classroom moves here remove it. A review also argues that verbal tests can understate subject knowledge when their symbolic form does not match how learners encoded it ([verbal achievement tests may underestimate subject-matter knowledge](../claims/verbal-achievement-tests-may-underestimate-via-symbolic-mismatch.md) [~W], a contention, not a tested result). A second reader catches problems the writer does not see: expert reviewers of draft attitude items found terms used inconsistently and statements with incomplete comparisons ([linguist review revealed item communication problems](../claims/linguist-review-revealed-item-communication-problems.md) [+W], one developer's account of one inventory). The time-limit and anxiety point is in step 10.
6. **Review content for bias before use.** Check every context an item depends on (a sport, a concert, a holiday, a job, the gender of the people in it): either it was taught in class, or every learner can be assumed to know it; otherwise change it. Remove stereotypes (all the doctors men, all the nurses women) and balance the people who appear. Basis: second-hand testimony that females did better on items with female or neutral figures than with male figures, and a count of 42 men to three women in 24 reading passages ([male-oriented item content biases test scores](../claims/male-oriented-item-content-biases-test-scores.md) [~W], a review of congressional testimony, standardised tests); the offensiveness and unfair-penalisation distinction is from the earlier page (Popham).
7. **Score against written criteria, with calibrated markers.** For constructed responses and performances, write a rubric with level descriptors and an anchor example for each level. Before marking, hold a calibration meeting (as a proposal, 30–45 minutes, 3–5 anchor scripts each marked independently, then discussed). Double-mark a sample (as a proposal, 20 per cent or at least 10 scripts in the first cycle) and compare. Basis: three trained raters scoring the same open-ended responses differed significantly in severity ([raters differed in severity](../claims/raters-differed-severity-mfrm.md) [~M], one many-facet Rasch analysis); rubric-guided training helped MOOC peer raters place writing samples at the right level more often than no guidance, mainly on subjective, complex criteria and hardly on simple objective ones ([rubrics changed peer reviewers' ratings](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M], two quasi-experiments with peer raters, carried to teacher markers).
8. **Look at the items after the first administration, before the scores are used.** List each item's percentage correct and flag items most learners missed, items the strongest learners got wrong more often than weaker ones, and items on which one group did much worse than its overall scores predict. Re-score, drop or rewrite flagged items before the grade is final. Treat a group difference as a question to investigate, not a verdict and never as something about the group: a gap by race or ethnicity, gender, socioeconomic status or language background reflects circumstances and treatment, including possible bias in the item itself, which is what this step looks for. Two bias-detection methods applied to the same state performance assessment agreed on only three of their seven flagged items each ([ICC and LDF methods identify different DIF items](../claims/icc-and-ldf-methods-identify-different-dif-items.md) [~M]), and on one physics test response-time differences between boys and girls did not predict which items were flagged ([DIF not predictable from response times](../claims/dif-not-predictable-from-differential-response-times.md) [~M]). Both are large-scale testing analyses; the classroom version, with small groups, is an untested proposal and will flag by chance.
9. **Treat decisions near a cut score as uncertain.** Where a grade boundary or pass mark falls among many scores, seek a second piece of evidence (another task, a short interview, an earlier result) for learners within a few points of it before deciding; do not report Cohen's kappa as the consistency of a pass/fail decision. Basis: in a simulation, decision consistency was lowest when the cut score sat at the peak of the score distribution ([decision consistency lowest at the distribution peak](../claims/dc-lowest-at-score-distribution-peak.md) [~M]); a worked example gave kappa 0.64 at 95 per cent observed agreement ([Cohen's kappa paradox](../claims/cohens-kappa-paradox-decision-consistency.md) [~W], illustrative); an essay argues adjacent scores across a cutoff are not reliably different ([grade cutoffs are arbitrary at adjacent scores](../claims/grade-cutoffs-arbitrary-adjacent-scores.md) [~W], argument). Where decision consistency is computed (a common examination, a department pass mark), the three usual methods agreed within 0.044 on normal or near-normal score distributions ([DC methods similar for normal distributions](../claims/dc-methods-similar-normal-distributions.md) [~M]) and differed most with the cut at the mode of a skewed distribution, by 0.101 in a simulation ([DC estimates differ most at the mode](../claims/dc-estimate-differences-largest-at-mode.md) [~M]), where W-DC or LL-DC are preferred and PS-DC is likely to overestimate ([W-DC and LL-DC preferred for skewed data](../claims/dc-methods-skewed-data-w-dc-ll-dc-preferred.md) [~M]); all three are one study of simulated and licensure data. Precision also varies along the scale: a review of state tests notes that reliability is usually highest near the mean or the cut and much lower for very high or very low scores ([conditional reliability low at the score tails](../claims/conditional-reliability-low-at-score-tails.md) [~M]), so an extreme score is the less precise one.
10. **Set administration conditions to the construct.** Use a time limit only where speed is part of what is assessed; keep the order of items the same across versions, or check that a version's later items are not harder because they come later; let anxious learners take low-stakes versions first. Basis: across 223 studies, mathematics anxiety correlated r = −.28 with mathematics achievement ([math anxiety degrades performance](../claims/math-anxiety-degrades-performance.md) [~M], correlational, so it does not show that removing time limits helps); an item placed later in one booklet was harder for that booklet's takers, which the authors attribute to fatigue ([later-positioned item more difficult](../claims/later-position-item-more-difficult-fatigue-effect.md) [~M], a national examination). On low-stakes checks, and computer-based ones above all, watch for disengaged responding: scoring models assume effortful answers ([measurement models assume effortful responding](../claims/measurement-models-assume-effortful-responding.md) [+W], argument), an answer given faster than the item can be read is a different response process from the one being assessed ([rapid guesses are construct-irrelevant](../claims/rapid-guessing-construct-irrelevant-response-process.md) [+W]), including such answers is argued to pull scores down ([rapid guessing distorts scores](../claims/rapid-guessing-distorts-test-scores-and-measurement.md) [+W], two articles by one author, no data on the page), and uneven rapid guessing often left test events with distorted content coverage ([differential rapid guessing distorts content representation](../claims/differential-rapid-guessing-distorts-content-representation.md) [+M], no effect size). Where response times are visible, re-ask or set aside rapid answers before using an individual's score: on low-stakes computer-based tests, a proctor notification triggered by detected rapid guessing was followed by more engagement, higher performance and stronger convergent validity evidence ([proctor notification](../claims/proctor-notification-improves-performance-and-validity.md) [+M], no effect size printed; [rapid guessing signals disengagement](../claims/rapid-guessing-signals-disengaged-test-taking.md) [+W]). For a class or school average the risk is smaller: school means on a PISA-based test stayed highly stable despite differential engagement, partly because disengagement pushed individual scores both up and down ([school means stable](../claims/school-means-stable-despite-differential-engagement.md) [~M]; [distortions cancel](../claims/positive-negative-distortion-cancellation.md) [~M]).

**Across a term (dose and progression; all quantities are untested proposals).** First unit: full table of specifications, a colleague's item review (about 30 minutes per test), a calibration meeting before marking, double-marking of 20 per cent, and item analysis before grades are released. Second and third units: reuse items that were not flagged, rewrite or drop flagged ones, and lower double-marking to 10 per cent if markers agreed on the exact level for roughly four scripts in five in the first round; raise it back to 20 per cent when agreement falls, a new marker joins, or the task changes. (The nearest precedent is a research interview, not classroom marking: a streamlined two-rater procedure reached .96 reliability with 73 per cent agreement, the interviewer's own ratings agreed with final ratings 87 per cent of the time, and rating moved to the interviewer alone with periodic reliability checks; [LoU interview reliability](../claims/lou-interview-interrater-reliability-high.md) [+W].) Each new term or new marker: recalibrate on fresh anchors. Once a year: check criterion validity where the score is used to predict (for example, do autumn scores identify the learners who later needed help?), and revisit the bias review with that year's group results. An item or task moves from "draft" to "bank" after one administration without flags; a score moves from "check" to "grade" only when it rests on the amount of evidence in step 4.

When a condition is not met: if no second marker exists, mark a sample twice yourself a week apart, blind to the first marks, and compare; if there is no time for item analysis, at least look at the three items most learners missed before using the scores; if the group is too small for group comparisons, rely on the content review in step 6.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the score will be used for, and how much rides on it.** A check that steers tomorrow's lesson tolerates a few items and one marker; a grade, placement or certification needs more items, calibrated scoring and caution at the cut. If unstated, ask: what decision will this result change, for whom, and can it be reversed?
- **Which demands besides the target capability the task makes, and whether they differ across the learners.** Language, reading load, cultural reference, speed, anxiety and the response mode are the usual ones. If unstated, ask: who are the learners (languages, literacy, background, access needs), and what must they do to show the capability that is not the capability?
- **Who scores, and how.** Selected-response items remove rater variation but narrow what can be assessed; performances and constructed responses widen it but depend on the rater. If unstated, ask: is the response scored by key or by judgement, by how many people, and with what criteria and examples?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a stated task under stated conditions (time, aids, mode, language, who scored it, with what criteria). "Understands fractions", "is weak at writing" or "the test is biased" are inferences. A score is evidence about the capability only after the alternatives in the table have been considered.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A learner scores low on a written test but explains the idea well in class | Reading or language load, not the capability; anxiety in the test setting; the test sampled content the learner had not been taught; the class explanation was prompted | Give a matched item orally, or with a glossary and no time limit; check the item against the table of specifications. |
| Two markers give the same script levels that differ by more than one | Criteria too vague at that level; one marker is consistently harsher; the script mixes strong and weak features the rubric does not weigh | Have both mark three anchor scripts blind; see whether the gap is constant across scripts (severity) or varies (criterion ambiguity). |
| One group does much worse on a few items than its overall scores predict | The items draw on background not taught in class; wording or format unfamiliar to that group; chance, in a small group | Read the items for outside knowledge and stereotypes; re-administer a version with a changed context; check whether the pattern recurs next administration. |
| Strong learners get an item wrong more often than weaker ones | The key is wrong; the item is ambiguous and strong learners see a second reading; the item rewards a guessing strategy | Check the key; ask two strong learners how they read it. |
| Scores are high on recall items and low on the performance task for the same objective | The recall items did not sample the capability; the performance task adds demands beyond it (time, materials, writing); the learner has knowledge without the skill | Compare with a short supervised performance with reduced extra demands. |
| Many scores fall just either side of the pass mark | The cut sits where scores bunch, so many decisions would change on a re-test | Add a second piece of evidence for learners near the cut before deciding. |
| A learner's score on a low-stakes computer-based check is far below their class work, and many answers took a few seconds | Disengaged responding (rapid guessing), not the capability; the content was not taught; anxiety or a device problem | Look at response times item by item; re-ask the rapidly answered items with the teacher present; compare with a short task done in class. |
| English-language learners do much worse than other learners on the word problems of a mathematics test but not on its computation items | The wording of those problems, not the mathematics; the problem contexts draw on unshared background; a real gap in the problem-solving skill | Give the same problems rewritten in plain language, or read aloud or in the learner's stronger language, and see whether the gap closes; compare with a problem-solving task done orally in class. |
| A learner's questionnaire reports frequent use of a strategy their work shows no sign of | The report reflects what the learner believes or thinks is wanted, or a reconstruction from memory; the strategy is used in other tasks; the work does not show the strategy even when used | Ask about this task straight after it; look at the work or a think-aloud on a similar task. |
| An AI-scored response gets a much lower mark than a teacher would give | The response is mid-range or its language is weak, where AI scoring is least accurate; the rubric rewards surface wording; the response really is weak | Have a teacher mark it blind; check whether the gap recurs for the same learner or the same kind of response. |

These rows are proposals, untested with teachers or learners as diagnostics. Each probe is a further assessment with its own error, so record it and keep explanations open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests whether classroom assessments built with these moves lead to more accurate decisions about learners. The claims below test parts of the relationship, mostly outside the classroom.

- [Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis](../claims/raters-differed-severity-mfrm.md) [~M]: a many-facet Rasch analysis of three raters scoring open-ended items in a crossed design found the rater facet significant, so the raters differed in severity; item and person reliabilities were still above .80 (`q3`, associational, no effect size). It supports calibration and double-marking; it does not say how much calibration reduces the difference.
- [In two online comparisons, a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on (one study) and helped peer raters place writing in quality tiers, most for complex criteria (the other); the first study says the rubric restrained reflective thinking](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M]: in a MOOC writing course, peers given guided-rubric training placed writing samples into novice, intermediate and advanced levels more accurately than unguided peers, with the benefit on subjective, technically complex criteria and little on simple objective ones; a second study with 47 graduate students found a rubric changed feedback content but restrained reflective commentary. Two `q2` quasi-experiments, no standardised effect size. It is the nearest test of one lever on this page (clearer criteria → more accurate rating), with peer raters, not teachers.
- [In simulation, ignoring local dependence overestimates reliability](../claims/ignored-local-dependence-overestimates-reliability-simulation.md) [~M]: across 20 simulated data sets modelled on a large-scale English reading test, passage-based alpha was 4.8–7.4 per cent lower than item-based alpha under a small passage effect and 17.0–21.8 per cent lower under a larger one (`q2`, design). It qualifies "more items, more reliability": items sharing a passage add less independent evidence than their count suggests.
- [Decision consistency is lowest when the cut score is at the peak of the score distribution](../claims/dc-lowest-at-score-distribution-peak.md) [~M]: in simulated data, all three decision-consistency methods gave their lowest value with the cut at the mode (`q2`, design). It supports extra evidence near a pass mark; it is about pass/fail examinations, not classroom grades.
- [Mathematics anxiety degrades performance](../claims/math-anxiety-degrades-performance.md) [~M]: a multilevel meta-analysis of 223 studies (332 samples, about 385,441 participants) found a correlation of r = −.28 between mathematics anxiety and achievement, moderated by grade level, ability and the type of scale and assessment (`q4`, `i2`, correlational). It makes anxiety a plausible construct-irrelevant source of low scores; it does not separate anxiety lowering scores from low achievement raising anxiety, and does not test any change to administration.
- [The ICC and LDF methods identify substantially different sets of items as exhibiting DIF](../claims/icc-and-ldf-methods-identify-different-dif-items.md) [~M] and [DIF could not be anticipated from differential response times](../claims/dif-not-predictable-from-differential-response-times.md) [~M]: on a state science performance assessment with about 16,000 examinees, two methods agreed on three items each; on a 32-item physics test, response-time differences between boys and girls did not predict which four items were flagged (both `q2`, associational). **They limit the bias step**: statistical flags depend on the method and are not self-explaining, so a flagged item calls for a content review, not automatic removal.
- [Test items with male-oriented content are a source of bias](../claims/male-oriented-item-content-biases-test-scores.md) [~W]: congressional testimony reports a 1979 ETS finding that females did better on items with female or neutral figures, and a count of 42 men to three women in 24 SAT reading passages (`q2`, review `r1`, second-hand, no effect size). It supports the content review; the claim's title states as established what its one entry reports second-hand.
- [Some 2003 MCAS fourth-grade math word problems exhibited differential item functioning for English-language learners](../claims/mcas-math-word-problems-dif-for-ells.md) [+M] and [textual analysis identifies the linguistic features of those problems](../claims/linguistic-features-of-dif-mcas-word-problems.md) [+M]: one study (Martiniello, 2008; `q3`, associational, no effect size on the claim pages, abstract only) flagged word problems disproportionately hard for ELLs matched on overall mathematics score and traced the difficulty to their language. **It is the closest direct evidence for the construct-irrelevant-demand part of the model**, on a state test: language load beyond the target changed what the score said about ELLs' mathematics. - [Peer assessment is equivalent to teacher assessment for exact-science exams with clear marking schemes](../claims/peer-assessment-valid-exact-science-exams.md) [~M]: on two exams in one online course (68 and 94 papers), peer marks did not differ significantly from the teacher's (Cohen's d 0.03–0.11 as printed) and correlated .88–.91 (`q2`, associational `r1`). - **Self-report instruments.** Three arguments from one journal issue on measuring strategy use ([memory reconstruction](../claims/off-line-self-reports-memory-reconstruction-problem.md) [~W], [prompting effects](../claims/prompting-effect-distorts-retrospective-self-reports.md) [~W], [perception rather than performance](../claims/questionnaires-measure-perception-not-performed-strategies.md) [~W]) and a review on young children ([questionnaires poor fit for young children](../claims/questionnaire-measures-poor-fit-young-children.md) [+W]) qualify any questionnaire used as classroom evidence: its score is a report, with its own construct-irrelevant demands (memory, reading, social desirability). None reports data on the claim page.
- **Internal consistency is not always the goal.** [A high alpha can misrepresent domain knowledge as a general factor](../claims/reflective-model-misrepresents-domain-knowledge.md) [~W] (`q1`, theoretical `r3`) qualifies step 4: for a test that samples separate topics, high alpha is not evidence the test works.
- **Disengaged responding.** [Differential rapid guessing distorts content representation](../claims/differential-rapid-guessing-distorts-content-representation.md) [+M] (`q3`, associational) and [proctor notification improved performance and validity evidence](../claims/proctor-notification-improves-performance-and-validity.md) [+M] (`q2`, coded causal) are the empirical core of a cluster from one research group on low-stakes computer-based tests; the other claims in it are the authors' arguments. - **Prediction.** Associational studies of US administrative records ([GPA predicts graduation more than tests](../claims/hsgpa-predicts-college-graduation-more-than-tests.md) [+M], [ACT adds little beyond HSGPA](../claims/act-adds-little-beyond-hsgpa.md) [~M], [GPA most predictive for English Learners](../claims/gpa-most-predictive-attainment-english-learners.md) [+M]) bear on criterion validity: accumulated course grades predicted later attainment better than one test did. ## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective here is a defensible decision: a score that supports the conclusion drawn from it, for each learner it is drawn about. Learners value something related but different. Some want a fair grade, some want to be seen as able, some want the result for what it opens (a course, a job, a licence), and some want to know what to work on. Ask them, before a high-stakes assessment, what they need the result for and what they expect it to show; ask afterwards whether it showed what they can do, and if not, what got in the way. A learner who says "I knew it but couldn't read the question" or "I ran out of time" is reporting a possible construct-irrelevant demand; record it as an observation to check, not as an excuse or a finding.

For example, an adult nursing assistant may want a certificate that lets them take on medication rounds, while the designer's objective is a reliable score on a written dosage test. A high written score supports the designer's inference about calculation; the learner's goal needs an observed performance on the ward as well. Record both, so that a test score is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if classroom studies find that decisions made from assessments built with a table of specifications, more items, calibrated scoring and bias review are no more accurate (against an independent judgement of the learners' capability) than decisions made from ordinary teacher-made tests, or that the gain does not change what happens to learners next. If calibration meetings do not reduce differences in teacher severity, step 7 should drop to double-marking alone. If content bias review in classrooms does not change group differences, the review stays as a fairness duty, not an accuracy claim. If low-language and untimed versions do not change the scores of second-language or anxious learners, the construct-irrelevant explanation should lose weight for those groups. Do not protect the model by calling every unexpected score "invalid" after the fact; the checks have to be made before the scores are used.

Content validity, construct validity, criterion validity, consistency across occasions, items and raters, and group fairness are separate claims, each needing its own evidence. The present evidence establishes no minimum number of items, no acceptable level of rater agreement for classroom marking, and no amount of calibration that is enough.

## Description
**Validity** is "the evaluation of the adequacy and appropriateness of the interpretations and uses of assessment results" for a given group (Linn & Miller, 2005, p. 68) — crucially, validity describes the *interpretation and use* of a result, not the assessment procedure itself. A mathematics test on fractions given to recent immigrants might validly assess a fluent-English student's understanding of fractions while invalidly assessing a limited-English student's (whose low score may just as easily reflect English proficiency as math skill). Validity is a matter of degree, not an all-or-nothing property, and rests on three kinds of evidence: **content validity** (does the assessment actually cover the content and tasks it's supposed to, in the right proportions?) — a **Table of Specifications**, cross-tabulating content areas against instructional objectives with a planned item count in each cell, is the standard tool for checking this before writing a test, since it makes over-sampled and under-sampled content visible in advance; **construct validity** (is performance actually explained by the intended underlying construct — e.g., mathematical reasoning or reading comprehension — rather than by an irrelevant factor like test anxiety, English proficiency, or reading speed that happens to be entangled with it?); and **criterion-related (predictive) validity** (does the assessment actually predict the outcome it's meant to predict — e.g., do fall-semester reading scores actually predict which students will need help passing the spring state test?). Effective assessment design in general depends on aligning the assessment technique to the actual learning goal: if the goal is for students to conduct an experiment, the assessment should have them conduct one, not answer questions about conducting one.

Validity and reliability apply beyond classroom assessment to measurement generally, including research instruments measuring **constructs** — personality traits, emotional states, attitudes, and abilities that can't be observed directly, either because they're tendencies rather than constant states (being "highly extroverted" doesn't mean acting extroverted at this exact moment) or because they involve internal processes like thoughts or feelings. Reliability itself breaks down into three distinct kinds of consistency: over time (**test-retest reliability**), across items measuring the same thing (**internal consistency**), and across different raters or researchers (**interrater reliability**). Crucially, reliability does not entail validity: a kitchen scale that is miscalibrated will consistently under- or overestimate a weight — highly reliable, since the same amount poured on produces the same reading each time, but not valid, since the reading is wrong. Any valid measure is, by necessity, reliable; the reverse does not hold.

**Reliability** is the consistency of a measurement (Linn & Miller, 2005) — would a student's score be similar if the test were given on a different day, with different items from the same content domain, or graded by a different rater? Perfect consistency is never realistic, since memory, attention, fatigue, and anxiety all fluctuate, and even trained raters vary somewhat scoring the same essay or performance. But reliability is not fixed — three concrete levers raise it: more tasks or items (a single confusing item drags down a 5-item test's score by 20 percent, but only 2 percent on a 50-item test, so more items dilute the effect of any one chance factor); clearer directions and item wording (ambiguity forces guessing about what's actually being asked, which is noise, not signal); and clearer scoring criteria (the less a score depends on which rater happened to grade it, the more reliable it is).

**Freedom from bias** means the assessment method or its administration doesn't distort a student's measured performance because of personal characteristics like gender, ethnicity, or social class (Popham, 2005). Two distinct forms matter for classroom assessment specifically: **offensiveness** (negative stereotypes embedded in the assessment itself — e.g., a health-class item where all the doctors are men and all the nurses are women — can distract or disengage the students the stereotype targets, degrading their measured performance for reasons unrelated to the content); and **unfair penalization** (an item disadvantages a group not through offense but through differential background experience — e.g., a math item assuming familiarity with American football disadvantages recent immigrants unfamiliar with the sport, or a teamwork item using a symphony orchestra as its reference point is easier for students from families who attend concerts). Unfair penalization is a real, specific failure mode, distinct from simply "some students doing worse" — asking about a sport actually covered in a physical-education class isn't unfair penalization, since the relevant knowledge was taught in class rather than assumed from outside background.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit awareness that validity is a property of an interpretation, not of the test itself — the same test can support a valid conclusion for one group of students and an invalid one for another
- A Table of Specifications or equivalent planning tool used *before* writing test items, not as an after-the-fact check
- Enough items or tasks, in a content domain, to dilute the effect of any single confusing or unrepresentative item
#### Constraints
- A test can be highly reliable (very consistent) while still being invalid for a given interpretation — reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity
- Reducing extraneous factors that inflate or deflate a score (anxiety, unfamiliar language, reading speed) improves construct validity specifically, and is a design responsibility, not just a scoring one
- Bias review by developers from varied backgrounds and statistical item analysis both help, but do not fully eliminate content or format bias — see [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md) for documented cases that survive this kind of review

### Target Learners
- All students; unfair penalization and offensiveness specifically affect students whose background, culture, or social class differs from the assumptions embedded in an assessment's content or framing

### Target Learning Objectives
- Assessment results that support the specific instructional or accountability conclusion being drawn from them, rather than results that are merely consistent or merely convenient to score
