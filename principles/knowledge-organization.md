# Knowledge Organization

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 19 claims (13 for, 6 mixed) · 23 studies (8 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 2 design, 2 theoretical, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 23 report an effect size · 14 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **organization a learner ends up holding** for a body of knowledge: which ideas sit at the top, how the rest hangs from them, and what learners do to make that organization their own rather than merely see it. For a learner who holds a topic's ideas as separate items (a `novice` on this material: they can recall facts but cannot say how they connect), and who will later need those ideas for tasks that cut across them (recalling the topic as a whole, explaining, solving problems that need several parts at once; `concept` and `principle` targets, `conceptual-understanding`, `near-transfer`), teaching the same content with an explicit organization (a few central ideas with the rest nested beneath them, chosen to serve the tasks to come) and having learners work with it (sort items into it, mark it, explain its links, rebuild it from memory) is expected to improve performance on those cross-cutting tasks compared with the same content taught as a single-level list. It is **not** expected to improve tasks that need only one detail at a time.

The relationship is conditional on four things:

- **Match between organization and task.** An organization helps the tasks it was built for. Ideas placed high in it are recalled and used better; ideas placed low are recalled worst. There is no organization that is good in general.
- **The learner's starting knowledge and ability.** In the one direct test, the lowest-ability learners showed no benefit; learners who already hold an organization of their own may find a given one redundant.
- **What the learner does with the organization.** Tasks that make the learner place items into it and mark its structure beat a task that only asks for a well-organized summary of the same material.
- **The comparison.** Against reading a list, organization helps; against an equally long retrieval activity, building a map while looking at the text loses.

**What is tested, and what is not.** One report (Eylon & Reif, 1979: three small experiments with college physics students and an advanced high-school physics class) tests the core contrast directly, the same content taught with a hierarchical or a single-level organization, and its claim pages carry the match, ability, detail-task and acquisition-task qualifications below. It is one study in one domain, with samples of 8 to 36, and the claim pages print F tests but no effect sizes. Two concept-mapping meta-analyses test one visual way for learners to build an organization. No claim tests other kinds of organization (causal chains, sequences, comparison matrices) as organization, a course-long framework revisited unit by unit, or how to fade a given organization across sessions. The default design below keeps the earlier page's guidance as labelled proposals around that tested core.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page: [Clear Structure](clear-structure.md) holds the relationship that making the *material's* organization visible (signals, previews, matching graphics) helps learners who lack one; [Graphic Organizers](graphic-organizers.md) holds the *visual form* (which diagram, who builds it); [Chunking](chunking.md) holds the *size* of units and where their boundaries fall; [Activation](activation.md) holds connecting new material to what learners already know before instruction; [Self-Explanation](self-explanation.md) and [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) hold the two activities this page asks learners to do with an organization. This page asks a prior question: **which organization should the learner end up holding, given the tasks they will face, and how do they come to hold it?**

## Default design

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. The core contrast (organized against single-level) has one direct test; each step says what stands behind it.

1. **Start from the tasks, then choose the top level.** Write down three to five tasks learners must eventually do with the topic. Put at the top of the organization the ideas those tasks turn on: governing principles if learners must derive or solve, a timeline if they must trace how ideas developed, causes if they must explain outcomes, categories if they must classify. If two kinds of task matter, build two short organizations of the same content rather than one that serves neither. Basis: in advanced high-school physics, students given principles at the top did better on deductive tasks and students given history at the top did better on historical tasks, two weeks later ([A hierarchical organization facilitates tasks only when matched to the task domain](../claims/organization-task-match-determines-benefit.md) [+M]).
2. **Put what matters most high, and keep the top level small.** Three to five top-level ideas, two or three levels deep per view; split a larger topic into an overview and one view per branch. Recall falls with depth: in the same report, recall two weeks later was about 50% for the top two levels, 27% for the third and about 5% for the lowest ([Information at higher levels of an acquired hierarchical organization is recalled better than information at lower levels](../claims/higher-hierarchy-levels-recalled-better.md) [+M]). The counts are an untested proposal; the reason for a small top level is the limit on what learners can hold at once ([Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], whose recent estimate is three to five chunks for novel material, a memory finding, not a test of lesson design).
3. **Show the whole organization before the detail (5 minutes).** Open the topic with the top level and how the parts relate, then teach each part as a branch of it, saying where it sits. Basis: college physics students taught the same content with a hierarchical organization did better on complex recall and problem-solving tasks than those taught it at a single level, presented once or twice ([A hierarchical internal knowledge organization facilitates performance on complex recall and problem-solving tasks more than a single-level organization of the same knowledge](../claims/hierarchical-organization-improves-complex-task-performance.md) [+M]). Showing it first, rather than at the end, is an untested proposal; for organizers given before instruction, see [Activation](activation.md).
4. **Have learners place items into the organization, not just summarize it (10–15 minutes a session).** Give a chart of the organization and a set of items (facts, examples, terms) to sort into it, then have learners mark which level each belongs to and label the links. Basis: with study time equal, students whose tasks included marking organizational charts and arranging items into a chart outperformed students who wrote a structured summary ([Strong acquisition tasks explicating the organization produce better internalization than weak tasks asking only for a structured summary](../claims/strong-acquisition-tasks-improve-internalization.md) [+M]; one small study). The durations are untested proposals.
5. **Ask for the links in words.** For two or three links per session, ask "why does this sit under that?" or "what would change here if that changed?". Basis: prompting learners to explain improved understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount ([Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]); explaining *links in an organization* is a carried application, not what the studies tested.
6. **Then have learners rebuild it from memory (5 minutes), and check.** Close the materials; learners sketch the top two levels and as much below as they can, then compare with the reference and fix gaps. Basis: building concept maps without the texts in view gave better short-answer performance a week later than building them while studying the texts ([The same quizzing or concept-mapping activity produces more learning when done without viewing the material, as retrieval, than while viewing it](../claims/closed-book-versions-of-quizzes-and-concept-maps-outperform-open-book-versions.md) [+M], reported second-hand); building a map with the text open lost to time-matched retrieval practice ([Concept mapping improves learning over most comparison conditions by a moderate average amount, but not over time-matched retrieval practice](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [~S]).
7. **Return to the same frame as detail is added.** Each later session opens on the organization, places the new material in it, and closes with a short rebuild. Untested; from the earlier page's "sequenced unit frameworks".
8. **Test with both kinds of task, and expect the gain only on one.** Check learning with at least one task that needs several parts together and one that needs a single detail. Basis: the hierarchical organization made no significant difference to tasks relying only on individual detailed steps ([Hierarchical versus single-level organization does not significantly affect performance on local tasks relying only on individual detailed steps](../claims/no-organization-effect-on-local-tasks.md) [~M]; equivalence was not tested). If detail matters on the assessment, keep detail practice as well.
9. **Plan the arc across sessions.** A proposed default, untested as a whole: *sessions 1–2*, the organization is given complete, learners sort items into it and explain two links; *sessions 3–4*, a partial frame (top level given, lower levels and links left blank), learners place new material and label links, then rebuild from memory; *later sessions*, learners rebuild from memory at the start, extend it with new material, and reorganize it once for a different task (for example, from a timeline to a cause-and-effect organization of the same events). Move a learner on when they can rebuild the top two levels from memory and explain two of three links they are asked about; give learners who already do so on the first day the partial or blank frame at once ([Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]).
10. **Watch the weakest learners.** In the direct test, the lowest-ability third gained nothing from the hierarchical organization while the middle third did ([Ability moderates the hierarchical-organization advantage: low-ability subjects showed no significant benefit, while medium-ability subjects did](../claims/ability-moderates-hierarchical-organization-benefit.md) [~M]). For learners who cannot yet use the organization, slow the sort-and-rebuild cycle, use fewer items per sort, and check the top level alone before adding levels; these adjustments are untested.

When a condition is not met: if the tasks learners will face are unknown, ask before choosing a top level (step 1), because an organization built for the wrong task gives no benefit on the right one; if the content really is a list of independent items (vocabulary, isolated facts), use retrieval practice on the items and do not impose a hierarchy; if learners already state the organization and its links unaided, skip steps 3–4 and use steps 6 and 8 only.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner will have to do with the knowledge.** The organization should be built for those tasks; one built for another task can help nothing or help the wrong task. If unstated, ask: name three things learners must do with this topic afterwards, unaided, and when.
- **How the learner holds the topic now.** A learner with isolated facts needs a given organization; one with an organization of their own may need it challenged or extended, not replaced; one who cannot yet use any organization may not benefit. If unstated, ask the learner to sort ten items from the topic into groups and say why, before teaching.
- **Whether the content has relational structure at all.** Causes, hierarchies, principles and developments have it; a list of independent items does not, and imposing one adds work without benefit. If unstated, ask: do the tasks need learners to connect items, or only to recall each one?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: how a learner sorts items into groups, the order and grouping of their free recall, a sketch they draw from memory, which links they can explain, and performance on tasks that need one part or several. "Has a well-organized understanding" is an inferred state. A neat map drawn with the materials open shows what the learner can copy, not what they hold.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Recalls many details but cannot say how they relate | No organization taught; one taught but never used by the learner; the test rewards details only | Ask for a sketch of the main ideas from memory; give one task that needs two parts together and compare. |
| Rebuilds the organization correctly but fails problems that need it | The organization learned is not the one the task needs (mismatch); learned as a picture without its meaning; the problem needs a procedure not yet practised | Ask which branch the problem belongs to and why; give a problem matched to the organization taught and one matched to another. |
| Sorts items correctly with the chart in view, fails to rebuild it from memory | Copying rather than holding; too many items or levels at once | Shrink to the top level only and rebuild again; compare with a rebuild after a day. |
| Learner with prior knowledge resists or ignores the given organization | They hold a different, possibly valid organization; the given one is redundant for them; it conflicts with a misconception | Ask them to draw their own first, then compare the two on a task each should serve. |
| Recall of lower-level detail is poor while the top level is solid | Detail was placed low and is expected to be recalled worst; detail was never practised on its own | Separate detail-recall check; move a detail that matters higher, or practise it by retrieval. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners. A sort or rebuild is itself practice and changes what it measures, so record each one, and keep open the explanations a single probe does not rule out.

## Evidence and qualifications

The direct evidence is one report, Eylon & Reif (1979), read through five claim pages that draw on different experiments in it. All five are small, all are physics, and none prints an effect size (the i codes on these pages rest on F tests or percentages, not on a printed effect size).

- [A hierarchical internal knowledge organization facilitates performance on complex recall and problem-solving tasks more than a single-level organization of the same knowledge](../claims/hierarchical-organization-improves-complex-task-performance.md) [+M]: Experiment 1, 36 paid volunteers from an introductory college physics course, blocked by ability and randomly assigned to a hierarchical organization or a single-level one presented once or twice. The hierarchical group scored higher on composite complex-task scores (F(2,27) = 10.84). This is the core contrast of this page, tested once.
- [Hierarchical versus single-level organization does not significantly affect performance on local tasks relying only on individual detailed steps](../claims/no-organization-effect-on-local-tasks.md) [~M]: in the same experiment, local tasks showed no significant differences between treatments; equivalence was not tested. It limits the page: organization is expected to help integrative tasks, not detail tasks.
- [Ability moderates the hierarchical-organization advantage: low-ability subjects showed no significant benefit, while medium-ability subjects did](../claims/ability-moderates-hierarchical-organization-benefit.md) [~M]: within Experiment 1, low-ability subjects showed no difference between treatments, medium-ability hierarchical subjects did better, and high-ability hierarchical and twice-presented single-level subjects did not differ. A second entry, from 8 subjects, is correlational. It limits the page for the learners who might seem to need organization most.
- [A hierarchical organization facilitates tasks only when matched to the task domain](../claims/organization-task-match-determines-benefit.md) [+M]: Experiment 2, 20 students in an advanced high-school physics class, randomly assigned within ability blocks to organizations with deductive or historical information at the top, tested two weeks later; each group did better on tasks matching its organization, on every recall and problem-solving task. No effect size is printed.
- [Information at higher levels of an acquired hierarchical organization is recalled better than information at lower levels](../claims/higher-hierarchy-levels-recalled-better.md) [+M]: free recall two weeks later fell with depth in the organization (about 50%, 50%, 27% and 5% from the top level down in one group), in Experiments 2 and 3.
- [Strong acquisition tasks explicating the organization produce better internalization than weak tasks asking only for a structured summary](../claims/strong-acquisition-tasks-improve-internalization.md) [+M]: in Experiment 2, with the same well-organized materials and equal time, tasks that included marking organizational charts and arranging items into a chart beat writing a structured summary (F(1,12) = 5.96).
- [Concept mapping improves learning over most comparison conditions by a moderate average amount, but not over time-matched retrieval practice](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [~S]: two meta-analyses (Grade 4 to postsecondary) find learning with concept maps beats most comparison conditions (g = 0.58 overall; constructing g = 0.72, studying a supplied map g = 0.43), but in two experiments with undergraduates, building a map with the text open lost to time-matched retrieval practice a week later (d = 1.50) and did not beat rereading. Mixed here because the page recommends a way to build organization that has a stronger rival.
- [The same quizzing or concept-mapping activity produces more learning when done without viewing the material, as retrieval, than while viewing it](../claims/closed-book-versions-of-quizzes-and-concept-maps-outperform-open-book-versions.md) [+M]: a review chapter reports that students who built concept maps without the texts did better on a short-answer test a week later than those who built them while studying the texts. Second-hand; learners and sample not given on the claim page.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes (g = .55) and smaller studies; immediate tests, little delayed or classroom evidence. It supports asking for explanations of links; it did not test organizations.
- [A review chapter (Karpicke 2017) reports, second-hand, experiments in which retrieval practice produced more learning than concept mapping (Karpicke & Blunt 2011) and than imagery strategies such as the keyword mnemonic (Karpicke & Smith 2012)](../claims/retrieval-practice-produces-more-learning-than-concept-mapping-and-imagery-elaboration.md) [~M]: the same comparison as Karpicke & Blunt (2011), reported in a review chapter. It qualifies map-building as the default activity; it is why the default design rebuilds from memory.

Do not rank these by effect label: physics students on hierarchies, pooled concept-map studies and retrieval experiments compare different things. None measures retention beyond a week or two, and none tests a course-long organization.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually unaided performance on integrative tasks (explaining, solving problems across parts of a topic, recalling it as a whole) at a stated delay. Ask separately what the learner wants from the topic: to pass a test of facts, to make sense of something that has confused them, to use it in a job. A learner who values passing a detail-heavy test may see the organization exercise as a detour, and on detail items they may be right; a learner who wants to understand may welcome it. Record both, and if they diverge, show the learner one task they care about that the organization helps with.

For example, an adult apprentice electrician may want to find faults quickly on site, while the course objective is recall of the regulations by section. An organization built around the fault-finding decisions (what to check first, what each reading rules out) serves the learner's goal; one built around the regulation chapters serves the exam. Building both from the same content, and saying which task each serves, keeps the two from being confused.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if further comparisons of organized against single-level teaching of the same content, with learners outside physics and with aligned integrative tasks, show no advantage, or if an advantage at two weeks disappears at a longer delay. If closed-book retrieval of the content's items, without any taught organization, matches organized teaching on integrative tasks, the organization adds nothing beyond retrieval and this page should defer to [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md). If low-attaining learners consistently gain nothing, the model should say who it is for. If studies of causal, sequence or comparison organizations behave unlike hierarchies, the top-level and depth advice is hierarchy-specific. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong organization" after the fact; name the matching task before teaching.

Assisted sorting, unaided rebuilding, integrative-task performance, detail recall and use in the learner's own setting are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of levels, no session dose and no criterion for moving on.

## Description
Knowledge organization is the principle of helping learners structure information into meaningful relationships, categories, sequences, or frameworks rather than leaving it as disconnected facts. It is useful when understanding depends on how ideas fit together.

## Implications

Knowledge organization matters because learners rarely understand complex content by accumulating isolated facts alone. They need categories, hierarchies, sequences, causal links, and conceptual maps that show how ideas relate. Strong instructional organization gives learners a structure they can think with: organizing information into meaningful units reduces memory burden [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S], and learners often strengthen that structure further by explaining the relationships among ideas [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Weak organization leaves them with fragments that are harder to interpret, remember, and transfer. The challenge is balance: enough structure to support comprehension, but not so much that the representation hides useful complexity or becomes a rigid template. Explicit structure is especially helpful for novices when it reduces unproductive search rather than adding another arbitrary framework [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A structure learners can use to arrange or connect ideas** — organizers, maps, categories, sequences, or frameworks give learners a way to hold complexity together
- **Prompts that highlight relationships among concepts** — learners often need help seeing cause-effect, part-whole, compare-contrast, or hierarchy relations
#### Constraints
- **Overly rigid structures can hide productive complexity**
- **Too many competing structures can confuse rather than help**
- **Organization is not mere formatting** — headings and layout help, but conceptual relationships matter more

### Target Learners
- Learners working with concept-rich, content-dense, or highly interconnected material
- Learners who need help moving from isolated recall to meaningful conceptual structure
- Novices who benefit from an explicit map of how ideas fit together

### Target Learning Objectives
- Improve comprehension, recall, and transfer by organizing information meaningfully
- Help learners recognize relationships among ideas rather than storing them as fragments
- Support later explanation, retrieval, and application through stronger schema

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Schema-building traditions — structured knowledge supports comprehension and later retrieval
- [Cognitive Load Management](cognitive-load-management.md) — organization can reduce extraneous load and preserve effort for meaning-making
- [Information Processing Theory](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) — organization affects how information is encoded and retrieved

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — learners still need to actively build and test the structure rather than merely receive a finished framework
