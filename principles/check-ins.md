# Check-ins

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (2 for, 7 mixed, 1 against) · 20 studies (9 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 associational, 2 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 20 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

A check-in is a short, recurring prompt that asks a learner to report something about their own state: confidence, confusion, progress, workload, or what support they need. For learners of any expertise or age, in classroom, online-instructor-led or workplace settings, a check-in is expected to help only through what follows it. A report can change the next activity (clarify, regroup, slow down, offer support), and that change may then affect later performance, persistence or the learner's sense of being attended to. A check-in that changes nothing has no route to an effect in this model.

The relationship this page owns is narrower than the one on the [formative assessment principle](formative-assessment.md), and differs from it in one way. Formative assessment interprets an **aligned task response** (an explanation, an application) and uses it to choose a next action. A check-in mostly collects a **self-report about the learner's state**, often affective or social as well as cognitive, on a routine schedule. So it is one cheap input to the formative loop, and a weaker one wherever the question is what the learner can do: a learner's judgment of their own learning is an observation of that judgment, not of their learning. Where a check-in asks for a short task response, such as an exit question, the formative assessment model applies directly. The [check-in element](../elements/check-in.md) describes the component itself.

No claim in this wiki tests a check-in routine as such. Nothing here compares learners who were checked in with against learners who were not, on any outcome or at any horizon. The model below is assembled from evidence on adjacent relationships: support adjusted to the learner's response, the accuracy of learners' self-judgments, and self-monitoring as a mechanism.

## Observation, state and explanation

What a check-in yields is a report, made under stated conditions: the prompt wording, whether the response was public or private, the stakes, when it was asked relative to study, and what the learner expected to happen next. The state behind the report (understanding, overload, disengagement, low belonging) is inferred, and several states produce the same report. Silence and "fine" are responses too, and they are the hardest to read.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| High confidence, then errors on the task | Fluency or familiarity mistaken for mastery; a confident misconception; the prompt asked about a different capability from the task | Ask for a short unassisted application of the same capability, preferably after a delay. Compare which items carry confident errors with what was taught most fluently. |
| Low confidence, then correct performance | Accurate awareness of a fragile skill; general anxiety or low self-efficacy; a norm that discourages claiming competence | Repeat the task on a new case. Ask privately what the rating meant. Check whether the pattern holds across topics or is specific to this one. |
| "Fine" or no response, every time | Genuinely no difficulty; the channel feels unsafe or public; past reports changed nothing; disengagement | Offer a private or anonymous channel and see whether reports change. Act visibly on one report and see whether later reports become more specific. Compare with task performance and attendance. |
| A request for help that the next session's performance does not reflect | Help was given and worked; the learner improved without it; the request was about something other than the task (time, wellbeing) | Record what support was actually given and when. Ask what the request was for before attributing a later gain to it. |
| Reports of overload or stress | Task demand; demands from outside the course; low belonging during a transition | Ask about time available and other commitments. Compare reports before and after a change in task demand. |

These rows are proposals. They have not been tested with learners, and a single probe shifts confidence among the explanations rather than settling one. The probe itself, and acting on the report, can change the state being measured.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] This is the evidence for the follow-through. In one-to-one long-division tutoring of fourth and fifth graders (n=40, 8 per condition), fully contingent support beat fixed, moderate, high and no support on both immediate and 1-month tests. In an eighth-grade circulatory-system study, more interactive tutoring matched less interactive tutoring on immediate learning and did better on transfer. A meta-analysis of dynamic assessment found contingent scaffolding beat coaching but did *not* beat explicit strategy training. The claim page stresses that most effectiveness evidence is one-to-one, that a review found only 8 effectiveness studies, and that contingency across a whole class is hard to achieve. It does not test a self-report check-in as the trigger for adjustment; the support in these studies responded to task responses. Its entries have not been confirmed against their sources.
- [Judgments of learning are often inaccurate](../claims/fluent-illusions-mislead-self-assessment.md) [-M] This limits confidence check-ins. In two laboratory experiments, undergraduates who watched a fluent instructor *predicted* more learning but recalled no more than those who watched a disfluent one, and words in a larger font got higher judgments of learning with no better recall. The bias survived repeated study-test cycles and an explicit warning. The claim page's discussion adds that judgments made right after study tend to be overconfident and delayed ones more accurate. The materials were word lists and a short science video, not classroom courses, and neither study measured what an instructor did with the ratings. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M] This qualifies the limit above, and says what can change it. In a randomized 2×2 experiment, 80 Dutch pre-university students (mean age about 15) who studied modelling examples of self-assessment rated their genetics problem-solving more accurately than students who did not (ηp²=.10, as printed). A meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies found the match between self-assessed and teacher marks varied: it was closer in better-designed studies, in advanced courses and in science. So how accurate a check-in's self-report is depends on criteria, training and experience. Neither entry tests a check-in routine, and the entries have not been confirmed against their sources.
- [Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+W] This is the mechanism a check-in would recruit on the learner's side. A review and a theoretical synthesis place monitoring against a goal or criterion at the centre of self-regulated learning, and argue that feedback helps when learners compare current performance with a target. Neither reports a comparison, an outcome or an effect size, so this is a mechanism hypothesis, not a tested effect of check-ins. The marker is lowered from the page's former `[+M]` for that reason. The claim's discussion notes that self-monitoring is strongest with explicit criteria and a realistic chance to act. Its entries have not been confirmed against their sources.
- [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~W] This is a neighbour, not a test, and it qualifies the relational aim. Brief belonging-reframing interventions, not check-ins, raised first-year GPA for African American students in one randomized trial (n=92) and first-year full-time completion in a 22-institution randomized experiment (n=26,911). In the larger study the effect appeared only where students' groups were given opportunities to belong. That matches the old page's caution that check-ins do not create belonging by themselves. It does not show that a check-in raises belonging. Both entries were checked by the judge against abstracts.

Keep the learners, setting, what was compared with what, the outcome and its horizon for each of these when transporting them, and do not rank them: they answer different questions. No entry here reports a standardized effect for anything resembling a check-in.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective for a check-in is usually information: which learners need what, soon enough to act. The learner's valued goal may be quite different: being noticed, getting help without being singled out, or not being asked at all. Ask what the learner wants from the routine and why, record it separately from the designer's objective, and treat agreement as something to find out rather than assume. A learner who values privacy will give "fine" on a public check-in whatever their state.

For example, in an online instructor-led course, a designer may want a weekly 1–5 confidence rating per topic to decide where to reteach. A learner working full-time may value something else: a way to say "I'm behind this week and need the deadline moved" without explaining in front of peers. A confidence scale cannot carry that message, and a low rating from that learner may reflect lost time rather than misunderstanding. A design serving both might pair a private free-text "what would help this week" with a short task item on each topic. The confidence rating would then be read beside the task item, not instead of it. These are local design choices; no claim here establishes them.

## What would revise this model?

The model would gain support from a comparison of matched groups with and without a check-in routine, where instructors acted on the reports, showing a difference on a stated outcome at a stated horizon. It should weaken if such comparisons find no difference even when follow-through is documented. It should also weaken if acting on self-reports does no better than acting on short task responses alone, since a check-in would then add cost without information. If trained or delayed self-reports turn out to track performance closely in a given setting, the warning against reading confidence as mastery can be relaxed there. If learners' reports become less candid once they see them acted on, for instance because acting meant regrouping them publicly, the follow-through itself needs redesign.

An immediate change in what learners report, a change in engagement, later performance, and persistence are separate outcomes, and evidence for one does not establish the others. The present evidence does not establish an effective check-in frequency, prompt or format, or any benefit for belonging from check-ins.

## Examples
- **Confidence meters**: Learners rate readiness or understanding on a 1-5 scale before or after a task.
- **Opening prompts**: A meeting begins with a quick response to "What is one thing you need today to be successful?"
- **Exit check-ins**: Learners note one point of confusion, one success, and whether they want follow-up.
- **Private digital pulse checks**: In online environments, short forms or chat reactions let learners signal status without speaking publicly.

## Description
Check-ins are brief, recurring opportunities for learners to signal how they are doing, what they understand, and what kind of support they need. They can be social, emotional, or task-focused, but their instructional value comes from turning invisible learner state into something that can guide the next move. Used well, check-ins help establish belonging, normalize support-seeking, and give instructors a low-cost way to detect confusion, overload, or disengagement early.

## Implications
Check-ins work when they convert private uncertainty into usable instructional information. A short confidence rating, confusion prompt, or support request can improve self-monitoring for learners [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M] and create openings for responsive scaffolding by the instructor [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M], but only if something actually changes as a result. That is why the core design requirement is not the prompt itself but the follow-through: regrouping, clarifying, slowing down, or offering support in response to what learners report. In some settings, check-ins can also surface confident misunderstanding early enough to correct it before it hardens [High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Predictable routine**: Check-ins work best when they are expected and lightweight rather than unusual high-pressure events.
- **Actionable follow-through**: Learners need to see that their responses influence support, pacing, regrouping, or discussion.
- **Appropriate scope**: Prompts should match the context, ranging from quick confidence checks to brief social connection questions.
- **Low-friction participation**: Multiple response modes help, especially in online or mixed-comfort settings.
#### Constraints
- **No follow-up**: Check-ins become hollow if learners disclose need and nothing changes.
- **Oversharing pressure**: Social-emotional prompts can feel intrusive if they ask for more than the setting can hold safely.
- **Routine drift**: Check-ins lose value when they become formulaic and disconnected from instruction.
- **Time creep**: Extended check-ins can crowd out the lesson if they are not bounded clearly.

### Target Learners
- **Learners in online or distributed settings**: Check-ins can reduce isolation and provide a regular signal channel that would otherwise be absent.
- **Learners with variable readiness or stress**: Brief check-ins help surface barriers before they compound into disengagement.
- **Groups building trust and belonging**: Especially useful early in a course, team, or collaborative project.
- **Instructors managing dynamic groups**: Check-ins provide fast formative evidence about confidence, understanding, and support needs.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Belonging and connection**: Helping learners feel seen and included in the learning environment.
- **Self-monitoring**: Prompting learners to notice and report confidence, confusion, or progress.
- **Instructional adjustment**: Giving instructors usable evidence to regroup, reteach, or change pace.
- **Support-seeking habits**: Normalizing asking for help before frustration or non-participation escalates.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Social constructivist views of learning — social participation and interaction shape readiness to engage.
- Humanistic learning perspectives — learners are more likely to engage when safety and belonging are addressed.
- Self-regulated learning — even very brief prompts can help learners monitor their current state and signal when they need support.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Check-ins do not create belonging by themselves; they only help if the surrounding environment is responsive.
- Some learners prefer lower-visibility response modes and may disengage if all check-ins require public disclosure.
