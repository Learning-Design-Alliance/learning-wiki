Peer Learning¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 17 claims (10 for, 7 mixed) · 26 studies (12 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review, 3 qualitative, 2 associational, 2 design, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 7 of 26 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the relationship between learners of similar standing helping one another learn: one learner explains, teaches, models or judges, and another asks, answers and responds. For learners who have already studied the material (they are not meeting it for the first time in the pair), and for goals that gain from explaining and checking ideas (concept, principle and procedure knowledge, reading comprehension, writing), a structured exchange in which each learner must explain to a peer and answer that peer's questions is expected to raise individually measured achievement (immediate-performance, course or test achievement) over studying the same material alone. The expectation runs in both directions: the learner who explains is expected to gain from preparing and explaining, and the learner who is helped from the explanation, the questions and the feedback.

Two kinds of claim in the wiki test parts of this. Meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes compare tutored learners, and the learners who served as tutors, with learners who had no tutoring (Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]). A meta-analysis of 28 studies compares preparing to teach, and preparing and then teaching, with studying the same material with no expectation of teaching (Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]). Several of their entries were read from abstracts only, and neither compares peer learning with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining alone. No claim in the wiki tests the doses, the role rotation or the hand-over points in the default design below; those are labelled proposals.

The relationship is conditional on four things the evidence points to: structure (defined roles, trained tutors, a script for questions and feedback; structured tutoring beat unstructured [+M]), interaction (the gain was larger when the teaching responded to a learner than when it did not), the explainer knowing enough to explain correctly (peers can pass on errors; no claim measures how often), and an individual check of what each learner can now do. It is weakened where the exchange is co-presence or answer-swapping, and it does not forecast delayed-retention or far-transfer, which the recorded claims mostly do not measure.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Collaborative Learning owns joint work on a shared task of any structure; Cooperative Learning and Social Interdependence own group goals with individual accountability. This page owns the narrower one-to-one exchange of explanation and response (tutoring, teaching a peer, reciprocal roles, peer assessment as a learning activity) and does not repeat their models. Peer Discussion owns commit-then-discuss on a conceptual question, specified as a routine in the Peer Instruction pattern; the Structured Peer Review pattern owns the comment-and-revise cycle on drafts; Guided Practice and Scaffolding and Fading own how support during practice is raised and withdrawn, which applies when the helper is a peer.

Default design¶

The earlier page asked for tasks that require learners to explain and respond, norms for accountable interaction, monitoring of explanation quality, and structures so that some learners do not dominate while others hide. Below that guidance is made concrete. The relationship as a whole has claim support (peer tutoring against no tutoring; teaching against studying); every dose, time and threshold here is an untested proposal, and each step names its evidence status.

  1. Study first, then explain. Give the pair material each learner has already met (a reading, a worked example, a lesson segment), not material the explainer sees for the first time. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's caution that peers can amplify misconceptions; the self-explanation claim [~S] notes in its own discussion that a learner without the background to explain correctly may reinforce a misconception.
  2. Prepare to teach (5–10 minutes). Each learner writes a short plan of what they will explain: the main idea, one example, and the question they expect to be asked. Preparing to teach alone gave a smaller benefit than preparing and then teaching, in one meta-analysis (Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]); the minutes are an untested proposal.
  3. Explain to a partner who must respond (5–8 minutes per turn). The explainer explains without reading from notes; the partner must restate the idea in their own words and ask at least two "why" or "how" questions; the explainer answers. Interactive teaching outperformed non-interactive teaching in the same meta-analysis; explaining is the interpersonal form of prompted self-explanation [+S] (a moderate average gain on immediate tests, mostly individual and laboratory studies). The two-question minimum is an untested proposal.
  4. Swap roles. Each learner explains a different part, or the same part to a new partner, so no one is fixed as the helper or the helped. Untested proposal: the recorded tutoring claims discuss reciprocal roles but do not test them.
  5. Give the roles a script, and train it once before relying on it (one 20–30 minute session). A role card for each side: the explainer's moves (state, give an example, check the partner's restatement) and the partner's moves (restate, ask why, say what is still unclear). Model one exchange in front of the group, with one deliberate error the partner should catch. Basis: structured peer tutoring [+M] (structured programmes produced larger achievement gains than unstructured ones; the second synthesis concerns social and behavioural outcomes); rater training was the strongest moderator of peer-assessment effects in one meta-analysis (assessor-role claim [~M]). Session length is an untested proposal.
  6. Check explanation quality while pairs talk. The teacher or facilitator listens to two or three pairs per round, notes any wrong explanation, and corrects it for everyone at the end of the round with a correct model; respond to what the learner said rather than a fixed reteach. Basis: contingent scaffolding [~M] (one-to-one tutoring studies; its classroom effect is not shown). How many pairs to sample is an untested proposal.
  7. End with an individual check. Each learner answers one new item alone, or explains the idea in writing, and rates how sure they are before seeing the answer. Learners compare their answer with the criterion and note one thing to fix. Basis: self-monitoring [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis; no effect size). The individual check is what separates a learner's own understanding from the pair's; Cooperative Learning owns individual accountability in groups.
  8. Plan the exchange across sessions, not only one. Untested proposal for a 4–8 week arc with two or three 20–30 minute peer sessions a week: early sessions (weeks 1–2) with full role cards, modelled exchanges and the teacher sampling pairs each round; middle sessions with the partner's prompts reduced to one card ("restate, ask why"), learners choosing which part to explain, and peer assessment against the course's own criteria; late sessions with learners running the exchange unprompted and the teacher sampling only. Programme duration and session timing were significant moderators in the mathematics peer-tutoring meta-analysis (Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]); session length and frequency were not; the direction of the duration effect is not recorded on the claim page.
  9. Move on when the explanation is correct and the individual check succeeds. A learner who explains correctly, answers the partner's questions and gets the individual check right on two consecutive sessions moves to harder material, or becomes the explainer for a learner meeting it for the first time; one who cannot explain after two rounds goes back to studying the material (or a worked example) with the teacher before explaining again. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can listen to pairs at all, give the partner a worked answer or criterion to check the explanation against, and keep the individual check; if the explainer does not yet have the content, replace step 3 with studying a worked example together and explaining it to each other line by line.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether the explainer knows enough to explain correctly. A learner meeting material for the first time can pass on errors; one who has studied it gains from explaining. If unstated, ask: have the learners studied this material, and can each answer one question on it alone?
  • Who can hear the exchange and correct it, and how often. The tutoring and scaffolding evidence leans on trained tutors, scripts and monitoring; with one teacher and thirty learners, or no teacher, the structure has to be in the role cards and the check items. If unstated, ask: during peer work, who will hear an explanation, and what happens when it is wrong?
  • What each learner must be able to do alone, and when. A pair can produce a correct answer neither learner could give alone. If unstated, ask: what will each learner do unaided after the peer work, with what aids, and how long after?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: what each learner said when explaining (record a sentence or two), which questions the partner asked, whether the partner's restatement matched, what the facilitator corrected, and each learner's individual check. "Understands it", "was carried by the partner" and "learned a wrong explanation from a peer" are inferred states. A correct pair answer shows what the pair can do with each other's help, not what either can do alone.

One learner does all the explaining, the other only agrees Uneven confidence or status; the partner has nothing to ask; the role card is not being used Swap roles with a fresh part; require the partner's restatement before the explainer continues; ask the quieter learner privately what was unclear.
Explanations are fluent but repeat the text Recitation rather than reorganising; the material is too easy for this learner; the partner asks no real questions Ask a "what if" question the text does not answer; give the learner harder material to explain.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Listening in changes the exchange it observes, so record when the facilitator joined a pair, and keep explanations open rather than choosing the one whose label fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

The claims below are the model's core. They test peer arrangements against no peer arrangement or against studying alone, mostly in school and university settings, mostly at the end of a programme or session, and several were read from abstracts only.

  • Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]: two meta-analyses of school peer-tutoring programmes. In 65 evaluations, tutored learners outperformed controls on examinations and the learners who served as tutors showed the same pattern (abstract only, no pooled effect size); in 50 mathematics studies the pooled effect was small (g = 0.333), moderated by educational stage, study design, programme duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size, but not by age, assigned roles, tutee ability, session length or frequency. Its ## Studies bearing on this claim table counts 4 distinct studies, all quantitative syntheses. The claim page says neither entry shows tutors gain more than tutees.
  • Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]: across 28 studies, preparing and then teaching improved the teacher's own domain knowledge over studying without expecting to teach (g = 0.56), preparing alone less (g = 0.35), with interactive teaching better than non-interactive and the benefit reported for deep as well as surface learning and after a delay (abstract only). It also cites the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis for tutor gains. It does not compare teaching with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining or retrieval practice.
  • Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured [+M]: in the 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis (read in its conference version), structured programmes produced larger achievement effects than unstructured ones, though unstructured tutoring still helped; in 36 elementary peer-assisted-learning studies, structured roles were related to the size of social, self-concept and behavioural effects. Marked M here, below its cap, because only one synthesis bears on achievement.
  • Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount [+M]: 54 control-group studies, primary to tertiary, g = 0.31 against no assessment and 0.28 against teacher assessment, not different from self-assessment. It does not separate the assessor's gain from the assessee's.
  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount [+S]: explaining to oneself, g = .55 across 69 effect sizes, with classroom and delayed evidence much more limited. It is the mechanism this page's explaining step relies on, carried from individual prompting to explaining to a peer, and it is also the comparison the peer claims do not make: whether a partner adds anything over explaining alone is not shown in the wiki.

The claims that limit or qualify the model:

  • Cross-age peer tutoring showed no statistically significant effect on oral language proficiency [~M]: a randomised trial of 42 Spanish-speaking migrant students in grades 3–5, daily 20-minute sessions over three months, bilingual or English-only tutors against teacher-led whole-group teaching; effect sizes of −0.16 and 0.05, neither significant. Peer tutoring does not raise every outcome, and equivalence was not tested.
  • The quality of feedback students give peers predicts gains in their own work, but no recorded study isolates a causal benefit of the assessor role [~M]: 43 teacher-education undergraduates, correlational; the meta-analysis in the same claim (58 studies, d = 0.291) pools both roles and finds rater training the strongest moderator. The explainer's benefit in peer assessment is plausible, not shown.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support [~M]: the evidence for support that responds to each attempt is mostly one-to-one tutoring by adults; its table counts 20 distinct studies across it and its narrower claims. Carried to peers as helpers, it supports training peers to question rather than tell, but nothing in the wiki shows peers can be contingent.
  • Collaborative learning opportunities largely disappeared when courses shifted to remote instruction [~W] and Social engagement in gateway math builds confidence [~W]: descriptive interview and case-study evidence that peer exchange depends on being designed in and on a climate where mistakes are safe; neither measures learning.
  • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation [~M]: a review and a theoretical synthesis that monitoring against a criterion lets learners notice gaps; it supports the individual check and the earlier page's point that interaction alone is not enough, without an effect size.

Learners, outcomes and comparisons differ across these claims (elementary peer tutoring, undergraduate teaching-expectancy studies, peer assessment across levels), so do not rank them by effect label. None compares peer explanation with an equal-time individual generative activity.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does explaining or teaching material to a peer improve the explainer's own learning, over studying it alone? Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]
  • Does structured help from peers (tutoring, peer-assisted learning, peer assessment) raise the achievement of the learners helped, over learning without it? Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims that bear on peer learning but are not part of the model above, each with what it is and how far it bears on the page.

  • Tutoring benefits both tutors and tutees [+S]: carries the same two meta-analyses as Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement; a duplicate of it in substance, linked here rather than counted twice.
  • Peer assisted learning is an effective learning approach that promotes knowledge construction, collaboration ability, and interpersonal interaction [+W]: a definition of peer-assisted learning and its forms from a descriptive report; the effectiveness statement is the report's own framing, not a tested result.
  • An ICAP article reports, second-hand, a study in which concept mapping with a peer enhanced learning more than concept mapping alone [+W]: the one comparison in the wiki of the same task done with a peer against alone, but reported without its design.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually what each learner can do alone afterwards: answer, explain or perform on a representative task, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what learners want from working with a peer: to be told the answer quickly, to avoid looking slow in front of a classmate, to help a friend, to practise saying things aloud before a real audience. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values getting the answer may push the partner to tell rather than explain; one who values not looking slow may agree with an explanation they did not follow.

For example, a nursing student may want to be able to explain a medication change to a patient's family, while the course objective is a correct written dosage calculation. Peer teaching of the calculation serves the objective; a rehearsal in which a peer plays the family member and asks what a family would ask serves the learner's goal. Record both, so that a correct calculation is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of peer explanation with an equal-time individual alternative (self-explaining, retrieval practice, a worked example) find no advantage for the peer arrangement; then the page should recommend explaining, with or without a partner, rather than peer learning as such. If the explainer's gain disappears when the partner asks no questions, the interactive element is the active ingredient and the role card should centre on questioning. If studies of untrained, unscripted peer pairs show gains as large as structured programmes, the training and script steps are a preference, not a condition. If delayed tests show the peer-tutoring gains fade, the model should say "immediate" throughout. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too little structure" after the fact.

Pair performance, each learner's immediate unaided performance, delayed retention, transfer and the learner's own valued use are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no session length, no number of rounds and no rule for when to swap roles.

Examples¶

Illustrative¶

Peer Instruction — Learners answer a conceptual question individually, discuss reasoning with peers, then revise their answer.

Jigsaw Method — Each learner becomes a temporary expert and teaches part of the whole to peers.

Peer revision or peer explanation routines — Learners respond to drafts, interpretations, or solutions and improve their own understanding through explaining what works and why.

Peer Teaching — Learners teach concepts, processes or interpretations to one another.

Structured Peer Review — Learners comment on drafts against criteria and each author revises.

Learning by Teaching — Learners prepare and deliver instruction to peers or tutees.
