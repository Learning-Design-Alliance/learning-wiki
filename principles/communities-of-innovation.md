# Communities of Innovation

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
West's (2014) Communities of Innovation (COI) framework identifies characteristics at three levels that predict a group's capacity for collaborative creativity, positioned as an evolution of Communities of Practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998) specifically for groups whose purpose is producing innovative output.

**Individual (but socially influenced) characteristics:**
- **Hacker motivation** — Himanen's "hacker ethic": work motivated by intrinsic value to others (not primarily money), a playful and passionate approach, and open sharing of tools and information. Companies that grant employees time for self-directed, intrinsically motivating projects (e.g., Google's "20% time") have produced some of their most creative output this way.
- **Dynamic expertise** (Hakkarainen et al.) — treating expertise as the ongoing ability to surpass one's current competence, rather than a fixed accumulation of skill. Groups whose members show more dynamic expertise are measurably more innovative.
- **Entrepreneurial autonomy** — autonomy over the *means* of accomplishing a task (not necessarily the ends) sustains intrinsic motivation and creativity (Amabile); perceived organizational control over members impedes it.

**Group-level characteristics:**
- **Group flow** (Sawyer, extending Csikszentmihalyi) — ten elements (shared goal, close listening, complete concentration, sense of control, blended egos, equal participation, familiarity, communication, moving ideas forward through improvisation, and productive risk) that correlate with peak innovative output, though it is fragile and hard to sustain, especially online.
- **Idea prototyping** — rapidly making ideas tangible (as IDEO does with products, spaces, and even organizational structures) so the group can learn from and discuss a concrete artifact rather than an abstraction.
- **Cognitive and skill diversity ("innoversity")** — diverse perspectives, including "weak ties" to people outside the group, generate more creative syntheses; conventional brainstorming underperforms because groupthink suppresses this diversity, whereas techniques like the nominal group technique (individual idea development before group sharing) protect it.
- **Culture of critique and reflection** — the ability to give and receive criticism productively, contingent on an organizational culture where failure is recoverable and treated as a source of learning ("productive failure").
- **Common vision** — a group's shared understanding of what it hopes to accomplish (not necessarily how), which is most effective when clear, widely shared, and attainable.

**Organizational-level characteristics:** a flexible, organic structure (rather than rigid hierarchy) that can quickly reorganize teams and approve ideas for prototyping; a climate of mastery, purpose, and autonomy (Pink; Amabile) rather than reliance on extrinsic rewards, which tend to diminish creativity; and psychological safety (Rogers, 1954) — unconditional acceptance of the individual, an absence of external evaluation of the person (as opposed to the work), and empathic understanding.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A culture where failure is recoverable and treated as a source of learning, so members are willing to critique and be critiqued
- Structures that protect individual idea generation before group evaluation, to avoid groupthink and preserve cognitive diversity
- Flexibility to reorganize teams and reduce approval friction for prototyping new ideas
#### Constraints
- Group flow is fragile, difficult to predict or engineer in advance, and some research suggests it may not transfer to fully online, asynchronous collaboration at all
- Autonomy over means (not ends) is the specific mechanism that sustains creativity; unconstrained autonomy over goals is not what the research supports, and appropriate constraints can actually improve creativity
- Extrinsic rewards for creative work tend to diminish intrinsic motivation and creativity rather than enhance it

### Target Learners
- Learners and teams engaged in collaborative, open-ended creative or design work (studio-based courses, capstone projects, design challenges), rather than individual skill practice

### Target Learning Objectives
- Collaborative creativity and innovation specifically, as distinct from individual creative ability
- Skills in giving and receiving critique productively as part of an iterative design process

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) [+M] — autonomy (over means), a core SDT need, is repeatedly identified as central to sustaining the intrinsic motivation that drives innovative work
- Builds explicitly on Communities of Practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998) as a specifically innovation-focused evolution of that framework

## Examples
- Google's "20% time" policy, credited with producing AdSense, Gmail, and Google News through hacker-motivated, self-directed projects
- IDEO's studio structure — flat organization, rapid prototyping of products, spaces, and team structures themselves
- BYU's Center for Animation studio, where biweekly student-run critique sessions over a year and a half drove collaborative innovation on an animated short
