Evidence-Based Teaching and Scientific Reasoning¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 21 claims (10 for, 9 mixed, 2 against) · 21 studies (6 causal, 5 associational, 4 review, 4 theoretical, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 0 of 21 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision: on what grounds a teacher, designer, school leader or purchaser adopts a teaching approach, programme or product for other people's learners. For a decision-maker (an adult, usually a novice at reading research) whose starting response is to judge an approach by a striking experience in their own class, a colleague's endorsement, a developer's "research-based" label or its fit with a favoured philosophy, the principle says: write the belief as a claim that some result could show wrong, look for a comparison that could have come out otherwise (a randomised or credibly matched comparison, with an outcome aligned to the goal, on learners like these), deliberately look for the case against it, and where no such comparison exists, adopt it as a recorded local trial rather than as settled practice. The expectation is that approaches chosen this way do more for learners, on the outcome that matters to the decision, than approaches chosen by impression, popularity or philosophy.

No claim in the wiki tests that expectation for a teacher's or designer's own decisions. The nearest test is at school level: one federal evaluation of Comprehensive School Reform (Orland 2010; one study behind three claims below) related schools' choice of reform models rated as having a scientific research base to later achievement, with mixed results: low-performing elementary schools gained more in mathematics with better-supported models, elementary reading gains showed no relationship to the model's research base, and only a third of schools chose a rated model at all. The rest of the evidence here supports the principle's premises rather than its outcome: rigorous comparisons do separate programmes that look alike (four first-grade maths curricula); popular practices with a ready story have failed rigorous tests (matching instruction to learning styles; multiple intelligences as a basis for placement); confident impressions of a pattern can persist with nothing behind them (the full-moon belief); and a prompt to consider the opposite is reported to reduce judgment bias. Against it: for adult education and most ed-tech products there is often no rigorous comparison to consult, some programmes cannot be randomised at all, and a null finding has several possible causes.

The relationship is conditional on whether a usable comparison exists for this kind of approach and these learners (early-grade maths curricula: yes; most ed-tech products and adult-education strategies: often not); whether the outcome the evidence measured is the one the decision is about (a state test, a reading posttest, near-transfer, delayed-retention); how the comparison groups were formed (volunteer schools and grades leave selection open); and the horizon (a three-year school gain is not a five-year one).

How this sits beside the converted siblings. Epistemic Cognition holds the general relationship for teaching learners to judge a kind of evidence (teach the standard explicitly, practise it on real claims, justify each judgment; gains stay with the standard taught). When this page's default design is used as professional learning for teachers, that is epistemic cognition with teachers as the learners and study reports as the evidence, and its model applies; the arc below gives the dose for that case without repeating it. Evaluating Sources holds judging a web source's credibility; checking who is behind a developer's study or a vendor's claim is that move. Considering the Opposite is the debiasing step this design uses at the moment of decision. The epistemic half of the art-and-science view in Educational Psychology as Both Art and Science is what this page makes operational.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier pages' guidance (including the folded page "Implement pedagogy based on research and data rather than untested theories that fit a favored philosophy"), kept as a concrete default a decision-maker can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. Minutes are for one adoption decision by one person or a small team; quantities are untested proposals unless a claim is cited.

  1. State the decision and its outcome (10 minutes). Name the learners (age, stage, starting point), the outcome that would count (an aligned test, a skill observed on a task, attendance, completion), and the horizon (end of unit, end of year, a year later). Untested proposal; without this, no study can be judged relevant.
  2. Write the belief as a falsifiable claim (5 minutes). "Approach X raises outcome Y for learners like ours, compared with what we do now, by the end of the year." Then write what result would show it wrong. If no possible classroom result could, it is not a claim about what works, however confidently held; do not adopt on it. Untested here; from the earlier page (Popper's criterion, via Thornton 2005).
  3. Treat a striking personal experience as a hypothesis (5 minutes). For "it worked brilliantly in my class", write down the cases where it did not work, or the classes where it was not tried, before counting the successes. Confident beliefs about a pattern can persist when the underlying rate is constant (Illusory correlations, like the belief that a full moon affects behavior, persist through confirmation bias despite having no basis in evidence [+M]; a meta-analysis of nearly 40 studies of lunar phase and behaviour, carried here to teachers' beliefs about their own practice, which it did not test).
  4. Look for a comparison that could have come out otherwise (30–60 minutes). Search a clearinghouse such as the What Works Clearinghouse, then systematic reviews, then individual studies; ask a developer for the study behind any "research-based" label and who ran it. Rank what you find: a randomised comparison on similar learners and an aligned outcome first; a matched comparison second, noting how the groups chose to take part; a before-and-after study or testimonials last. Basis: The RCT is considered the gold standard of research evidence because randomization ensures only the intervention could cause treatment-control outcome differences [+W] (a methodological report's stated rationale, not a finding); Educators can more effectively engage with researchers to advance practice by using the What Works Clearinghouse and other sources of research findings as a base [+W] (an article's stated aim; no data). Where randomised comparisons exist, they can separate programmes that all look reasonable: First-grade math achievement is significantly higher under Math Expressions and Saxon Math than under Investigations in Number, Data, and Space and Scott Foresman-Addison Wesley Mathematics [+M].
  5. Make the case against before deciding (10 minutes). Ask one person, ideally from outside the group that already agrees, to argue that the approach will not work here, and list three reasons the opposite conclusion could be right; look specifically for a study that found no effect. Basis: A systematic review of bias-mitigation training names considering the opposite as a previously validated way to reduce judgment biases, without testing it itself [+M] (a systematic review that names the strategy as previously validated on hindsight bias and anchoring, second-hand; untested on teachers' adoption decisions). From the earlier page: discussing an approach only with colleagues who already agree reinforces the bias rather than correcting it.
  6. Do not sort, place or match learners on an untested theory of types. Before adopting an approach that classifies learners (learning styles, multiple intelligences) and changes instruction by type, require research that tests the classification against outcomes, ideally followed over time, before classroom-wide use (the folded page's requirement of "empirical, longitudinal research ... before classroom implementation"). Match the format of instruction to the content (diagrams for spatial content), not to a learner's measured style. Basis: Matching Instruction To Styles No Effect [+M] (a systematic review and a randomised experiment with fifth graders found no style-by-format interaction; its synthesis table counts 4 distinct studies; marked M here because it bears on this page as an example, not as a test of the decision rule); Mi Lacks Empirical Theory Testing [+M] (a review reporting critics, second-hand).
  7. Where no rigorous comparison exists, adopt as a trial, not as practice (one term). Run a small comparison: two sections or classes, one with the new approach and one without; or alternating units; or a staggered start where some classes begin a term later. Write down before starting the outcome, the date, and what result would mean dropping it. For software, the program can assign learners at random and keep the assignment from teachers. Untested proposal as a local procedure. Basis for needing it: There is not yet much rigorous evidence to guide decision making around instructional and support strategies for adult learners [~W]; No readily accessible sources of rigorous evidence exist for most ed-tech products [~W]; Not all intervention programs can be evaluated using an RCT, so non-experimental study designs may be needed to build an evidence base [~W]. That blinded assignment can be run through software: Double-blind experimental design can be successfully implemented in CALL-based pedagogical intervention research [+W] (one vocabulary study's demonstration).
  8. Read a null or a gain before acting on it (20 minutes). A null result may reflect weak implementation, too few learners, an outcome the approach did not target or too short a horizon, not only an approach that does not work: check each before dropping it (A null study finding can have multiple possible causes and requires interpretation before it informs decisions [~W]). A gain in classes that volunteered may reflect the volunteers (The ST Math evaluation's quasi-experimental design cannot rule out selection bias because treatment grades volunteered to participate [~W]).
  9. Record and make it public to someone who can correct it. Keep a one-page record of the decision: the claim, the evidence found, the case against, the local result, and the date to revisit (a year is a reasonable default). Share it beyond those who already agree (a department meeting, another school, a research network). Untested proposal; from the earlier page (Goldacre 2013 on networks; Stanovich 2010 on public, self-correcting knowledge).
  10. Check the five common misreadings by name before accepting or rejecting a piece of evidence (10 minutes; one line each in the record). Each is a move a reader or a learner can be asked to make on any study, report or claim.
    • A fair test judged by its wording. "Randomised", "controlled", "research-based" or "evidence-informed" in a description does not make a comparison fair. Ask what was compared with what, how the groups were formed (by chance, by matching, or by volunteering) and whether the outcome was fixed in advance. Basis: The RCT is considered the gold standard of research evidence because randomization ensures only the intervention could cause treatment-control outcome differences [+W] (the logic that makes a test fair is the assignment, not the label); Most surveyed ed tech developers report their products are informed by research, but purchasers struggle to evaluate that research [~W]; The ST Math evaluation's quasi-experimental design cannot rule out selection bias because treatment grades volunteered to participate [~W].
    • Public reporting read as evidence. That a practice is published, widely reported, popular, endorsed by peers or used in many schools does not show it works. Public reporting is what lets others check and correct a claim (the earlier page, after Stanovich 2010); it is the start of scrutiny, not its result. Ask whether anyone who reported it tested it, and whether anyone has tried to refute it. Basis: Matching Instruction To Styles No Effect [+M] (a widely held and widely reported practice that failed its tests); More than 70 percent of the 180 most popular literacy applications referenced no research in their design [~M] (popularity without research, as recorded here); Districts rely more on peer recommendations and pilots than on rigorous evidence when choosing ed-tech products [~W].
    • Trust read as accuracy. Confidence in a source (a respected colleague, a vendor, an AI chatbot, a fluent textbook) is not a check of what it says. Check a sample of its statements against an independent source before relying on it. Basis: A physics-education review reports one study (Ding et al. 2023) in which high-trust students agreed with ChatGPT's answers 100% of the time although those answers were 82% accurate [+W] (40 undergraduates in physics, reported in a review; carried to other sources).
    • A null result read as "does not work" (or a gain read as "works"). Check implementation, number of learners, outcome alignment, horizon and how groups were formed before concluding either way. Basis: A null study finding can have multiple possible causes and requires interpretation before it informs decisions [~W].
    • A striking case read as a pattern. Count the cases that did not fit as well as those that did. Basis: Illusory correlations, like the belief that a full moon affects behavior, persist through confirmation bias despite having no basis in evidence [+M].
  11. When the brief names its own assessment, rubric or revision task, run it as given, inside the stated time. If a session or course must end with the brief's own assessment (a certification task, a district rubric, an evidence-review exercise, a task in which contradictory data arrive and learners must revise a conclusion), schedule that assessment in full within the session's time, in the brief's own form and wording, and plan backwards from it. Do not replace it with this page's steps or describe it for later; use this page's moves only as the practice before it, and only for the time left once the assessment is scheduled. Where the task supplies contradictory data, keep that part: learners state their conclusion, receive the new data, revise in writing, and say which earlier claim the data contradicted and why their revision follows (step 2's falsifiable claim, used live). Untested proposal.

Across sessions, when this is taught to teachers. A default arc of five sessions of about 45 minutes across one term (untested proposal; the dose and fading follow Epistemic Cognition, whose evidence is about other learners and other kinds of evidence):

  • Session 1: each teacher writes one belief from their own practice as a falsifiable claim (steps 2–3); the facilitator models the full-moon example and one of their own.
  • Session 2: the facilitator walks through one study chosen for the group, thinking aloud: who was compared with whom, how groups were formed, what was measured and when (step 4).
  • Session 3: pairs read a clearinghouse entry or study the group chose, with a one-page card listing the questions and the five misreadings (step 10); the facilitator only checks answers.
  • Session 4: each teacher, unaided, plans a one-term local comparison for an approach they want to try (step 7), with the outcome and the dropping rule written before it starts.
  • Session 5 (end of term): teachers report results, nulls included, to a group from outside the department, and read each other's results with steps 8 and 10.

Where the course has its own assessment, it replaces the session it falls in, not this arc's practice: run it as given (step 11).

Move a teacher from the card to unaided reading once they can name, for two consecutive new studies, the comparison, how groups were formed, and whether the outcome matches their own goal (untested threshold). Teachers who already do this skip to session 4.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time for a search, decide to trial, not to adopt (step 7); if the only evidence is the developer's, say so in the record and set an earlier revisit date.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether a usable comparison exists for this approach and these learners. For early-grade maths curricula there are randomised comparisons; for most ed-tech products and adult-education strategies there are often none, and the decision becomes a local trial. If unstated, ask: has anyone compared this approach with an alternative, on learners like ours, with an outcome we care about?
  • Who is deciding and how much they can read. A teacher choosing for one class, a district purchasing for many schools and a volunteer tutor need different routes to the evidence (primary studies, clearinghouse summaries, a one-paragraph plain summary). If unstated, ask: who makes this decision, how much time do they have, and have they read a study report before?
  • What outcome and horizon the decision rests on. A state test at the end of the year, a skill used on the job, completion of a programme and learners' enjoyment are different targets, and evidence for one does not carry to another. If unstated, ask: what result, measured how and when, would make you keep or drop this?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is what a decision-maker says and does: the reasons they give for an approach, the sources they name, whether they can state what result would show it wrong, whether they looked for a contrary study, and what their local trial recorded. "Is evidence-minded", "is defending a prior view" and "cannot read research" are inferred states.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
"It worked brilliantly in my class" A real effect; a good class or a good term; only the successes were remembered; novelty Ask for the classes where it was not used or did not work, and the outcome measured; propose a one-term comparison and see whether they accept the dropping rule.
"Everyone in the department agrees" Shared good experience; a group that has not met a contrary view; deference to a senior colleague Ask one member to argue the other side in writing; invite someone from outside the department to read the evidence.
Rejects a study whose result conflicts with their experience The study's learners or outcome really differ; confirmation bias; the study is weak Ask what result in a study of their own learners would change their mind; if none would, the disagreement is not about evidence.
Accepts a developer's "research-based" label without asking further Trust in the vendor; no time; does not know what to ask Give the question card and see whether they can now say what was compared; time spent is the cost to plan for.
Drops an approach after one null study A real null; weak implementation; too few learners; misaligned outcome Check the study's implementation and outcome against their own setting before deciding.

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking someone to justify a belief can harden it, so record what was asked and keep the explanations that remain open.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki tests whether teachers or designers who decide this way produce better learner outcomes than those who decide by impression. The claims below test parts of the relationship or its premises.

  • School-level test of the relationship, mixed (one study behind three claims). The fifth-year evaluation of the federal Comprehensive School Reform programme (Orland 2010) related schools' choice of reform model to its rated research base. Low-performing CSR elementary schools that adopted models with scientific research bases gained in mathematics achievement after three years [+W]: about 2 percentage points more students proficient in mathematics with a limited research base and 4 with a moderate or moderately strong base; no improvement in non-low-performing schools. No relationship existed between elementary reading achievement gains and selection of a model with a scientific research base [-W]. Only one-third of CSR schools adopted reform models identified as having a scientific research base [-W], so most schools did not choose this way when they could have. All three are coded q2, school-level achievement data from 2002 to 2005, no effect size printed. They describe whether a research base predicted gains, not whether choosing by evidence caused them.
  • Rigorous comparisons separate programmes. First-grade math achievement is significantly higher under Math Expressions and Saxon Math than under Investigations in Number, Data, and Space and Scott Foresman-Addison Wesley Mathematics [+M]: first graders in 39 schools assigned to curricula (randomized at school level, q3, first cohort); no effect size printed on the claim page. It shows that the choice of curriculum mattered and that only a comparison revealed which; it does not test teachers' decision process.
  • Popular theories have failed rigorous tests. Matching Instruction To Styles No Effect [+M]: a systematic review found almost no studies with the crossover design needed and judged the one candidate unconvincing; a randomised experiment with fifth graders found no style-by-modality interaction. Its synthesis table counts 4 distinct studies (2 reviews, 2 causal, q2–q3), none with an effect size coded. Mi Lacks Empirical Theory Testing [+M]: a review reports critics' view that few serious theory-testing publications of multiple-intelligences theory exist; second-hand, q2, r1. Both support refusing classification-and-matching approaches without outcome evidence; neither tests what teachers who refuse them achieve instead.
  • Impressions mislead. Illusory correlations, like the belief that a full moon affects behavior, persist through confirmation bias despite having no basis in evidence [+M]: a meta-analysis of nearly 40 studies found no relationship between lunar phase and behaviour records, while the belief persists. The page's mechanism (confirming cases are noticed and remembered) is the reason for step 3; it is carried to teachers' judgments, not tested on them.
  • A debiasing move. A systematic review of bias-mitigation training names considering the opposite as a previously validated way to reduce judgment biases, without testing it itself [+M]: a systematic review of bias-mitigation training names "consider the opposite" as a previously validated strategy (hindsight bias, anchoring), but its own synthesis does not test it, and the claim page says it rests on a second-hand report with magnitude unassessed. It supports step 5 as a plausible move, not as a tested part of adoption decisions.
  • Limit: the evidence often is not there. There is not yet much rigorous evidence to guide decision making around instructional and support strategies for adult learners [~W] (a systematic review, q2); No readily accessible sources of rigorous evidence exist for most ed-tech products [~W] (district survey and interviews); Not all intervention programs can be evaluated using an RCT, so non-experimental study designs may be needed to build an evidence base [~W] (a methodological report's premise). Together they are why the default ends in a local trial rather than "adopt only what an RCT supports": a rule requiring prior rigorous evidence would leave most adult-education and ed-tech decisions with nothing to choose from.
  • Limit: weaker designs and nulls need reading, not counting. The ST Math evaluation's quasi-experimental design cannot rule out selection bias because treatment grades volunteered to participate [~W] (two evaluations, authors' own stated limitation); A null study finding can have multiple possible causes and requires interpretation before it informs decisions [~W] (a guidance brief, q1).

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (whole schools, first graders, fifth graders, adults, district purchasers), and almost none prints an effect size, so do not rank them.

The evidence by question¶

  • When a school or district chooses an ed-tech product, is there rigorous evidence to choose by, and do purchasers use it? No readily accessible sources of rigorous evidence exist for most ed-tech products [~W]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims the earlier page cited, or that bear on the decision, that are used above only for a single step or row, or not at all; none has been read as a test of the model. Each says what it is and how far it bears on this page.

  • The RCT is considered the gold standard of research evidence because randomization ensures only the intervention could cause treatment-control outcome differences [+W] — a methodological report's stated rationale for randomisation; used above for the ranking in step 4, not a finding.
  • Educators can more effectively engage with researchers to advance practice by using the What Works Clearinghouse and other sources of research findings as a base [+W] — an article's stated aim (q1, no data); supports starting from a clearinghouse only as a suggestion.
  • Most surveyed ed tech developers report their products are informed by research, but purchasers struggle to evaluate that research [+W] — a cited survey statistic (90 percent of developers); describes the label, not product effects.
  • More than 70 percent of the 180 most popular literacy applications referenced no research in their design [+M] — a market scan of children's literacy apps reported in a white paper and recorded here; describes the market, does not test the decision rule.
  • Double-blind experimental design can be successfully implemented in CALL-based pedagogical intervention research [+W] — a design demonstration from one vocabulary software study; bears on how a local trial can be run, not on whether to run one.
  • Districts rely more on peer recommendations and pilots than on rigorous evidence when choosing ed-tech products [~W] — a survey of how districts decide (81 percent peer or consultant recommendations against 49 percent rigorous evidence); describes the starting response this page addresses.
  • Focal math teachers relied primarily on their own observations of student behavior to understand students' feelings about math, supplemented by surveys, with disaggregated data rare [-W] — interviews with 10 middle-school maths teachers nominated as early adopters; describes reliance on personal observation for one kind of judgment; bears on this page only as a description of practice.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Here the learner is usually the practitioner making the decision. The designer's (or school's) objective is that adoption decisions rest on comparisons that could have come out otherwise, with the outcome and horizon stated. Elicit separately what the teacher wants: less time wasted on approaches that do not work, the freedom to use professional judgment, something that keeps a difficult class engaged, or not being told by outsiders what to do. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A teacher who values autonomy may hear "use the evidence" as being overruled; a local trial they design themselves serves both their goal and the objective.

For example, a primary teacher may want to keep a phonics game their class loves; the department's objective is a decision based on reading outcomes. Running the game for one term in one class against the existing practice in another, with the reading outcome fixed beforehand and enjoyment recorded as well, tests the objective without discarding what the teacher values. Record both, so that the class's enjoyment is not read as a reading gain, nor a null reading result as a reason to stop something learners value for other reasons.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if schools or teachers who choose approaches by rigorous comparisons, with comparable learners and aligned outcomes, show no better results over several years than those who choose by experience and peer recommendation, as elementary reading did in the reform evaluation. It should change shape if local trials run by teachers turn out too small or too short to tell approaches apart, in which case the default should route decisions to pooled trials across schools rather than to single classrooms. If teachers taught to read research choose differently but their learners do no better, the professional-learning arc serves epistemic goals only. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the evidence was not rigorous enough" after the fact.

Choosing on evidence, the quality of the evidence chosen on, the outcome for learners and the horizon are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no threshold of evidence that should be required before adoption and no minimum size for a local trial.
