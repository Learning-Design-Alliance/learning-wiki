Validity, Reliability, and Bias in Classroom Assessment¶

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the quality of the inference a teacher or designer draws from a classroom assessment score, whatever the format. A score on a quiz, test, performance task or rating is expected to support a decision about a learner (reteach, regroup, grade, place, certify) only to the extent that three distinct conditions hold for that learner and that use:

  • Validity of the interpretation. The tasks sample the intended capability in the intended proportions (content), performance depends on that capability and not on something entangled with it, such as reading load, English proficiency, speed or anxiety (construct), and, where the score is used to predict, it does predict the later outcome (criterion). Validity belongs to an interpretation and a use, not to the test: the same fractions test can support a conclusion about a fluent-English learner's understanding of fractions and not about a recent immigrant's.
  • Reliability. The score would be similar on another occasion, with other items from the same domain, and from another rater. It rises with more items or tasks, clearer wording and clearer scoring criteria. A reliable score can still be invalid; an invalid one gains nothing from being consistent.
  • Freedom from bias. Neither the content nor its administration changes measured performance because of group membership: no offensive stereotypes, and no item that rewards background experience some learners had outside class and others did not.

The outcome this relationship is about is assessment-accuracy: whether the decision made from the score is the one the learner's actual capability warrants. It is not a learning outcome. Learning follows only if a more accurate decision leads to a better next step, which is the subject of the converted pages beside this one.

No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as a whole: none compares decisions made from classroom assessments built with a table of specifications, more items, calibrated scoring and a bias review against decisions made without them, or measures whether those moves change what happens to learners. The claims below test parts of it in other settings: that raters differ in severity, that rubric guidance improved peer raters' placement of writing samples on complex criteria, that ignoring passage-based item dependence overstates reliability, that item position changes difficulty, that bias-detection methods disagree, that a pass/fail decision is least consistent where scores bunch, that anxiety is associated with lower mathematics scores, that rapid guessing and low effort distort individual scores (and that alerting a proctor to it was followed by higher performance and better convergent validity), that AI and LLM scorers agree with teachers only moderately and least well on mid-range and linguistically weak responses, and that high school grades predicted later attainment better than single test scores did, that state-test word problems were disproportionately hard for English-language learners because of their language, that peer marks tracked a teacher's on exams with a clear marking scheme, that grades and test scores differ by group in ways neither measure alone accounts for, and that teachers' judgements of their students' performance ran high. Most come from large-scale testing, research instruments or single classes, and are carried to classroom assessment by extrapolation.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Competency-Based Assessment applies these properties to one kind of judgement, a criterion-referenced "demonstrated / not yet" decision against a threshold, and owns its threshold-setting and assessor-agreement model; this page holds the general measurement properties any classroom score needs and does not repeat that model. Assessment for Learning owns how evidence is used during instruction; it presupposes evidence trustworthy enough to act on, which is this page's subject. The Constructive Alignment pattern owns whether outcome, activities and assessment call for the same capability at the same level, which is the content-validity question at course scale; the Authentic Assessment pattern is one design that raises construct match for performance goals at a cost to scoring consistency. Standardized Test Fairness and Bias holds large-scale cases.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher or designer can act on and revise. It is a procedure for building and using an assessment, run over a term, not a lesson. Each step's evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Name the decision before writing an item. Write down what the score will be used for (decide whom to reteach tomorrow, award a unit grade, place in a set, predict who needs help before a spring test) and for whom. Every later check is made against that use. Untested; from the earlier page's definition of validity as the justification of an interpretation and use, and close to one article's argument that a classroom assessment should be judged by whether it serves the decision its user has to make (shift from validity to effectiveness [~W], argument, no data).
  2. Plan the sample with a table of specifications. Cross content areas against objectives, and set a planned item count in each cell before writing, in proportion to teaching time and importance, so over- and under-sampled content shows in advance. As a proposal, no objective that the decision rests on gets fewer than three items or one full performance task. Untested; from the earlier page (Linn & Miller).
  3. Ask for the performance the objective names. If the objective is to conduct an experiment, have learners conduct one; do not ask questions about conducting one. Untested here; the course-scale version is Constructive Alignment.
  4. Use enough items or tasks for the decision. One confusing item moves a 5-item score by 20 per cent and a 50-item score by 2 per cent (the earlier page's arithmetic). As proposals: a quick check that steers tomorrow's lesson can rest on 3 to 5 items; a grade or placement for an individual should rest on at least 20 selected-response items or three or more performance tasks, across more than one occasion. Where several items hang on one passage or scenario, count the passage, not its items, when judging how much evidence there is: in a simulation of passage-based reading data, treating dependent items as independent overstated reliability, by 4.8–7.4 per cent under a small passage effect and 17.0–21.8 per cent under a larger one (ignoring local dependence overestimates reliability [~M], a simulation of a large-scale English reading test; untested for classroom quizzes). Do not aim for the highest possible internal consistency on a test that deliberately samples separate topics: where items cover distinct pieces of knowledge, a very high alpha may mean the items are measuring something general (reading, diligence, general ability) rather than each topic (reflective modelling can misrepresent domain knowledge [~W]; a high alpha can signal a knowledge test failing its design [~W]; one theoretical article, no data). Judge such a test by its coverage of the table of specifications and by consistency across occasions and markers, not by alpha alone. If the result will be reported per objective (a subscore or a mastered / not-yet flag for each), give each objective items of its own: in a diagnostic physics test, mastery of fine-grained objectives was classified at acceptable accuracy for 19 of 22 objective–test combinations, and the three that fell short were energy objectives that shared about 70 per cent of their items and rested on noisier items (classification accuracy by objective [~W]; why the energy objectives fell short [~W]; one preprint, a research diagnostic, not a classroom quiz); and a state kindergarten readiness assessment's overall score predicted grade 3 achievement about as well as combinations of its subscores (overall score as predictive as subscores [~W], one state report). Where objectives share most of their items, report the total and treat a per-objective reading as a hypothesis to check with a further task.
  5. Strip demands that are not the construct. Have a colleague read each item for wording that could be misread, vocabulary and sentence length beyond the objective, unfamiliar formats and unneeded time pressure. Keep the reading load of a mathematics or science item below the learners' reading level; give a glossary of non-target words, or read items aloud, where reading is not what is being assessed. Basis: on a state fourth-grade mathematics test, some word problems were disproportionately hard for English-language learners compared with non-ELLs of the same overall mathematics score, and a textual analysis traced the difficulty to those items' linguistic features (MCAS word problems showed DIF for ELLs [+M]; linguistic features of the flagged problems [+M]; one large-scale study, abstract only on the claim pages, no effect size), and the same article summarises earlier work finding that linguistic complexity raises item difficulty more for ELLs of equal proficiency (linguistic complexity raises ELL item difficulty [+W], second-hand). It is the nearest the wiki comes to testing this step's premise, on a state test rather than a classroom one; it shows the language demand distorts the score, not that the classroom moves here remove it. A review also argues that verbal tests can understate subject knowledge when their symbolic form does not match how learners encoded it (verbal achievement tests may underestimate subject-matter knowledge [~W], a contention, not a tested result). A second reader catches problems the writer does not see: expert reviewers of draft attitude items found terms used inconsistently and statements with incomplete comparisons (linguist review revealed item communication problems [+W], one developer's account of one inventory). The time-limit and anxiety point is in step 10.
  6. Review content for bias before use. Check every context an item depends on (a sport, a concert, a holiday, a job, the gender of the people in it): either it was taught in class, or every learner can be assumed to know it; otherwise change it. Remove stereotypes (all the doctors men, all the nurses women) and balance the people who appear. Basis: second-hand testimony that females did better on items with female or neutral figures than with male figures, and a count of 42 men to three women in 24 reading passages (male-oriented item content biases test scores [~W], a review of congressional testimony, standardised tests); the offensiveness and unfair-penalisation distinction is from the earlier page (Popham).
  7. Score against written criteria, with calibrated markers. For constructed responses and performances, write a rubric with level descriptors and an anchor example for each level. Before marking, hold a calibration meeting (as a proposal, 30–45 minutes, 3–5 anchor scripts each marked independently, then discussed). Double-mark a sample (as a proposal, 20 per cent or at least 10 scripts in the first cycle) and compare. Basis: three trained raters scoring the same open-ended responses differed significantly in severity (raters differed in severity [~M], one many-facet Rasch analysis); rubric-guided training helped MOOC peer raters place writing samples at the right level more often than no guidance, mainly on subjective, complex criteria and hardly on simple objective ones (rubrics changed peer reviewers' ratings [+M], two quasi-experiments with peer raters, carried to teacher markers).
  8. Look at the items after the first administration, before the scores are used. List each item's percentage correct and flag items most learners missed, items the strongest learners got wrong more often than weaker ones, and items on which one group did much worse than its overall scores predict. Re-score, drop or rewrite flagged items before the grade is final. Treat a group difference as a question to investigate, not a verdict and never as something about the group: a gap by race or ethnicity, gender, socioeconomic status or language background reflects circumstances and treatment, including possible bias in the item itself, which is what this step looks for. Look at the grades you give the same way, not only at test items: in one district's records, grades differed systematically by race, ethnicity and gender among students with the same test scores, attendance, courses and teachers (grade differences by group net of test scores [~M], associational, Chicago high schools). The records cannot say why: a grade carries work habits and course work a test does not, a test carries its own format and timing, and either the marking or the test may treat one group less accurately; the move is to check the marking (blind-mark a sample, compare with the rubric), never to read the gap as a fact about the group. Two bias-detection methods applied to the same state performance assessment agreed on only three of their seven flagged items each (ICC and LDF methods identify different DIF items [~M]), and on one physics test response-time differences between boys and girls did not predict which items were flagged (DIF not predictable from response times [~M]). Both are large-scale testing analyses; the classroom version, with small groups, is an untested proposal and will flag by chance.
  9. Treat decisions near a cut score as uncertain. Where a grade boundary or pass mark falls among many scores, seek a second piece of evidence (another task, a short interview, an earlier result) for learners within a few points of it before deciding. Make that second piece a task or a record, not an impression: 46 secondary teachers' judgements of 406 students' reading comprehension deviated from the students' actual scores by about 22 per cent, mostly overestimating (teachers' judgements overestimated performance [~W], one exploratory study, no effect size). Do not report Cohen's kappa as the consistency of a pass/fail decision. Basis: in a simulation, decision consistency was lowest when the cut score sat at the peak of the score distribution (decision consistency lowest at the distribution peak [~M]); a worked example gave kappa 0.64 at 95 per cent observed agreement (Cohen's kappa paradox [~W], illustrative); an essay argues adjacent scores across a cutoff are not reliably different (grade cutoffs are arbitrary at adjacent scores [~W], argument). Where decision consistency is computed (a common examination, a department pass mark), the three usual methods agreed within 0.044 on normal or near-normal score distributions (DC methods similar for normal distributions [~M]) and differed most with the cut at the mode of a skewed distribution, by 0.101 in a simulation (DC estimates differ most at the mode [~M]), where W-DC or LL-DC are preferred and PS-DC is likely to overestimate (W-DC and LL-DC preferred for skewed data [~M]); all three are one study of simulated and licensure data. Precision also varies along the scale: a review of state tests notes that reliability is usually highest near the mean or the cut and much lower for very high or very low scores (conditional reliability low at the score tails [~M]), so an extreme score is the less precise one.
  10. Set administration conditions to the construct. Use a time limit only where speed is part of what is assessed; keep the order of items the same across versions, or check that a version's later items are not harder because they come later; let anxious learners take low-stakes versions first. Basis: across 223 studies, mathematics anxiety correlated r = −.28 with mathematics achievement (math anxiety degrades performance [~M], correlational, so it does not show that removing time limits helps); an item placed later in one booklet was harder for that booklet's takers, which the authors attribute to fatigue (later-positioned item more difficult [~M], a national examination). On low-stakes checks, and computer-based ones above all, watch for disengaged responding: scoring models assume effortful answers (measurement models assume effortful responding [+W], argument), an answer given faster than the item can be read is a different response process from the one being assessed (rapid guesses are construct-irrelevant [+W]), including such answers is argued to pull scores down (rapid guessing distorts scores [+W], two articles by one author, no data on the page), and uneven rapid guessing often left test events with distorted content coverage (differential rapid guessing distorts content representation [+M], no effect size). Where response times are visible, re-ask or set aside rapid answers before using an individual's score: on low-stakes computer-based tests, a proctor notification triggered by detected rapid guessing was followed by more engagement, higher performance and stronger convergent validity evidence (proctor notification [+M], no effect size printed; rapid guessing signals disengagement [+W]). For a class or school average the risk is smaller: school means on a PISA-based test stayed highly stable despite differential engagement, partly because disengagement pushed individual scores both up and down (school means stable [~M]; distortions cancel [~M]).

Across a term (dose and progression; all quantities are untested proposals). First unit: full table of specifications, a colleague's item review (about 30 minutes per test), a calibration meeting before marking, double-marking of 20 per cent, and item analysis before grades are released. Second and third units: reuse items that were not flagged, rewrite or drop flagged ones, and lower double-marking to 10 per cent if markers agreed on the exact level for roughly four scripts in five in the first round; raise it back to 20 per cent when agreement falls, a new marker joins, or the task changes. (The nearest precedent is a research interview, not classroom marking: a streamlined two-rater procedure reached .96 reliability with 73 per cent agreement, the interviewer's own ratings agreed with final ratings 87 per cent of the time, and rating moved to the interviewer alone with periodic reliability checks; LoU interview reliability [+W].) Each new term or new marker: recalibrate on fresh anchors. Once a year: check criterion validity where the score is used to predict (for example, do autumn scores identify the learners who later needed help?), and revisit the bias review with that year's group results. An item or task moves from "draft" to "bank" after one administration without flags; a score moves from "check" to "grade" only when it rests on the amount of evidence in step 4.

When a condition is not met: if no second marker exists, mark a sample twice yourself a week apart, blind to the first marks, and compare; if there is no time for item analysis, at least look at the three items most learners missed before using the scores; if the group is too small for group comparisons, rely on the content review in step 6.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the score will be used for, and how much rides on it. A check that steers tomorrow's lesson tolerates a few items and one marker; a grade, placement or certification needs more items, calibrated scoring and caution at the cut. If unstated, ask: what decision will this result change, for whom, and can it be reversed?
  • Which demands besides the target capability the task makes, and whether they differ across the learners. Language, reading load, cultural reference, speed, anxiety and the response mode are the usual ones. If unstated, ask: who are the learners (languages, literacy, background, access needs), and what must they do to show the capability that is not the capability?
  • Who scores, and how. Selected-response items remove rater variation but narrow what can be assessed; performances and constructed responses widen it but depend on the rater. If unstated, ask: is the response scored by key or by judgement, by how many people, and with what criteria and examples?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response to a stated task under stated conditions (time, aids, mode, language, who scored it, with what criteria). "Understands fractions", "is weak at writing" or "the test is biased" are inferences. A score is evidence about the capability only after the alternatives in the table have been considered.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
A learner scores low on a written test but explains the idea well in class Reading or language load, not the capability; anxiety in the test setting; the test sampled content the learner had not been taught; the class explanation was prompted Give a matched item orally, or with a glossary and no time limit; check the item against the table of specifications.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki tests whether classroom assessments built with these moves lead to more accurate decisions about learners. The claims below test parts of the relationship, mostly outside the classroom.

  • Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis [~M]: a many-facet Rasch analysis of three raters scoring open-ended items in a crossed design found the rater facet significant, so the raters differed in severity; item and person reliabilities were still above .80 (q3, associational, no effect size). It supports calibration and double-marking; it does not say how much calibration reduces the difference.
  • In two online comparisons, a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on (one study) and helped peer raters place writing in quality tiers, most for complex criteria (the other); the first study says the rubric restrained reflective thinking [+M]: in a MOOC writing course, peers given guided-rubric training placed writing samples into novice, intermediate and advanced levels more accurately than unguided peers, with the benefit on subjective, technically complex criteria and little on simple objective ones; a second study with 47 graduate students found a rubric changed feedback content but restrained reflective commentary. Two q2 quasi-experiments, no standardised effect size. It is the nearest test of one lever on this page (clearer criteria → more accurate rating), with peer raters, not teachers.
  • In simulation, ignoring local dependence overestimates reliability [~M]: across 20 simulated data sets modelled on a large-scale English reading test, passage-based alpha was 4.8–7.4 per cent lower than item-based alpha under a small passage effect and 17.0–21.8 per cent lower under a larger one (q2, design). It qualifies "more items, more reliability": items sharing a passage add less independent evidence than their count suggests.
  • Decision consistency is lowest when the cut score is at the peak of the score distribution [~M]: in simulated data, all three decision-consistency methods gave their lowest value with the cut at the mode (q2, design). It supports extra evidence near a pass mark; it is about pass/fail examinations, not classroom grades.
  • Mathematics anxiety degrades performance [~M]: a multilevel meta-analysis of 223 studies (332 samples, about 385,441 participants) found a correlation of r = −.28 between mathematics anxiety and achievement, moderated by grade level, ability and the type of scale and assessment (q4, i2, correlational). It makes anxiety a plausible construct-irrelevant source of low scores; it does not separate anxiety lowering scores from low achievement raising anxiety, and does not test any change to administration.
  • The ICC and LDF methods identify substantially different sets of items as exhibiting DIF [~M] and DIF could not be anticipated from differential response times [~M]: on a state science performance assessment with about 16,000 examinees, two methods agreed on three items each; on a 32-item physics test, response-time differences between boys and girls did not predict which four items were flagged (both q2, associational). They limit the bias step: statistical flags depend on the method and are not self-explaining, so a flagged item calls for a content review, not automatic removal.
  • Test items with male-oriented content are a source of bias [~W]: congressional testimony reports a 1979 ETS finding that females did better on items with female or neutral figures, and a count of 42 men to three women in 24 SAT reading passages (q2, review r1, second-hand, no effect size). It supports the content review; the claim's title states as established what its one entry reports second-hand.
  • Some 2003 MCAS fourth-grade math word problems exhibited differential item functioning for English-language learners [+M] and textual analysis identifies the linguistic features of those problems [+M]: one study (Martiniello, 2008; q3, associational, no effect size on the claim pages, abstract only) flagged word problems disproportionately hard for ELLs matched on overall mathematics score and traced the difficulty to their language. It is the closest direct evidence for the construct-irrelevant-demand part of the model, on a state test: language load beyond the target changed what the score said about ELLs' mathematics. It does not test whether simplifying classroom items, glossing or reading aloud closes the gap.
  • Peer assessment is equivalent to teacher assessment for exact-science exams with clear marking schemes [~M]: on two exams in one online course (68 and 94 papers), peer marks did not differ significantly from the teacher's (Cohen's d 0.03–0.11 as printed) and correlated .88–.91 (q2, associational r1). It qualifies "who scores": with a clear scheme and several peers, peer marks tracked the teacher's; a non-significant difference is not proof of equivalence, and the claim's own title says more than one course can show.
  • Self-report instruments. Three arguments from one journal issue on measuring strategy use (memory reconstruction [~W], prompting effects [~W], perception rather than performance [~W]) and a review on young children (questionnaires poor fit for young children [+W]) qualify any questionnaire used as classroom evidence: its score is a report, with its own construct-irrelevant demands (memory, reading, social desirability). None reports data on the claim page.
  • Internal consistency is not always the goal. A high alpha can misrepresent domain knowledge as a general factor [~W] (q1, theoretical r3) qualifies step 4: for a test that samples separate topics, high alpha is not evidence the test works.
  • Disengaged responding. Differential rapid guessing distorts content representation [+M] (q3, associational) and proctor notification improved performance and validity evidence [+M] (q2, coded causal) are the empirical core of a cluster from one research group on low-stakes computer-based tests; the other claims in it are the authors' arguments. No effect size is printed for any of them. They support step 10's attention to effort as a construct-irrelevant source of low individual scores; stable school means [~M] limits that concern to individual, not aggregate, decisions.
  • AI and LLM scoring. A dozen single studies, mostly 2025–2026 preprints, compare model scores with teachers' or examiners'. Their common finding is moderate and uneven agreement: weaker on mid-range responses (mid-range degradation [~M]) and on linguistically weak explanations (AI scoring underestimates linguistically weak explanations [-M], q3, i2, 116 Grade 9 explanations), varying by model, prompting strategy, rubric dimension and run. They are about particular models and tasks, and they date quickly; they support checking an AI scorer locally before use, not a general level of trust. One preprint qualifies the reading that AI scoring is simply worse: on a double-marked computer-science exam, several closed-model configurations came within the difference between two human markers (LLM graders within the human floor [~M]), which is why the AI row compares a scorer with teacher-to-teacher disagreement rather than with a perfect key.
  • Prediction. Associational studies of US administrative records (GPA predicts graduation more than tests [+M], ACT adds little beyond HSGPA [~M], GPA most predictive for English Learners [+M]) bear on criterion validity: accumulated course grades predicted later attainment better than one test did. They do not show that combining measures improves a classroom placement decision; they show which measures predicted in those systems.
  • Group differences in grades and scores. Grades differed by race, ethnicity and gender among students with the same test scores, attendance, courses and teachers [~M] (Allensworth & Luppescu, 2016, Chicago, q3, associational r3; for example 0.26 GPA points lower for Black students than White students net of controls), and SAT mathematics underpredicts women's college mathematics performance [~W] (a practice guide citing earlier work, no effect size). Read together they show that a test and a grade can disagree about the same learners, in different directions for different groups. Neither establishes which measure is wrong for whom: a grade can carry work the test does not and judgements that differ by group, and a test can carry timing and format demands. They qualify step 8 and the placement row: look at both measures by group and check the marking and the items, and do not read a group difference as a fact about the group, since gaps by group reflect circumstances and treatment, including possible bias in the measures.
  • Decision consistency methods. One study of simulated and licensure data (DC estimates differ most at the mode [~M]) qualifies step 9: how consistent a pass/fail decision looks depends on the method as well as on where the cut falls.

The learners, instruments and settings differ (large-scale examinations, simulations, peer raters online, research questionnaires), so do not rank these by effect label. None measures the classroom decisions this page is about.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do markers scoring the same responses agree, and do written criteria bring their judgements closer? Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis [~M]
  • How far can a pass/fail or grade decision near a cut score be trusted, and how should its consistency be reported? DC estimates tend to be lowest when the cut score is set at the peak of the score distribution, where inconsistent decisions are most likely [~M]
  • Does language load beyond the target distort what a score says about second-language learners? Some 2003 MCAS fourth-grade math word problems exhibited differential item functioning for English-language learners versus non-ELLs [+M]
  • Can peers' marks stand in for a teacher's? Peer assessment is equivalent to teacher assessment for exact-science exams with clear marking schemes [~M]
  • Do grades and test scores tell the same story about every group of learners? Systematic racial, ethnic and gender differences in grades persist among students with the same test scores, attendance, courses and teachers [~M]
  • Can an AI scorer be compared with how far two teachers disagree? Six closed-model configurations grade at or below the human inter-grader floor of 2.61/35 on the CV exam [~M]
  • Can peer markers be made more accurate? Giving students feedback about their grading bias increased their subsequent grading accuracy [+W]
  • Does disengaged test taking (rapid guessing, low effort) distort scores, and when does it matter for the decision? One measurement article argues that low test-taking motivation biases an individual student's achievement test score downward [+W]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective here is a defensible decision: a score that supports the conclusion drawn from it, for each learner it is drawn about. Learners value something related but different. Some want a fair grade, some want to be seen as able, some want the result for what it opens (a course, a job, a licence), and some want to know what to work on. Ask them, before a high-stakes assessment, what they need the result for and what they expect it to show; ask afterwards whether it showed what they can do, and if not, what got in the way. A learner who says "I knew it but couldn't read the question" or "I ran out of time" is reporting a possible construct-irrelevant demand; record it as an observation to check, not as an excuse or a finding.

For example, an adult nursing assistant may want a certificate that lets them take on medication rounds, while the designer's objective is a reliable score on a written dosage test. A high written score supports the designer's inference about calculation; the learner's goal needs an observed performance on the ward as well. Record both, so that a test score is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if classroom studies find that decisions made from assessments built with a table of specifications, more items, calibrated scoring and bias review are no more accurate (against an independent judgement of the learners' capability) than decisions made from ordinary teacher-made tests, or that the gain does not change what happens to learners next. If calibration meetings do not reduce differences in teacher severity, step 7 should drop to double-marking alone. If content bias review in classrooms does not change group differences, the review stays as a fairness duty, not an accuracy claim. If low-language and untimed versions do not change the scores of second-language or anxious learners, the construct-irrelevant explanation should lose weight for those groups. Do not protect the model by calling every unexpected score "invalid" after the fact; the checks have to be made before the scores are used.

Content validity, construct validity, criterion validity, consistency across occasions, items and raters, and group fairness are separate claims, each needing its own evidence. The present evidence establishes no minimum number of items, no acceptable level of rater agreement for classroom marking, and no amount of calibration that is enough.

Examples¶

  • Selected-Response Assessment Items — trades some validity for high reliability, since objective scoring removes rater variation but constrains what can be assessed

  • Constructed-Response Assessment Items — the reverse tradeoff: broader validity for complex goals, at real risk to reliability without a rubric or model answer

  • Constructive Alignment — the course-scale check that outcomes, activities and assessment call for the same capability, which is content validity applied to a whole course

  • Authentic Assessment — a design that raises construct match for performance goals and depends on calibrated scoring to keep it consistent
  • Use multiple raters for principal-assigned professional practice ratings to improve consistency
  • Authentic intellectual work scoring rubrics with double-scoring design for inter-scorer reliability
  • Use performance assessments in conjunction with selected-response items, counting enough toward final scores to matter
  • Route divergent or linguistically weak AI-scored explanations to teachers as diagnostic signals for mindful human review
  • Select a DC estimation method based on score distribution shape, the user's measurement background, and the intended audience
  • Ground coaching conversations in the work of children
  • Administer the Inquiry with classroom test procedures and interpret by item-by-item comparison with expert votes
  • Use external instruments rather than teacher judgment to measure learning strategies for differentiation decisions
