# Support Early Language Development

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Language develops fastest where children have real reason to communicate, hear varied language, and receive immediate, responsive feedback from a communication partner. A widely cited early study (Hart & Risley, 1995) framed this in terms of a large raw "word gap" between children from different socioeconomic backgrounds; more recent work qualifies that finding substantially — later replications suggest the gap in total words heard is considerably smaller than originally reported, and part of the original gap likely reflected a mismatch between the researchers' expectations and the actual (but different) linguistic practices of the homes studied, rather than a simple deficit. More recent research (MIT McGovern Institute) finds that **conversational turns** — the back-and-forth of a real exchange — predict language-relevant brain development (specifically in Broca's area) better than raw word count or socioeconomic status alone. The practical implication is the same either way: responsive, reciprocal interaction, not sheer quantity of words a child overhears, is what drives development.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Frequent adult-child interaction that is genuinely reciprocal, not one-directional talking at a child
- Adults who adjust their language complexity to the child's current level and respond to the child's own communicative attempts (including pre-verbal gestures and pointing)
- Opportunities for the child to actually produce language and receive feedback, not just receive input passively
- Multiple communication partners (peers as well as primary caregivers), since variety in interaction partners broadens language experience
#### Constraints
- Passive exposure (background media, overheard adult conversation not directed at the child) does not substitute for direct, responsive interaction
- Raw word-count metrics can mask what actually matters (responsiveness and reciprocity) and risk stigmatizing home language practices that differ from a researcher's expectations without being deficient
- Over-simplification of language input, sustained too long, can limit a child's exposure to the complexity they are ready to handle next

### Target Learners
- Infants and toddlers (birth–3 years), during the period of most rapid phoneme, vocabulary, and grammar development
- Preschool and early-elementary learners during continued rapid vocabulary growth
- Children with speech or language delays, or who are deaf or hard of hearing, where early responsive interaction (including early sign-language exposure) is especially time-sensitive

### Target Learning Objectives
- Phoneme discrimination and vocal/gestural production
- Vocabulary growth and word-meaning acquisition
- Grammatical rule acquisition through natural corrective feedback (e.g., a caregiver recasting "I goed" as "You went?" rather than explicitly correcting)
- Communicative competence for social goals: requesting, commenting, connecting with others
