# Support Early Language Development

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (12 for, 2 mixed, 2 against) · 21 studies (10 quant-synthesis, 5 causal, 2 review, 2 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 21 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a young `child` (birth to about five) who is building first-language vocabulary, grammar and conversational skill, frequent exchanges in which an adult responds to what the child says, points at or attempts, lets the child take a turn, and expands or recasts the child's words are expected to produce more growth in the child's **own talk** (expressive vocabulary, number and variety of utterances) than the same amount of language the child only hears: adult talk directed at the child with little room for the child to reply, overheard conversation, or background media. The outcome is the child's oral language measured shortly after a period of weeks of such interaction (`immediate-performance` on vocabulary and language measures); later reading and school achievement are a separate, further claim.

**What tests this, and how far.** No claim in the wiki compares responsive, turn-taking interaction with passive exposure in general. The general statement rests on associational evidence: [conversational turns predict language development and brain measures better than the number of words heard or family socioeconomic status](../claims/conversational-turns-predict-language-development.md) [+M], reported second-hand and with no sample or effect size. One routine has been tested experimentally: **dialogic reading**, in which the adult prompts the child to talk about the book, praises and expands the child's answers and gradually hands more of the telling to the child. Against ordinary shared reading of the same books, it [raises expressive vocabulary and oral language](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [+S] (two meta-analyses). So the relationship is tested for one setting (shared book reading with children of about two to five) and carried from there to everyday talk, play and care routines as an untested proposal.

The relationship is conditional on the child's age and starting language (the dialogic-reading gain is substantially smaller for four- to five-year-olds and for children at risk of language impairment), on who delivers it (gains obtained by researchers did not replicate when children's own teachers did the reading in ordinary classrooms), on how often it actually happens (centres that ran dialogic reading less often got smaller oral-language effects), and on the outcome (no effect on phonological processing). It does not say that the amount of talk is irrelevant, nor that homes whose language practices differ from a researcher's expectations are deficient: the claim page records that the original "30 million word gap" estimate has not fully replicated and that part of the gap reflected that mismatch.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) owns the general relationship of contingent support raised after a failed attempt and lowered after a success; expanding and recasting a child's words is that move applied to talk, and this page does not repeat its model. [Explicit Vocabulary Instruction](explicit-instruction-vocabulary.md) owns the teaching of a selected set of words, which becomes a larger share of the design as children reach four and five. [Phonics](phonics.md) owns code-focused teaching, which this relationship does not reach. The theories behind it are [Language Acquisition Theory](../theories/language-acquisition-theory.md) (the social-interactionist account) and [Sociocultural Theory](../theories/sociocultural-theory.md) (support in the zone of proximal development).

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer, parent educator or early-years lead can act on and revise. Steps 4 to 6 rest on the tested dialogic-reading routine; the rest are untested proposals, labelled.

1. **Follow the child's lead and answer it.** Notice what the child is looking at, pointing to, babbling or saying, and respond to that, in words, within a few seconds; then pause long enough (a slow count of five is a working proposal) for the child to take another turn. With infants, gestures, pointing and sounds count as turns. Untested as an intervention; the reason for it is the associational turn-count finding ([conversational turns](../claims/conversational-turns-predict-language-development.md) [+M]) and the [child-directed speech](../elements/child-directed-speech.md) element.
2. **Talk with, not at, the child.** Count exchanges, not words: a stream of adult talk the child cannot reply to, overheard adult conversation, or a screen playing in the background is not a substitute. Untested proposal; no claim here tests passive exposure against interaction.
3. **Pitch language just above the child's, and expand it.** Use short sentences near the child's own length plus one step (child: "dog"; adult: "a big dog, running"). Recast errors rather than correcting them ("I goed" → "You went to the park?"). Do not hold language simple for longer than the child needs (the earlier page warns that sustained over-simplification limits exposure to what the child is ready for). Untested as a separate rule; expansion is one component of the dialogic-reading routine in step 4.
4. **Run shared reading as a conversation (dialogic reading).** On each page or two, prompt the child (complete a sentence, recall what happened, an open-ended "what's happening here?", a wh- question, or a question linking the book to the child's own life), praise and expand the answer, and ask the child to say the expansion. Over rereadings of the same book, hand more of the telling to the child. Basis: [Dialogic reading improves language outcomes](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [+S] (meta-analyses; moderate effect on expressive vocabulary against typical shared reading).
5. **Dose: often and short, for weeks.** A working default is one dialogic session of about 10 minutes a day, at least five days a week, for 6 to 8 weeks, with the same book read two or three times across a week. These numbers are untested proposals; what the evidence says is only that frequency matters: [centres with lower compliance got smaller oral-language effects](../claims/implementation-level-moderates-dialogic-reading-effects.md) [~M].
6. **Keep the group small enough for every child to talk.** One adult with one child, or with up to four or five children, so that each child gets several turns per book; a whole-class read-aloud gives most children few. Group size is an untested proposal (the hub claim's Discussion says whole-class read-alouds dilute turn-taking); the meta-analyses pooled one-to-one and small-group delivery.
7. **Train the adults by doing, with feedback.** Show the routine (live or on video), have each adult practise it with their own child or group while a coach watches, give feedback on two or three things (did the child talk on most pages; was the child's answer expanded), and check again after a week or two. Basis, carried from parent training on child behaviour: [coaching with practice outperforms information alone](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M]; and for language specifically, the teacher-delivered replication failure inside the [dialogic reading hub](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [~S] says the routine is lost when adults are only told about it.
8. **Widen the partners.** Beyond the main caregiver, give the child regular conversation with other adults and with peers (play, meals, routines), so the child meets varied language and partners. Untested; from the earlier page.
9. **Progression across the early years (untested arc).** Under about 18 months: responsive naming and turn-taking in care routines and play, books as objects to point at and talk about. About 2 to 3 years: daily dialogic reading with completion and recall prompts, plus talk during play. About 4 to 5 years: open-ended and "your own life" prompts, longer stories and retelling by the child, with explicit teaching of selected words ([Explicit Vocabulary Instruction](explicit-instruction-vocabulary.md)) because the dialogic gain is smaller at this age. Within any 6- to 8-week block, move a child to harder prompts when the child answers most prompts on a familiar book unaided; return to completion prompts and shorter books when the child mostly stays silent.

When a condition is not met: if no adult can give children one-to-one or small-group time, schedule short rotating small groups and drop whole-class reading as the main language activity; if the adults cannot be observed and coached, give them one routine (one prompt type and expansion) rather than the whole sequence, and check its frequency.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **The child's age and current language.** An infant who points but does not yet speak, a two-year-old with a few hundred words, a four-year-old who tells stories, and a child with a diagnosed language delay need different prompts, group sizes and adult support, and the tested effect shrinks at four to five and for children at risk. If unstated, ask: how old are the children, what do they say now (single words, short phrases, stories), and has anyone flagged a delay or hearing loss?
- **Who will carry out the interaction, and whether they can be coached.** Parents at home, an early-years teacher with a full room, a home visitor, or volunteers each need different training and a different amount of the routine. If unstated, ask: who spends time talking with these children, how many children per adult, and can anyone watch them do it once and give feedback?
- **Which language outcome counts.** Expressive vocabulary and the child's own talk are where the tested gain lies; receptive vocabulary, phonological awareness, letter knowledge and later reading need other or additional teaching. If unstated, ask: what should the children be able to say or do differently, how will anyone know, and when?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is an exchange under stated conditions: who started it, how many turns each side took, what the child said or did (words, gestures, sounds), how long the adult waited, what the adult did with the child's turn (ignored it, repeated it, expanded it, corrected it, asked another question), and the setting (book, play, meal, one child or a group). "Has a delay", "is shy", "the home is language-poor" and "the adult is not responsive" are inferred states. One observation of a child in an unfamiliar setting, with an unfamiliar adult, in a language that may not be the child's strongest, shows little about what the child can do at home.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Child stays silent when prompted during reading | Prompts pitched too high; unfamiliar adult or group; the book's language is not the child's strongest; a hearing or language difficulty; the child is used to listening during reading | Try completion prompts on a familiar book, one-to-one, with a familiar adult and in the home language; ask caregivers how much the child talks at home; arrange a hearing check if silence is general. |
| Child answers but in one word every time | Prompts invite one word (naming questions); the child's current level; adult does not expand or ask for the expansion | Change to open-ended and recall prompts for a week and count words per answer; check whether the adult repeats back an expanded version. |
| Adults report doing the routine, but children's talk does not change | Sessions happen less often than reported; adults read with questions bolted on but do not wait or expand; the outcome measure does not capture the child's talk | Watch or record one session (with consent) and count child turns and expansions; compare with the logged frequency. |
| A child talks freely at home but rarely in the setting | Unfamiliar language or partners; group too large to get a turn; the setting's talk does not connect with home topics | Observe in a small group with a familiar peer, use topics from home, and ask the family what the child talks about. |
| Gains on words from the books, none on a general language test | The gain is specific to taught or read words; too little time for general growth; the test measures receptive language while the routine trains production | Test with items from the books and with an expressive measure; extend the block and retest. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe is itself an interaction and changes the child's response, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain rather than choosing the one that fits a label.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Conversational turn-taking predicts language development better than raw word count or socioeconomic status.](../claims/conversational-turns-predict-language-development.md) [+M]: three entries, all `q2` and none with an effect size. A neuroimaging finding attributed to Gabrieli's group at MIT (reported second-hand through a textbook; sample and statistics not available) links turn counts to Broca's-area measures beyond word count and socioeconomic status; a Learning Policy Institute report quotes the same finding; Hart and Risley (1995, 42 families) is included as the historical baseline that later work qualified. It is associational: it says turns go with development, not that adding turns causes it. It is the basis of the general statement on this page, and the reason the page treats volume of exposure as secondary.
- [Dialogic Reading Improves Language Outcomes](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses by Mol and colleagues. Against typical shared reading, dialogic parent–child reading raised expressive vocabulary (d = 0.59, 9 studies, 322 children), substantially less for 4- to 5-year-olds and for children at risk of language and literacy impairments; across 31 experiments and quasi-experiments with 2,049 children where educators were trained in interactive reading, the effect on oral language was moderate, but effects obtained by experimenters did not replicate with children's own teachers in natural classrooms. This is the one experimental test of the relationship, for one routine. Both entries are coded `r?`.
- [Dialogic Reading Improves Expressive Language](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+S]: the same two meta-analyses, scoped to expressive measures. Its Discussion notes that the expected larger effect for children with lower starting vocabulary is not what the evidence shows: the effect was smaller for children at risk. It is a duplicate of the claim above.
- [Dialogic Reading has positive effects on preschool children's oral language, per WWC synthesis of five studies](../claims/dialogic-reading-positive-oral-language-effects.md) [+M]: a What Works Clearinghouse review of five studies (over 300 preschool children, mostly from low-income families): significant positive effects in three, indeterminate in two.
- [Level of implementation moderates Dialogic Reading's effects on children's oral language](../claims/implementation-level-moderates-dialogic-reading-effects.md) [~M]: within one trial (75 children), centres that ran sessions more often got larger oral-language effects. A secondary, non-randomised split; it supports making frequency a design target but gives no number.
- [Parent-implemented dialogic reading showed no statistically significant differences from a no-treatment comparison on any language measure (Crain-Thoreson & Dale, 1999)](../claims/crain-thoreson-dale-1999-parent-led-null-effects.md) [-M]: 19 three- to five-year-olds with language delays; parent-led dialogic reading against group story time, no significant differences (average effect size 0.11). **This is the main limit on the model**: with children who have delays, a parent-led routine may not be enough on its own. The claim title says "no-treatment comparison" while its evidence entry describes a group story-time comparison.
- [Dialogic reading favored significant gains on lexical diversity during play and total utterances during book reading, but not on four other measures (Dale et al., 1996)](../claims/dale-1996-significant-lexical-diversity-play-total-utterances-book-reading.md) [+M]: 33 mother–child dyads, children three to six with language delays, against a conversational language training programme; gains on two of six measures (effect sizes 0.92 and 1.67). Against another active, interaction-based programme, the gains were in the children's own talk.
- [Dialogic reading has potentially positive effects on communication and language competencies for children with disabilities](../claims/dialogic-reading-potentially-positive-communication-language-disabilities.md) [+M]: two trials, 52 children with language delays; average improvement index +11 percentile points (range −2 to +45). Small evidence base.
- [Dialogic Reading shows no discernible effects on phonological processing](../claims/dialogic-reading-no-phonological-processing-effects.md) [-M]: one trial, 66 children, no significant effects on four phonological measures. Bounds the outcome: the routine builds talk, not sound awareness.
- [Parent Coaching Outperforms Information Only](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 77 parent-training evaluations (children 0–7) and a trial with 305 preschoolers; practising skills with one's own child in sessions, with a practitioner, went with larger effects than information alone. Outcomes are parenting behaviour and child behaviour, not language; it is carried here to how adults are trained.

Learners, outcomes and comparisons differ across these claims (expressive vocabulary against typical reading, language measures against story time, child behaviour after parent training), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures whether gains last after the intervention ends, or whether they reach later reading.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually growth in the child's own spoken language (more words used, longer and more varied utterances, taking part in conversation) over a period of weeks. A young child does not state a learning goal; what the child values shows in what they choose to talk about, point at and ask for again: a favourite book, a pet, a game. The caregiver's goals are separate and also matter: to enjoy time with the child, to get through bedtime, to prepare the child for school, to keep the family's language. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A parent who values a calm bedtime story may resist a routine that turns every page into questions; a family that values its home language may stop taking part if advised to switch to the setting's language.

For example, a home visitor's objective may be more expressive vocabulary at the two-year check, while the parent wants the child to sit still for a story. A routine that starts from the child's favourite book, asks one question per page and stops when the child loses interest serves both; record the child's talk and the parent's view of how the routine went, so that the objective is not read as the family's goal.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if trials comparing responsive, turn-taking interaction (outside book reading) with the same amount of adult talk directed at the child found no difference in the child's own language, or if dialogic-reading gains disappeared at a delay of months. If gains depend on the routine being delivered by researchers or trained specialists, and not by parents or ordinary staff even with coaching, the design should move effort to specialist small-group delivery for the children who need it most. If later work shows the turn-count association is explained by other differences between homes, the general statement on this page rests on dialogic reading alone and should be narrowed to it. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "low fidelity" after the fact; record frequency and what adults did before the outcome is known.

Child talk during sessions, vocabulary on taught words, general expressive language, receptive language, phonological skills and later reading are separate outcomes. The present evidence sets no dose, no group size and no age at which to change routines.

## Description
Language develops fastest where children have real reason to communicate, hear varied language, and receive immediate, responsive feedback from a communication partner. A widely cited early study (Hart & Risley, 1995) framed this in terms of a large raw "word gap" between children from different socioeconomic backgrounds; more recent work qualifies that finding substantially — later replications suggest the gap in total words heard is considerably smaller than originally reported, and part of the original gap likely reflected a mismatch between the researchers' expectations and the actual (but different) linguistic practices of the homes studied, rather than a simple deficit. More recent research (MIT McGovern Institute) finds that **conversational turns** — the back-and-forth of a real exchange — predict language-relevant brain development (specifically in Broca's area) better than raw word count or socioeconomic status alone. The practical implication is the same either way: responsive, reciprocal interaction, not sheer quantity of words a child overhears, is what drives development.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Frequent adult-child interaction that is genuinely reciprocal, not one-directional talking at a child
- Adults who adjust their language complexity to the child's current level and respond to the child's own communicative attempts (including pre-verbal gestures and pointing)
- Opportunities for the child to actually produce language and receive feedback, not just receive input passively
- Multiple communication partners (peers as well as primary caregivers), since variety in interaction partners broadens language experience
#### Constraints
- Passive exposure (background media, overheard adult conversation not directed at the child) does not substitute for direct, responsive interaction
- Raw word-count metrics can mask what actually matters (responsiveness and reciprocity) and risk stigmatizing home language practices that differ from a researcher's expectations without being deficient
- Over-simplification of language input, sustained too long, can limit a child's exposure to the complexity they are ready to handle next

### Target Learners
- Infants and toddlers (birth–3 years), during the period of most rapid phoneme, vocabulary, and grammar development
- Preschool and early-elementary learners during continued rapid vocabulary growth
- Children with speech or language delays, or who are deaf or hard of hearing, where early responsive interaction (including early sign-language exposure) is especially time-sensitive

### Target Learning Objectives
- Phoneme discrimination and vocal/gestural production
- Vocabulary growth and word-meaning acquisition
- Grammatical rule acquisition through natural corrective feedback (e.g., a caregiver recasting "I goed" as "You went?" rather than explicitly correcting)
- Communicative competence for social goals: requesting, commenting, connecting with others
