# Autonomy

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 9 claims (2 for, 7 mixed) · 13 studies (5 review, 3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 13 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner whose participation is driven by pressure, threat, or a promised reward, a learning environment that supports autonomy is expected to sustain intrinsic motivation and voluntary engagement (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`) more than one that controls them. Autonomy support here means choices that matter within a stated structure, a reason for work that is not negotiable, and informational rather than controlling language and feedback. The rationale comes from [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md), where autonomy is one of three basic needs. The design variable is `autonomy-support`, and so, on its controlling side, are `rewards-incentives` and `praise`.

The relationship weakens, or reverses, under four conditions:

- **Which outcome is measured.** The evidence for motivation outcomes (free-choice persistence, interest) is stronger than the evidence for learning outcomes. Learner control in educational technology, pooled across studies, did not change achievement measurably.
- **What the learner controls and when.** In one motor-learning experiment, controlling feedback helped retention and transfer only when the learner chose after seeing their own attempt. Choosing before the attempt gave no benefit. Controlling pace helped only on complex questions.
- **The learner's starting state.** Prior knowledge of the task and the ability to direct one's own learning both matter. Some learners in open, self-directed courses could not direct their learning and reported demotivation. For a `novice` on a high-element-interactivity task, [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) predicts that open choice adds search costs. No claim here tests that prediction for choice itself.
- **Horizon.** Most of the motivational evidence measures engagement after a reward is withdrawn, in single sessions. Durable effects in a course over a term (`term-plus`) are not shown here.

[Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) holds the conditional model for what a learner does with the room that autonomy gives: planning, monitoring against a criterion, and choosing a next action. This page does not repeat it. Autonomy is the condition that leaves room for regulation. Whether the learner can use that room is self-regulated learning's question. No pattern page instantiates this principle yet. [Learner Choice](../elements/learner-choice.md) and [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) are the nearest elements.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

This is the page's earlier guidance, kept as a concrete default that a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal, with its evidence stated beside it. No claim in the wiki tests autonomy-supportive teaching, as a package, against controlling teaching on learning outcomes.

1. **Fix the destination and vary the path.** State the objective and the success criterion, then offer choices that change the substance of how it is reached or shown: topic, medium, order or pace. Cosmetic choices such as colour or font do not count. All options must target the same objective at the same rigour. *Untested here; from the earlier page.* Pace control has one experiment behind it, for complex material only ([learner pacing](../claims/learner-paced-beats-system-paced-complex-material.md) [~M]).
2. **Give a reason for required work.** Open fixed activities with why the task matters, in terms the learner might value. *Untested here.* The suggestion comes second-hand through a theoretical article ([autonomy-supportive versus controlling teaching](../claims/autonomy-support-versus-controlling-teaching.md) [+W]), which describes rationale, choice and promoting the activity's value as autonomy-supportive strategies. It reports no study.
3. **Use informational, not controlling, language and incentives.** Prefer "here is what would help" to "you must". Do not promise tangible rewards for doing an activity the learner already finds interesting. Feedback about competence is fine. *Partly supported:* expected tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement in one meta-analysis, a competing meta-analysis found the effect minimal, and both found that verbal praise did not undermine engagement ([rewards](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]).
4. **Bound the choice to what the learner can judge.** For learners new to the task, offer two or three vetted options and model how to choose, then widen the options as competence shows on unsupported tasks. *Untested here; from the earlier page.* The claims on open, self-directed courses ([connectivist courses](../claims/connectivist-courses-student-agency-problems.md) [~W]) and on learner control ([learner pacing](../claims/learner-paced-beats-system-paced-complex-material.md) [~M]) argue against unbounded choice. Neither tests bounded choice.
5. **Put the learner's choice after their own performance where possible.** Let the learner ask for feedback, a hint or a worked step once they have seen how an attempt went, not before. *One laboratory motor-learning experiment* ([deciding after the trial](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-after-trial-improves-motor-retention.md) [~M]). Do not carry it to academic tasks as a finding.
6. **Leave room to plan, monitor and reflect**, and support those steps as [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) describes. *See that page for its evidence.*
7. **Judge the design on the outcome that matters.** Check a learning measure as well as a motivation measure. Reported enjoyment or satisfaction alone does not show that the design worked.

How far each neighbouring claim may be carried: the reward meta-analyses are mostly laboratory studies of already-interesting tasks. The feedback-timing study used a slider-positioning task with university volunteers. The learner-control synthesis covers educational technology in general. The connectivist report is second-hand and describes open online courses. None of them tests a classroom autonomy-support programme.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behaviour under stated conditions: whether a learner starts or returns to a task when nothing requires it, what they choose when given options, how long they persist, what they say about why they are doing the work, and how they respond to a reason or an incentive. "Feels autonomous", "is intrinsically motivated" and "is controlled" are inferred states. A questionnaire score of perceived autonomy is a self-report, not a measure of the state. Compliance also looks like engagement. Record what options were offered, what was said about why, what incentives were in place, and what the learner could already do.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Works only while a reward, grade or check is present; stops when it is removed | Engagement controlled by the incentive; the task holds no interest for this learner; the learner cannot do the task unaided and the check provided structure | Remove the incentive but keep the structure and a stated rationale, and record free-time engagement; give a short unsupported probe of the skill; ask what the learner would do with the task if it were not graded. |
| Given a choice, picks the easiest option or does not choose | Avoiding effort or risk; cannot tell the options apart (too little task knowledge); options are trivial or irrelevant to them; choosing is unfamiliar or culturally unexpected | Ask the learner to say what each option involves; offer fewer, more distinct options with a model of choosing; check whether the choice changes when the options are tied to something the learner said they value. |
| Reports enjoying the choice but learns no more | Motivation moved and learning did not; the choice concerned features unrelated to learning; the chosen option was weaker; the learning measure is not aligned with what was chosen | Compare learning across options on the same aligned measure; check whether the options were equal in rigour; reobserve at a delayed horizon. |
| Disengages or becomes anxious in an open, self-directed task | Missing task knowledge or regulation skill rather than missing autonomy; too many decisions; does not value the goal | Supply a plan or narrow the options, keep the choice of topic, and observe whether execution follows; give a content probe with support; ask what the learner is trying to achieve. |
| Engages more after a rationale is given | Reason accepted as personally meaningful; teacher attention or relationship; novelty | Record whether the learner can restate the reason in their own terms; compare engagement on later required tasks without a fresh rationale. |

These rows are proposals and have not been tested with learners. A probe shifts confidence among explanations; it does not identify a cause. Offering a choice or asking about values is itself an intervention, so record every exposure.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests the full relationship as stated. None compares an autonomy-supportive classroom or course with a controlling one on both motivation and learning, with assignment and horizon reported. The claims below each test a part of it.

- [Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]: rests on three sources. A narrative review presents the self-determination framework, in which autonomy-supportive conditions are said to give higher intrinsic motivation than controlling ones; the entry prints no data. A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected tangible rewards contingent on engagement undermined intrinsic motivation for already-interesting tasks (d = −0.4), while informational feedback did not. A conceptual framework distinguishes organisational, procedural and cognitive autonomy support, from teacher vignettes, as a proposition. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries pass, and the rest could not be confirmed (abstract). Its one experimental basis is about rewards as a controlling condition, so the direct test of autonomy support is review-level. The marker is held below its `S` cap for that reason. It does not predict learning gains, and it does not test choice.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]: two meta-analyses of experiments. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (128 studies) found that engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced free-choice behaviour after the reward was withdrawn (d = −0.28 to −0.40), more for children than for college students, while positive verbal feedback increased it. Cameron and Pierce (96 studies) found no overall undermining, with a minimal negative effect confined to expected tangible rewards for merely doing a task, on free-choice behaviour only. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). It qualifies the model: how controlling the incentive is matters, and the size of the effect is disputed. The outcomes are free-choice persistence and interest, not learning.
- [Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies](../claims/learner-paced-beats-system-paced-complex-material.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 18 studies (29 effects) of learner control in educational technology found an overall effect near zero (g = 0.05), near zero across most characteristics of control and context. An experiment with primary-school students learning from animation found that learner-paced versions gave higher test performance and lower cognitive load, on difficult, high-element-interactivity questions only. Checked by the judge: all 2 entries pass (abstract). It limits the model: handing control to learners is not, in general, a learning intervention. Any benefit depends on what is controlled and on task complexity.
- [Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-after-trial-improves-motor-retention.md) [~M]: a laboratory experiment with university volunteers learning a slider-positioning task under six feedback conditions. Learners who chose after the trial were more accurate on retention than those who chose before (Self-After M = 10.04, Self-Before M = 29.18 absolute error) and than yoked learners who received the same feedback schedule without choosing. The same held for [transfer to a new distance](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-after-trial-improves-motor-transfer.md) [~M]. [Choosing only before the trial](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-before-trial-shows-no-benefit-over-yoked.md) [~M] did not differ significantly from yoked learners. That is not equivalence, which was not tested. Checked by the judge: all entries pass (full text). Group sizes are not reported on the claim pages; rigour is coded `r1`. It qualifies the model: in this task, control helped when it let the learner act on their own performance, and having control in itself showed no benefit. It is one motor task and does not transport to academic content without a test.
- [Not all students in connectivist courses could autonomously direct their own learning, and some felt disconnected and demotivated](../claims/connectivist-courses-student-agency-problems.md) [~W]: a survey's second-hand report of studies of open connectivist online courses. Not all students could direct their own learning, and some felt disconnected, demotivated and disturbed. Not yet checked against its sources. It limits the model: wide self-direction without structure can harm some learners' motivation, which is the outcome autonomy is meant to raise. It reports no comparison.
- [Choice in retention programs is linked to retention gains, but autonomy is rarely a tested variable](../claims/choice-autonomy-rarely-tested-in-retention-programs.md) [~W]: a narrative review reports, second-hand, that in college learning communities the one study with random, no-choice assignment showed no persistence or achievement gains, while voluntary programmes showed some. The review adds that autonomy was rarely a tested variable. Not yet checked against its sources. Voluntary participation also selects who takes part, so the contrast cannot separate choice from self-selection. It shows the evidence gap more than an effect.

Taken together: controlling incentives can reduce voluntary engagement with interesting tasks, though the size is disputed. Learner control in general does not raise achievement. In two experiments, specific, well-timed control helped: pacing on complex material, and feedback chosen after an attempt. Unstructured self-direction can demotivate some learners. Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them by effect label. A nonsignificant difference is not evidence of no effect.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is a capability, measured by a stated instrument at a stated horizon. Autonomy support is a means to it, and also, sometimes, an objective in itself (sustained voluntary engagement). Elicit separately what the learner wants, and why: to pass, to make something of their own, to stop being told what to do, to be left alone. Agreement, divergence and "I don't know" are all observations. A learner's valued goal is also what makes a rationale land. A reason the learner does not share is just one more instruction.

For example, an adult apprentice electrician may value wiring a real circuit at a job site, while the designer's objective is correct calculation of load on a written test. A choice between two calculation worksheets offers autonomy over something the learner does not care about. A choice of which real installation to plan the load calculation for, with the written test kept as the criterion, ties the choice to the learner's goal. Record both the engagement and the test result, so that a gain in one is not read as a gain in the other.

## What would revise this model?

The model should weaken if well-controlled comparisons of autonomy-supportive against controlling conditions, in real courses, show no difference in voluntary engagement or persistence at a delayed horizon. It should also weaken if the reward-undermining effect turns out to be confined to laboratory tasks. If choice keeps showing motivation gains with no learning gains, keep that split rather than calling both "engagement". If well-timed, performance-contingent control (as in the feedback experiment) replicates in academic tasks while open choice does not, the principle should be restated around when the learner controls what, not around choice in general. Do not protect the model by calling every failure of choice a lack of structure, or every success of direct instruction a hidden form of autonomy support.

Motivation during a task, free-choice engagement afterwards, immediate learning, delayed retention and the learner's own valued use are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how many options to offer, how to sequence widening choice, or how culture and age moderate any of it.

### Illustrative

**[Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)** — A grid of task options (e.g., demonstrate understanding via podcast, diagram, or essay) that lets learners select how to show mastery while all options target the same objective. Effective when options are genuinely equivalent in rigor and aligned to the same [learning objective](../elements/objective.md).

**[Academic Choice (planning, working, reflecting)](../strategies/academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md)** — A Responsive Classroom routine in which learners plan what they will do, work with that plan, and then reflect on their choices — building self-regulation alongside ownership.

**Project-based learning with negotiated topics** — Curricula like [Expeditionary Learning / EL Education](https://eleducation.org) give students voice in selecting expedition topics and products within teacher-defined constraints, pairing autonomy with strong structural scaffolds.

**Rationale-first task framing** — Rather than changing the task, the teacher opens with "This practice matters because…" before assigning required work. Reeve's research shows rationale-giving alone measurably shifts students' perceived autonomy, even for non-negotiable activities.

**[Duolingo](https://www.duolingo.com)** — Combines a fixed skill sequence (structure) with learner control over daily goals, lesson order within unlocked units, and session length — a commercial example of autonomy-within-structure design.

## Description
Autonomy is the experience of acting with volition and self-endorsement — feeling that one's learning behavior originates from oneself rather than from external pressure. As a design principle, it recommends structuring learning environments so learners make meaningful choices about approach, pace, topics, or expression, and understand the reasons behind required activities. Autonomy is one of three basic psychological needs in [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md), and autonomy-supportive environments reliably increase intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. # Learner Choice

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (1 for, 3 mixed) · 6 studies (2 review, 2 theoretical, 1 causal, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q3` · 1 of 6 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
Learner choice is the principle of giving learners meaningful decisions about aspects of their learning, such as topic, format, sequence, pacing, strategy, or demonstration of understanding. The instructional value of choice comes from agency with purpose: when learners can make decisions that matter, they are more likely to experience ownership, motivation, and relevance. Effective choice is not unlimited freedom. It is bounded in ways that preserve learning goals, quality standards, and sufficient support.

## Implications
Learner choice improves motivation and ownership when the options are meaningful and the learner has enough support to choose well. Choice can strengthen self-regulation because learners must weigh options, commit to a path, and monitor whether that path is working [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]. But too much freedom too early can create overload or inequity, especially for novices who do not yet know which option fits the goal [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M]. The practical design move is bounded choice: keep outcomes and standards clear [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~S] while varying the path, topic, or product in ways that genuinely matter.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Meaningful options**: Choices should affect how learners engage, not merely offer cosmetic variation.
- **Clear criteria and boundaries**: Learners need to know what stays fixed and what is flexible.
- **Support for decision-making**: Some learners need models, examples, or prompts to choose well.
- **Alignment to the target outcome**: Different paths or products should still aim at comparable learning goals.
#### Constraints
- **Choice overload**: Too many options can increase uncertainty and reduce participation.
- **Unequal option quality**: If some choices are clearly easier, richer, or better supported, the design can become inequitable.
- **False autonomy**: Learners can be offered "choice" that changes little about the actual learning experience.
- **Premature freedom**: Novices may need more structure before choosing among tasks, strategies, or goals.

### Target Learners
- **Adult learners**: Particularly relevant when learners bring clear goals, interests, and prior experience.
- **Learners needing motivation and ownership**: Choice can increase commitment when the task still has enough support.
- **Learners developing self-regulation**: Choice becomes productive when paired with planning and reflection.
- **Learners with diverse strengths or needs**: Multiple pathways can make participation more accessible and meaningful.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Ownership of learning**: Helping learners see themselves as active participants rather than passive recipients.
- **Motivation and persistence**: Increasing willingness to invest effort in meaningful work.
- **Metacognitive decision-making**: Practicing how to choose tools, strategies, or products that fit a goal.
- **Flexible demonstration of understanding**: Allowing different valid ways to show competence.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Self-determination theory — meaningful choice can support autonomy and increase intrinsic motivation.
- Andragogical views of learning — adults are more engaged when learning connects to their goals and allows some self-direction.
- Expectancy-value perspectives — choice can increase task value when learners see the work as relevant and self-endorsed.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Choice is not inherently motivating if learners feel unprepared to choose or unclear about the consequences of options.
- Strong learning design often requires more structure for novices before broad choice becomes productive.

### Claims
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — choice becomes educative when learners track whether their selected path is actually helping them progress
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M] — novices often need more guidance in how to choose and proceed before broad autonomy becomes productive
- [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~S] — choice works better when standards and success criteria remain explicit even as pathways vary
- [Young children's video interpretation of their own mathematical play reveals both mathematical and social-affective sensemaking](../claims/childrens-video-interpretation-of-play-reveals-mathematical-and-social-sensemaking.md) [+W] — when children chose what to notice and interpret in their own unscripted play, they surfaced real mathematical reasoning alongside social and affective meaning

- [Flexible Grouping](flexible-grouping.md) — choice can include partner, role, or grouping structures.

- **Topic selection within a fixed rubric**: Learners choose the issue, case, or text they will analyze while using common criteria.

- **Paced pathway choices**: Learners decide whether to review, practice more, or move into extension work.
