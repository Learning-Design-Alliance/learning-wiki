# Ask Experts

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 14 claims (4 for, 10 mixed) · 22 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 associational, 2 review, 2 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 22 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **expert consultation**: a learner brings a specific question, attempt or piece of work to someone with more expertise in the domain (an instructor, practitioner, mentor or more knowledgeable peer), and the expert responds to it. For a learner who is `novice` or `intermediate` on the task (they can attempt it, but their attempt stalls, misses what matters, or they cannot say what a good result would look like), a consultation in which the expert first has the learner explain their attempt, then responds contingently (a question, then a hint, then modelling the decision aloud, explanation last), and in which the learner applies what they heard to a revision or a new attempt soon afterwards, is expected to produce better unaided performance on that task (`immediate-performance`, `near-transfer`) than either no access to the expert or an expert who simply supplies the answer. Across a run of consultations the expert does less and the learner more. The knowledge type is usually a `principle` or `complex-skill` where expert judgement is not visible in the finished product (a diagnosis, a design critique, a revision decision, a debugging choice); the settings range from one-to-one tutoring to `classroom` office hours and `workplace-clinical` mentoring.

The relationship is conditional on what the expert does, not on their expertise alone: the nearest evidence suggests the learning comes from what the learner is prompted to construct, and one meta-regression found no tutor-background category (content experts included) that predicted learning in problem-based courses. It is also conditional on the learner's starting response (an `advanced` learner gains less from explanation and more from critique), on whether the advice is applied before it is forgotten, and on whether the learner can ask at all without social cost. **No claim in the wiki tests access to an expert, as such, against no access, or compares ways of running a consultation.** The nearest evidence is from one-to-one tutoring (contingent against fixed support; prompting against explaining), from prompted self-explanation, from fading support, and from process against outcome goals, all carried here to a setting they did not test.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) holds the general model of raising support after a failure and lowering it after a success, and its withdrawal; this page applies it to one kind of encounter and does not repeat it. The [Cognitive Apprenticeship pattern](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) is the full modelling–coaching–scaffolding–fading–articulation–exploration sequence, of which a consultation is usually the coaching or articulation step. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) holds the practice phase after a model. [Communities of Practice](communities-of-practice.md) holds sustained participation alongside experienced members over a term or more; a single consultation, or a short series of them, is the narrower object here.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Bring real work, prepared.** Before each consultation the learner writes a short question card: the task, what they tried, where it stalled, and what they think the answer might be. Unguided "ask me anything" sessions are replaced by consultations around an artefact, case or attempt. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement of "a concrete learner question, artifact, case, or task".
2. **Learner explains first.** The expert opens by asking the learner to talk through their attempt and their reasoning at the point where it stalled, before saying anything about it (about the first third of the time). Carried from [prompted self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] (learners studying examples or solving problems, not consultations) and from tutoring where prompting alone matched explaining-and-feedback ([tutoring claim](../claims/tutoring-effectiveness-comes-from-scaffolding-and-feedback.md) [~M], eighth graders, 11 tutor–student pairs).
3. **Respond on a ladder, smallest help first.** A question that points at the decision; then a hint; then the expert thinking aloud through the same decision on a parallel case, naming the cues they look at and the criteria they apply; a full explanation only if those fail. Evidence: [contingent support](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] beat fixed or no support in one-to-one tutoring of children on long division, and interactive tutoring (questions and hints) gave better transfer than explaining; untested for experts consulted by older learners.
4. **Point at process and criteria, not only the verdict.** Name the strategy or criterion the learner's work needs ("what would you check before choosing this?"), not just whether it is right. Carried from [process goals](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M] (high-school writing revision; once the skill was established, outcome goals helped), which is about goal-setting, not expert talk.
5. **Close with one next action.** End each consultation with the learner stating, in their own words, the one or two changes they will make and why. Untested proposal.
6. **Apply within a week.** The learner revises or attempts a comparable task within about a week (sooner for a single-session visit), and brings the revision to the next consultation or submits it for brief written comment. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("expert advice that is not applied quickly is easily forgotten").
7. **Hand responsibility back across the series.** A default arc of three to five consultations over a term, 15–20 minutes each per learner or small group (untested quantities): early, the expert models and explains on parts of the learner's work for up to half the time; middle, the learner presents attempt and diagnosis first and the expert asks and hints; late, the learner proposes the critique of their own work and the expert confirms, corrects or adds one point. Move a learner to the next stage when, on two consecutive consultations, they name the criterion the expert would have applied before the expert does, and their revision deals with the previous points without further help. The shape follows [fading support](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S] (faded worked examples, near transfer); the thresholds are untested proposals.
8. **Make it safe to be uncertain.** Ask for questions in writing or in small groups as well as aloud, accept partial and wrong ideas without comment on the person, and let low-confidence learners bring a question with a peer. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement of psychological safety.

When a condition is not met: if the expert has no time to see each learner, collect question cards in advance and spend the session on the three or four commonest stalls, thinking aloud through each; if no expert is available live, record the expert thinking aloud through two or three representative pieces of work and pair it with a written response to each submitted question card.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner can already do and say about the task.** A learner who cannot yet attempt the task needs a model before a consultation is useful; one who can already diagnose their own errors needs critique, not explanation. If unstated, ask: can the learner attempt one representative task now, and say where it went wrong?
- **How much of the expert's time each learner gets, and in what form.** The evidence for contingent help is one-to-one; a guest visit, a drop-in office hour and a weekly mentor meeting allow very different designs. If unstated, ask: how many learners per expert, how many minutes each, how often, and live or written?
- **What the expert is expected to do.** A practitioner with no teaching experience tends to tell; the design may need to brief them to ask first and to think aloud. If unstated, ask: who is the expert, have they taught or mentored before, and will someone brief them?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed in a consultation is what the learner brought, what they said when asked to explain it, which level of help was needed (question, hint, model, explanation), what they said they would change, and what the next revision or attempt shows. "Understands the expert's reasoning", "is dependent on the expert" and "is afraid to ask" are inferred states. Agreement during the conversation is not evidence of understanding; the next unaided attempt is.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Brings no question, or only "is this right?" | Cannot yet locate the difficulty; fears exposing ignorance; does not know what the expert could help with | Ask them to talk through the work and stop them at the first choice; offer a written or private channel and see whether questions appear. |
| Nods and agrees, then the revision ignores the advice | Did not understand and did not say so; understood but could not apply it; did not value the change | Before leaving, ask for the change in their own words; at the next meeting, ask what they tried and what stopped them. |
| Applies the advice to this piece of work but repeats the error on the next | Learned a fix, not the criterion behind it; the next task looks different enough to hide the same decision | Give a changed task where the same criterion applies and ask which of the expert's points is relevant before they start. |
| Returns with the same kind of question every time | Dependent on the expert's judgement; the expert has been answering rather than prompting; the task is too far beyond them | Have them propose an answer first and rate their confidence; compare accuracy of proposals with and without the expert's confirmation. |
| Asks sharp questions but performs poorly alone | Understands the criteria but lacks practice; the consultation has replaced practice | Give a short unaided attempt between consultations and record its accuracy. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe is itself part of the consultation and changes what it measures, so record each one, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests consulting an expert against not doing so, or compares ways of running a consultation. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]: fourth and fifth graders learning long division one-to-one solved more problems under fully contingent support than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition, `q3`, no effect size printed); in tutoring of eighth graders, interactive tutoring (questions and hints) gave similar immediate learning but better transfer than explaining. A meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching, which qualifies a consultation built only on prompting. The claim's Discussion notes that most evidence is one-to-one and that contingency is scarce in whole-class teaching.
- [Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback](../claims/tutoring-effectiveness-comes-from-scaffolding-and-feedback.md) [~M]: a review reports human tutoring at d = 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems at d = 0.76 against no tutoring (abstract only); in one study of eighth graders (11 pairs), tutors told to withhold explanations and feedback and only prompt produced learning as good as ordinary tutoring. The tutors were inexperienced college students, not domain experts. It supports "learner explains first" and qualifies the value of the expert's own explanations.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports found that prompting learners to self-explain while studying or solving problems improved learning by a moderate average amount (g = .55), across task types, subjects and levels; a mathematics meta-analysis found gains in classroom settings and at a delay much less established. It is evidence for prompts, not for expert consultations; the page carries it to the expert's opening question.
- [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]: across three experiments (ninth graders and college students), fading worked examples into incomplete examples and then problems favoured near transfer over static example–problem arrangements; a review names fading as one of the three defining features of scaffolding. It is marked `~` because it is a condition on expert help (help that never fades), not support for expert help as such. It does not establish a fading rate, or that fading prevents long-term dependence.
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]: high-school students learning writing revision with process goals outperformed those with outcome goals, and shifting to outcome goals once the skill was established was beneficial (sample size not stated on the claim page), with a theoretical chapter synthesising similar findings. It concerns goals the learner sets, carried here to what the expert directs attention to.
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies; the reason to shift advanced learners from explanation to critique. It gives no point at which a learner crosses over.

**The limiting claims.** [In a re-analysis of PBL tutor-background data, neither a meta-regression nor subgroup comparisons found that tutor expertise predicts student learning](../claims/tutor-background-meta-regression-not-predictive.md) [~M] and its companion, [In a re-analysis of PBL tutor-background data, neither a meta-regression nor subgroup comparisons found that tutor expertise predicts student learning](../claims/tutor-background-meta-regression-not-predictive.md) [~M], re-analyse one problem-based-learning data set: content-expert tutors did not produce more learning than other tutor backgrounds. A non-significant difference does not show equivalence, and PBL tutors facilitate groups rather than answer individual questions, but it is the nearest test of whether domain expertise by itself matters, and it does not show that it does. [In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems](../claims/peer-feedback-accuracy-depends-on-expertise.md) [~M] qualifies in the other direction: in secondary writing, more expert peers gave more helpful feedback but were not more accurate at finding problems, and with a well-specified rubric peer ratings were as valid in lower- as in higher-performing schools. Its own Discussion says the evidence does not support its title.

Learners, tasks and settings differ across these claims (children with a tutor, eighth graders, high-school writers, PBL courses), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a learner's later unaided performance after consulting a domain expert against a comparison.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually better unaided performance on a representative task, with the learner able to apply the criteria an expert would apply, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the expert: a verdict on today's work, reassurance, a shortcut, contact with someone in the profession they hope to join, or to understand how experts think. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants a verdict will treat questions as delay; one who wants contact with the profession may value the conversation more than the advice.

For example, a nursing student may want to hear from an experienced nurse what the job is really like, while the designer's objective for the consultation is prioritising patient observations. A consultation that answers only the objective may leave the learner's reason for coming unmet, and one that answers only the learner's question teaches no criterion. Record both, so that a well-liked visit is not read as a learning gain, and a useful critique is not read as a failure because the learner found it uncomfortable.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing consultations with domain experts against comparable time on practice with worked feedback show no advantage in later unaided performance, or show that a gain at the end of a consultation disappears at a delay. If prompting-only consultations match those with expert explanation across settings, as one tutoring study suggests, the model should say "an expert who prompts" rather than "an expert who explains". If content expertise continues not to predict learning when tutors' behaviour is held constant, what matters is the consulting skill, and the page should say so in its title. If structured peer consultation with a rubric matches expert consultation for some goals, the expert's time should be reserved for the goals where it does not. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong expert" or "not enough follow-through" after the fact.

The present evidence establishes no length or frequency of consultation, no number of consultations in a series, and no criterion for withdrawing the expert.

## Description
Give learners structured access to people with deeper domain expertise so they can ask questions, hear expert reasoning, receive targeted feedback, and compare their own thinking to more mature practice. Expert access is most valuable when it is interactive rather than performative: learners bring a real task, problem, or uncertainty, and the expert responds with explanation, modeling, questioning, and calibrated support.

## Implications

Asking experts works best when the interaction helps learners move from vague confusion to more precise reasoning. The expert's role is not only to provide answers, but to make disciplinary judgment visible, surface key vocabulary and distinctions, and redirect learners toward productive next steps. This is strongest when support is contingent on what the learner actually understands [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M], when the expert prompts learners to explain their own reasoning instead of merely listening to answers [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and when the expert gradually hands responsibility back to the learner rather than remaining the permanent problem-solver [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]. In practice this often overlaps with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md), [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), and [Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to a relevant expert, mentor, instructor, practitioner, or more knowledgeable peer who can explain decisions, not just outcomes
- A concrete learner question, artifact, case, or task; unguided "ask me anything" formats are usually less productive than interaction around real work
- Psychological safety so learners can reveal uncertainty, partial understanding, or misconceptions without social penalty
- A mechanism for follow-through such as revision, practice, or reflection; expert advice that is not applied quickly is easily forgotten

#### Constraints
- Hard to scale with fidelity in large-group settings; responsive expert interaction is time-intensive and depends on scarce human attention
- Quality varies with the expert's instructional skill; deep content knowledge alone does not guarantee clear explanation or effective questioning
- Can produce dependency if experts solve problems for learners instead of diagnosing understanding and fading support [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]
- Less effective when learners lack enough background knowledge to ask meaningful questions or interpret expert answers without additional scaffolding
- Can intimidate learners with low confidence, low status, or limited language proficiency unless the interaction is deliberately structured and supportive

### Target Learners
- Novices or near-novices who need access to expert vocabulary, heuristics, and standards of quality
- Learners entering a profession, discipline, or authentic practice community where tacit knowledge matters
- Learners developing confidence in help-seeking, discussion, and disciplinary communication
- Learners working on complex or ambiguous tasks where feedback on judgment is more useful than answer keys alone
- Less necessary for advanced learners who can already self-diagnose many errors and may benefit more from independent performance or peer critique

### Target Learning Objectives
- Clarify concepts, terminology, and decision criteria in a domain
- Improve problem formulation by helping learners ask better, more specific questions
- Develop metacognitive awareness by comparing novice reasoning to expert reasoning
- Support transfer on complex tasks through contingent hints, questioning, and feedback rather than answer-giving
- Build professional or disciplinary identity through contact with authentic practice and standards

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Social constructivist accounts (Vygotsky, Bruner) support expert interaction as a way to extend learner performance through guided dialogue and shared meaning-making
- Social learning theory (Bandura) supports learning through observation, modeling, and guided imitation of more capable others
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) operationalizes the principle by making expert thinking visible and combining modeling, coaching, scaffolding, and fading
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) qualifies expert support as most useful when it reduces unproductive search and clarifies decisions for novices

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Overly directive expert help can suppress generative processing if learners are positioned as passive recipients rather than active explainers
- The value of expert access depends on contingency; interactive questioning and hints outperform simple explanation for transfer in tutoring studies [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Support should fade as competence grows; keeping experts as constant problem-solvers can interfere with independence [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]

### Claims
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] — expert interaction is more effective when support is responsive to learner understanding, especially through hints and questioning rather than explanation alone
- [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — expert questioning is especially valuable when it prompts learners to explain their own reasoning instead of only listening to answers
- [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S] — expert involvement should decrease as competence increases so learners assume more responsibility for performance
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M] — experts can improve novice learning by redirecting attention from "getting the right answer" to strategies, criteria, and process

## Related Principles
- [Mentoring/Coaching](mentoring-and-coaching.md) — a broader relationship structure in which expert guidance is sustained over time rather than episodic
- [Communities of Practice](communities-of-practice.md) — extends expert access into sustained participation in a practice community
- [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) — turns expert explanation into coached performance on real tasks
- [Explaining Their Thinking](self-explanation.md) — experts are most useful when they elicit learner reasoning rather than replacing it
- [Purposeful Reflection](purposeful-reflection.md) — follow-up reflection helps consolidate what was learned from the interaction and what remains uncertain
