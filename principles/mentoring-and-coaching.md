# Mentoring and Coaching

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 63 claims (30 for, 26 mixed, 7 against) · 46 studies (12 causal, 10 review, 10 associational, 8 qualitative, 3 design, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 5 of 46 report an effect size · 59 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **relationship itself**: a learner who is doing real work in a role, programme or practice that is new to them, paired over time with a more experienced person (a mentor teacher, an instructional coach, a success coach, a faculty or research mentor, a near-peer, or, increasingly, a software coach). The relationship is expected to help when the more experienced person **sees the learner's actual work** (a lesson taught, a draft, a lap flown, a semester's choices), responds to what that work shows with modelling, questions and specific feedback, meets often enough to follow the learner through a cycle of attempts, and **hands judgement over to the learner** as the learner succeeds. Under those conditions, mentoring and coaching are expected to improve observed performance in the practice (`skill-performance`, observed in the setting) and persistence in the role or programme (staying in teaching, re-enrolling in college), more than no such relationship or one that is infrequent, generic, mostly about logistics, or tied to the learner's evaluation. Effects on downstream outcomes (the coached teacher's students' test scores, a degree completed, earnings) are expected to be smaller and less certain than effects on the practice coached.

The relationship is conditional on four things: contact is anchored in the learner's own work, not in general advice; there is enough continuity for a cycle of attempt, feedback and reattempt; the person in the support role has the skill and time to observe and give specific feedback; and the role is separated from judging the learner, so that the learner can show what they cannot yet do.

**What the wiki's evidence tests, and what it does not.** The strongest test is of **teachers coached on their classroom practice**: a meta-analysis of 60 causal studies (Kraft et al. 2018, several claim pages) found coaching raised observed instruction by a pooled 0.49 SD and students' test scores by 0.18 SD (0.12 SD on state tests), with effects shrinking as programmes grew and no relationship with hours of coaching. For **college students**, a randomised evaluation of one success-coaching programme (InsideTrack, reviewed by the What Works Clearinghouse) found higher persistence (about five percentage points, effect size 0.13) and no discernible effect on degree completion, and a matched comparison of another (Success Boston) found higher persistence and no difference in grades. For **new teachers**, one large randomised study of comprehensive induction (intensive mentoring plus professional development) found no first-year improvement in practice, retention or achievement, while correlational surveys and policy reviews associate strong mentoring with plans to stay and lower attrition. For **software coaches**, one small study found large within-learner gains in a motor skill from an adaptive coach and weaker or no gains from simpler baselines, and one pilot found no advantage for a scaffolded study coach. The rest of the cited claims are descriptive: how much mentoring people receive, what it covers, what mentees and coaches value. No claim in this wiki isolates the components this page names (observation of real work, contingent feedback, fading, role separation) as causes; they are carried from neighbouring evidence and labelled where used.

How the converted siblings sit around this page: the [Professional Development pattern](../patterns/professional-development.md) owns the full coaching cycle for one teaching practice (diagnose, model, rehearse, enact with feedback, fade, check maintenance) and its doses, and is where the teacher-coaching meta-analysis is read in detail; this page does not repeat it. [Ongoing study teams and peer coaching](ongoing-study-teams-peer-coaching-in-service.md) owns which ongoing school structure carries teachers' learning (study teams, peer coaching, a school-based coach) alongside occasional sessions. [Mentor Support in Self-Paced Online Learning](../patterns/mentor-support-in-self-paced-online-learning.md) owns mentoring when learners study asynchronously: on-demand and proactive contact, progress signals and the split between automated and human outreach. [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) owns the modelling, coaching, scaffolding and fading of a complex cognitive skill within instruction. [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) owns how support within a task is raised and withdrawn. This page holds what is common to all of them: who the mentor or coach is, what the relationship is for, how often and about what they meet, how the role is kept separate from evaluation, and how the relationship ends.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (a clear developmental focus, trust and continuity, calibrated support, a path toward independence; risks of overdependence, uneven quality, role confusion and unequal access), kept as a concrete default and extended into an arc. Each step says what evidence, if any, stands behind it; numbers are untested proposals unless a claim is named.

1. **Name what the relationship is for, in terms of the learner's work.** One or two targets the mentee can see in their own work ("questions that get more than one student talking", "a weekly plan you keep", "a lap without a collision"), agreed in the first meeting and revisited each month. In one state survey, first-year teachers' mentoring was most often about noninstructional topics ([The mentoring topics first-year teachers most frequently received guidance on were noninstructional rather than instructional](../claims/mentoring-focused-on-noninstructional-topics.md) [~W]); and in coached student teaching, coaching on specific areas of instruction went with better-prepared and more effective first-year teachers ([Student teachers who perceived their mentors modeled effective instruction and provided coaching, feedback, and job assistance felt better prepared, and domain-specific coaching was related to higher first-year REACH ratings](../claims/cps-mentor-coaching-and-modeling-associated-with-preparedness-and-effectiveness.md) [+W]; one Chicago survey, associational). The targeting rule itself is untested.
2. **Anchor every contact in an observation of real work.** The mentor watches the learner do the work (sits in on a lesson, reads the draft, reviews the semester plan, watches the run) or reviews a recording or a shared folder, before talking about it. Contact without observation becomes advice. Access to work in progress is what made coaching possible in one web-based course ([Instructor access to students' individual Web folders enabled coaching on work-in-progress in the Web-based course](../claims/web-folders-enable-coaching.md) [+W]); in one state, a fifth of first-year teachers had never been observed by their mentor ([Time spent on specific mentoring activities varied widely, with some teachers never observed by mentors and others observed an hour or more a month](../claims/mentoring-activity-time-varied-widely.md) [~W]). The rule is a proposal.
3. **Respond to what the work shows: model, ask, give specific feedback, set the next attempt.** More help after a failure, less after a success; show how an expert would do the step that went wrong, ask the learner to explain their choice, and agree one change for the next attempt. Contingent support: [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (one-to-one tutoring of children and a dynamic-assessment meta-analysis; carried from tutoring a task to mentoring a practice). An adaptive software coach that varied its help by the learner's skill produced large pre-post gains in an unassisted motor task, where simpler baseline coaches did not ([L2C coaching significantly reduces learners' lap times and failure counts from pre- to post-coaching in FPV drone racing](../claims/l2c-within-subject-lap-time-failure-gains.md) [+M]; [Baseline coaches produced weaker or absent learning gains: RBF improved only failure count, MIA neither outcome](../claims/baselines-weak-or-absent-skill-gains.md) [-W]; 33 adults, drone racing, one study).
4. **Meet on a regular rhythm, and protect it.** A default of a short check-in weekly and an observation with a debrief every two to four weeks for the first term, then monthly. Hours alone are not the lever: across causal studies of teacher coaching, more hours did not go with larger effects ([Coaching dosage shows precisely estimated null effects: more coaching hours are not associated with larger program effects](../claims/coaching-dosage-null-effect.md) [~M]; exploratory, between programmes). The rhythm is an untested proposal; what it protects is step 2.
5. **Keep the mentor out of judging the mentee.** The mentor or coach is not the person who rates, grades or decides on the learner's continuation; if that cannot be avoided, say which conversations are evaluative and which are not. Teachers who saw coaching as voluntary and non-evaluative reported more improvement ([Teachers who report coaching as a partnership report more improvement in classroom challenges and higher technology-use confidence; voluntary participation and non-evaluative support correlate with improvement in classroom challenges](../claims/coaching-partnership-voluntary-non-evaluative.md) [+W]; one programme's survey correlations); teachers whose principal both coached and evaluated described hiding what they struggled with ([Administrators' dual role as evaluator and coach can undermine the observation process's professional learning benefits](../claims/dual-evaluator-coach-role-undermines-learning.md) [-W]; interviews in six schools).
6. **Choose and prepare mentors for the job, not only for their own performance.** Select mentors who can observe and explain, give them preparation in giving feedback, and check what their meetings cover. In Chicago student teaching, mentors' formal qualifications were generally unrelated to their student teachers' outcomes while their own evaluation ratings were related ([Mentor teachers' own evaluation ratings were positively related to their student teachers' first-year effectiveness as teachers-of-record](../claims/cps-mentor-evaluation-ratings-related-to-student-teachers-first-year-effectiveness.md) [~W]); principals in another Chicago study often lacked the coaching skill for deep conversations about practice ([Principal-teacher conferences were perceived as more reflective and objective than past evaluations, but many principals lacked the instructional coaching skills for deep discussions of practice](../claims/conferences-reflective-but-coaching-skills-lacking.md) [~W]). Preparation content and length are untested.
7. **Offer a say in the match.** Let mentees state preferences (field, background, language, schedule) and change a match that is not working, without making matching on any one characteristic a rule. Apprentices of colour in one programme described mentors of colour as especially valuable and asked for more intentional matching ([Mentors of color were especially valuable to apprentices of color, who wanted more intentional mentor matching](../claims/rtap-mentors-of-color-especially-valuable.md) [~W]; interviews in one programme); a review in sport reports same-gender coach-athlete pairs rating their relationship higher ([Same-gender coach-athlete dyads perceive higher relationship quality (closeness, commitment, complementarity) than other-gender dyads](../claims/same-gender-dyads-higher-relationship-quality.md) [~W]). These are learners' perceptions of a relationship, not learning outcomes, and they describe what mentees value, not what any group needs.
8. **Reach the learners who would not ask.** Assign a mentor or coach to every learner in the target group, with the mentor making the first contact, rather than relying on learners to seek one out. In the one randomised evaluation of student coaching recorded here, coaches were assigned by lottery and reached out to students, and persistence rose ([InsideTrack© Coaching has potentially positive effects on college credit accumulation and persistence, based on one RCT meeting WWC standards](../claims/insidetrack-coaching-potentially-positive-persistence.md) [+M]). The earlier page's warning that informal mentoring favours learners who already know how to seek support is a proposal the wiki holds no test of.
9. **Hand over judgement, then end or change the relationship.** Over the arc, the mentee moves from watching the mentor model, to doing with the mentor's prompts, to doing alone and explaining their reasoning, to setting their own next target; the mentor asks more and tells less. End or change the relationship when the mentee can meet the agreed targets unaided and plan their own next step. [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M] (a scaffolding review and worked-example experiments; carried from fading within a task to fading a relationship).
10. **Plan the arc across sessions.** A default, all untested proposals:
    - *First two to four weeks (setting up).* One meeting to agree targets and ways of working; one observation of the learner's work and a debrief; weekly 15–30 minute check-ins. The mentor models more than asks.
    - *Rest of the first term (cycles).* An observation and 30–45 minute debrief every two to four weeks, each ending with one change to try; the learner brings evidence of the change to the next meeting. The mentor asks the learner to analyse their own work before giving feedback.
    - *Later terms (handover).* Monthly meetings led by the learner, who brings their own target, evidence and plan; the mentor comments rather than directs. The relationship ends, or becomes occasional, when the learner meets the targets unaided for two cycles in a row.

When a condition is not met: if the mentor cannot observe real work, treat the relationship as support and advice and do not expect change in the practice; if the mentor must also evaluate, keep evaluative and developmental meetings apart and tell the learner which is which; if there is no time for regular contact, put the time into fewer observed cycles rather than more unobserved meetings.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner is doing and what the relationship should change.** Coaching an experienced teacher's practice, inducting a new teacher, keeping a first-generation student enrolled and helping a trainee master a motor skill are different jobs with different outcomes and different evidence. If unstated, ask: whose work will the mentor see, and what would be different in it after a term?
- **Who the mentor is and what else they are to the learner.** A trained coach, a colleague, the learner's evaluator, a near-peer and a software coach bring different skill, time and power. If unstated, ask: who is available, how much time do they have each month, and do they also rate or grade this learner?
- **Scale.** One mentor for a few learners can follow each learner's work; a programme across many sites cannot assume the same quality. If unstated, ask: how many learners per mentor, how many sites, and who prepares and supports the mentors?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: who meets whom, how often and for how long; whether the mentor saw the learner's work before the meeting; what each meeting covered (instruction, logistics, emotional support, career); the change agreed and whether it appears in the learner's next piece of work; the learner's unaided performance on the target; and whether the learner stays in the role or programme. "Supported", "dependent", "trusting" and "ready to work alone" are inferences.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| The mentee reports the mentoring helped, but their observed practice does not change | The meetings were about logistics or reassurance, not the practice; the mentor never saw the work; the target is too broad; the change needs longer than a term | Read the meeting notes for topics; check whether an observation preceded each debrief; look for the agreed change in the next lesson or draft. |
| The mentee's work improves while the mentor is present and falls back without them | The mentor is still doing part of the work (prompting, deciding); the support was never withdrawn; the improvement was in compliance, not in judgement | Observe one cycle with no prompts; ask the mentee to plan the next change themselves and see whether it is sound. |
| The mentee stops coming or says little in meetings | The mentor also evaluates them; the meeting time clashes with something they value; the match is poor; the mentee does not see what the meetings are for | Ask privately (or through someone else) whether meetings feel evaluative; offer a different mentor or time; restate the targets in the mentee's own terms. |
| A programme shows good retention but no change in practice or achievement | The relationship works as support and belonging rather than as instruction; the practice measure is not aligned with what was coached; selection (those who stayed would have stayed anyway) | Compare those assigned with those not assigned, if assignment was by lottery or rule; check whether the practice measure covers what meetings worked on. |
| Mentoring helps some learners and not others | Mentor quality differs; contact time differs (some never observed); learners differ in what they wanted from it | Tabulate outcomes by mentor and by contact actually received; ask learners what they hoped for at the start. |

## Evidence and qualifications

**Teachers coached on their practice: one meta-analysis of causal studies, plus later trials.** Kraft, Blazar and Hogan (2018) pooled 60 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of teacher coaching (pre-kindergarten to grade 12, mostly by a dedicated coach). All its findings below are one source; only the pooled instruction estimate is marked S, within the cap its recorded evidence allows (a `q4` synthesis, restated in a second report), and the rest are marked M at most.

- [Teacher coaching programs improve teachers' instructional practice by a pooled 0.49 SD across 43 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-instruction-effect-0-49-sd.md) [+S]: observed instruction, with a between-programme SD of 0.33, so programmes differ widely. It is the closest the wiki comes to testing this page's relationship, for one population and one outcome.
- [Teacher coaching raises student achievement on standardized tests by a pooled 0.18 SD across 31 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-achievement-effect-0-18-sd.md) [+M]: 0.12 SD on state tests; many achievement measures were aligned with the programmes.
- [Coaching effects on instruction and achievement are positively correlated, but large instructional improvements are needed for modest achievement gains](../claims/instruction-achievement-weak-link-coaching.md) [~M]: a between-study correlation of 0.37 across 20 studies, not significant.
- [Coaching program effects shrink substantially at scale: effectiveness trials of larger programs show only a fraction of efficacy-trial effects](../claims/coaching-scale-up-diminishing-effects.md) [~M], [Coaching dosage shows precisely estimated null effects: more coaching hours are not associated with larger program effects](../claims/coaching-dosage-null-effect.md) [~M], [Pairing coaching with group trainings is associated with larger effects on instruction and achievement; instructional materials with larger, and video libraries with smaller, effects on instruction](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [~M] and [Coaching effects do not differ significantly across school levels, and in-person versus virtual delivery shows no significant difference](../claims/coaching-no-school-level-or-mode-differences.md) [~M]: exploratory between-programme regressions; the authors say they are descriptive, not causal.
- [A teacher-coaching CAL program produces significant intent-to-treat gains in elementary and middle school math performance of 0.12-0.22 standard deviations](../claims/cal-teacher-coaching-itt-math-gains-012-022-sd.md) [+M]: two field experiments of coaching teachers to use computer-assisted learning; achievement, not practice.
- [Supervisory teacher coaching shows mixed effects, with six of sixteen reviewed studies reporting improved teacher results and four reporting improved student behaviors](../claims/supervisory-coaching-mixed-teacher-student-outcomes.md) [~M]: a review of 16 quasi-experimental and single-case studies, six meeting all quality indicators.

**New teachers: one randomised study against correlational surveys.** [Comprehensive teacher induction generated no significant improvements in novice teachers' classroom practices in the first year](../claims/comprehensive-induction-no-classroom-practice-gains-first-year.md) [-M]: Mathematica's randomised study of two comprehensive induction programmes (intensive mentoring with professional development) found no first-year improvement in practice, retention or achievement; the claim records two reports of the same study, so it counts as one. Against it, [Strong mentoring is linked to novice elementary teachers being 25 percentage points more likely to plan to remain in the same school, with a similar but non-significant pattern for high school teachers](../claims/strong-mentoring-25-point-retention-plan-elementary.md) [+W] and [Combining intensive mentoring with other supports is associated with the largest retention-plan gaps: 48 percentage points for elementary and 55 for high school novices](../claims/combined-mentoring-supports-largest-retention-gain.md) [~W] are one Chicago survey of plans, reported in a press release; teachers who got strong mentoring may differ in other ways. The review and policy statements ([Mentoring strongly influences new teachers' decisions to stay in or leave teaching (review attribution)](../claims/mentoring-influences-new-teacher-retention.md) [+W], [New teachers who receive little mentoring are twice as likely to leave the classroom as well-mentored beginners](../claims/mentoring-halves-new-teacher-attrition.md) [+W], [High-quality induction and mentoring programs are reported to reduce new-teacher attrition, and early-career teachers without coaching are reported twice as likely to leave](../claims/mentoring-coaching-reduce-teacher-attrition.md) [+W], [Well-prepared and well-mentored teachers stay in teaching at more than twice the rate of unprepared entrants, and teacher residency programs typically retain more than 80% over five years](../claims/teacher-preparation-mentoring-retention.md) [+W]) report earlier correlational research second-hand. In Missouri, [No statistically significant mentoring–retention relationships emerged in districts with any characteristics examined, and subgroup differences exceeding 10 percentage points showed no clear pattern](../claims/no-mentoring-retention-relationships-by-district-subgroups.md) [~W] (small subgroups). Together: the wiki holds a correlation between mentoring and staying, and one randomised test that did not find a first-year effect. The randomised study's later years are not recorded here.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a change in the learner's practice (observed teaching, a skill performed unaided) or continued participation (staying in teaching, re-enrolling), at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the mentee values: to survive the first year, to be seen as competent, to get a job or a promotion, to belong among colleagues, to have someone to ask. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A mentee who values being seen as competent may hide difficulties from a mentor who also evaluates them; one who values having someone to ask may want reassurance when the design offers observation and feedback.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if randomised comparisons of mentoring or coaching anchored in observed work show no advantage over unobserved advice or no relationship; if the teacher-induction null effect holds in later years and in other programmes; if student-coaching persistence gains disappear at scale as teacher-coaching effects shrink; or if software coaches match human ones on unaided performance, in which case the relationship is a delivery choice rather than the active ingredient. If role separation, matching or mentor preparation turn out to make no difference when tested, the default's steps 5–7 are costs without benefits. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "not real mentoring" after the fact.

Observed practice, the coached person's students' achievement, persistence, completion, earnings and the mentee's own valued goal are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no contact frequency, session length, mentor-to-mentee ratio or matching rule.

## Description
Mentoring and coaching are relationship-based supports in which a more experienced peer, instructor, or practitioner helps a learner interpret goals, practice skills, reflect on progress, and navigate challenge. The key difference from simple advice is that mentoring and coaching are developmental: they combine encouragement, modeling, questioning, feedback, and calibrated support over time. In learning settings, they often improve persistence and transfer because support is attached to real performance and real decisions rather than delivered only in the abstract.

## Implications
Mentoring and coaching are effective because they attach support to the learner’s actual decisions, performance, and context. Responsive questioning, modeling, and feedback can make tacit expert judgment visible and help learners progress toward more independent performance over time [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+S]. The main design risk is dependence or uneven quality: if the mentor becomes the solver of every problem, or if support varies wildly by relationship, the benefits become inconsistent. Strong mentoring and coaching therefore require clear purpose, regularity, and an explicit shift from help-giving toward learner judgment, especially when the work being coached is authentic enough to support later transfer beyond the relationship itself [A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A clear developmental focus**: The relationship should support skill growth, reflection, navigation, or identity development rather than only logistics.
- **Trust and continuity**: Mentoring and coaching are stronger when learners experience psychological safety and some regularity over time.
- **Calibrated support**: Effective mentors and coaches adjust prompts, examples, and feedback to the learner's current level [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].
- **A path toward independence**: Guidance should help the learner make better decisions and perform more independently over time [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+S].
#### Constraints
- **Overdependence**: If the mentor or coach becomes the constant problem-solver, learner agency can shrink.
- **Uneven quality**: Mentoring varies greatly depending on listening skill, modeling, and feedback quality.
- **Role confusion**: Coaching, supervision, friendship, and evaluation can blur unless expectations are explicit.
- **Access inequality**: Informal mentoring often benefits learners who already know how to seek support.

### Target Learners
- **Learners entering unfamiliar domains**: Strong fit when learners need help interpreting norms, expectations, and pathways.
- **Learners practicing complex applied skills**: Coaching is especially useful when performance includes judgment, timing, or tacit knowledge.
- **Learners needing confidence and belonging**: Mentoring can provide relational support alongside instruction.
- **Peer-supported learners**: Peer tutoring and peer coaching can be effective when roles and structure are clear.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Skill development through supported performance**: Applying feedback and modeling to improve competence.
- **Reflection and self-regulation**: Learning to plan, monitor, and adjust with guidance.
- **Navigation and persistence**: Understanding how to move through a program, role, or challenge more effectively.
- **Transfer to authentic practice**: Improving performance in real or realistic settings over time.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Apprenticeship and cognitive apprenticeship traditions — learners improve by working near more experienced others who make expert thinking visible.
- Social learning theory — observation, modeling, and guided participation support learning.
- Situated learning perspectives — mentoring is often strongest when tied to participation in authentic practices and communities.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Mentoring alone is not a substitute for coherent instruction, curriculum, or equitable structures.
- Relationship quality matters enough that poor mentoring can produce little benefit or even discourage learners.
