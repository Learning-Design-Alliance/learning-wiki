# Physical Activity

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 28 claims (17 for, 9 mixed, 2 against) · 25 studies (10 causal, 7 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 25 report an effect size · 22 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **movement placed in or around learning time to change the state learners bring to the next task**: a short bout of physical activity between seated segments, a physically active lesson, organised active play at recess, and exercise scheduled after a learning episode. For learners whose on-task behavior falls during long seated stretches (restless, drifting, slow to start the next segment), a brief bout of movement placed between segments, followed at once by a named task, is expected to increase on-task behavior in the segment that follows (`persistence`, measured as observed time on task). It is a readiness support, not instruction: it teaches no content by itself, and any learning benefit runs through better use of the time that follows.

**Two systematic reviews in the wiki test this for children in classrooms, and they bound it.** Classroom-based activity (movement breaks and physically active lessons) in primary-school children increased observed on-task behavior with a medium pooled effect, and in a registered review of single bouts with children aged 4 to 17, time on task improved after the bout in 9 of 11 interventions, depending on its length and intensity. Neither review found consistent gains on cognitive tests (executive function, attention tests) or academic performance, and one notes that the few studies with longer follow-up suggest the on-task gain may fade ([Classroom physical activity increases children's on-task behaviour, with no consistent gain on attention or other cognitive tests](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+S] for on-task behavior, [~S] for attention and achievement). A school study of a programme of 10-minute classroom activity breaks (Energizers) in third and fourth grade agrees and adds the condition on initial state: on-task behavior during the academic instruction that followed improved by 8% overall (ES = 0.60) and by 20% among the students who were least on task beforehand (ES = 2.20) ([Systematic implementation of Energizers improves on-task behavior…](../claims/energizers-improved-on-task-behavior.md) [+M], [The least on-task students show the largest improvement after Energizers activities…](../claims/energizers-least-on-task-students-largest-gain.md) [+M]; one study, a subgroup result). So the tested relationship is **movement → on-task behavior shortly afterwards, in school-age children, most for those who drift most**. That movement improves attention as measured by tests, achievement, or anything in adults during a lesson is not shown by any claim in this wiki.

A second, separate relationship concerns memory: **when** exercise happens relative to learning. In adult laboratory studies a bout of exercise during learning slightly hurt later memory, a bout before had a small non-significant effect, and a bout after learning helped, most clearly when it came some hours later ([The timing of acute exercise relative to learning modulates memory consolidation](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~S]). That model is owned by [Memory Consolidation](memory-consolidation.md); this page uses it only to decide where a bout goes, and does not repeat it.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's observed state before the bout (an already-engaged group has little on-task behavior to gain, and in the one study that looked, the least on-task students gained most), on the bout's length and intensity, on what follows it (a named task, not open time), on whether the movement competes with encoding of hard new material, and on the horizon (the evidence is about the minutes after a bout, not weeks).

How the neighbouring converted pages sit beside this one: [Mindfulness Activities](mindfulness-activities.md) owns the quiet settling routine at a transition; a movement break is the active alternative for the same moment, and the two can be offered as a choice. [Check-ins](check-ins.md) owns the short report of a learner's state and what the teacher does with it; a movement check-in ("show me your energy level") is a check-in, not this page. [Memory Consolidation](memory-consolidation.md) owns what happens between learning and the moment it is needed, including exercise timing. [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md) owns checking one's own work against a criterion; noticing one's own restlessness and choosing to move is where the two meet.

## Default design

The two reviews test movement against no movement for on-task behavior in school-age children; they do not set a schedule, and nothing in the wiki tests the design for adults. The steps below are a default a designer can act on and revise; each says whether a claim tests it or whether it is an untested proposal.

1. **Find the seated stretch where on-task behavior drops, and put the bout there.** Watch or ask where learners start drifting (commonly after 20 to 30 minutes of continuous seated work for primary children, longer for older learners; an untested proposal). Place the bout between two segments, not in the middle of an explanation. Basis: the reviews test bouts placed between or within lessons ([Classroom physical activity…](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+S]); the drift point is a local observation.
2. **Choose one of two doses.** About 5 minutes of vigorous movement (learners breathe hard: jumping, fast stepping in place, a short active game), or more than 10 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity (an active lesson segment, a brisk walk). Time on task improved after either kind in 9 of 11 interventions, in children aged 4 to 17 ([Classroom physical activity…](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+S]); a programme of 10-minute classroom breaks in grades 3 and 4 also raised on-task behavior in the lesson that followed and raised children's in-school step counts against classes without it ([Energizers improved on-task behavior…](../claims/energizers-improved-on-task-behavior.md) [+M], [Energizers increased in-school steps](../claims/energizers-increased-in-school-steps.md) [+M]; the step count is an activity outcome, not a learning one). Where the next task needs sustained attention, prefer the 5-minute vigorous bout: the same review reports attention-test gains after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after 10 to 12 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. Shorter bouts (1 to 3 minutes of stretching) are common in practice and are untested here.
3. **End the bout with a fixed transition and a named next task.** Use the same closing cue every time (a count-down, a breathing cycle, "feet still, eyes on the board"), and state the task that starts now. Untested as a routine; teacher reports from a randomized recess programme found transitions from recess into class less difficult in programme schools ([Playworks made transitions from recess to classroom learning less difficult, per teacher reports](../claims/playworks-easier-recess-to-classroom-transitions.md) [+M]), carried here from organised recess to classroom breaks.
4. **Do not put strenuous movement in the middle of learning hard new material.** In adult laboratory studies, exercise during encoding slightly hurt later memory ([Acute exercise timing…](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~S], adults, lab tasks; carried to classrooms untested). Place bouts after a segment of new material, not during it. Physically active lessons that make learners move to answer (stand for true, walk to the correct corner) suit review and practice of material already met better than a first explanation; that split is an untested proposal. On on-task behavior itself, a review reports that the 10-minute breaks raised time on task when done between lessons, while activity built into a classroom task in another study did not raise it (it did not lower it either) ([Classroom energizers improved on-task behaviour, but PA during a classroom task showed no improvement](../claims/energizers-on-task-behaviour-effect.md) [~M]; two observational school studies as one review reports them), which is a further reason to treat the stand-alone break, not the active lesson, as the default for on-task behavior.
5. **Put longer activity after demanding learning, not before it, when the goal is retention.** If the timetable allows a choice, schedule recess, PE or a walk after the lesson that introduced hard material rather than before it; the adult evidence favours activity after learning, most clearly some hours after ([Acute exercise timing…](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~S]). See [Memory Consolidation](memory-consolidation.md) for the model.
6. **Where the movement can carry meaning, map it onto the concept, then onto the symbol.** A gesture or whole-body enactment of a quantity or process (spreading fingers for wavelength, tilting arms for a slope, walking a timeline) gives a physical experience to link to the formal representation afterwards. A brief physical experience of angular momentum improved college physics quiz scores, but physical and virtual manipulation were equally effective in another study ([Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning](../claims/physical-experience-enhances-science-learning.md) [~M]); in a pilot, physics students reported that a pinch-and-spread gesture helped them "feel" wavelength and amplitude, a perception survey only ([AR pinch gesture pilot](../claims/ar-pinch-gesture-students-feel-wavelength-amplitude-pilot.md) [+W]). Always follow the movement with an explicit link to the diagram or formula.
7. **Offer options and a no-questions opt-out.** Give a seated or standing version of every move, a low-intensity option, and the choice to stretch at the desk instead. Untested proposal, from the earlier page (comfort, mobility, health and cultural expectations differ).
8. **Plan the arc across sessions, then keep only what shows a gain.** A default, all untested proposals: in the first two weeks the teacher leads one or two scripted 5-minute bouts per session at fixed points, and records for each the on-task behavior in the first 10 minutes after (a quick scan count every 2 minutes is enough), noting separately the few learners who are least on task before the bout, since they are where the one school study found the largest gain ([The least on-task students show the largest improvement…](../claims/energizers-least-on-task-students-largest-gain.md) [+M]). In weeks three and four, learners take turns leading from a menu of four to six moves, and the teacher adds a bout when the class shows the drift signs from step 1, not only at the fixed points. From week five, a learner or group that stays on task through the long segments can move to a self-chosen bout (stand, stretch, walk to the back) taken when they notice restlessness, rather than a whole-class one. Every four to six weeks compare on-task counts after bouts with those in segments without one, and drop or change bouts that show no difference, since one review reports the gain may fade with longer follow-up ([Classroom physical activity…](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [~S]).

When a condition is not met: if the group is already on task through long segments, a scheduled whole-class bout has little to gain; keep it optional. If the room or the learners' health rules out vigorous movement, use the longer moderate dose or desk-based movement and record whether on-task behavior changes. If the outcome that matters is test performance rather than behavior, do not expect the bout to deliver it.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learners do in the seated stretch now.** Movement is expected to help a group that drifts off task; for a group already on task the reviews give little to gain, and in one school study the least on-task students gained most. If unstated, ask: during a long seated segment, when do learners start to drift, and how many are off task by then?
- **What the outcome is.** On-task behavior soon after a bout is what the reviews support; test-measured attention, achievement and retention are not. If unstated, ask: what should be different after the break, how would you see it, and when?
- **Who can move, where, and how.** Age, mobility, health, space, dress and mode (in the room, remote, on a phone) decide which moves are possible and safe. If unstated, ask: how much space is there, are all learners able to stand and move, and is anyone joining remotely?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behavior under stated conditions: how many learners are on task in the minutes before and after a bout, how long the class takes to settle into the next task, who takes part and who opts out, and what learners say about how they feel. "Restored attention", "regulated" and "fatigued" are inferred states. A class that is quieter after a bout has shown that it is quieter, not that it is learning more.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| More on task in the 10 minutes after a bout, no change in quiz scores | The reviews' pattern: behavior changes, test performance does not; the quiz measures something the bout cannot reach; the gain is real but the quiz comes too late | Compare quiz scores on segments that followed a bout with segments that did not, at the same delay; check whether the quiz aligns with the segment's content. |
| Harder to settle after the bout than before | The bout was too long or too exciting for this group; no clear transition cue; the next task was not named | Run the same bout with a fixed closing cue and a named task, then a shorter bout, and count time to settle each way. |
| On-task gain in the first weeks, gone by week six | Novelty wore off; the routine became predictable and is done half-heartedly; the gain fades, as one review suggests | Change the moves or the leaders and see whether the gain returns; compare with segments without a bout. |
| The same few learners opt out every time | Physical discomfort or a health condition; self-consciousness; the moves do not suit them; they do not need it | Ask privately and offer a seated, upper-body or calm alternative; watch their on-task behavior in segments with and without the bout. |
| The class average barely moves, but the few learners who were least on task are much more on task after the bout | The pattern the one school study reports (largest gain for the least on-task); regression to the mean, since learners picked out on a low count tend to score higher next time anyway; the observer watched them more closely after the bout | Pick the learners to follow before the first bout, on several earlier counts rather than one, and compare their counts after bouts with their counts after segments with no bout. |
| Remote or phone learners do not return on time after the bout | They left the screen and lost the thread; the closing cue cannot be heard; the next task was not visible | Put the named task and the time to return on the screen during the bout, and note how many are back within a minute. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Observing a class also changes it, so record when and how each count was made, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Classroom physical activity increases children's on-task behaviour, with no consistent gain on attention or other cognitive tests](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+S] for on-task behavior, [~S] for attention and achievement: two systematic reviews (`classroom`, `child` and `adolescent`). Watson et al. (2017) pooled four studies of classroom-based activity in primary-school children: a medium positive effect on observed on-task and off-task behavior (SMD = 0.60, 95% CI 0.20–1.00), and no significant pooled effect on cognitive functions such as executive function (SMD = 0.33, CI crossing zero); most behavior studies reported an acute gain, the few with longer follow-up suggested it may fade, and the authors warn heterogeneity rules out firm conclusions. Daly-Smith et al. (2018), a registered review of 18 single-bout studies with children aged 4 to 17: time on task improved after the bout in 9 of 11 interventions, after longer moderate-to-vigorous bouts (more than 10 minutes) or shorter more intense 5-minute bouts; no consistent evidence of better cognition or academic performance; attention improved after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after 10–12 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. This is the only claim here that tests the page's relationship, and it supports behavior, not learning. **The claim's discussion speaks of effects on attention after 5–20 minutes of moderate activity and of larger benefits for younger learners and those with attention difficulties, which its two evidence entries do not report**; the page takes its doses from the entries only.
- [Systematic implementation of Energizers improves on-task behavior during academic instruction in third- and fourth-grade students](../claims/energizers-improved-on-task-behavior.md) [+M], with [The least on-task students show the largest improvement after Energizers activities, improving on-task behavior by 20%](../claims/energizers-least-on-task-students-largest-gain.md) [+M] and [A classroom-based physical activity program (Energizers) increases children's daily in-school physical activity compared with no program](../claims/energizers-increased-in-school-steps.md) [+M]: one study, Mahar et al. (2006), `classroom`, `child`. In a multiple-baseline design across classrooms (62 third- and fourth-grade students), on-task behavior during academic instruction after the 10-minute classroom activity breaks was 8% higher than before them (ES = 0.60), and 20% higher among the least on-task students (ES = 2.20; a subgroup selected on low baseline, so regression to the mean is a rival explanation). In a quasi-experimental comparison (243 students), programme classes took more in-school steps than classes without it (ES = 0.49). It confirms the reviews' behavioral finding in one programme, adds the initial-state condition, and measures no learning outcome.
- [Classroom energizers improved on-task behaviour, but PA during a classroom task showed no improvement](../claims/energizers-on-task-behaviour-effect.md) [~M]: a systematic review of acute activity and children's attention ([Janssen et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/2193-1801-3-410)) reporting Mahar et al. (2006) beside Grieco et al. (2009), in which activity built into classroom tasks did not raise time on task (and did not lower it, against an inactive lesson). Two observational school studies as the review reports them; it qualifies the active lesson, not the break.
- [The timing of acute exercise relative to learning modulates memory consolidation](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 25 experiments with healthy adults aged 18 to 84 found one bout during encoding slightly hurt episodic memory (d = −0.12), before encoding had a small non-significant effect (d = 0.11, helping young adults and hurting older ones), within 4 hours after learning helped (d = 0.47), and 4 or more hours after helped most (d = 1.05, two studies, wide interval); in one randomized three-group experiment, exercise 4 hours after learning improved 48-hour recall of picture-location pairs and exercise immediately after did not. Laboratory tasks with adults; it sets where a bout goes for retention, not whether a classroom break helps behavior.
- [Teachers in Playworks schools reported significantly better student behavior at recess and readiness for class](../claims/playworks-better-recess-behavior-and-class-readiness.md) [+M], with the narrower [Playworks made transitions from recess to classroom learning less difficult, per teacher reports](../claims/playworks-easier-recess-to-classroom-transitions.md) [+M] and [Students in Playworks schools reported better behavior and attention in class after sports, games, and play](../claims/playworks-students-reported-better-behavior-attention-after-play.md) [+M]: one randomized evaluation of a programme placing full-time coaches in schools to organise active recess play; teacher and student reports, no effect sizes printed. It bears on organised activity outside lessons and the transition back, not on classroom breaks, and the outcomes are reports of behavior and readiness, not learning. See [Playworks](../products/playworks.md).
- [Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning](../claims/physical-experience-enhances-science-learning.md) [~M]: a brief physical experience of the forces in angular momentum improved college physics quiz scores (lab experiments and a randomized classroom experiment; abstract only, no effect size); in another study of undergraduate heat-and-temperature concepts, physical, virtual and combined manipulation were equally effective and all beat traditional instruction. It supports enacting a concept, and qualifies it: what helped was manipulation, not physicality.
- [Exercise Increases Hippocampal Volume](../claims/exercise-increases-hippocampal-volume.md) [~S]: a year-long randomized trial in older adults found aerobic walking increased anterior hippocampal volume by about 2% against a declining stretching control; a meta-analysis of 14 controlled trials (737 participants) found no significant effect on total hippocampal volume and a positive effect only on the left hippocampus, largely by preventing decline. **It limits the page**: it is a slow structural pathway in mostly older adults, not a learning gain, and does not justify classroom breaks on brain-growth grounds.

Learners, outcomes and settings differ across these claims (primary children's time on task, one programme's step counts, adults' lab recall, teachers' reports of recess, college physics quizzes, older adults' brain volume), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a learning gain from classroom movement breaks at a delay.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective for a movement break is usually more of the next segment spent on task. Elicit separately what learners want from it: to stop feeling stiff or sleepy, to talk to a friend, to have fun, to avoid being watched while moving, or nothing at all. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the social part may come back louder, not calmer; one who dislikes moving in front of others may sit out the very routine meant for them.

For example, adults on a full-day compliance training may want the day to end on time and to stay comfortable, while the designer's objective is attention during the afternoon cases. A five-minute walking pair discussion of the next case serves both: it moves them, it does not lengthen the day, and it starts the next task. Record which one learners actually take up.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of segments with and without a bout, in comparable groups, show no difference in observed time on task, or if the gain disappears within a few weeks of routine use, as one review's longer follow-ups hint. If it holds for children but not for adolescents or adults in the same kind of comparison, restrict the page to children. If the larger gain for the least on-task learners does not appear when they are chosen in advance on several counts and compared with segments without a bout, drop the drifting-learners row's targeting and treat that result as regression to the mean. If classroom studies show movement during new-material segments does not hurt later recall, the advice to keep encoding still (taken from adult lab studies) should be dropped. If studies of active lessons show achievement gains that breaks do not, the page should separate the two. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "the wrong kind of movement" after the fact.

On-task behavior in the minutes after a bout, test-measured attention, achievement, delayed retention and well-being are separate claims. The present evidence supports the first for school-age children, and establishes no schedule, no number of bouts per day and no effect on learning.

## Description
Physical activity in learning contexts means intentionally incorporating movement, exercise, or embodied breaks to support attention, energy, regulation, and readiness to learn. This can range from short movement breaks to physically active tasks or longer routines that help learners recover from fatigue and re-engage. The instructional value is usually indirect: physical activity does not teach content by itself, but it can improve the conditions under which focus, mood, and persistence are more available.

## Implications
Physical activity is most useful as a readiness and regulation support rather than as content instruction by itself. Brief movement can help some learners reset attention, notice internal state, and return to demanding work with more control [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]. The instructional implication is not that movement is always better, but that it should be timed and framed to improve the next learning action. When movement is disconnected from task demands, inaccessible, or overly generalized, it becomes disruption rather than support.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A clear purpose**: Movement should support transition, energy regulation, alertness, or embodied learning rather than feeling random.
- **Feasible participation**: Activities need to be physically, socially, and spatially workable for the group.
- **Respect for variation**: Learners need options or modifications so participation is not exclusionary.
- **Connection to the learning flow**: The activity should support readiness for the next task or deepen the task itself.
#### Constraints
- **One-size-fits-all movement**: Learners differ in comfort, mobility, health, and cultural expectations around movement.
- **Lost instructional coherence**: Breaks can feel disruptive if they are not integrated intentionally.
- **Overclaiming**: Physical activity can support readiness and attention, but it is not a universal cognitive solution.
- **Accessibility and safety**: Activities require attention to environment, health needs, and opt-out paths.

### Target Learners
- **Learners dealing with fatigue or long sedentary sessions**: Strong fit when sustained sitting reduces alertness and engagement.
- **Learners who benefit from regulation support**: Movement can help some learners reset emotionally or cognitively.
- **Adult learners balancing stress and attentional fragmentation**: Brief movement can improve readiness for the next task.
- **Groups in embodied or applied learning contexts**: Physical activity can directly support tasks that involve gesture, movement, or spatial reasoning.

### Target Learning Objectives

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Benefits depend on duration, intensity, timing, and learner fit; more movement is not always better for every task.
- Physical activity should support learning rather than function as a mandatory performance unrelated to instructional goals.
