Flexible Grouping¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 37 claims (18 for, 16 mixed, 3 against) · 40 studies (13 causal, 11 quant-synthesis, 8 review, 3 qualitative, 3 theoretical, 2 associational), q1–q4 · 9 of 40 report an effect size · 26 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns who works with whom, for what purpose, for how long, and how that changes. In a class whose learners differ on the current task (readiness on this skill, an error pattern, interest, language, or the role they can take), and where those differences shift from task to task, forming temporary groups for a stated purpose from recent evidence, and re-forming them when the evidence changes, is expected to give more learners the support or challenge they currently need, more chances to explain and hear explanations, and better individually measured achievement (general-achievement, immediate-performance on the task grouped for) than either one fixed whole-class format or fixed groups set by a general ability label. Two kinds of group do different jobs: short similar-need groups (learners who show the same gap or are ready for the same extension) for targeted teaching, and mixed groups (different strengths, languages or readiness) for discussion, projects and peer explanation.

The relationship is conditional on four things: the grouping evidence is recent and specific to the task, not a general ranking; the groups actually change, so that membership does not become a status label (quiet tracking); each group has a purpose, a task that needs the group and explicit success criteria; and every grouping cycle ends with each learner's own unaided check, so that support does not become a fixed placement.

No claim in the wiki tests flexible grouping itself. None compares flexible with fixed ability groups, or regrouped small-group teaching with whole-class teaching. What the wiki does now hold is evidence on the comparator, fixed sorting by attainment, and it is more mixed than the earlier model assumed. Besides a review's general statement, A narrative review reports that the literature generally finds attainment grouping (streaming) harms learning outcomes, while noting the research is contested [~W], one research programme (Nomi, Allensworth and colleagues at the University of Chicago Consortium, several reports on one policy) studied Chicago's 2003 double-dose algebra policy, which gave ninth graders below the national median a second algebra period and, as a side effect, led schools to sort algebra classes by entering skill (The double-dose policy led schools to track algebra classes by students' entering math skills [~M]). Its findings, read against this model:

  • Sorting's effect depends on the outcome and on where a learner sits in the class. Test scores rose under sorting, mainly for high-skilled students, while low-skilled students' scores were slightly lower (Average test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skill, driven by large learning gains for high-skilled students and slightly lower scores for low-skilled students [~M]); but grades and pass rates moved the other way: high-skilled students' grades and pass rates declined and low-skilled students' grades improved (Sorting by ability affects grades and pass rates differently from test scores: high-skilled students' grades and pass rates decline while low-skilled students' grades improve [~M]). The mechanism the authors report is relative standing: a student well below the class average was less likely to pass and one above it more likely (Fish pond effects: above-norm students became more likely to fail algebra when their skills were low relative to classroom peers after sorting [~M]). So "fixed groups are worse" holds for some outcomes and some learners, not across the board.
  • Added instruction, not composition, carried the low-attainers' gains. Below-norm students' test scores improved even though their classmates were now lower-skilled (Below-norm students' algebra test scores improved despite having lower-skill peers post-policy, consistent with additional supports offsetting compositional decline [+M]), with the second period, more interactive teaching and more academic demand doing the work (Double-dose algebra substantially increased interactive pedagogy for low-skill students (.55 SD, p<.001), unexplained by classroom compositional changes [+M]).
  • Mixed classes whose weaker members got extra support helped the stronger members too. Above-norm students whose algebra class included classmates taking the support course gained more on the test than those without such classmates (Above-norm students in heterogeneous classrooms with supported below-norm classmates gained .4 additional test score points post-policy [+M]).
  • Sorting concentrated disruption in the low classes. (Tracking intensified concentration of behavioral and absentee problems in double-dose algebra classes, explained by declines in peer ability [-M].)

None of this is flexible grouping: the groups were whole classes, fixed for a year, in one district's ninth-grade algebra, and every one of these claims rests on the same research programme, so they count as one line of evidence, not several. What they change in the model: the advantage expected of flexible grouping is located in three things fixed sorting could not do together, namely adding targeted instruction for those who need it, keeping a mixed core class whose members benefit from each other, and avoiding judgements and climates set by a learner's rank within a fixed group. On individual test scores alone, the model no longer assumes that fixed sorting is worse for high attainers. The two studies in this page's Key Sources (Burris et al. 2006 on heterogeneous grouping in mathematics; Castle et al. 2005 on flexible grouping in a high-needs school) have no claim page yet. The claims below test neighbouring parts: support adjusted to each learner's responses (one-to-one), using formative evidence to adapt teaching, group rewards resting on each member's learning, learning by explaining and teaching, explicit goals, and fading support. The rest is a default design, labelled as proposals. The evidence on composition (similar-need versus mixed) recorded here is opinion and observation only.

How the converted siblings sit around this: Cooperative Learning and Social Interdependence hold how a mixed group's work is structured (interdependence and individual accountability) and what that does; this page uses that structure for its mixed groups and does not repeat it. Collaborative Learning holds when joint work of any structure changes what each member can do. Adaptive Learning holds the general relationship of choosing each learner's next task from their responses; flexible grouping is a group-level form of it, run by a teacher, where the unit of adaptation is a small group rather than one learner. Scaffolding and Fading holds how support inside a group is raised and withdrawn.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (purpose for each grouping, ongoing formative evidence, low-stigma movement, tasks that need the group, independent performance kept), kept as a concrete default and extended into an arc across sessions. Each step says what evidence, if any, stands behind it; numbers are untested proposals unless a claim is named.

  1. Name the purpose of each grouping before forming it. One of: targeted support on a shown gap, extension for those already secure, discussion of a shared question, practice, peer teaching, or an interest or project group. Tell learners the purpose and the success criteria for the session ("by the end, each of you can solve two of these without the worked example"). Explicit, specific criteria: Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [+M] (a narrative review of goal-setting research, mostly adults at work; carried to group tasks). The purpose list is from the earlier page; untested.
  2. Form similar-need groups from a recent, task-specific check, not from a general label. Use a short check on the skill in hand (an exit ticket or three to five items, from the last one or two sessions) and sort by what the responses show (the same error, the same missing step, ready to go further). Never form them from last year's placement, a reading level used across all subjects, or a diagnosis. Using elicited evidence to adapt teaching: Assessment for learning improves achievement [+M] (K-12 meta-analyses of formative assessment, small average effects; they do not test grouping as the adaptation). The check size and age are untested proposals.
  3. Run a similar-need group short and contingent. Three to six learners, 10 to 20 minutes, one gap per session; the teacher watches each attempt and gives more help after a failure and less after a success. Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] (fourth and fifth graders, one-to-one tutoring of long division; the claim's own Discussion says genuine contingency is scarce with 30 students at once, which is the case for teaching a few at a time). Where the timetable allows, make the support session added time (a support block, an extra period, time while others do independent practice they can check) rather than time taken from the mixed core lesson: in the Chicago double-dose algebra policy, low attainers' test gains came with a second period and more interactive teaching, not from who their classmates were (Below-norm students' algebra test scores improved despite having lower-skill peers post-policy, consistent with additional supports offsetting compositional decline [+M]; Double-dose algebra improved algebra test scores for targeted students just below the cutoff, with a larger post-policy gain than for students just above the cutoff [+M]; a whole extra course for a year in ninth-grade algebra, carried to short sessions). Group size and length are untested proposals.
  4. Make mixed groups depend on every member's learning. Three or four learners, distinct roles that rotate, a shared product plus a short individual check, and any group recognition computed from members' individual results, not from the product alone. In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards [+M] (elementary and secondary studies of four weeks or more; Slavin also reports structured-interaction methods that work without rewards). The structure itself is modelled in Social Interdependence.
  5. Make explaining the work of a mixed group. Ask each member to explain a step, a reason or a prediction to another member, and rotate who explains, so the learner who already knows the answer explains instead of doing the task for others, and the learner who does not is asked to explain back. Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+M] (meta-analyses: preparing to teach and then teaching improved the tutor's own knowledge; school tutoring programmes benefited tutors as well as tutees); Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+M] (prompted self-explanation, mostly individual study; carried to explaining to a peer, which it did not test).
  6. Dissolve a support group as soon as its members succeed unaided. A learner leaves a similar-need group after two consecutive unaided successes on the target (or a passed recheck), and goes to independent, mixed or extension work; do not keep learners who are already secure in a support group. Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [~M] (a scaffolding review and three experiments fading worked examples, near transfer; carried from fading within a task to leaving a group); Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M] (cognitive-load studies; gives no crossover point). The two-success rule is an untested proposal.
  7. Keep movement low-stigma and keep a record that would show tracking. Name groups by task or colour, never by level; let any learner join a support session by choice; vary formats so that no group meets only "the strugglers". Log each session's groups and the reason for each placement. Review the log every two weeks: if the same learners have been in the support group for three cycles in a row, or groups split along language, ethnicity, disability or gender, change the instruction or the grouping rather than the label. Watch the climate of any group that collects the lowest attainers: when Chicago's algebra classes were sorted by skill, absences and disciplinary problems concentrated in the low classes (Tracking intensified concentration of behavioral and absentee problems in double-dose algebra classes, explained by declines in peer ability [-M]; When classes are sorted by skill level, low-skilled students are at higher risk of being in disruptive classrooms, weakening their instructional environment [-M]; whole classes fixed for a year, not short sessions). A teacher's account of why random grouping appealed (it "disrupted the institutional process of labeling kids") is in Contrasting workshop practices (random vs. purposeful grouping) framed the problem of labeling students around local context and teacher agency [~W] (one qualitative case of teachers' talk; no learner outcomes). The review rule is an untested proposal.
  8. Assign groups when mixing is the purpose; let learners choose when interest is. When the purpose is to bring together different languages or backgrounds, the teacher assigns groups, because self-chosen groups tend to sort by similarity. Students segregated into language cliques whenever allowed to choose peer interactions, despite the 50-50 enrollment design [~W] (classroom observation in one dual-language school). Interest groups and project choices can be learner-chosen; untested.
  9. End every grouping cycle with individual work. Each session or task that used groups ends with a short individual check, and the week keeps time for independent work, so that no learner's record rests on group output. Judge that check against the stated criteria, not against the rest of the group: under Chicago's skill sorting, grades and pass rates followed a student's standing relative to classmates, so students who became the weakest in a stronger class were graded down although their test scores rose (Fish pond effects: above-norm students became more likely to fail algebra when their skills were low relative to classroom peers after sorting [~M]; Sorting by ability affects grades and pass rates differently from test scores: high-skilled students' grades and pass rates decline while low-skilled students' grades improve [~M]). Cooperative learning has drawbacks including slow learners depending on fast achievers, so teachers should stress both cooperative and individual work [~W] (an opinion essay, no data); individual accountability as above [+M].
  10. Plan the arc across sessions, not only one lesson. A default, all untested proposals:
    • Weeks 1–2 (routines). Whole-class lesson most of the time, plus one grouping format per lesson. Teach the moves explicitly: how to form a group in under two minutes, the role cards, the signal to regroup, what to do when finished. Form one similar-need group per lesson from the previous day's check; mixed groups use fixed roles the teacher assigns.
    • Weeks 3–6 (rotation). A typical 50–60 minute lesson: whole-class model or mini-lesson 10–15 minutes; groups 20–25 minutes (one similar-need group with the teacher while mixed groups practise or discuss with roles); individual check 5–10 minutes, which forms tomorrow's groups. Mixed groups keep membership for one unit or two to three weeks; similar-need groups last one to three sessions.
    • Weeks 7 onwards (handover). Learners read their own check results against the criteria and sign up for a support or extension session themselves, with the teacher adding anyone the check flags; interest and project groups become learner-chosen within the teacher's mixing rules; roles rotate without prompting.
    • A learner moves on (out of a support group, into extension) on two consecutive unaided successes; a whole-class reteach replaces small groups when more than about half the class shows the same gap.

When a condition is not met: if there is no recent task-specific evidence, run a short check before grouping or keep the lesson whole-class for that segment; if routines are weak and regrouping costs more than five minutes, use fewer, longer-lived groups until the moves are taught.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the grouping is for, and what evidence it rests on. Targeted support needs similar-need groups formed from a recent check; discussion and projects need mixed groups with interdependence; neither should be formed from a general ranking. If unstated, ask: what should each group be able to do at the end of the session, and what did the last check on this task show?
  • How much facilitator attention there is. The evidence for responsive support is one-to-one; with one teacher and many groups, or a volunteer for a few minutes, the support has to come from roles, materials, peers and a short individual check. If unstated, ask: how many adults, for how many minutes, and who sees each learner's work before the next session?
  • How the groups could become labels. Fixed or visible "levels", self-chosen groups in a class split by language, and support groups that never empty all turn grouping into tracking. If unstated, ask: who decides membership, how often does it change, and what do learners and families see?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: the check items and each learner's responses, who was placed in which group and why, how long each group ran, the roles taken, the talk (who explained, who asked, who was silent), each learner's individual check afterwards, and the group history over weeks. "Needs support", "is ready for extension", "is carried by the group" and "feels labelled" are inferences. A group product is an observation about the group, not about any member.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares flexible grouping with fixed ability grouping or with whole-class teaching. Fixed sorting of whole classes by attainment, with achievement measured, is now recorded (one research programme, below); the other claims test neighbouring parts.

  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders tutored one-to-one in long division under fully contingent support solved more problems than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition, a quasi-experiment); interactive tutoring of eighth graders gave similar immediate learning and better transfer; a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding. Its Discussion notes most evidence is one-to-one and contingency is scarce with whole classes. It supports teaching a few learners at a time when the aim is responsive support; it does not show that a similar-need group of five gets the one-to-one benefit.
  • Assessment for learning improves achievement [+M]: K-12 meta-analyses of formative assessment with control conditions find small positive effects (d = .29 across 33 US studies; about .20 across 13 studies, after a published correction), larger where students self-assess. It supports forming groups from elicited evidence; it does not isolate regrouping as the adaptation, and the feature estimates are comparisons across studies.
  • In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards [+M]: a best-evidence review of 99 school studies of four weeks or more (median +.32 with group rewards on individual learning against +.07 without), an elementary peer-assisted-learning meta-analysis (d = 0.33, larger with interdependent rewards), and a randomised component study with at-risk 4th and 5th graders. It governs how mixed groups are run; it does not test how they are composed or how often they change.
  • Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+M]: across 28 studies, preparing to teach and then teaching improved the tutor's domain knowledge against studying without that expectation (g = 0.56; preparing alone g = 0.35), more when the teaching was interactive; school tutoring programmes benefited tutors as well as tutees. Abstracts only. It answers the worry that higher-attaining members lose out in mixed groups, when they explain; it does not compare tutor and tutee gains.
  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+M]: a meta-analysis of 64 reports (g = .55) and a mathematics meta-analysis, both of prompted self-explanation, mostly immediate tests. The earlier page cited it for peer explanation; it tests explaining to oneself, so it is carried, and the marker is lowered from the earlier [+S] for that reason.
  • Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [+M]: one narrative review of goal-setting research. The earlier page marked it [+S]; one study at q2 caps it at M. It supports explicit success criteria for each group; it is not a study of groups or of learners in classrooms.
  • Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [~M]: a scaffolding review and three experiments in which fading worked examples into problems favoured near transfer. It is the reason a support group should end; it concerns support within a task, not membership of a group, and gives no timing.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies and an experiment with electrical trainees. It is the reason not to hold secure learners in support groups; it gives no crossover point.

Fixed sorting by attainment: one research programme on one policy. Chicago Public Schools' 2003 double-dose algebra policy, studied by Nomi, Allensworth and colleagues at the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research in a regression discontinuity combined with cohort comparisons (ninth graders in 55 schools; the reports are coded q2–q3, causal, i? except where noted). The several reports and the many claim pages drawn from them are one line of evidence, so no marker here goes above M. The groups were whole classes for a year, not flexible groups.

  • Double-dose algebra improved algebra test scores for targeted students just below the cutoff, with a larger post-policy gain than for students just above the cutoff [+M]: the one claim in the family with a printed effect size (.26 below the cutoff against .15 above). It is the effect of a second algebra period, not of grouping.
  • Below-norm students' algebra test scores improved despite having lower-skill peers post-policy, consistent with additional supports offsetting compositional decline [+M] and Double-dose algebra substantially increased interactive pedagogy for low-skill students (.55 SD, p<.001), unexplained by classroom compositional changes [+M]: the low attainers' gains came with added time and changed teaching, and their scores rose even though their classmates were weaker. This is the basis for making support sessions added instruction (step 3).
  • Average test scores are higher when classes are sorted by skill, driven by large learning gains for high-skilled students and slightly lower scores for low-skilled students [~M]: the report's summary; it limits the earlier model's assumption that fixed groups are simply worse. No effect size is printed.
  • Sorting by ability affects grades and pass rates differently from test scores: high-skilled students' grades and pass rates decline while low-skilled students' grades improve [~M], Fish pond effects: above-norm students became more likely to fail algebra when their skills were low relative to classroom peers after sorting [~M] and Policy effects on grades and failure rates differed by incoming ability: most below-norm students' grades improved slightly, but the very lowest-ability students' algebra grades declined [~M]: grades and passing followed rank within the class. This is the basis for judging individual checks against criteria (step 9).
  • Above-norm students in heterogeneous classrooms with supported below-norm classmates gained .4 additional test score points post-policy [+M] and In one Chicago study, above-norm students' post-policy algebra test gains were partly explained by higher peer ability and by having classmates who took support algebra [+M]: the same spillover finding in two reports. Peer ability also explained part of the high attainers' gain, so both composition and the support classmates received mattered to them. It is the closest the wiki comes to this page's configuration (a mixed core class plus added targeted support), at the scale of a course, not of in-class groups.
  • Tracking intensified concentration of behavioral and absentee problems in double-dose algebra classes, explained by declines in peer ability [-M] and When classes are sorted by skill level, low-skilled students are at higher risk of being in disruptive classrooms, weakening their instructional environment [-M]: the climate cost of a fixed low group (step 7).
  • The double-dose policy led schools to track algebra classes by students' entering math skills [~M]: the sorting was a side effect of a support policy, which is how quiet tracking arises.

Outside that programme the wiki holds only second-hand statements: A narrative review reports that the literature generally finds attainment grouping (streaming) harms learning outcomes, while noting the research is contested [~W] (a narrative review's summary, q2, no effect size; the Chicago findings qualify it by outcome and by learner), A practice-framework article reports, citing Boaler and Staples (2008), that students in heterogeneous mathematics classrooms learn more, enjoy mathematics more and reach higher understanding than in streamed settings [+W] (the same review citing one earlier study) and Grade retention and tracking students into remedial instruction undermine achievement, while formative assessment with acceleration strategies improves learning [+W] (a policy report's summary of research).

Limiting and qualifying claims, all weak. On composition and labelling the wiki holds only opinion and observation, and they point in different directions:

  • Heterogeneously grouped teams show more benefits than homogeneously formed teams [+W]: one sentence in an introductory chapter citing earlier research; no study details (q1).
  • Homogeneous grouping for reading instruction and heterogeneous grouping for discussion groups each serve distinct purposes [~W]: an essay arguing from experience for both plans, against all-day heterogeneous grouping (q1). It matches this page's split between similar-need and mixed groups, and is no evidence for it.
  • Cooperative learning has drawbacks including slow learners depending on fast achievers, so teachers should stress both cooperative and individual work [~W]: the same essay; the reason the default design ends every cycle with individual work.
  • Students segregated into language cliques whenever allowed to choose peer interactions, despite the 50-50 enrollment design [~W]: observations in one dual-language school (q2, qualitative). It limits learner-chosen grouping when mixing is the goal.
  • Contrasting workshop practices (random vs. purposeful grouping) framed the problem of labeling students around local context and teacher agency [~W]: teachers' talk in one professional-development debrief, where one valued random grouping for disrupting the labelling of students amid unofficial tracking. It names the tracking risk; it measures no learner outcome.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these (one-to-one tutoring of children, K-12 syntheses, one district's ninth-grade algebra, undergraduates and adults in reviews), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures belonging, motivation or stigma under different grouping policies, which the earlier page named as goals; the Chicago programme measures grades, pass rates, test scores and classroom climate.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually individual unaided performance on the task grouped for, at a stated horizon, and in many settings also participation of every learner in shared work. Elicit separately what learners value: to work with friends, not to be seen as needing help, to get to the harder task, to use their own language, to finish quickly. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being seen in "the help group" may refuse support they need; one who values working with friends may prefer self-chosen groups that defeat a mixing goal.

For example, an adult learner in an English class at work may want to handle phone calls with customers, while the designer's objective for the week is past-tense forms. A similar-need group on past-tense errors serves the objective; a mixed group role-playing a customer call, with that learner in the caller role, tests what the learner came for. Record both, so that a passed grammar check is not read as the capability the learner values.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of regrouped small-group teaching with whole-class teaching, with comparable learners and individual outcomes, show no advantage; if flexible grouping in practice converges on stable groups that behave like ability tracks (the same learners in the same groups across a term), in which case "flexible" is a description of intent only; or if mixed groups with explaining roles show losses for higher-attaining learners on individual measures. If similar-need groups help only when the teacher can give near one-to-one attention, the similar-need step is a staffing decision, not a grouping one. If short, task-specific similar-need groups turn out to show the same relative-standing effects on grades, or the same concentration of disruption, that whole-class sorting showed in Chicago, then "temporary" does not remove those costs and the low-stigma steps carry more weight than the model gives them; if a mixed core class with added support does not reproduce the Chicago spillover outside ninth-grade algebra, the default's preference for it rests on one district. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not flexible enough" after the fact.

Achievement on the grouped task, delayed retention, participation, belonging and the learner's own valued goal are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no group size, duration, regrouping interval or composition rule.
