# Holistic Learning

## Description
Holistic learning is the principle of helping learners grasp the overall structure, purpose, or whole of a domain before becoming lost in isolated details. It is useful when understanding depends on seeing how parts relate within a larger conceptual system.

## Implications

Holistic learning matters when learners are likely to lose meaning by encountering details before they have a coherent sense of the larger structure. The principle does not reject detail or analysis. It argues that details are more learnable when learners can place them inside a meaningful whole, because organizing parts into a larger structure can reduce memory burden and improve coherence [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S]. In practice, holistic designs often start with an overview, model, framework, or authentic whole task and then elaborate parts without severing them from the larger purpose [[claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer]] [+S]. The main design risk is vagueness: a “big picture” that is too abstract to guide later understanding does not help much, and some novices still need staged simplification before the whole becomes usable [[claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices]] [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A coherent whole or organizing framework to present** — learners need a visible structure, process, or conceptual map that shows how details will fit together
- **Connections between the overview and later detail** — the whole must continue to matter as instruction elaborates parts
#### Constraints
- **Too much abstraction too early can feel empty without later grounding**
- **Not all learners benefit from the same level of overview first** — some may still need local examples or concrete anchors before the whole becomes meaningful
- **Holistic design can become rhetorical rather than instructional** — saying “here is the big picture” is weaker than giving learners a structure they can actually use

### Target Learners
- Learners studying conceptually dense or highly interconnected material
- Novices who need help orienting to the logic of a domain before working on parts
- Learners at risk of fragmenting knowledge into disconnected facts or procedures

### Target Learning Objectives
- Build big-picture understanding and integrative mental models
- Help learners connect parts, purposes, and relationships across a domain
- Improve transfer by preserving the coherence of the whole while learning the parts

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Sequencing and schema-building traditions — learners often understand details better when they can place them into a larger structure
- [[principles/cognitive-load-management|Cognitive Load Management]] — a useful overview can reduce disorientation and help learners organize incoming detail
- [[theories/information-processing-theory|Information Processing Theory]] — organized, meaningful structures improve encoding and later retrieval

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [[claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices]] [~M] — very complex wholes may still need simplification or staged entry for absolute novices

### Claims
- [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [+S] — holistic structures can reduce memory burden when they help learners organize parts into meaningful units
- [[claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer]] [+S] — seeing and practicing the whole can improve transfer in complex domains
- [[claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices]] [~M] — wholes still need calibration when the initial complexity is too high

## Related Principles
- [[principles/creating-visual-representations|Creating Visual Representations]] — diagrams and maps often make the whole visible
- [[principles/scaffolding|Scaffolding]] — holistic designs still need supports so learners can move from overview to workable understanding
- [[principles/knowledge-organization|Knowledge Organization]] — holistic learning depends on preserving meaningful conceptual structure

## Examples

### Illustrative

**[[patterns/reigeluths-elaboration-theory|Reigeluth's Elaboration Theory]]** — Learners begin with an epitome or simple whole, then elaborate progressively while keeping the larger structure in view.

**Whole-task previews before subskill focus** — In writing, programming, or design, learners first see the complete workflow or product before analyzing individual components.

**Concept maps that return throughout a unit** — The same framework is revisited as new detail is introduced, helping learners keep parts integrated into the whole.
