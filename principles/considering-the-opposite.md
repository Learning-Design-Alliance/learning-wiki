# Considering The Opposite

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 13 claims (6 for, 7 mixed) · 21 studies (7 causal, 6 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 5 of 21 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one move at one moment: **after a learner has committed to a judgment** (a diagnosis, a prediction, an interpretation of a source, a verdict on a design or an argument) on a task where the evidence could point more than one way, **prompting them to generate specific reasons why the opposite, or a named alternative, could be right**, and then to decide again, is expected to reduce confirmation bias, anchoring and overconfidence in that judgment more than a general instruction to "be objective". The knowledge type is mainly `principle` (causal explanations, diagnoses, interpretations) and `metacognitive-strategy` (checking one's own judgment); the outcomes are the quality of the revised judgment, `assessment-accuracy` of the learner's confidence, and, where the alternative was wrong, `conceptual-understanding` of why. Whether the habit transfers to judgments made without the prompt, or lasts, is a separate question that nothing here answers.

**No claim in this wiki tests the relationship directly.** The one claim about the strategy, [A systematic review of bias-mitigation training names considering the opposite as a previously validated way to reduce judgment biases, without testing it itself](../claims/considering-the-opposite-reduces-bias.md) [+M], rests on a systematic review (12 studies, 17 experiments) that names "consider the opposite" as an established debiasing method and cites primary experiments on hindsight bias and anchoring, but does not test it in its own data; the primary experiments (Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984; Mussweiler, Strack & Pfeiffer 2000, in Key Sources) are not recorded as evidence in the wiki. The rest of the evidence below is carried over from neighbouring relationships: comparing contrasting cases, conflict-based instruction for misconceptions, refutation text and argumentation. Those test instruction in which learners meet or produce an opposing case, not the self-generated reversal at the moment of judgment.

The relationship is conditional on four things: that the learner has actually formed a position (the move has nothing to act on otherwise); that the learner knows enough of the domain to produce a plausible alternative, which a `novice` often cannot, so the alternative may have to be supplied; that evidence bearing on both sides is available to them; and that the question is not a personal conviction the learner is unlikely to examine on request ([Critical disposition is least spontaneous when confronting one's most basic prejudices or deeply held convictions](../claims/critical-temper-weakest-on-basic-prejudices.md) [~W], a theoretical argument only).

**How this sits beside converted pages.** [Cognitive Disequilibrium](cognitive-disequilibrium.md) holds the general model of confronting a learner's explanation with evidence it cannot account for and then supporting the resolution; this page is the self-directed, judgment-stage version of that move, and does not repeat its resolution policy. [Evaluating Sources](evaluating-sources.md) holds the trust-a-source judgment, where checking what others say about a source is a specific way of looking for the disconfirming case. The [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) and [Structured Academic Controversy](../patterns/structured-academic-controversy.md) patterns build arguing the other side into a group sequence (SAC's position reversal resembles this move); use them when the opposite is to be argued by a group rather than considered by one learner. [Reflection](reflection.md) and [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md) hold the wider habits this prompt is one instance of.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default. Every step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Commit first, in writing, with a confidence rating.** Before any prompt, each learner records their judgment and how sure they are (for example 50–100%). The commitment gives the prompt something to act on and the rating gives a before-and-after observation. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("the moment of commitment is exactly when bias is strongest").
2. **Ask for reasons for the opposite, not for open-mindedness.** The prompt is specific: "Give two or three reasons the other diagnosis (prediction, reading) could be right", or "Suppose you turn out to be wrong: what is the most likely reason?" Do not substitute "be objective" or "consider other views". Basis: [A systematic review of bias-mitigation training names considering the opposite as a previously validated way to reduce judgment biases, without testing it itself](../claims/considering-the-opposite-reduces-bias.md) [+M] (second-hand: a review naming the method; it contrasts it with generic warnings, which it reports fail because people believe they are already objective).
3. **Supply the alternative when the learner cannot produce one.** If the learner's alternatives are absent, implausible or straw men, give the alternative (a second case, a rival explanation, a statement of the common misconception) and ask them to make the best case for it, then compare it side by side with their own. Basis, carried from neighbouring relationships: [comparing cases side by side](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M] (meta-analysis of 57 experiments, d = .50, against studying cases singly or in sequence; larger when learners look for similarities and when the principle comes after the comparison) and [refutation text and conflict interventions](../claims/misconceptions-interfere-with-new-learning.md) [+M] (science and reading-education syntheses, read from abstracts). Neither tests a learner-generated opposite.
4. **Check the alternative against evidence, then decide again.** Give the learner the evidence that discriminates the two (a test result, a source, an observation, the outcome of the prediction) and ask for a final judgment and a second confidence rating, with one sentence on what changed or why it did not. Untested as a step here; from the earlier page ("time and structure for genuine evaluation of the alternative, not a token gesture"). Conflict alone may not be enough: in one experiment staged contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused ([conflict-based instruction](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [~M]), so the resolution step is the part not to cut.
5. **Treat a changed mind as a good outcome.** Say so before the first use, and in feedback credit the quality of the reasons on both sides, not whether the first answer survived. Untested proposal; from the earlier page.
6. **Bound it.** One alternative and a fixed time per judgment (about 3–5 minutes for a single judgment in class; one line in a fast-moving task), then commit. Use it on judgments that are uncertain or consequential, not on every step, so it does not become second-guessing. Untested proposal; the earlier page warned of decision paralysis.
7. **Plan the arc across sessions, and fade the prompt.** A default, untested: *sessions 1–2*: the teacher models the move aloud on one judgment, and the alternative is supplied for learners to argue for; *sessions 3–5*: learners generate the alternative themselves from a prompt card, and a partner or the teacher checks that it is plausible; *sessions 6 and after*: the card is removed, and the teacher records whether learners state an alternative unprompted before committing. A learner moves on from supplied to self-generated alternatives once they give at least two domain-relevant reasons for the alternative on two consecutive cases, and from prompted to unprompted once they do so without the card on two consecutive cases. These thresholds are untested proposals. How to withdraw the support is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).

When a condition is not met: if no position has been formed, ask for one first; if there is only one defensible answer reached by a set method, this move has nothing to work on, so contrast two worked methods instead (row below); if no discriminating evidence is available, stop at step 3 and record the judgment as open rather than resolved.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the learner can produce a plausible alternative.** A `novice` in the domain often cannot, and a supplied alternative or contrasting case does the work; an `advanced` learner or professional needs only the prompt. If unstated, ask: when this learner is wrong on a task like this, what is the usual alternative, and could they name it themselves?
- **What the judgment is about.** An open diagnostic, predictive or interpretive judgment suits the move; a procedure with one correct method does not; a personal or identity-linked conviction needs a different, more private design. If unstated, ask: is there a defensible alternative answer here, and would asking for it touch something the learner holds personally?
- **What counts as success.** A better final judgment, better-calibrated confidence, a written counterargument, and a changed explanation of a phenomenon are different targets with different checks. If unstated, ask: after the activity, what should the learner be able to do or decide, and how will you see it?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: the committed judgment and its confidence rating; the alternative the learner wrote (how many reasons, whether they are plausible and use domain evidence); the final judgment and confidence; and what the learner said changed. "Debiased", "open-minded" and "has the habit" are inferred states. A changed answer is not by itself a better one, and an unchanged answer is not by itself bias.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Writes a weak or straw-man alternative | Lacks the domain knowledge to build one; protecting the first answer; does not see why the task matters | Supply a strong alternative and ask for its best case; if this produces good reasons, knowledge was the gap. Ask what would have to be true for the alternative to be right. |
| Changes the answer after every prompt | Low confidence or wish to please; the prompt read as a signal that the first answer was wrong; genuine uncertainty | Use the prompt on a judgment that was right too, and compare; ask for confidence before and after. |
| Never changes the answer, high confidence throughout | Answers were right; anchoring; the question touches a conviction | Score the first judgments against outcomes; offer a low-stakes question of the same form. |
| Confidence falls but accuracy does not change | Calibration improved; the prompt induced general doubt | Track calibration across several judgments, not one. |
| Uses the move with the card, not without it | Not yet a habit; the unprompted task does not look like one that calls for it | Give an unprompted judgment of the same kind, then one of a different kind, and record whether an alternative appears. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe is itself a prompt and changes what it measures, so record each one.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests a considering-the-opposite prompt against a generic warning or no prompt, in any learning setting. The claims below test the claim's second-hand basis and the neighbouring relationships it borrows from.

- [A systematic review of bias-mitigation training names considering the opposite as a previously validated way to reduce judgment biases, without testing it itself](../claims/considering-the-opposite-reduces-bias.md) [+M]: a systematic review of cognitive-bias-mitigation training (Korteling et al. 2021; 12 studies, 17 experiments, mostly serious games) names "consider the opposite" in its background as a previously validated method, citing primary studies on hindsight bias and anchoring. Its own synthesis does not isolate the strategy; no effect size is recorded. The claim page's own Discussion says it rests on a second-hand report and should not be treated as established until primary studies are ingested.
- [Comparing cases side by side improves learning and transfer by a moderate average amount](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]: 3 studies; a meta-analysis of 57 experiments (d = .50) against single or sequential case study; an experiment in which undergraduates who compared two negotiation cases transferred the principle more often (48% vs 19%); and an algebra experiment with seventh graders in which comparing methods raised procedural knowledge and flexibility but not conceptual knowledge. It supports putting two accounts side by side; it is about learning a principle from cases, not about judgment bias.
- [Conflict-based instruction improves science conceptual learning, though staged contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [~M]: its table counts 5 distinct studies. A meta-analysis of 218 science-education studies found large effects for conceptual change strategies, with cognitive-conflict strategies at g = 1.10 (k = 150) and high heterogeneity; an older meta-analysis found effective interventions shared conceptual conflict; a within-subjects experiment with 32 undergraduates found contradictions helped only when learners reported confusion. It supports confronting a first answer with an alternative, and limits it: the conflict has to be experienced and resolved.
- [Instruction that confronts science misconceptions (conceptual-conflict interventions, refutation text) produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition](../claims/misconceptions-interfere-with-new-learning.md) [+M]: its table counts 4 distinct studies; both syntheses were read from abstracts and print no pooled effect. Refutation text, which names the misconception and answers it, is the supplied-alternative version of this move.
- [Argumentation Improves Reasoning](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 46 studies (5,415 students) of online science argumentation found medium effects on argument skill and on content learning, larger in K-12; a multiyear dialogue intervention with young adolescents transferred to better individual essays with more attention to evidence than a comparison group. Counterargument is part of argumentation; the studies do not isolate it.
- [Prior Knowledge Needed For Accurate Self Assessment](../claims/prior-knowledge-needed-for-accurate-self-assessment.md) [~M]: self-evaluations correlate only moderately with performance (mean r = .29 across 22 meta-analyses), and in one course of 96 undergraduates the lowest performers were grossly overconfident. It describes the overconfidence this move targets; it does not test the move as a remedy.

Limits:

- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: its table counts 14 distinct studies of cognitive-load guidance. Carried here in both directions: a supplied alternative may be needed by novices and redundant for experts.
- [Critical disposition is least spontaneous when confronting one's most basic prejudices or deeply held convictions](../claims/critical-temper-weakest-on-basic-prejudices.md) [~W]: a theoretical argument, no data. It is the reason for the private-conviction row.
- [Peer conflicts did not always produce conceptual change](../claims/peer-conflicts-conditional-on-reflection.md) [~M]: in a qualitative study of seven dyads, some students changed without conflict and some met conflict without changing; its table counts 4 distinct studies bearing on when conflict fails.

The learners, tasks and outcomes differ (undergraduates on negotiation cases, school science classes, online argumentation, course exams), so do not rank them by effect size or add them up into a verdict for this move.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a better judgment under uncertainty, with confidence that matches accuracy, on a stated kind of task. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to be right, to look competent, to finish, to win the argument, or to get the real decision right at work. These can pull against the move. A learner who values looking competent may treat a prompt to consider the opposite as an accusation of error and defend the first answer; one who values winning may produce only straw-man alternatives. Record what the learner says they want, and use it: a trainee clinician who wants to avoid missing a serious condition will take "name the most dangerous alternative" more seriously than "consider other views".

For example, a nursing student may want to pass the practical assessment, while the designer's objective is diagnostic reasoning that holds up on the ward. Practice on assessment-style cases serves the first; an unprompted case on placement, where the student is watched for whether they name an alternative before acting, tests the second. Record both, so that a good assessment score is not read as the habit.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if primary studies, once recorded, show that the prompt does not change judgments in learning tasks beyond what a generic warning does, or that its effect disappears once the prompt is removed. If supplied alternatives work as well as self-generated ones for every learner, the model should prefer the supplied form, which is cheaper. If confidence falls without accuracy rising across several judgments, the move is producing doubt, not debiasing. If the habit never appears in unprompted judgments, the move is a task feature, not a capability, and the arc above should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "a token gesture" or "too little domain knowledge" after the fact.

A better judgment on the prompted task, calibrated confidence, a written counterargument, conceptual change and an unprompted habit in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence establishes none of them for this move directly, and sets no dose, no threshold and no number of sessions.

## Examples

### Illustrative

**"Consider the opposite" prompts in clinical education** — Medical and nursing educators ask trainees who have committed to a diagnosis to list three reasons the patient might have a different condition before finalizing the chart. This mirrors the original Lord et al. (1984) manipulation and is a standard element of diagnostic-error curricula.

**Devil's advocate / red-team structures** — In design critiques, debate formats, and intelligence analysis (e.g., the structured analytic techniques taught in the U.S. intelligence community's *Tradecraft 101* tradition), one participant or team is formally assigned to argue against the leading proposal, institutionalizing the opposite-consideration step.

**Prediction journals with confidence ratings** — Learners record a prediction with a confidence level, then write the strongest case against it before checking the outcome. Used in statistics and research-methods courses to build calibrated judgment.

**Misconception confrontations in science class** — After students commit to an answer on a concept inventory item (e.g., "heavier objects fall faster"), the teacher asks them to explain how someone who believed the opposite would justify it, then confronts both accounts with evidence — a pattern central to physics education research curricula such as [Tutorials in Introductory Physics](https://www.phys.washington.edu/groups/peg/tut.html) (University of Washington).

**Counterargument drafting in writing instruction** — Before finalizing a persuasive essay, students must draft the strongest objection to their own thesis and respond to it, a routine embedded in many argumentation frameworks (e.g., the [Claim–Evidence–Reasoning](https://www.k12.wa.us) structures used in NGSS-aligned science writing).

## Description
Considering the opposite is a debiasing technique in which learners are prompted to explicitly generate reasons why their initial conclusion, diagnosis, or hypothesis might be wrong — and why an alternative might be right. It counters confirmation bias, anchoring, and overconfidence by forcing the activation of disconfirming evidence that would otherwise be filtered out. The technique originated in social psychology (Lord, Lepper & Preston, 1984) and has since been applied to clinical judgment, forecasting, and classroom argumentation.

## Implications

Because people naturally test hypotheses by seeking confirming evidence, simply asking learners to "be unbiased" has little effect; instructing them to *consider the opposite* works because it redirects the same confirmatory machinery toward the alternative hypothesis [Considering the opposite reduces judgmental biases better than generic warnings to be unbiased.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.6.1231) [+M]. In learning contexts, the technique pairs naturally with activities that contrast competing cases or explanations [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M], and it can trigger the productive state of [cognitive disequilibrium](../theories/constructivism.md) that motivates conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. It is most valuable when learners already hold a plausible first answer — the moment of commitment is exactly when bias is strongest and the counter-consideration prompt is most needed.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task with a defensible initial judgment (diagnosis, prediction, interpretation, design decision) — the technique has nothing to act on if no position has been formed
- Prompts that require *generating* alternative reasons, not merely acknowledging that alternatives exist — listing one's own reasons for the opposite is the active ingredient
- Time and structure for genuine evaluation of the alternative, not a token gesture ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) or [Eliciting Student Thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md) formats help)
- A culture where changing one's mind is treated as intellectual strength rather than failure

#### Constraints
- Ineffective as a generic bias warning; "be objective" instructions do not change judgments [-W]
- Can backfire with learners who lack enough domain knowledge to generate a plausible opposite, producing shallow or contrived alternatives [-M]
- Overuse can induce unproductive second-guessing or decision paralysis in tasks requiring timely commitment [-W]
- Less useful for well-structured procedural tasks with a single correct method, where there is no meaningful "opposite" to consider

### Target Learners
- Learners making diagnostic or evaluative judgments (clinical reasoning, peer review, historical interpretation, scientific hypothesis testing)
- Learners with entrenched misconceptions or strong prior beliefs who need to confront disconfirming evidence
- Advanced learners who have enough domain knowledge to construct a substantive alternative explanation
- Less suitable for novices who cannot yet generate credible opposing hypotheses

### Target Learning Objectives
- Critical thinking and evaluative judgment under uncertainty
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect models
- Argumentation quality: anticipating and addressing counterarguments
- Calibrated confidence: reducing overconfidence in predictions and diagnoses

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — conceptual change requires learners to confront the inadequacy of their current model; considering the opposite manufactures that confrontation deliberately
- [Information Processing Theory](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) — the technique counteracts selective encoding and retrieval by forcing activation of disconfirming information that confirmatory search would not retrieve
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — prompts to consider alternatives function as metacognitive monitoring checks on one's own judgments

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — for novices, simultaneously holding a primary hypothesis and a serious alternative can overload working memory; the technique presupposes sufficient schema development

### Claims
- [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M] — structured comparison of competing explanations shares the mechanism of contrastive evaluation
- [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M] — encountering contradictions with one's current model drives knowledge restructuring

## Related Principles
- [Purposeful Reflection](purposeful-reflection.md) — considering the opposite is a specific, high-leverage reflection prompt applied at the moment of judgment
- [Explaining Their Thinking](self-explanation.md) — self-explanation and counter-consideration both force generative processing of one's own reasoning
- [Pairing Non-Examples With Examples](pairing-non-examples-with-examples.md) — non-examples externalize the "opposite" case, supporting discrimination between correct and incorrect applications
- [Cognitive Disequilibrium](cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — deliberately engineered contradiction is the motivational engine this technique exploits

## Examples

### Illustrative

**"Consider the opposite" prompts in clinical education** — Medical and nursing educators ask trainees who have committed to a diagnosis to list three reasons the patient might have a different condition before finalizing the chart. This mirrors the original Lord et al. (1984) manipulation and is a standard element of diagnostic-error curricula.

**Devil's advocate / red-team structures** — In design critiques, debate formats, and intelligence analysis (e.g., the structured analytic techniques taught in the U.S. intelligence community's *Tradecraft 101* tradition), one participant or team is formally assigned to argue against the leading proposal, institutionalizing the opposite-consideration step.

**Prediction journals with confidence ratings** — Learners record a prediction with a confidence level, then write the strongest case against it before checking the outcome. Used in statistics and research-methods courses to build calibrated judgment.

**Misconception confrontations in science class** — After students commit to an answer on a concept inventory item (e.g., "heavier objects fall faster"), the teacher asks them to explain how someone who believed the opposite would justify it, then confronts both accounts with evidence — a pattern central to physics education research curricula such as [Tutorials in Introductory Physics](https://www.phys.washington.edu/groups/peg/tut.html) (University of Washington).

**Counterargument drafting in writing instruction** — Before finalizing a persuasive essay, students must draft the strongest objection to their own thesis and respond to it, a routine embedded in many argumentation frameworks (e.g., the [Claim–Evidence–Reasoning](https://www.k12.wa.us) structures used in NGSS-aligned science writing).
