# Supporting Students with Intellectual Disabilities

## Description
**Intellectual disability** (or intellectual developmental disorder) is assessed on two dimensions together, not IQ alone: cognitive capacity and **adaptive functioning** — how well a person handles everyday life tasks. About 1% of the U.S. population, disproportionately male, meets diagnostic criteria; some children lose the classification later as they develop better everyday functioning. Students with intellectual disabilities score poorly on standardized intelligence tests and may have limited language or impaired speech, but the defining feature by definition is the combination of both intellectual *and* daily-functioning limitations, not either alone.

Historically, severity was defined purely by IQ score, but because such scores are insensitive to a person's actual daily social functioning, current practice instead classifies **levels of support needed**, ranging from **intermittent** (occasional, as-needed help for one or two specific activities or classes) through **limited** and **extensive** to **pervasive** (continuous, lifelong support across nearly all settings) (AAMR, 2002; Schalock & Luckasson, 2004). A classroom teacher is most likely to encounter students needing only intermittent support — help with a specific routine like reading or dressing, mainly under time pressure — with more intensive-support students spending more of their time with specialists (special education teachers, speech-language specialists) elsewhere.

Most specific classroom techniques for teaching students with mild-to-moderate intellectual disabilities reduce to three general strategies. **More time and practice**: these students can often learn fundamental academic content (basic arithmetic, sight-word reading) but need more repetition and a longer runway; frequent, specific praise for genuine effort and success ("you added that one correctly" rather than the vaguer "you're a hard worker") helps sustain motivation through the extra practice, and breaking skills into achievable steps makes that specific praise possible without becoming discouraging — while setting expectations too low, or using materials clearly designed for much younger children, is itself an ethical failure that denies the student rightful learning opportunities (Bogdan, 2006). **Adaptive/functional framing**: since there is never enough time to teach everything, tying learning goals to the student's own everyday life and functioning (e.g., practicing addition/subtraction through purchasing and making change, learning to read a clock starting from personally meaningful times like when school starts) both teaches the academic content and directly builds the adaptive-functioning skills the disability itself affects. **Full inclusion**: participating in the class's social and academic life as fully as possible — assemblies, group games, group assignments — benefits everyone, not just the student with the disability: classmates learn that school is partly about creating opportunity rather than only ranking performance, while the included student gains practice at both academic content and "belonging" skills (greeting classmates, asking a teacher a question) that generalize well beyond the classroom.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Willingness to break target skills into small, genuinely achievable steps so that specific, honest praise is actually available to give
- Existing age-appropriate-but-simplified curriculum materials, which for many subject areas already exist and specialist teachers can often help locate (Snell et al., 2005)
#### Constraints
- Setting expectations too low, or substituting materials designed for much younger children, is a serious ethical and professional error, not a kindness — it deprives the student of learning opportunities they were capable of using
- The level-of-support framework (intermittent through pervasive) should guide *how much* specialist involvement is needed, not be treated as a fixed ceiling on what the student can eventually learn

### Target Learners
- Students meeting intellectual-disability criteria on both cognitive-capacity and adaptive-functioning measures, most commonly those requiring only intermittent or limited support in a general classroom
- Students at risk of being under-challenged through well-intentioned but overly cautious expectation-setting

### Target Learning Objectives
- Academic fundamentals (basic literacy, basic numeracy) achieved through extended time and practice rather than accelerated pacing
- Adaptive, daily-life functioning skills taught in tandem with academic content rather than as a separate track
- Social inclusion and "belonging" skills alongside academic participation

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Reinforcement Theory](reinforcement-theory.md) [+M] — the recommended practice of specific, effort-contingent praise is a direct application of reinforcement principles, distinguishing genuine reinforcers from vague positive consequences
