# Productive Failure

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 20 claims (8 for, 7 mixed, 4 against, 1 unmarked) · 18 studies (9 causal, 5 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 7 of 18 report an effect size · 17 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the decision of **whether a new concept is taught first or attempted first**. When the goal is conceptual understanding of a new concept and transfer to problems not yet seen, and learners already have enough prior knowledge to generate several partial or wrong solutions of their own, letting them first attempt problems the concept solves (problem solving followed by instruction, PS-I), and then teaching the concept by comparing their attempts with the canonical solution, is expected to improve conceptual knowledge and transfer over teaching first and practising after (I-PS). It is not expected to improve procedural skill.

The relationship is conditional on five things, each from the evidence below:

- **The outcome.** Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons the advantage was g = 0.36 on conceptual knowledge and transfer, and g = −0.03, no difference, on procedural knowledge (Sinha & Kapur 2021).
- **How the attempt and the teaching are designed.** The advantage grew with fidelity to the productive-failure design: problems that admit several solution methods, learners actually generating several, group work, and, most of all, instruction that builds on the learners' own solutions (g = 0.56 where it did, 0.20 where it did not).
- **Who the learners are.** The effect was negative for second to fifth graders and rose with grade level. Young learners may not have enough prior knowledge to generate several solutions.
- **What is being learned.** For domain-general skills, such as the control-of-variables strategy, the instruction-first order did better.
- **How long the intervention runs.** Packing the full design into a few hours reversed the fidelity benefit; it paid off over days.

How this sits beside its neighbours: [Guided Discovery Learning](inquiry-based-learning.md) and the evidence against minimal guidance concern learning *without* the instruction that follows; productive failure always ends with full instruction, and the question is only its order. [Worked Examples](worked-examples.md) and [Cognitive Load Management](cognitive-load-management.md) argue for teaching first, especially for novices learning procedures; the evidence here agrees for procedural outcomes and for young learners, and differs for conceptual outcomes in learners old enough to generate solutions. [Pretesting](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) is a smaller cousin: an attempt before study, without the generation of methods.

## Default design, while the dose is untested

The meta-analysis establishes the direction, the outcomes it holds for and which design features go with larger effects; it does not set a dose. This default is a **labelled proposal**: the two phases and the features follow the evidence, the timings are untested choices.

1. **Check the condition.** Is the target a concept with an underlying structure (variance, ratio, a physical law, a statistical idea), and is the outcome conceptual understanding or transfer rather than fluent execution of a procedure? Are learners past the early primary years, with the prior knowledge to attempt the problem in several ways? If not, teach first.
2. **Choose one rich problem** the concept is the answer to: one that admits several reasonable methods, most of them partial or wrong (for variance: which of three players is the most consistent scorer?). Give it a story or a reason to care.
3. **Problem-solving phase, 30–60 minutes, in small groups.** Ask for as many ways of solving it as they can find. Do not teach, give hints toward the canonical method, or grade correctness. Say plainly that not solving it is expected and that trying several ways is the task.
4. **Collect the attempts.** Note the three or four most common or most instructive methods, including wrong ones.
5. **Instruction phase, building on the attempts.** Present the learners' methods, ask what each captures and misses, compare them with each other and then with the canonical method, and show which features of the problem the canonical method handles that theirs did not. Then teach the concept in full.
6. **Then practise** the procedure, as you would after instruction-first teaching: the attempt phase does not replace it.
7. **Across sessions**, run the attempt and the instruction on different days or at least with a break between them where the course allows; do not compress the whole design into one short lesson.
8. **Judge it on a conceptual or transfer test**, not on the attempt phase, which will look like failure, and not on a procedural test alone, where no difference is expected.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Four facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: **is the outcome conceptual and transfer, or procedural?**; **how old are the learners and what do they already know?**; **is the target a domain concept or a domain-general skill?**; and **how much time is there?**

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| The goal is conceptual understanding or transfer of a new concept (mathematics, science, statistics) | Use the attempt-first design above | [Productive failure improves conceptual learning](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M] (g = 0.36 over 53 studies) |
| The goal is fluent execution of a procedure (`procedure`) | Teach first; an attempt phase adds time without a procedural gain | [PS-I shows no pooled benefit for procedural knowledge](../claims/psi-procedural-knowledge-null-pooled-effect.md) [~M] (g = −0.03) |
| Learners are in the early primary years (roughly ages 7–11) | Teach first | [PS-I effects are negative for 2nd–5th graders and increase with age](../claims/psi-effect-increases-with-grade-level.md) [-M] |
| The target is a domain-general skill (the control-of-variables strategy, a general reasoning heuristic) | Teach first | [For domain-general skills, one meta-analysis finds a negligible effect (g = −0.17) in favour of I-PS over PS-I](../claims/domain-general-skills-favor-ips.md) [-M] |
| The teacher cannot or will not build the instruction on the learners' attempts | Expect a much smaller effect; if the attempts will not be used, the attempt phase loses most of its value | [PF design fidelity predicts larger PS-I effects](../claims/pf-fidelity-predicts-psi-effect-size.md) [+M] (0.56 when instruction built on student solutions, 0.20 when not) |
| Only a single short session is available | Keep the attempt short and the design simple, or teach first; do not cram every feature into a few hours | [The fidelity benefit of PS-I reverses for very short interventions](../claims/psi-fidelity-time-span-interaction.md) [~M] |
| Learners work alone (`online-self-paced`) | Group work went with larger effects; in a self-paced course, show learners other learners' attempts or a set of typical attempts before the instruction | [PF design fidelity predicts larger PS-I effects](../claims/pf-fidelity-predicts-psi-effect-size.md) [+M]; the self-paced version is an untested proposal |
| The question is whether to help learners succeed during the attempt (hints, guidance toward the method) | Do not steer toward the canonical method during the attempt; guidance raised solution quality but not later learning | [Guidance during initial problem solving does not improve learning from subsequent instruction](../claims/initial-guidance-does-not-improve-learning-from-instruction.md) [~M] |
| Learners will meet later material that builds on the concept (a course, not a lesson) | Use the attempt-first design for the foundational concepts; its benefit shows when learners later learn from new resources | [Invention tasks prepare future learning](../claims/invention-tasks-prepare-future-learning.md) [+M] |
| Learners are novices with little prior knowledge of the domain, and the problem cannot be attempted without it | Teach first, or give the minimum prior knowledge before the attempt; an attempt with nothing to generate from is minimal guidance, not productive failure | [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [-M] |
| Learners are anxious about failure or the setting grades every attempt | State that the attempt is not graded and that not solving it is expected; keep the attempt phase out of the grade | untested proposal |
| One teacher for a large class | Collect attempts from a sample of groups (or a quick poll of methods) and build the instruction on the three or four most common | untested proposal |

## Observation, state and explanation

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Learners produce one method, or none, during the attempt | The problem admits too few methods; learners lack the prior knowledge to generate; they are waiting to be told | Ask what they would try if the first method were not allowed; check the prior knowledge the problem assumes. |
| Learners disengage or say the task is unfair | The failure is not framed as expected; the attempt is too long; the attempt is graded | Ask how they understood the task; shorten the attempt and restate that it is not graded. |
| Conceptual test no better than with teaching first | The instruction did not build on the attempts; the learners were too young or too new to the domain; the test measured procedure | Check whether the instruction compared their methods with the canonical one; check what the test asked. |
| Procedural test worse than with teaching first | Practice time was lost to the attempt phase | Give the same amount of practice after instruction in both designs before comparing. |

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Productive failure improves conceptual learning](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]: Sinha & Kapur's (2021) meta-analysis of 53 studies and 166 comparisons, g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] on conceptual knowledge and transfer, larger (g up to 0.58) with high fidelity to the productive-failure design; and Kapur's (2014) two randomised studies, where the number of solutions students generated predicted what they learned.
- [PS-I shows no pooled benefit for procedural knowledge](../claims/psi-procedural-knowledge-null-pooled-effect.md) [~M]: g = −0.03, not significant. The design is not a way to teach procedures.
- [After accounting for publication bias, the estimated true effect favoring PS-I is strong](../claims/publication-bias-corrected-psi-effect-strong.md) [+M]: a bias-corrected estimate of g = 0.87. A correction of this kind is model-dependent; the observed 0.36 is the safer figure to plan with.
- [PF design fidelity predicts larger PS-I effects](../claims/pf-fidelity-predicts-psi-effect-size.md) [+M]: of the seven features coded, group work and instruction building on student solutions showed significant subgroup differences.
- [The fidelity benefit of PS-I reverses for very short interventions](../claims/psi-fidelity-time-span-interaction.md) [~M].
- [PS-I effects are negative for 2nd–5th graders and increase with age](../claims/psi-effect-increases-with-grade-level.md) [-M].
- [For domain-general skills, one meta-analysis finds a negligible effect (g = −0.17) in favour of I-PS over PS-I](../claims/domain-general-skills-favor-ips.md) [-M].
- [Solving novel problems before instruction improves conceptual understanding and transfer but not procedural knowledge](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]: Kapur's (2016) review of the classroom experiments with ninth-graders learning standard deviation.
- [Guidance during initial problem solving does not improve learning from subsequent instruction](../claims/initial-guidance-does-not-improve-learning-from-instruction.md) [~M]: Kapur (2011) and Loibl & Rummel (2013), as reviewed by Kapur (2016).
- [Invention tasks prepare future learning](../claims/invention-tasks-prepare-future-learning.md) [+M]: Schwartz & Martin (2004); ninth-graders who invented a procedure before a worked example transferred better, but only when a learning resource was available at test.
- [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [-M]: the boundary with discovery learning that ends without instruction.

Taken together: for conceptual understanding and transfer in learners past the early primary years, attempting first and being taught second, with the teaching built on the attempts, beats teaching first by about a third of a standard deviation; for procedures, for young children and for domain-general skills it does not. No claim in the wiki tests a dose (how long the attempt, how many problems) or the design in a self-paced online course.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is understanding deep enough to use a concept on problems not yet seen. Ask separately what the learner wants: a learner who must pass a procedural test next week is not served by an attempt phase that costs practice time, and a learner who reads failure as a verdict on ability needs the framing in step 3 before anything else.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if new comparisons with comparable learners, holding total time and practice equal, find no conceptual or transfer advantage for attempting first; or if the advantage disappears when the instruction-first condition also builds on typical student errors, which would locate the effect in the instruction rather than the order. It should be extended, not reversed, by studies of self-paced online versions and of learners outside mathematics and science.
