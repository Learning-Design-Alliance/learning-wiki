Personalization¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 52 claims (27 for, 22 mixed, 3 against) · 60 studies (20 review, 16 causal, 8 qualitative, 7 theoretical, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 3 design), q1–q4 · 6 of 60 report an effect size · 47 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the question of what to tailor to each learner, and on what basis, when a group of learners shares one goal. Where learners differ in what they already know on the target task, in what interests them, in how they can access the material (a disability, a communication system, a language) or in what they prefer among equivalent means, tailoring the pace, the support, the context of problems and the means of working to that learner, while the goal, the criterion and the final assessment stay common, is expected to raise engagement and interest (motivation-affect, persistence) and, where the tailoring rests on a diagnosis of the learner's responses, achievement on an aligned measure (general-achievement, learning-time, mostly immediate). Tailoring on a learner's self-reported "learning style" is expected to add nothing.

The relationship is conditional on four things: what the tailoring rests on (responses on the task and stated access needs are better grounds than a style inventory or a guessed interest); what is tailored (support and pace for knowledge differences; context and choice for interest and motivation; means for access and preference); whether the learner's own account is asked for, since a designer's guess at a learner's interest often misses; and whether the tailoring is actually carried out across the weeks, not only adopted.

No claim in the wiki tests personalization as a whole against uniform instruction. One randomized experiment compares a diagnosis-based personalized path with a fixed sequence over the same content (undergraduates, probability theory). A meta-analysis compares intelligent tutoring systems with non-individualized instruction. No claim here tests interest-based problem contexts against standard contexts on achievement; the one randomized field experiment on a related move (localizing character names) found gains only in some schools. The choice evidence is about autonomy-supportive versus controlling conditions and rewards, not about choice of task as such.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this: Adaptive Learning owns the narrower, system-driven case, choosing each next task, hint or check from a learner's recent responses, and its default design and evidence on tutoring systems, learner models and fidelity apply here unchanged; this page does not repeat them. Autonomy owns giving learners choice and voice; this page uses choice only as one thing that can be personalized. Mastery Learning owns the gate-correct-recheck rule that lets learners progress at their own rate. Flexible Grouping owns regrouping learners by current need, the classroom alternative to one-to-one tailoring.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance and the recommendations of the two pages folded into it, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Fix what stays common before deciding what varies. Write the unit's goal, the criterion for success and the final assessment once, for everyone; personalize how and when (path, pace, support, context, means), not what counts as mastery. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement that shared goals keep outcomes comparable.
  2. Find out where each learner is, at the start of each unit. Give a 10–15 minute diagnostic on the unit's prerequisite skills (a short set of items scored by part, not a total), and ask each learner, in their own words, what they are interested in or what they use outside school. Dose is an untested proposal. Diagnosis-based paths beat a fixed sequence in one experiment: In a randomized experiment, personalized paths reduced learning time by 22.0% and improved post-test scores versus a fixed sequence [+M] (undergraduates, one session's content). Asking rather than assuming: Students frequently reported that personalized problem topics did not match their interests [-W].
  3. Tailor support and pace to the diagnosis, and withdraw support as performance grows. Learners who cannot begin get worked or partly worked examples and closer items; learners who succeed unaided skip ahead to harder or transfer items. Check on unsupported items before removing support. Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]. The response-by-response version of this step is modelled in Adaptive Learning.
  4. Let each learner advance at their own rate through a sequenced continuum. Lay the unit out as an ordered series of short sections (about one lesson each), each ending with a short unaided check; a learner moves to the next section on passing it, and others get correction and a recheck. This works in whole-class, small-group or individual settings and does not need a one-to-one ratio. Untested proposal; the recommendation of the folded continuous-progress page (a 1974 curriculum guide, no claim cited). The gate is modelled in Mastery Learning.
  5. Offer bounded choice among options that serve the same objective. Two to four tasks or media of equal rigour (a choice board), with the criterion shown on every option. Give novices and younger learners fewer options. Choice and autonomy support are expected to raise motivation, not achievement directly: Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning. [+M]; moderate rather than full agency, in one game-based study reported second-hand: Moderate learner agency in game-based learning yields better outcomes than no agency or high agency [~W]. The number of options is an untested proposal.
  6. Set problems in contexts the learner named, and check that they fit. Rewrite (or generate) practice problems in the contexts learners gave in step 2, keeping the mathematics or structure unchanged; ask learners after a few problems whether the context is one they care about, and let them change it. Expert guidance recommends it: Students engage more actively and perform better when math problems are presented in personalized contexts that align with their backgrounds and interests [+W]. Limits: assigned topics often missed students' interests (topic mismatch [-W]), and localizing names helped only in some schools (Localizing word-problem character names improved MATHia performance only for specific subgroup-majority schools, with no improvement in sense of belonging overall [~M]).
  7. Where learners use different means to the same end, assess preference with the individual. For a communication system (speech-generating device, picture exchange, manual signs), an assistive tool or a format for access, offer the options in turn over several sessions, record which the learner chooses when free to choose and how quickly they acquire the target skill with each, and decide per learner, not by system type. Individuals with developmental disabilities tend to prefer speech-generating devices over picture exchange and manual signs, with substantial individual differences [+M]. The folded AAC page also reported that requesting skills were acquired faster with a preferred system for some children; the claim page records preferences, not that acquisition result. The number of sessions is an untested proposal.
  8. Do not tailor on learning styles. Do not sort learners by a visual, auditory or kinesthetic inventory and match the format to it; choose the format the content needs, for everyone. Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-S].
  9. Review the tailoring weekly, and check it was enacted. Ten minutes a week: who advanced, who has been held at one section for two or more weeks, who chose the same easy option every time, and whether anyone has been routed around content the final assessment needs. Judge the personalization only where it was used. Personalized Learning Effects Vary With Fidelity [~M]; the weekly dose is an untested proposal.

Dose and progression across sessions (untested proposal). Early sessions in a unit: the teacher sets each learner's path from the diagnostic, choices are between two options, and problem contexts are drawn by the teacher from the learner's list. Middle sessions: learners choose among three or four options and move through sections at their own pace with a self-check at the end of each. Late sessions: learners propose their own context or topic and plan the remaining sections, with the teacher reviewing the plan. A learner moves on from a section after passing its unaided check (for example, four of five items), and moves from teacher-set to self-chosen paths once they have passed two consecutive sections on the first attempt. Re-run the diagnostic at the start of each new unit, since expertise is specific to the topic.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time for a diagnostic, personalize only context and choice (steps 5–6) and keep the path common; if the options cannot be made equal in rigour, do not offer a choice; if no one will review the tailoring (step 9), keep the number of separate paths to two or three that a teacher can track.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learners differ in, on this task. Differences in prior knowledge call for tailored support and pace; differences in interest for tailored contexts and choice; differences in access for tailored means. If unstated, ask: on a short diagnostic, how far apart are the learners, and do any need a different way of taking part?
  • Who or what will do the tailoring, and how often they can look. Software can tailor every item; a teacher with thirty learners can tailor sections and groups weekly; a volunteer who checks in briefly can tailor one thing. If unstated, ask: who sees each learner's work, and how often?
  • Which outcome counts, and whether it is common. Engagement, achievement on an aligned test, time saved and a learner's own goal move separately, and a personalized path is only comparable if the final criterion is shared. If unstated, ask: what must every learner be able to do at the end, and how will it be checked?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a learner's response under stated conditions: diagnostic items scored by part, which option or context the learner chose, how long they stayed at a section, their check results, what they said they were interested in, and, for a communication system, which device they selected when free to choose and how many trials a request took. "Is a visual learner", "is interested in football", "is ready to move on" and "prefers the device" are inferred states. An interest a learner names once is a report, not a trait, and it can change.

Evidence and qualifications¶

None of the claims below tests personalization as a whole against uniform instruction. Each tests one kind of tailoring, or a limit on it.

  • In a randomized experiment, personalized paths reduced learning time by 22.0% and improved post-test scores versus a fixed sequence [+M]: 120 undergraduates randomly assigned to a diagnosis-based personalized path or a fixed order over the same probability-theory content; the personalized group took 57.6 against 73.8 minutes (d = 1.52) and scored higher on the post-test after controlling for pre-test (η² = 0.065). The companion claim reports lower self-reported load on all six adapted NASA-TLX dimensions (d 1.12 to 1.80): Personalized paths reduced self-reported cognitive load on all six adapted NASA-TLX dimensions with large effect sizes [+M]. It supports tailoring path and pace to a diagnosis; it is one experiment, one topic, one session's content and an immediate test. Its evidence line counts "2 studies", but both entries are the same experiment (Feng and Huang 2026), so the marker here is held at M.
  • ITS significantly outperform large-group human instruction, individual computer-based instruction, and textbooks or workbooks, but not small-group or individual human tutoring [+M]: a meta-analysis (Ma et al. 2014) finds intelligent tutoring systems ahead of large-group instruction (g = .44), individual computer-based instruction (g = .57) and textbooks (g = .36), and not different from small-group (g = .05) or individual human tutoring (g = -.11); equivalence was not tested. It supports individualized tutoring over non-individualized instruction and says that people tailoring for small groups do as well; it bundles tailoring with feedback and practice.
  • Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning. [+M]: an SDT review, a meta-analysis of 128 experiments showing that expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d = -0.4) and a conceptual framework of organizational, procedural and cognitive autonomy support. Its table gathers 8 distinct studies, one with an effect size coded. It supports offering choice for motivation; it is not a test of choosing tasks against assigned tasks on achievement, and its discussion says choice works best with structure and can make novices anxious. Marked M here, below its cap, because the strong study is about rewards.
  • Individuals with developmental disabilities tend to prefer speech-generating devices over picture exchange and manual signs, with substantial individual differences [+M]: an overview of two reviews (7 studies with 12 individuals; 17 studies); 50% showed a high preference for the speech-generating device against 30% for picture exchange, and 67% preferred the device across three options. It supports deciding the means per child, since a minority preferred each alternative; it does not show that the preferred system is learned faster.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies and a circuit-diagram experiment; guidance that helps novices can lose its value or hurt experienced learners. Its table gathers 13 distinct studies, one with an effect size coded. It is the reason to tailor support to prior knowledge and withdraw it; it gives no crossover point and measures expertise per topic. The narrower The effectiveness of instructional techniques and procedures interacts with levels of learner prior knowledge, a pattern known as the expertise reversal effect [+W] (Kalyuga 2007, a review) is one of those studies.

Limits and qualifications:

  • Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-S]: a systematic review found only one study with the crossover design needed to test matching, and judged it unconvincing; a randomized study of 125 fifth graders found no style-by-modality interaction. Its table gathers 4 distinct studies. It bears against tailoring on style; it says nothing against tailoring on prior knowledge or access needs.
  • Students frequently reported that personalized problem topics did not match their interests [-W]: of 235 student comments on 47 teacher-personalized problems, 83 said the topic was one they liked and 160 that it was not; a related claim records students asking to input their own interests, and one objecting to personalization itself (Students critiqued the broad grain size of teacher-in-the-loop personalization and personalization itself met resistance [~W]). It limits interest personalization done by group label or teacher guess.
  • Localizing word-problem character names improved MATHia performance only for specific subgroup-majority schools, with no improvement in sense of belonging overall [~M]: a randomized field experiment in five districts; performance improved in some subgroup-majority schools and not others, and belonging did not change. No effect size is printed. Surface personalization does not reliably transfer across groups.
  • Latent learner profiles did not moderate the effectiveness of the adaptive ITS [~M]: in an experiment with 132 early-primary pupils, profiles built from prior knowledge, conscientiousness, interest and self-concept did not moderate an adaptive tutor's effect, and the adaptive tutor did not beat the non-adaptive one. Tailoring on a learner profile is not shown to help here.
  • Personalized Learning Effects Vary With Fidelity [~M]: about 40 schools implementing personalized-learning practices, matched to virtual comparison groups; one-year effects +0.09 SD in mathematics (significant) and +0.07 SD in reading (not significant), larger where implementation was reported fuller or longer, which the authors call suggestive. Its table gathers 4 distinct studies. A school-level program is far smaller than the experiments above suggest, and an adopted program is not an enacted one.

Keep each finding's comparator, population, outcome and horizon with it. Do not rank the undergraduate path experiment beside the school-level SD gains, or read engagement findings as achievement findings.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before it was rewritten, with what each is and how far it bears on the model above. None has been re-read for the model, so treat them as candidates for it.

  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] — one-to-one tutoring adjusted to each response beats fixed support; bears on step 3 through Adaptive Learning, which owns it (its table gathers 20 distinct studies). The earlier page linked it under the title "Personalizing problem contexts to learner interests improves performance", which is not what the claim says.
  • Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes. [~W] — novices learn more from sequences that start with a worked example; bears only on the support given to low starting responses in step 3. The earlier page linked it as "Adaptive tutoring approaches human tutoring effectiveness", which is not what the claim says.
  • Intuitive learners tend to outperform sensing learners in media-based presentations [~W] — a conference experiment series on Myers–Briggs-style preferences and media formats; format, not preference match, drove the reversal. The earlier page linked it as "Teaching to learning styles does not improve outcomes" [X]; it is a different finding, and the learning-styles claim above now carries that point.
  • The direction of difficulty adaptation, not merely its presence, determines AI-GBL outcomes [~W] — one VR shooting study reported in a review; dynamic beat progressive difficulty. Bears weakly on step 3.
  • One-shot path generation raises Validity but lowers Adaptivity and Efficiency relative to step-by-step interaction [~W] — model-generated learning paths scored by metrics, not learners; bears on the AI-tutor row only.
  • Emotion features improve tutor performance only when grounded in education-theory-based pedagogical strategies; emotion information alone misguides a smaller model [~W] — an ablation of an affective math tutor's prompts, not learner outcomes; bears on tailoring to emotional state.
  • Included studies suggest engagement increases when adaptive platform activities are tailored to learners' personal needs and emotional state [+W] — a review's summary; engagement only.
  • Post-session subjective ratings favored the adaptive system for the expressive persona but showed a reversed adaptivity disadvantage for the guarded persona [~W] — 20 participants rating an adaptive virtual patient for therapist training; tailoring helped one persona and not another.
  • The three refinement strategies make distinct trade-offs when personalizing problems to topics that resist natural alignment with the original math [~W] — a case analysis of LLM-personalized problems; bears on step 6 (some topics do not fit the mathematics).
  • Robot adaptiveness effects on human autonomy are mixed: autonomy-supportive behaviours and as-needed assistance help, but mere choice or high interactivity may not [~W] — a review of human–robot interaction; supports step 5's bounded choice, at a distance.
  • Human-AI tutoring increases IXL skill proficiency by 36%, but the proficiency gap widens in favor of reactively tutored students [~W] — a difference-in-differences study of who the human tutor attends to; bears on the scarce-facilitator row.
  • Courseware can bridge gateway math learning gaps through tailored tutorials, study tools, diagnostics, and targeted practice [+W] — faculty and student interviews; perceptions, not outcomes.
  • ChatGPT and DeepSeek feedback differed from student to student in technical content, repertoire and address style, providing an individualized approach [+W] — AI feedback texts to 12 participants in violin training; shows feedback varied, not that it helped.
  • New baseline data are needed to support instructional planning for individual students and student groups after disrupted schooling [~W] — a guidance premise; supports re-running the diagnostic (step 2) after disruption.
  • Teachers who made productive adaptations were more likely to consider learning progressions and adapt lessons to below-grade-level content [+W] — teachers' content adaptations; bears on step 4's sequenced continuum.
  • Middle school teachers envisioned AI chatbots as adaptive instructional scaffolds providing differentiated support responsive to students' varying needs [+W] — teachers' expectations in focus groups; no outcomes.
  • A reinforcement-learning adaptive learning system improved course grades by 12%, engagement by 20%, and reduced dropout by 10% versus static curricula [+W] — an institutional case study reported in a technical review; no standardized effect size.
  • AI-driven personalized and adaptive learning pathways support learner engagement and satisfaction in online courses [+W] — a review's summary; engagement and satisfaction only.
  • AI technologies in education enhance teaching and learning through personalization and data analytics while reshaping administrative functions and decision-making [+W] — a review's framing statement; does not bear on the relationship beyond naming it.
  • AI tools support adaptive and personalized learning experiences that improve learner engagement and problem-solving skills in PBL [+W] — a thematic review of educators' AI use; does not test tailoring.
  • The review reports AI-driven personalization improves student engagement and learning outcomes, and virtual teaching assistants support diverse learning needs [+W] — a review relaying another study; no designs or effect sizes printed.
  • GAI in K-12 education offers opportunities to personalize learning, motivate students, improve assessment, and introduce innovative teaching practices [+W] — a review naming opportunities; no outcomes.
  • AI is implemented in SRL research primarily as an intervention, most often through adaptive systems and personalization [+W] — a mapping review of what studies do; does not bear on whether tailoring works.
  • Individualized instruction has largely receded in recent AI-focused literature, functioning as a pedagogical predecessor to AI-based personalization [+W] — a review's account of the literature; does not bear on the relationship, though it notes hybrid teacher-plus-system models.
  • AI-enhanced assessment tools are reported to facilitate adaptive testing, real-time learner support, and data-driven insights [+W] — a narrative synthesis; no studies or effect sizes.
  • ChatGPT was the most widely adopted AI platform in undergraduate higher education studies, with applications clustering around assessment automation and personalized learning support [+W] — a review of use cases; does not bear on the relationship.
  • Student disengagement on an adaptive interim assessment gradually increased across grade level [~W] — remote low-stakes testing in 2020; about an adaptive test, not adaptive instruction, and bears only on reading diagnostic results from older pupils with care.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a common capability (the unit's goal, at a stated criterion and horizon) reached by more learners, sooner, with tailoring as the means. Elicit separately what each learner wants from it: to finish faster, to work on things they care about, to avoid being placed on a slower path in front of others, to be able to communicate a request at home, or, for some, not to have their interests tied to a subject they dislike. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being seen as behind may refuse a tailored path that is visible to peers; one who values their own interests may engage with a context and still not learn the structure underneath.

For example, a child learning to request with an AAC system may value being understood by their family at home, while the designer's objective is a number of independent requests in class. The preferred device may serve the first and not the second, or the reverse. Record both, and choose the device with the family's setting in view.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if experiments that hold content and final assessment constant find personalized paths no better than a fixed sequence on delayed or transfer outcomes, or if the time saving in the one path experiment here does not replicate beyond one topic and one session. If learner-named contexts do not raise interest or achievement more than standard contexts in randomized comparisons, step 6 should be dropped to a motivational option. If preference-matched AAC systems are not acquired or maintained better than non-preferred ones, preference assessment becomes a matter of the child's voice rather than learning. If school-level personalization stays near +0.1 SD even with full implementation, the model's gains belong to the tutoring and diagnosis, not to personalization as a program. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "not personalized enough" after the fact.

Engagement, immediate achievement on an aligned test, time saved, delayed retention and the outcome a learner values are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of options, no diagnostic length and no pace threshold.

Related Principles¶

  • Adaptive Learning — the system-driven end of the personalization spectrum, where algorithms adjust difficulty and sequencing
  • Cognitive Load Management — personalization is one of the main levers for keeping load within each learner's capacity
  • Assessment for Learning — supplies the diagnostic evidence on which any personalization depends
  • Activation — diagnosing and engaging prior knowledge is the first step in deciding what to personalize

Validated¶

  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M] — Walkington (2013) personalized algebra word problems to individual learners' out-of-school interests (sports, music, gaming) and found improved performance relative to standard contexts, with the largest gains for struggling learners.

Illustrative¶

Adaptive Learning — Platform-driven personalization in which item difficulty and sequencing adjust continuously to response accuracy and latency. Used at scale in mathematics and language learning; effectiveness depends on mastery gating rather than mere content variety.

ASSISTments — Free web-based math platform (grades 6–12) that routes students through problem sets with step-level hints and immediate feedback, effectively personalizing the support each student receives during practice.

Khan Academy — Mastery-based personalization: learners progress through skill maps at their own pace, with the system recommending review of prerequisite skills when gaps are detected. Widely used in flipped and blended classrooms.

Choice Boards — A teacher-designed personalization pattern: learners select from a curated set of tasks aligned to the same objective, allowing choice of modality or topic while preserving common outcomes. Works best when all options are genuinely equivalent in rigor.

Carnegie Learning MATHia — Intelligent tutoring system that models each learner's knowledge at the skill level and personalizes problem selection and hint delivery, gating advancement on demonstrated mastery.

Duolingo — Language-learning app that personalizes review scheduling (spaced repetition of items the individual learner has struggled with) and adapts session difficulty; a consumer-scale example of adaptive item selection.
