Personalization¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 85 claims (43 for, 35 mixed, 7 against) · 88 studies (29 review, 21 causal, 11 theoretical, 9 qualitative, 8 associational, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 design), q1–q4 · 7 of 88 report an effect size · 80 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the question of what to tailor to each learner, and on what basis, when a group of learners shares one goal. Where learners differ in what they already know on the target task, in what interests them, in how they can access the material (a disability, a communication system, a language) or in what they prefer among equivalent means, tailoring the pace, the support, the context of problems and the means of working to that learner, while the goal, the criterion and the final assessment stay common, is expected to raise engagement and interest (motivation-affect, persistence) and, where the tailoring rests on a diagnosis of the learner's responses, achievement on an aligned measure (general-achievement, learning-time, mostly immediate). Tailoring on a learner's self-reported "learning style" is expected to add nothing.

The relationship is conditional on four things: what the tailoring rests on (responses on the task and stated access needs are better grounds than a style inventory or a guessed interest); what is tailored (support and pace for knowledge differences; context and choice for interest and motivation; means for access and preference); whether the learner's own account is asked for, since a designer's guess at a learner's interest often misses; and whether the tailoring is actually carried out across the weeks, not only adopted.

No claim in the wiki tests personalization as a whole against uniform instruction. One randomized experiment compares a diagnosis-based personalized path with a fixed sequence over the same content (undergraduates, probability theory). A meta-analysis compares intelligent tutoring systems with non-individualized instruction, and another compares AI-enabled personalized STEM teaching with teaching without AI (32 trials, g = 0.455), which tests the technology and its tailoring together. The one reported comparison of an adaptive system with static curricula is an institutional case study described second-hand in a review, with no assignment or effect size, so it is not counted as a test. No claim here tests interest-based problem contexts against standard contexts on achievement; the one randomized field experiment on a related move (localizing character names) found gains only in some schools. The choice evidence is about autonomy-supportive versus controlling conditions and rewards, not about choice of task as such.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this: Adaptive Learning owns the narrower, system-driven case, choosing each next task, hint or check from a learner's recent responses, and its default design and evidence on tutoring systems, learner models and fidelity apply here unchanged; this page does not repeat them. Autonomy owns giving learners choice and voice; this page uses choice only as one thing that can be personalized. Mastery Learning owns the gate-correct-recheck rule that lets learners progress at their own rate. Flexible Grouping owns regrouping learners by current need, the classroom alternative to one-to-one tailoring.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance and the recommendations of the two pages folded into it, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Fix what stays common before deciding what varies. Write the unit's goal, the criterion for success and the final assessment once, for everyone; personalize how and when (path, pace, support, context, means), not what counts as mastery. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement that shared goals keep outcomes comparable.
  2. Find out where each learner is, at the start of each unit. Give a 10–15 minute diagnostic on the unit's prerequisite skills (a short set of items scored by part, not a total), and ask each learner, in their own words, what they are interested in or what they use outside school. Dose is an untested proposal. Diagnosis-based paths beat a fixed sequence in one experiment: In a randomized experiment, personalized paths reduced learning time by 22.0% and improved post-test scores versus a fixed sequence [+M] (undergraduates, one session's content). Asking rather than assuming: Students frequently reported that personalized problem topics did not match their interests [-W]. Re-run the diagnostic after a break in schooling rather than carrying old placements forward, as reopening guidance for students with disabilities asserts without data: New baseline data are needed to support instructional planning for individual students and student groups after disrupted schooling [+W]. Read a low diagnostic score from older pupils with care, and look for rapid guessing before placing them on a slower path: on a remote, low-stakes adaptive test, Student disengagement on an adaptive interim assessment gradually increased across grade level [~W] (an adaptive test in spring 2020, not adaptive instruction).
  3. Tailor support and pace to the diagnosis, and withdraw support as performance grows. Learners who cannot begin get worked or partly worked examples and closer items; learners who succeed unaided skip ahead to harder or transfer items. Check on unsupported items before removing support. Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]. For a novice, start the sequence with a worked example rather than a problem: Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes. [+M] (secondary-school novices, circuit troubleshooting, immediate test; problem-first pairs did no better than problems only). Adjust support to each response rather than fixing it in advance: Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] (mostly one-to-one tutoring; in the dynamic-assessment meta-analysis it beats coaching but not explicit strategy training; Adaptive Learning owns it). Which way difficulty moves matters as well as whether it moves: In one quasi-experimental VR shooting study (n = 50), reported in a review, rule-based dynamic difficulty outperformed progressive difficulty [~W] (one VR shooting study, n = 50, reported in a review; dynamic beat progressive difficulty). The response-by-response version of this step is modelled in Adaptive Learning. Where a teacher, not software, chooses the easier or harder material, base the choice on the learner's diagnostic and check results and write the basis down, because judgement can track a group label rather than the work: Preservice teachers chose the easy video significantly more often for an ethnic minority student than for an ethnic majority student, all other information being equal [-M] (a vignette experiment with 108 preservice teachers; 82% against 11% chose the easy video for an otherwise identical description; a choice of material, not a learning outcome). In the same article's second experiment, which also varied the student's achievement, achievement decided the choice and ethnicity did not: Student achievement level, not ethnic background, predicted preservice teachers' text-versus-video material choice in Study 2 [~M] (177 preservice teachers). A recorded result to tailor on is what that second experiment had and the first did not.
  4. Let each learner advance at their own rate through a sequenced continuum. Lay the unit out as an ordered series of short sections (about one lesson each), each ending with a short unaided check; a learner moves to the next section on passing it, and others get correction and a recheck. This works in whole-class, small-group or individual settings and does not need a one-to-one ratio. Untested proposal; the recommendation of the folded continuous-progress page (a 1974 curriculum guide, no claim cited). Where a learner is behind, the continuum should reach back below grade level along a learning progression: Teachers who made productive adaptations were more likely to consider learning progressions and adapt lessons to below-grade-level content [+W] (an association among teachers' adaptations, not a test of learner outcomes). Learners who start furthest behind are not the ones to hold back from a personalized path: In mathematics, personalized-learning students in the lowest baseline quintile were the most likely to out-grow their matched comparison students [+W] (schools matched to virtual comparison groups; a share of students out-growing their comparison, no effect size; mathematics only). The gate is modelled in Mastery Learning.
  5. Offer bounded choice among options that serve the same objective. Two to four tasks or media of equal rigour (a choice board), with the criterion shown on every option. Give novices and younger learners fewer options. Choice and autonomy support are expected to raise motivation, not achievement directly: Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M]; moderate rather than full agency, in one game-based study reported second-hand: Moderate learner agency in game-based learning yields better outcomes than no agency or high agency [~W]. A choice alone may do little; support that answers the learner's state does more: Robot adaptiveness effects on human autonomy are mixed: autonomy-supportive behaviours and as-needed assistance help, but mere choice or high interactivity may not [~W] (a human–robot interaction review; perceived autonomy, not learning). The number of options is an untested proposal.
  6. Set problems in contexts the learner named, and check that they fit. Rewrite (or generate) practice problems in the contexts learners gave in step 2, keeping the mathematics or structure unchanged; ask learners after a few problems whether the context is one they care about, and let them change it. Expert guidance recommends it: An IES practice-guide presentation recommends personalized problem contexts, asserting without cited studies that students engage more actively and perform better in them [+W]. Limits: assigned topics often missed students' interests (topic mismatch [-W]), and localizing names helped only in some schools (Localizing word-problem character names improved MATHia performance only for specific subgroup-majority schools, with no improvement in sense of belonging overall [~M]). Some topics do not fit the mathematics, and a generated rewrite then trades the structure against realism: The three refinement strategies make distinct trade-offs when personalizing problems to topics that resist natural alignment with the original math [~W] (one LLM-personalized problem, a case analysis). Check every rewritten problem against the original's mathematics before it is used, and offer a neutral context where the learner's topic does not fit.
  7. Where learners use different means to the same end, assess preference with the individual. For a communication system (speech-generating device, picture exchange, manual signs), an assistive tool or a format for access, offer the options in turn over several sessions, record which the learner chooses when free to choose and how quickly they acquire the target skill with each, and decide per learner, not by system type. Individuals with developmental disabilities tend to prefer speech-generating devices over picture exchange and manual signs, with substantial individual differences [+M]. The folded AAC page also reported that requesting skills were acquired faster with a preferred system for some children; the claim page records preferences, not that acquisition result. The number of sessions is an untested proposal.
  8. Do not tailor on learning styles. Do not sort learners by a visual, auditory or kinesthetic inventory and match the format to it; choose the format the content needs, for everyone. Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-S]; one of the few tests with the needed design, reported in the same review, found no match effect among medical residents: Cook et al. (2009) found no support for sensing/intuitive style-by-sequence interaction in medical education [-M] (123 internal medicine residents, problem-first against content-first modules; no effect size reported). In one media experiment series, format rather than a preference match decided who did better: In one author's classroom media experiments, intuitive learners tended to score above sensing learners, though not in every format comparison [~W] (sensing learners with voice and diagrams outscored intuitive learners with text only; a conference report, sample sizes not given). For the same reason, do not match a model, character or example to a group category assumed for the learner (gender, ethnicity, background) and expect learning to rise: in one experiment with a video model, Model gender and observer gender do not affect learning outcomes or near transfer from video modeling examples [-W] (a 2 × 2 experiment crossing model and observer gender, about 160 analysed; no main effect and no interaction on learning or near transfer, equivalence not tested), and matching character names to schools' majority groups helped only in some schools (localized names [~M]). Where a learner wants a context or model that reflects them, let them name or choose it (step 6); that is the learner's own account, not a group label.
  9. Review the tailoring weekly, and check it was enacted. Ten minutes a week: who advanced, who has been held at one section for two or more weeks, who chose the same easy option every time, and whether anyone has been routed around content the final assessment needs. Also check whether the easier options or slower paths have gathered in one group of learners whose check results do not differ from their peers'; if they have, the assignment followed an expectation rather than the work, and those learners should be re-placed from their results (easy video for the minority student [-M], above). Judge the personalization only where it was used. Personalized Learning Effects Vary With Fidelity [~M]; the weekly dose is an untested proposal. Judge it on the common check, not on a change in what learners do: in one study, prompts tailored to each learner's detected study behaviour changed that behaviour and not the test score (Personalized scaffolds increased frequencies of high cognition and monitoring relative to control, but learning outcomes did not differ between conditions. [~W]).

Dose and progression across sessions (untested proposal). Early sessions in a unit: the teacher sets each learner's path from the diagnostic, choices are between two options, and problem contexts are drawn by the teacher from the learner's list. Middle sessions: learners choose among three or four options and move through sections at their own pace with a self-check at the end of each. Late sessions: learners propose their own context or topic and plan the remaining sections, with the teacher reviewing the plan. A learner moves on from a section after passing its unaided check (for example, four of five items), and moves from teacher-set to self-chosen paths once they have passed two consecutive sections on the first attempt. Re-run the diagnostic at the start of each new unit, since expertise is specific to the topic.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time for a diagnostic, personalize only context and choice (steps 5–6) and keep the path common; if the options cannot be made equal in rigour, do not offer a choice; if no one will review the tailoring (step 9), keep the number of separate paths to two or three that a teacher can track.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learners differ in, on this task. Differences in prior knowledge call for tailored support and pace; differences in interest for tailored contexts and choice; differences in access for tailored means. If unstated, ask: on a short diagnostic, how far apart are the learners, and do any need a different way of taking part?
  • Who or what will do the tailoring, and how often they can look. Software can tailor every item; a teacher with thirty learners can tailor sections and groups weekly; a volunteer who checks in briefly can tailor one thing. If unstated, ask: who sees each learner's work, and how often?
  • Which outcome counts, and whether it is common. Engagement, achievement on an aligned test, time saved and a learner's own goal move separately, and a personalized path is only comparable if the final criterion is shared. If unstated, ask: what must every learner be able to do at the end, and how will it be checked?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a learner's response under stated conditions: diagnostic items scored by part, which option or context the learner chose, how long they stayed at a section, their check results, what they said they were interested in, and, for a communication system, which device they selected when free to choose and how many trials a request took. "Is a visual learner", "is interested in football", "is ready to move on" and "prefers the device" are inferred states. An interest a learner names once is a report, not a trait, and it can change.

Evidence and qualifications¶

None of the claims below tests personalization as a whole against uniform instruction. Each tests one kind of tailoring, or a limit on it.

  • In a randomized experiment, personalized paths reduced learning time by 22.0% and improved post-test scores versus a fixed sequence [+M]: 120 undergraduates randomly assigned to a diagnosis-based personalized path or a fixed order over the same probability-theory content; the personalized group took 57.6 against 73.8 minutes (d = 1.52) and scored higher on the post-test after controlling for pre-test (η² = 0.065). The companion claim reports lower self-reported load on all six adapted NASA-TLX dimensions (d 1.12 to 1.80): Personalized paths reduced self-reported cognitive load on all six adapted NASA-TLX dimensions with large effect sizes [+M]. It supports tailoring path and pace to a diagnosis; it is one experiment, one topic, one session's content and an immediate test. Its evidence line counts "2 studies", but both entries are the same experiment (Feng and Huang 2026), so the marker here is held at M.
  • ITS significantly outperform large-group human instruction, individual computer-based instruction, and textbooks or workbooks, but not small-group or individual human tutoring [+M]: a meta-analysis (Ma et al. 2014) finds intelligent tutoring systems ahead of large-group instruction (g = .44), individual computer-based instruction (g = .57) and textbooks (g = .36), and not different from small-group (g = .05) or individual human tutoring (g = -.11); equivalence was not tested. It supports individualized tutoring over non-individualized instruction and says that people tailoring for small groups do as well; it bundles tailoring with feedback and practice.
  • AI-enabled personalized STEM education yields a statistically significant overall effect of g = 0.455, which its authors class as small, compared with traditional non-AI instruction in K-12 schools [+M] and School level significantly moderates effects: AI-enabled personalized STEM education is more effective in junior and senior high school than in kindergarten [~M]: a meta-analysis of 99 effect sizes from 32 randomized trials (Li et al. 2025) of AI-enabled personalized STEM teaching against teaching without AI; g = 0.455 overall, with kindergarten g = 0.118 and elementary, junior and senior high g = 0.42 to 0.53 (QB = 16.1, p = .001). The comparison is AI-supported teaching against none, so the technology, its feedback and its tailoring come as one package; the school-level difference is between studies and may reflect what was taught and how at each level, not the learners' age as such.
  • Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+M]: an SDT review, a meta-analysis of 128 experiments showing that expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d = -0.4) and a conceptual framework of organizational, procedural and cognitive autonomy support. Its table gathers 8 distinct studies, one with an effect size coded. It supports offering choice for motivation; it is not a test of choosing tasks against assigned tasks on achievement, and its discussion says choice works best with structure and can make novices anxious. Marked M here, below its cap, because the strong study is about rewards.
  • Individuals with developmental disabilities tend to prefer speech-generating devices over picture exchange and manual signs, with substantial individual differences [+M]: an overview of two reviews (7 studies with 12 individuals; 17 studies); 50% showed a high preference for the speech-generating device against 30% for picture exchange, and 67% preferred the device across three options. It supports deciding the means per child, since a minority preferred each alternative; it does not show that the preferred system is learned faster.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies and a circuit-diagram experiment; guidance that helps novices can lose its value or hurt experienced learners. Its table gathers 13 distinct studies, one with an effect size coded. It is the reason to tailor support to prior knowledge and withdraw it; it gives no crossover point and measures expertise per topic. The narrower The effectiveness of instructional techniques and procedures interacts with levels of learner prior knowledge, a pattern known as the expertise reversal effect [+W] (Kalyuga 2007, a review) is one of those studies.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders tutored in long division with fully contingent support solved more problems than those given fixed, high, moderate or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition); interactive tutoring matched explanatory tutoring immediately and did better on transfer; a dynamic-assessment meta-analysis ranks scaffolding above coaching and below explicit strategy training. Its table gathers 25 distinct studies, 5 with an effect size coded. It supports tailoring support to each response; it is mostly one-to-one, no effect size is printed for the contingency contrasts, and it is held at M here because it bundles contingency with tutoring.
  • Reviews relaying engagement gains from tailored platforms, all [+W] and none printing a design or an effect size for the result relayed: Included studies suggest engagement increases when adaptive platform activities are tailored to learners' personal needs and emotional state [+W]; AI-driven personalized and adaptive learning pathways support learner engagement and satisfaction in online courses [+W]; A systematic review of AI in problem-based learning reports, as a dominant theme of its 50 studies, that AI tools support adaptive, personalized learning linked to better engagement and problem-solving [+W] (a thematic review of educators' AI use); The review reports AI-driven personalization improves student engagement and learning outcomes, and virtual teaching assistants support diverse learning needs [+W] (citing one study); and A technical review reports that one reinforcement-learning adaptive platform raised average course grades by 12%, task completion by 20% and cut dropout by 10% against static curricula (uncontrolled case report) [+W] (an institutional case study described in a technical review, STEM undergraduates; no assignment or standardized effect reported). Students' own preference points the same way: Nearly nine in ten students ranked a personalized video as their top choice [+W] (an end-of-term ranking by 493 students in two cohorts; 88.4% ranked a personalized video, human-recorded or AI-generated, first; a preference, not a learning outcome). They agree in direction with the engagement half of the relationship; they cannot say what was tailored, against what, or how large the change was.

Limits and qualifications:

  • Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-S]: a systematic review found only one study with the crossover design needed to test matching, and judged it unconvincing; a randomized study of 125 fifth graders found no style-by-modality interaction. Its table gathers 4 distinct studies. It bears against tailoring on style; it says nothing against tailoring on prior knowledge or access needs. The same review's account of Cook et al. (2009), 123 internal medicine residents given problem-first or content-first modules, found no sensing/intuitive style-by-sequence interaction (Cook et al. (2009) found no support for sensing/intuitive style-by-sequence interaction in medical education [-M]); no effect size is printed.

  • Preservice teachers chose the easy video significantly more often for an ethnic minority student than for an ethnic majority student, all other information being equal [-M]: a between-subjects vignette experiment (Schlag and Glock 2024), 108 preservice teachers choosing an easy or difficult video for an online lesson; 82% chose the easy video for the minority-background student and 11% for the majority-background one (χ²(1) = 101.32). In the second experiment, 177 preservice teachers, which varied achievement as well, achievement predicted the choice and ethnicity did not (achievement, not ethnic background, predicted the choice [~M]). Read together: when a teacher tailors with little information about the learner's work, the tailoring can follow expectations attached to a group; the group is not a reason the learner needs easier material. Neither experiment measured learning.

  • Students frequently reported that personalized problem topics did not match their interests [-W]: of 235 student comments on 47 teacher-personalized problems, 83 said the topic was one they liked and 160 that it was not; a related claim records students asking to input their own interests, and one objecting to personalization itself (Students critiqued the broad grain size of teacher-in-the-loop personalization and personalization itself met resistance [~W]). It limits interest personalization done by group label or teacher guess.

  • Localizing word-problem character names improved MATHia performance only for specific subgroup-majority schools, with no improvement in sense of belonging overall [~M]: a randomized field experiment in five districts; performance improved in some subgroup-majority schools and not others, and belonging did not change. No effect size is printed. Surface personalization does not reliably transfer across groups.
  • Latent learner profiles did not moderate the effectiveness of the adaptive ITS [~M]: in an experiment with 132 early-primary pupils, profiles built from prior knowledge, conscientiousness, interest and self-concept did not moderate an adaptive tutor's effect, and the adaptive tutor did not beat the non-adaptive one. Tailoring on a learner profile is not shown to help here.
  • Personalized scaffolds increased frequencies of high cognition and monitoring relative to control, but learning outcomes did not differ between conditions. [~W]: self-regulation prompts whose options were tailored to each learner's study behaviour as detected in real time, against a control condition; the tailored group showed more high-level cognition and monitoring, with no difference in the overall temporal structure of studying and none in learning outcomes. The companion claim shows the tailoring did happen, unevenly across the five prompts (real-time detection personalizes scaffold content [+W], 18 learners). No effect size is printed; the null is not a test of equivalence. A change in process is not a gain in learning.
  • Model gender and observer gender do not affect learning outcomes or near transfer from video modeling examples [-W]: a 2 × 2 experiment crossing the video model's gender with the learner's; no main effect and no interaction on learning or near transfer (F(1, 158) = 1.71 for model gender, other Fs < 1). The authors' proposal to make male and female versions and let learners choose or allocate adaptively is not supported on learning; equivalence was not tested, and other outcomes (for example, self-efficacy) are not covered by this claim.
  • Personalized Learning Effects Vary With Fidelity [~M]: about 40 schools implementing personalized-learning practices, matched to virtual comparison groups; one-year effects +0.09 SD in mathematics (significant) and +0.07 SD in reading (not significant), larger where implementation was reported fuller or longer, which the authors call suggestive. Its table gathers 4 distinct studies. The same report finds that in mathematics the students with the lowest baseline scores were the most likely to out-grow their matched comparison students (lowest baseline quintile [+W]; a share of students, no effect size). A school-level program is far smaller than the experiments above suggest, and an adopted program is not an enacted one.

Keep each finding's comparator, population, outcome and horizon with it. Do not rank the undergraduate path experiment beside the school-level SD gains, or read engagement findings as achievement findings.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does setting problems in contexts drawn from learners' interests raise engagement and performance? An IES practice-guide presentation recommends personalized problem contexts, asserting without cited studies that students engage more actively and perform better in them [+W]
  • Do learners in personalized learning settings achieve more than peers taught the same content uniformly? A narrative review of intelligent tutoring systems asserts, citing one source, that students in personalized learning environments outperform peers in standardized settings [+W]
  • What should the adaptation be based on: responses, or learner styles and preferences? Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-S]
  • Does matching a video model's gender to the learner's raise learning? Model gender and observer gender do not affect learning outcomes or near transfer from video modeling examples [-W]
  • When a teacher chooses easier or harder material, does the choice follow the learner's results or a group label? Preservice teachers chose the easy video significantly more often for an ethnic minority student than for an ethnic majority student, all other information being equal [-M]; Student achievement level, not ethnic background, predicted preservice teachers' text-versus-video material choice in Study 2 [~M]
  • Do gains from AI-personalized teaching differ by school level? School level significantly moderates effects: AI-enabled personalized STEM education is more effective in junior and senior high school than in kindergarten [~M]
  • Do prompts tailored to learners' detected study behaviour improve learning? Personalized scaffolds increased frequencies of high cognition and monitoring relative to control, but learning outcomes did not differ between conditions. [~W]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a common capability (the unit's goal, at a stated criterion and horizon) reached by more learners, sooner, with tailoring as the means. Elicit separately what each learner wants from it: to finish faster, to work on things they care about, to avoid being placed on a slower path in front of others, to be able to communicate a request at home, or, for some, not to have their interests tied to a subject they dislike. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being seen as behind may refuse a tailored path that is visible to peers; one who values their own interests may engage with a context and still not learn the structure underneath.

For example, a child learning to request with an AAC system may value being understood by their family at home, while the designer's objective is a number of independent requests in class. The preferred device may serve the first and not the second, or the reverse. Record both, and choose the device with the family's setting in view.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if experiments that hold content and final assessment constant find personalized paths no better than a fixed sequence on delayed or transfer outcomes, or if the time saving in the one path experiment here does not replicate beyond one topic and one session. If learner-named contexts do not raise interest or achievement more than standard contexts in randomized comparisons, step 6 should be dropped to a motivational option. If preference-matched AAC systems are not acquired or maintained better than non-preferred ones, preference assessment becomes a matter of the child's voice rather than learning. If school-level personalization stays near +0.1 SD even with full implementation, the model's gains belong to the tutoring and diagnosis, not to personalization as a program. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "not personalized enough" after the fact.

Engagement, immediate achievement on an aligned test, time saved, delayed retention and the outcome a learner values are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of options, no diagnostic length and no pace threshold.

Illustrative¶

Adaptive Learning — Platform-driven personalization in which item difficulty and sequencing adjust continuously to response accuracy and latency. Used at scale in mathematics and language learning; effectiveness depends on mastery gating rather than mere content variety.

ASSISTments — Free web-based math platform (grades 6–12) that routes students through problem sets with step-level hints and immediate feedback, effectively personalizing the support each student receives during practice.

Khan Academy — Mastery-based personalization: learners progress through skill maps at their own pace, with the system recommending review of prerequisite skills when gaps are detected. Widely used in flipped and blended classrooms.

Choice Boards — A teacher-designed personalization pattern: learners select from a curated set of tasks aligned to the same objective, allowing choice of modality or topic while preserving common outcomes. Works best when all options are genuinely equivalent in rigor.

Carnegie Learning MATHia — Intelligent tutoring system that models each learner's knowledge at the skill level and personalizes problem selection and hint delivery, gating advancement on demonstrated mastery.

Duolingo — Language-learning app that personalizes review scheduling (spaced repetition of items the individual learner has struggled with) and adapts session difficulty; a consumer-scale example of adaptive item selection.
