# Dual Coding

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 15 claims (7 for, 6 mixed, 2 against) · 24 studies (10 review, 8 causal, 6 quant-synthesis), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 24 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

Some ideas have a structure that a picture can show directly: where parts sit, what causes what, the order of a process, how categories nest, how two things compare. For a learner who cannot yet describe that structure from words alone (a `novice` on this content), pairing the words that explain the idea with a visual that depicts the same idea is expected to improve comprehension and recall (`conceptual-understanding`, `immediate-performance`) over the words alone. The visual can be supplied with the text (a diagram, map, flow chart or labelled picture), or produced by the learner (a drawing, a concept map, a completed organizer). The visual has to carry the idea; a decorative image does not count and can cost something.

Claims test the two halves separately against words alone, mostly on immediate tests. A meta-analysis of 39 experiments found text with graphics beat text alone on reading comprehension, from elementary school to adults. Meta-analyses found learner drawing in STEM and learner-built concept maps beat comparison conditions. **No claim tests the sequence below, the dose, or the move from a supplied visual to a learner-made one**, and the two direct tests of constructing against studying run the other way: provided organizers gave better transfer than learner-built ones in three experiments, and building a concept map while viewing a text did no better than rereading, and worse than practising recall, a week later. Whether gains last (`delayed-retention`) or reach new problems (`near-transfer`) is a separate claim that the evidence here barely touches.

The relationship is conditional on four things: whether the content has a visual structure at all; the learner's prior knowledge of it (a supplied visual may be redundant for an `advanced` learner); whether a learner-made visual is checked against a correct one; and how much time construction takes compared with other study. It does not rest on Paivio's two-code mechanism ([Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) describes it): one set of experiments found the picture-superiority effect in memory followed physical distinctiveness, not dual coding. The design advice stands on comparisons of materials and activities, whatever the mechanism.

How the converted siblings sit beside this page: [Multimedia Learning](multimedia-learning.md) holds the presented explanation with a picture, where the picture goes, duplicated words, decorative additions and segmenting; this page does not repeat that model. [Multimodal Instruction](multimodal-instruction.md) holds the choice of modes beyond words and a picture, spoken against written words, and translation between representations. This page holds the wider study practice: a visual paired with the words of a reading or a lesson, and the learner's own visual of the same idea, across several sessions. [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) holds the comparison with recall from memory, which this page uses in its last steps. [Creating Visual Representations](creating-visual-representations.md), not yet converted, describes learner-made visuals as a general activity.

## Default design

A concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Steps 2 and 4 rest on claims that compare against words alone or no drawing; the rest are untested proposals, mostly from the earlier page.

1. **Check that the idea has a visual form.** Use a visual where the content is a structure, a spatial relation, a sequence, a process, a comparison or a hierarchy. Where it is a list of unrelated items or an argument with no spatial or causal shape, a visual imposes an arbitrary structure; skip it. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("clarify relationships, timing, scale, or process").
2. **Supply one relevant visual for each key idea, labelled in the words of the text.** A picture, pictorial diagram or flow diagram all serve; choose the one that shows the relation the words describe. Remove images that are there only to look good. Basis: [text with graphics against text alone](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (39 experiments, reading comprehension, elementary to adult; picture types did not differ); [decorative illustrations](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-M] (no gain, and they weakened the benefit of instructional pictures for 7th and 8th graders with lower prior knowledge). Placement and labelling are modelled on [Multimedia Learning](multimedia-learning.md).
3. **Ask for the link, not just the look.** On the first encounter, give two or three prompts per visual: point to the part of the diagram this sentence names; say in words what the arrow shows; what in the text does this part of the picture stand for? Untested proposal (the claim pages suggest, without a study, that a visual nobody connects to the words does less).
4. **Then have learners make the visual themselves, with support that fades.** Start from a partly completed diagram or map (key nodes given, links to label), then a template, then a blank page. Basis: [drawing in STEM](../claims/drawing-improves-learning.md) [+M] (a meta-analysis read from its abstract: drawing beat not drawing and beat imaging, explaining and re-watching; scaffolds saved time but did not raise outcomes); [concept mapping](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [+M] (constructing maps showed a larger benefit than studying them, each against its own comparison). Qualified by [learner-built against provided organizers](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [~M]: in three experiments with college readers, provided organizers gave better transfer than learner-built ones. The fading sequence is an untested proposal.
5. **Check each learner-made visual against a correct one.** Have learners compare their drawing or map with a model, mark what is missing or wrong, and fix it, before it is used for study. Untested proposal; the claim pages warn, without a study, that an unchecked wrong diagram can fix a misconception.
6. **Follow the visual with recall, not more viewing.** Once a learner has seen or made the visual, have them redraw it or explain it from memory, without the text, then check. Basis: [concept mapping against retrieval practice](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [~M]: with learning time matched, undergraduates who practised free recall beat those who built a concept map while viewing the text a week later, and mapping was not significantly better than rereading. That the pairing of a visual with recall helps is an untested proposal.
7. **Plan the arc across sessions.** A proposed default for a unit of about four sessions, untested: session 1, the supplied visual with the text and the linking prompts (about 10 minutes); sessions 2 and 3, a partly completed diagram to finish, then compare with the model (10–15 minutes); from session 4, open with a 5-minute redraw from memory of an earlier visual and check it, before new material. In mathematics, move from a picture of the quantity to the symbols across the same sessions, saying how each part of the picture maps onto the symbol ([concreteness fading](../claims/manipulatives-require-connection-to-concept.md) [~M], a review about concrete materials, carried here to pictures).
8. **Move on when the learner can redraw it.** A learner moves on when they can draw the visual from memory with every key relation correct and explain each link in words, on two separate days. Untested proposal; set the criterion locally and record it.
9. **Judge it on a task that needs the relation.** Use open-ended or mixed items (explain, predict, sketch), and decide in advance whether the visual may be consulted at the test. Basis: in the graphics meta-analysis, graphics helped more on open-ended and mixed-format tests than on true/false ones ([text with graphics](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]).

When a condition is not met: if learners already have the model (`advanced`), drop the supplied visuals and go straight to a redraw-from-memory check; if there is no time for construction, keep the supplied visual and the linking prompts and use recall rather than mapping; if no one can check learner-made visuals, supply the model answer with the task.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the content has a visual structure.** A diagram helps where it shows a relation the words describe; for list-like or purely verbal content it adds load and no meaning. If unstated, ask: what is the one relation (where, what causes what, in what order, which is part of which) a learner must grasp, and can it be drawn?
- **The learner's prior knowledge of this content.** A supplied visual is a support for a learner who cannot build the model alone; for one who can, it may add nothing. If unstated, ask: can these learners, before instruction, sketch or explain the structure unaided?
- **Time, and who checks learner-made visuals.** Drawing and mapping are slow, and a wrong diagram studied as right does harm. If unstated, ask: how many minutes per idea are there, and who will compare a learner's drawing with a correct one?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |

The large-class row is an untested proposal, and the redraw half of the advanced row is untested.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a learner's work under stated conditions: which visual they had or made, how long it took, what support they had, what they drew or labelled, what they got wrong, and what they could say or draw later without the material. "Has a mental model", "made a referential connection" and "is a visual thinker" are inferred states. A neat drawing shows drawing skill as well as understanding; a learner's liking for illustrated pages does not show that the pictures helped.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Labels the supplied diagram correctly but cannot explain the process in words | The labels were matched by position, not understood; the diagram shows parts but not the relation; the test asks for words the learner lacks | Ask what happens when one part changes, with the diagram present and then removed; allow an answer by drawing or pointing. |
| Learner-made drawing is detailed but leaves out the key relation | Effort went to appearance; the learner does not see which relation matters; the instruction asked for a picture, not a model | Ask for a second drawing limited to five labelled parts and the arrows between them; compare with a model and ask what is missing. |
| Does well with the visual in view, poorly a week later | The visual carried the structure and was not learned; little recall practice; interference from later material | Give a redraw-from-memory task at the delay, and record all practice in between. |
| Concept map has many nodes and few labelled links | Mapping was treated as listing; the learner lacks the relations; the format was not taught | Ask the learner to label three links with a verb and explain one aloud; give a partly completed map with links to label. |
| Spends most of the time drawing and learns no more than from reading | Construction cost exceeded its benefit; low drawing skill; the content had no visual structure | Compare with a supplied visual plus linking prompts for the next idea, with time held equal. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe is itself practice and changes what it measures, so record each one and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Dual Coding Improves Learning](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 39 experiments (2,103 participants, 1985–2018; `synthesis-experimental`) comparing reading text with graphics against text alone found g = 0.39, 95% CI [0.26, 0.51], with moderate heterogeneity and no significant publication bias. The effect held across grade levels, picture types did not differ, and graphics helped more on open-ended and mixed-format tests. The outcome is reading comprehension, and some included studies found diagrams reduced verbatim recall. A narrative review (abstract only) reaches the same direction. The same page records experiments finding the picture-superiority effect follows distinctiveness, not dual coding, which challenges the mechanism the claim's opening states, not the effect. The page's title is broader than its evidence (comprehension from reading).
- [Drawing Improves Learning](../claims/drawing-improves-learning.md) [+M]: a conference meta-analysis of 35 STEM studies (160 effects, 3,635 participants), read from its abstract: drawing beat not drawing (g = 0.362) and other active strategies (g = 0.400); scaffolds saved time (g = 1.693) but did not improve outcomes; larger benefit in secondary than primary school; online drawing benefited more from scaffolds than paper drawing. **The claim page's opening still says its evidence base "has not yet been populated"**, which its own entry contradicts.
- [Concept mapping improves learning](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [+M] for construction, [~M] against retrieval: across 142 effect sizes (11,814 participants, abstract only), learning with maps beat comparison conditions (g = 0.58), constructing (g = 0.72) more than studying (g = 0.43), in STEM and non-STEM domains; an earlier meta-analysis of 55 studies (Grade 4 to postsecondary) agreed in direction. But with time matched, 80 undergraduates who practised free recall after studying a science text beat those who built a concept map while viewing it a week later (d = 1.50), and mapping was not significantly better than rereading; a second experiment (120 students) replicated the retrieval advantage, even on a map-building test. The page's own Discussion still says no studies are catalogued, which is stale.
- [Learner Constructed Graphic Organizers Outperform Provided](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [~M], **the limiting claim**: in three experiments, college readers given author-provided graphic organizers did better on transfer than those who built their own (d = 0.24, not significant; 0.43; 0.84), with no difference on retention; the meta-analytic contrast favouring construction sets each condition against its own controls. The claim page says its title is a hypothesis the direct evidence does not support. It is why step 4 is scaffolded and checked rather than "let learners make their own".
- [Graphic Organizers Support Novice Comprehension](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [~M]: concept maps were associated with better retention overall (g = 0.60 across 67 effect sizes), less when studying a preconstructed map instead of text, lists or outlines (g = 0.37); in a few studies split at the median, lower-ability or lower prior knowledge learners benefited (g = 0.40, k = 5) and higher-ability learners did not (g = −0.13), subsets the authors call low-powered. It supports, weakly, supplying visuals to novices and withdrawing them for others.
- [Decorative Illustrations Do Not Improve Learning](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-M]: a seductive-details meta-analysis (abstract only), three experiments with 7th and 8th graders (decorative pictures neither helped nor harmed overall but weakened the benefit of instructional pictures, most for lower prior knowledge), and an e-learning study with undergraduates (a very small comprehension cost, η² = .04; in harder texts, harm only for students with low attentional inhibition). It limits "add a visual" to visuals that carry the idea.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (readers of a text, STEM drawing, concept maps against recall), so do not rank them by effect size. None tests a supplied-then-constructed sequence or a delayed outcome of the full design.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims the earlier page cited or that bear on this page but have not been used in the model above.

- [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~S] — cited by the earlier page for the idea that a visual reduces the burden of inferring structure. It is about working-memory capacity and chunking, with no visual condition, so it does not test dual coding; its sources are reviews and one experiment, read from abstracts.
- [Dual Coding Improves Recall](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [~M] — carries the same three evidence entries as Dual Coding Improves Learning (a merge candidate, as both pages say), and its outcome is comprehension, not recall, so its title overstates recall.
- [Different media combinations significantly affect the recall and retention of information](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+W] — one in-class conference experiment (n=37) reporting results in the direction dual coding theory predicts, with no effect size; no source text was available to check it.
- [Presenting words together with instructionally relevant pictures (multimedia principle) is associated with improved post-test learning outcomes, particularly when words are delivered orally](../claims/multimedia-principle-words-and-pictures-outcomes.md) [+W] — a practice-to-theory article reporting others' findings second-hand, no effect sizes; it belongs with [Multimedia Learning](multimedia-learning.md).

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually that the learner can explain, predict or use the structure the visual shows, unaided, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to get through the reading, to remember it for an exam, to understand how something in their own life works, to have notes they can revise from. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values neat notes may spend the time on drawing quality; one revising for a recall test may prefer to reread a diagram rather than redraw it, which is the weaker choice for that goal.

For example, a nursing student may want to understand why a patient's blood pressure drops, while the designer's objective is labelling the parts of the heart. A labelled diagram serves the objective; a learner-drawn flow diagram of what happens when one part fails, checked against a model, tests the capability the student came for. Record both.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with comparable learners and aligned outcomes find no advantage of a relevant supplied visual over text alone, or if the advantage seen on immediate tests is absent at a delay. If constructing visuals continues to lose to provided ones on transfer, or to recall practice with time matched, step 4 should give way to supplied visuals followed by redrawing from memory. If prompts to link words and visual add nothing over the visual alone, drop step 3. If the benefit of visuals turns out to depend on distinctiveness rather than meaning, the advice to remove decorative images would need re-examining. Do not protect the model by calling every failure a poor diagram or low drawing skill after the fact.

Comprehension while the visual is in view, recall a week later, transfer to a new problem and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of visuals per lesson, no length of drawing activity and no criterion for moving from supplied to learner-made visuals.
