# Annotating

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 9 claims (5 for, 3 mixed, 1 against) · 19 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 19 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a reader working through a text they will need to use later (an expository, disciplinary or argumentative passage, read alone or before discussion or writing), annotating means marking the text while reading: highlighting or underlining, margin codes, notes, questions, summaries. The relationship this page states has two parts, and the evidence for them is very different.

1. **Marking (highlighting, underlining).** Marking directs attention to the marked material. Later performance on test items about that material may improve. Items about unmarked material do not, and neither does comprehension of the passage as a whole. The wiki's evidence here is for immediate or short-delay tests (`immediate-performance`, some `delayed-retention`), mostly with university students (`adult`). What matters is *which* content gets marked, more than *who* marks it.
2. **Elaborative annotation (notes that paraphrase, justify, question or summarize).** The expectation is that such notes support comprehension (`conceptual-understanding`) and later use in discussion or writing, because they require the reader to select, explain or question. **No claim in the wiki tests learner-written margin notes against reading or against highlighting.** The nearest evidence is for neighbouring activities that are not annotation: trained question generation during reading, and self-explanation. This part of the relationship is a transported proposal, not an observed effect.

Learner state conditions both parts. A reader with no criteria for what is relevant (`novice` for this text and task, whatever their general reading level) may mark the wrong material, and marking then shifts attention toward it. The relationship is stated for a learner-made annotation with a follow-up use. Instructor-supplied highlighting is a different intervention and is noted under the evidence. Learners annotating together in a shared text is a configuration of this principle, described at [Social Annotation](../strategies/social_annotation.md). The element is [Text Underlining and Annotating](../elements/text-underlining-and-annotating.md). No converted pattern sits beside this principle.

## Observation, state and explanation

An annotated page is an observation: what was marked, how much, in what form, with what written content. Comprehension, monitoring and "deep processing" are inferred states, and the page itself does not show them. A densely marked page can come from careful selection or from marking almost everything. A sparse page can come from confident understanding or from disengagement. Record the task purpose given, the prompts or codes supplied, whether annotation was modelled, the reader's prior knowledge of the topic, and the follow-up task. Then interpret the annotations against a separate probe of comprehension.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Most of the text is highlighted; few or no written notes | No criteria for relevance; text too difficult to judge importance; marking used as a sign of effort to be seen | Ask the reader to name the three most important ideas and say why. Compare with a passage on a familiar topic. Ask what they thought the marking was for. |
| Marks fall on peripheral detail; main claims unmarked | Misreads the text's structure; purpose of the reading unclear; prior knowledge directs attention to familiar detail | Give the reading purpose explicitly and re-observe on a matched passage. Probe structure separately (e.g. "what is the author's main claim?"). |
| Margin notes copy phrases from the text | Does not know what an elaborative note looks like; text beyond current comprehension; time pressure | Show one modelled paraphrase or question and observe the next section. Check comprehension orally without the notes. Vary the time allowed. |
| Rich notes and questions, but weak performance on a later inference or writing task | Local understanding not integrated across the text; notes never used afterwards; the task demands something the notes did not target | Compare a section-level probe with a whole-text inference item. Check whether the reader returned to the notes during the follow-up task. |
| Annotates well with prompts or codes, little without them | Prompt-dependent strategy; prompts supplied the relevance judgement; no reason to annotate when nothing follows | Fade the prompts over matched texts and record what changes. Ask whether the reader annotates for their own purposes outside class. |

These rows are proposals. None has been tested with learners, and a single probe can shift confidence between explanations but does not identify a cause. A probe is itself an exposure that can change the reader's state, so record every one.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Learner highlighting of text gives a small memory benefit but no reliable comprehension benefit, and evidence on other forms of annotation is not yet recorded here](../claims/annotating-improves-learning.md) [~M]: the claim's title is broader than its evidence. Both entries concern highlighting or underlining, not written annotation. A meta-analysis (q4, 36 articles, 85 effect sizes, college and K-12 students, read from the abstract only) compared reading with and without being asked to highlight. Learner highlighting improved memory for the text (0.36) but not comprehension (0.20), and helped college students (0.39) but not school students (0.24). Highlighting supplied by the instructor improved both memory and comprehension (0.44 each). The second entry, a review, rates highlighting low utility. The claim does not test margin notes, questions or summaries, and its own Discussion says so. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [Highlighting shows low utility for improving learning outcomes](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-M]: a narrative review (q3, `review · r3`, no pooled effect size) of highlighting and underlining across Air Force trainees, children and undergraduates, with immediate and delayed tests (up to two months). Most of its primary studies found no benefit over simply reading. One found that underlining hurt performance on inference questions requiring connections across the text and left factual recall unaffected. This limits part 1 to marked content and recall, and is a reason to expect that unguided marking will not improve comprehension. Not settled: the abstract available could not confirm the entry.
- [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M]: one experiment with 67 college freshmen, who read passages with underlining on high-relevant or low-relevant content. Test scores rose on whichever content was underlined (F(1, 49) = 13.56; F(1, 40) = 6.98), regardless of its instructional importance. Testing was immediate. The `i2` code is not backed by a printed effect size, only F and p. This supports part 1's mechanism, and also its cost: marking the wrong content shifts performance toward it. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [In one experiment, students who generated their own underlining did not score significantly higher on tests than students given experimenter-generated underlining](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M]: in the same study, students who did their own underlining did not perform significantly better than students given the experimenter's underlining. The claim's title says "as effective as", but a non-significant difference does not establish equivalence, and this entry reports no n. Read with the relevancy claim, the evidence suggests that which content is marked matters more than the act of marking. It does not test elaborative annotation. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [Self-questioning improves comprehension](../claims/self-questioning-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]: the nearest evidence for part 2, though the activity is not annotation. Students in a review of intervention studies were *taught* to generate their own questions during reading. Comprehension on new material at the end of training improved, with a median effect of 0.36 on standardized tests and 0.86 on experimenter-developed tests (abstract only). A second review of 35 experiments with K-12 students, with and without disabilities, reports the strategy effective, with no effect size given. It supports question annotations only if they are taught and practised as question generation. It does not show that writing questions in the margin, untaught, has the same effect. Not yet checked against its sources.

Across these, outcomes are mostly immediate or short-delay tests of the passage read, and the populations are mostly university students. Do not read a highlighting null as evidence against written notes, or a question-generation gain as evidence for margin notes. They are different activities. The wiki has no claim that compares annotation formats directly, or that reports whether annotations used in a later discussion or essay improve it.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

Ask what the reader wants from the text and from annotating it, separately from the designer's objective. The two may agree, diverge, or be unknown; record which. Compliance with an annotation routine is not evidence that the reader values it. Then choose the outcome the annotation is meant to serve and the instrument that shows it: recall of marked content, comprehension of the whole passage, an inference across the text, or the quality of a later discussion contribution or essay. These need different evidence, and the annotated page is not that evidence.

For example, a nursing student may value getting through a long clinical guideline quickly and marking "what will be on the exam". The designer wants the student to be able to explain why one recommendation applies to one patient group and not another. Heavy highlighting serves the first aim and, on the evidence above, probably not the second. A design can keep both: let the student mark, then ask for one margin question per section about when the recommendation applies, and check that capability with a case item, not a recall item.

## What would revise this model?

Part 1 should weaken if well-selected learner highlighting fails to improve performance on the marked content in comparable readers, or if it improves comprehension of the whole passage, against the meta-analytic and review evidence above. Part 2 is the larger gap. It would gain support from a comparison of learner-written elaborative annotations against reading, or against highlighting, with comprehension or transfer outcomes and a stated delay. It would lose support if such a comparison found no difference once reading time is matched, or if trained question generation helped while margin annotation did not. If prompts or modelling explain the whole gain, the active ingredient is the prompt, not the annotation, and the model should say so. Do not protect it by attributing every null to poor annotation quality after the fact.

## Description
Annotating is the practice of marking a text with notes, questions, summaries, definitions, or visual cues while reading. Done well, it turns reading from passive exposure into active sense-making: learners identify what matters, track confusion, connect ideas to prior knowledge, and leave behind an external record of their thinking that can support discussion, writing, and review.

## Implications
Annotating improves reading when it makes thinking visible rather than just making the page colorful. Notes that paraphrase, question, summarize, or justify why a passage matters tend to support comprehension more than simple highlighting because they force learners to explain and organize ideas while reading [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Section summaries, margin codes, and lightweight note structures can also reduce the burden of holding a complex text together in working memory by turning it into manageable units [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. That is why annotation works best with a clear purpose, light structure, and a follow-up use such as discussion or writing; otherwise it easily becomes a ritual of marking without understanding, especially for learners who have not yet been shown what useful annotation looks like [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text or media artifact worth interrogating, not just skimming
- Clear annotation purposes such as tracking claims, defining terms, noting evidence, marking confusion, or summarizing sections
- Visible structures for annotation such as margin space, shared codes, prompts, or digital tools
- A follow-up task like discussion, synthesis, or writing so annotations become part of learning rather than busywork

#### Constraints
- Highlighting without explanation often creates the appearance of study without improving understanding
- Over-annotation can split attention and disrupt overall comprehension, especially in dense or unfamiliar texts
- Learners need modeling; many do not naturally know what useful annotations look like
- Annotation is less effective when texts are far beyond the learner's reading level or when the task lacks a clear purpose

### Target Learners
- Learners reading dense informational, disciplinary, or argumentative texts
- Learners who need support monitoring comprehension and distinguishing main ideas from detail
- Learners preparing for seminar discussion, evidence-based writing, or source evaluation
- Learners who benefit from externalizing thought processes to reduce memory burden during reading

### Target Learning Objectives
- Improve comprehension of complex texts through active engagement
- Strengthen metacognitive monitoring by noticing confusion, assumptions, and gaps
- Support evidence gathering for writing, discussion, and analysis
- Build habits of purposeful rereading, summarizing, and question generation

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Constructivist and sociocultural perspectives support annotation as a way for learners to actively construct meaning rather than merely receive it
- Metacognitive accounts support annotation as an external trace of comprehension monitoring, question generation, and repair
- Self-regulated learning frameworks support annotation when learners use it intentionally to set purposes, track understanding, and prepare next steps
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) qualifies annotation as helpful when it organizes attention, but potentially harmful if it adds unnecessary task demands

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Annotation quality matters more than annotation quantity
- Prompted annotation is usually stronger for novices than open-ended "mark whatever seems important"
- The strategy is most useful when learners explain why something matters, not when they only mark it [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
