# Handouts/Online Guides/Visual Reading Aids

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 17 claims (9 for, 6 mixed, 2 against) · 32 studies (11 causal, 10 quant-synthesis, 7 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 32 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **supplied reading aid as an artifact**: the printed handout, the online guide page or the visual sheet that a teacher, trainer or designer hands to learners to use beside a text, lecture, video or task. It decides what goes on the aid, what the reader is asked to do with it, what format it takes, and when it is withdrawn.

For a reader who cannot yet tell what matters in a dense or unfamiliar text or task (a `novice` on this material, whatever their general reading level), an aid that (a) marks the few essential points, (b) shows how the parts are organized, (c) explains unfamiliar terms beside where they occur, and (d) asks the reader to do something with it (fill a gap, answer a "why" prompt, complete an organizer) is expected to improve comprehension and recall of that material (`immediate-performance`, `conceptual-understanding`) over the same material without the aid. Whether the gain lasts (`delayed-retention`) or carries to reading new texts without the aid (`far-transfer`) is a separate question this page cannot settle.

The relationship is conditional on four things. **What the reader already knows**: cues and organizers that help a novice can add load for a reader who already sees the structure. **What the aid points at**: readers learn what is marked, whether or not it matters, so a wrongly chosen emphasis teaches the wrong thing. **What is on the aid besides the essentials**: decoration and interesting extras cost learning. **What the reader does with it**: an aid only read is weaker than one the reader completes or explains.

**What the evidence tests, and what it does not.** No claim in this wiki tests a handout, online reading guide or visual reading aid as a whole against the same text without one. Claims test the components one at a time: cues that mark the essential material (meta-analyses), guided notes (a meta-analysis read from its abstract and one quasi-experiment), concept maps and pictures added to text (meta-analyses), glosses for second-language readers (one review citation and one experiment), and prompts to self-explain (meta-analyses). The default design below assembles those components and labels each step with its evidence status.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page: [Signaling](signaling.md) holds the cue itself (headings, bold, arrows, colour links) and its dose; [Graphic Organizers](graphic-organizers.md) holds the diagram form and who builds it; [Note-Taking](note-taking.md) holds guided notes as a note-taking support during a lecture; [Annotating](annotating.md) holds marks the learner makes on the text; [Dual Coding](dual-coding.md) holds pairing words with a picture; [Clear Structure](clear-structure.md) holds making an organization explicit in general; [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) holds the wording; [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) holds withdrawing support. This page does not repeat those models; it says how to put their parts on one sheet or page a learner keeps, and how to use and retire it.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

A concrete default a designer can act on and revise. The whole-aid design is an untested proposal; each step's evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Decide what the reading is for, then what the reader must find.** Write the task in one line at the top of the aid ("Read to find the three causes and how they connect"; "Read to fill in the blanks, then answer the two questions"). Pick the three to seven points the task needs; leave the rest of the text unmarked. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("alignment to the reading task").
2. **Mark only those points, and mark the structure.** Use headings or numbered sections that follow the text's own organization, and bold or a margin arrow only for the essential terms and claims. Cues that mark the essential material help learning on average: [Signaling Improves Learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+S] (meta-analyses of multimedia lessons; the hub table counts 7 distinct studies behind it). But readers learn whatever is marked, including low-relevance material: [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M] (67 college freshmen, underlined text; its table counts 5 distinct studies). Check every mark against the task in step 1.
3. **Group the content into a few labelled chunks.** Put the essential points into three to five labelled groups rather than a long list. [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S] (memory research; its table counts 4 distinct studies; a chunk helps only if it is meaningful to the reader). The number of groups is an untested proposal.
4. **Where the content has a definite structure, add one diagram of it.** A sequence, a hierarchy, a comparison or a cause-and-effect chain goes in a simple node-and-link or table form beside the text; a picture of a process or object goes next to the words that explain it. [Graphic Organizers Support Novice Comprehension](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+S] (concept-map meta-analyses, Grade 4 to postsecondary; studying a provided map gave a smaller benefit than building one, against different comparisons). [Adding relevant pictures or graphics to text improves learning from it, with a small pooled effect on reading comprehension, though whether dual coding explains the benefit is disputed](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+S] (39 experiments, elementary school to adults). How to choose the form is in [Graphic Organizers](graphic-organizers.md).
5. **Gloss unfamiliar terms beside where they occur.** Give a short meaning in the margin or on hover, in the reader's first language where the reader is learning in a second, with a picture where the term names something visible. For second-language vocabulary: [Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses](../claims/glossing-medium-large-effects-l2-vocabulary.md) [+M] (a review citing earlier meta-analyses; outcome is vocabulary, not comprehension). For other readers, an untested proposal.
6. **Leave gaps for the reader to fill, and ask one or two "why" questions.** In a lecture or video guide, give an outline with blanks for key terms and points; at the end of each section, one prompt asking the reader to explain why a step follows or how two parts connect. Guided-note handouts produced more accurate notes and better course performance: [Guided Notes Improve Note Accuracy](../claims/guided-notes-improve-note-accuracy.md) [+S] (a meta-analysis read from its abstract, mostly school-age; one quasi-experiment with 65 undergraduates, including a delayed posttest). Prompting learners to explain improves understanding on immediate tests: [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] (meta-analyses; prompts on specific steps, not global summaries). Combining the two on one aid is an untested proposal.
7. **Cut everything else.** No decorative images, clip art, background patterns, extra "fun facts" or sidebars that the task does not need; keep generous spacing and readable type. [Coherence Principle Irrelevant Material Hurts Learning](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [-S] (laboratory and meta-analytic evidence on adding interesting but irrelevant material; its table counts 11 distinct studies). Legibility, contrast and device fit are untested here; from the earlier page ("accessible formatting").
8. **Use the aid in class, not only hand it out.** Spend the first two or three minutes showing learners how the aid maps onto the text (where the headings are, what the blanks are for); close the reading with two or three minutes in which learners use the completed aid to answer one question or explain one link to a partner. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("prompted to explain and use the guide actively").
9. **Plan its withdrawal across sessions.** Default arc for a course where learners will meet many texts of the same kind: in the first two or three sessions, a full aid (marks, structure, glosses, mostly-completed outline, prompts); in the middle sessions, a partial aid (headings and prompts only; the learner marks the essential points and fills the organizer); in later sessions, a blank template the learner completes for a new text, then no aid, with the learner writing their own one-page guide. Move a learner to the next stage when their completed aid on the current text matches a reference version on the essential points (for example, at least four of five) on two consecutive texts. The stages and threshold are untested proposals; the reason to fade is that guidance which helps novices can become redundant as they gain expertise ([Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]). How to fade is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).

**Dose within one reading** (untested proposal): one aid of one to two sides (or one scrollable page) per 20–40 minutes of reading or listening; about 2–3 minutes of orientation before, the reading itself with blanks filled as it goes, and 5 minutes at the end for the "why" prompts and one partner check.

When a condition is not met: if no one can check the completed aids, give a reference version to compare against after the learner has finished their own; if learners already find the essential points unaided on the first text, skip to the template or no aid.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the reader can already find unaided in this kind of material.** A reader who can already pick out the main points and structure needs a template or nothing; a reader who cannot needs the full aid. If unstated, ask: given one similar text with no aid, can the learner say its three main points and how they connect?
- **What the reading is for and how it will be checked.** Finding facts for a test, following a procedure, understanding an argument, or learning vocabulary call for different marks, organizers and prompts. If unstated, ask: what must the learner do with this material afterwards, with what aids, and when?
- **Who reads the aid, where, and on what.** A printed sheet in class, a page on a phone, a guide shared with remote learners, a patient handout read alone at home, and a guide translated for second-language readers each need a different format. If unstated, ask: on what device or paper will learners read it, and will anyone be there to explain it?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: the completed aid (which blanks are filled, and how), the answers to the "why" prompts, which marks the learner added or ignored, the time taken, and later answers on questions about marked and unmarked material. "Knows what matters", "relies on the guide" and "has learned the structure" are inferred states. A fully completed guided-notes sheet shows the learner copied or heard the points; it does not show they understood them.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Blanks filled correctly, "why" prompts blank or restated | The blanks were findable by matching words; the reader did not understand the link; the prompt was unclear or language was a barrier | Ask the "why" question orally, or allow a diagram or example instead of a sentence. |
| Good answers on marked points, poor on unmarked material the task also needed | The marks steered attention away from the rest; the unmarked material was harder; the task was not the one the marks served | Compare with a reader given the same text with no marks, or move one mark and see whether scores follow it. |
| Learner ignores the aid and reads the text alone | Already knows the material; the aid is hard to use on their device; the aid does not match the text | Ask the learner to state the main points unaided; check the aid on the learner's device. |
| Fills the organizer correctly but cannot explain it | Copied the structure from the text's headings; does not see why the parts relate | Give the organizer with two links removed and ask the learner to restore and justify them. |
| Does well with the aid, poorly on a new text without it | The aid carried the selection; the new text is a different genre; support was withdrawn too fast | Give a blank template for a new text of the same kind before removing the aid entirely. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe changes what it measures, so record each one.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests a handout, reading guide or visual reading aid as a whole. The claims below test its parts, each against the same material without that part.

- [Signaling Improves Learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+S]: an overview of meta-analyses (largest signaling meta-analysis g = 0.43, k = 209) and a meta-analysis of 29 experiments (d = 0.38), both on multimedia lessons; an eighth-grade field study found signals linking text to pictures helped low-prior-knowledge learners and harmed high-prior-knowledge learners. Its synthesis table counts 7 distinct studies. Supports marking the essential points (step 2). The model for cueing is in [Signaling](signaling.md).
- [Guided Notes Improve Note Accuracy](../claims/guided-notes-improve-note-accuracy.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of guided notes (mostly school-age, read from its abstract, no pooled effect recorded) and a quasi-experiment with 65 undergraduates in two intact classes, in which guided-note handouts produced better note quality and learning at immediate and delayed posttests than outline notes alone. The closest test of a supplied handout in the wiki; no effect sizes.
- [Graphic Organizers Support Novice Comprehension](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses of concept and knowledge maps (Grade 4 to postsecondary); studying a provided map gave g = 0.37 against text, lists or outlines, smaller than constructing one; the low-ability subset benefited and the high-ability subset did not, in small underpowered comparisons.
- [Adding relevant pictures or graphics to text improves learning from it, with a small pooled effect on reading comprehension, though whether dual coding explains the benefit is disputed](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 39 experiments (g = 0.39 on reading comprehension, elementary school to adults, regardless of grade level) and a narrative review; the mechanism is disputed.
- [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]: memory research (two reviews and an experiment) on grouping items into meaningful units; it supports grouping the aid's content but does not test a reading aid. Its table counts 4 distinct studies.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: meta-analyses (g = .55 across task types; mathematics), immediate tests. Supports the "why" prompts on the aid. The earlier page cited it as [~S]; the direction here is support for prompts, with its own limit: little evidence on delayed or classroom gains.
- [Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses](../claims/glossing-medium-large-effects-l2-vocabulary.md) [+M]: a review that cites earlier gloss meta-analyses; no values printed. Vocabulary only.

**What limits or qualifies the model:**

- [Coherence Principle Irrelevant Material Hurts Learning](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [-S]: adding interesting but irrelevant text, pictures or sound reduced transfer (13 of 14 experiments in one programme, median d = 0.97; a meta-analysis confirms the direction with a smaller average). Its table counts 11 distinct studies. A decorated aid can cost learning.
- [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M]: underlining low-relevance material raised scores on low-relevance questions (67 college freshmen). A mark helps whatever it points at.
- [Highlighting shows low utility for improving learning outcomes](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-M]: a review rating learner highlighting as low utility, with one study finding underlining hurt inference questions. Marking alone, by the learner, is a weak activity.
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: the reason to withdraw the aid; it gives no crossover point.
- [Learner-constructed graphic organizers are not shown to outperform provided ones: the one direct test, with college readers, favoured provided organizers on transfer](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [~M]: qualifies the earlier page's suggestion that learners build their own guides; for a textbook passage, provided organizers did better on transfer.

Learners, materials and outcomes differ across these claims (multimedia lessons, lectures, concept maps, second-language vocabulary, memory tasks), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures retention of a reading aid's benefit, or independent reading after the aid is withdrawn.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually comprehension of this material, and in the long run reading such material without the aid. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the aid: to finish the reading quickly, to have something to revise from, to follow a medical instruction correctly at home, to keep up in a second language. A learner who values a revision sheet may fill every blank and skip the prompts; one who wants to read independently may resent a full aid. Both are observations, and they decide whether the aid is withdrawn or kept as a reference.

For example, a patient given a medication handout wants to take the right dose at the right time; the clinician's objective may be that the patient understands why. A handout that marks the dose schedule with a picture serves the patient's goal; one "why" line serves the clinician's. Check the patient's goal by asking them to show what they will do, not by a quiz.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if a comparison of a complete reading aid with the same text alone, with comparable novice readers and an aligned comprehension test, showed no advantage, or showed that the gain disappeared when the aid was withdrawn and learners read a new text. If prompts on the aid add nothing beyond marks and structure, drop step 6's prompts. If learners given a full aid for several sessions read new texts less well than learners given a template from the start, the withdrawal arc is too slow. If guided notes help note accuracy but not learning, keep them as a record and stop claiming they teach. Do not protect the model by blaming every failure on a badly designed aid after the fact.

Comprehension with the aid, comprehension of a new text without it, delayed retention and independent reading habits are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of marks, no length of aid and no withdrawal schedule.

## Description
Handouts, online guides, and visual reading aids support learning by externalizing structure, highlighting key information, and reducing the amount learners must infer or hold in working memory while reading. They can take the form of annotated handouts, reading guides, visual cues, glosses, diagrams, highlighted sections, or structured digital supports. Their main value is not decoration but orientation: they help learners see what matters, where to look, and how parts of a text or task fit together.

## Implications
Handouts, online guides, and visual reading aids are most useful when learners would otherwise spend too much effort figuring out where to focus and how a text or task is organized. Well-designed cues, chunking, and readable formatting can reduce extraneous processing and make important relationships visible, especially in dense or unfamiliar materials [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. The instructional risk is over-support: if guides do all the interpretive work, learners may never develop independent reading strategies. These aids should therefore orient attention and model structure without permanently replacing the learner’s own sense-making, which is why they work better when learners are prompted to explain and use the guide actively rather than simply receive it [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Clear visual hierarchy**: Learners need cues that distinguish headings, key terms, examples, steps, and supporting details.
- **Alignment to the reading task**: The aid should support what learners are being asked to do, such as preview, annotate, compare, or synthesize.
- **Accessible formatting**: Legibility, spacing, contrast, and device compatibility matter as much as content.
- **Explanatory support**: Visual aids work better when learners are prompted to interpret or use them actively rather than just receive them.
#### Constraints
- **Decorative clutter**: Extra visual features can increase distraction instead of reducing it.
- **Over-support**: Highly guided aids can reduce independence if learners never transition to reading without them.
- **Mismatch to learner needs**: A single guide format may not suit all readers, devices, or contexts.
- **Static support**: Reading aids are less useful if they do not adapt to the actual comprehension demands of the task.

### Target Learners
- **Learners facing dense or unfamiliar texts**: Especially useful when readings contain complex structure, technical vocabulary, or multiple representations.
- **Learners with working-memory or attention constraints**: External structure can lower orientation demands while reading.
- **Multilingual learners and developing readers**: Guides can support navigation, vocabulary focus, and text interpretation.
- **Learners in digital or blended settings**: Online guides can reduce confusion about where to focus in asynchronous materials.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Improved reading navigation**: Helping learners locate central ideas, sequence, and key evidence.
- **Comprehension support**: Making difficult texts or multimodal materials more manageable.
- **Strategic reading habits**: Supporting previewing, note-taking, annotation, and synthesis.
- **Retention and transfer**: Providing a structured artifact learners can revisit during practice or review.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Multimedia and dual-coding perspectives — aligned visual and verbal supports can improve comprehension when they clarify rather than duplicate aimlessly.
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — reading aids can reduce extraneous processing by making task structure and key cues visible.
- Information processing views — guides help learners orient attention and encode important information more deliberately.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Reading aids should be faded or varied when the long-term goal is independent reading without heavy support.
- Not all visual supports help; poorly designed cues can fragment attention or oversimplify meaning.

### Claims
- [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S] — reading aids can lower extraneous processing by making structure and emphasis visible
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S] — guides are more effective when learners use them to interpret, annotate, or explain rather than only follow them passively
