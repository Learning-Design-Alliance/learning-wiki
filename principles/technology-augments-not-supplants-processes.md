# Technology should augment, not supplant, learning processes in student-centered environments

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 150 claims (96 for, 44 mixed, 10 against) · 118 studies (29 theoretical, 27 qualitative, 22 review, 19 causal, 12 design, 9 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 118 report an effect size · 146 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **division of a learning process between the learner and a tool that could do it for them**: a generative AI assistant that can produce the answer, the draft or the judgement, a simulation that can produce clean data, a virtual-reality module that can stand in for a hands-on procedure. For a learner who is meant to become able to do the process unaided (solve the problem, write the argument, run the measurement, carry out the procedure), using the tool so that the learner still does the process (the learner attempts first; the tool gives hints, questions, comparison or feedback on that attempt, or shows something the learner cannot otherwise perceive) is expected to produce better performance **once the tool is removed** (`immediate-performance` without the tool, `delayed-retention`, `near-transfer`) than using the tool to take the process over. Taking it over often raises performance while the tool is present; that gain is not the outcome this page is about. For simulation and virtual reality the same relationship reads: the virtual version is expected to add what the physical task cannot show, and to sit beside the physical practice that carries what the virtual version lacks (touch, real measurement error, the feel of the workplace), not to replace it.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's prior knowledge of the task (`novice` learners may gain from tool output that more `advanced` learners are hindered by), on whether the outcome is measured **with the tool removed**, on what the tool is configured to do (give solutions, or give hints and questions), on whether anyone checks what the tool says, and on the horizon.

**What tests it in the wiki.** For generative AI, several claims compare ways of using the tool on an unaided outcome: a randomised experiment in which adults using an AI assistant solved fewer fraction problems once it was removed, a quasi-experiment in which bounded AI support gave the best no-AI essays at week 8 while open collaboration gave the best supported essays, a multisite experiment in which reflective and hybrid feedback beat direct AI feedback on a delayed AI-free task, and a 9-study table on whether using AI for solutions rather than hints costs independent performance. They are mostly single studies with adults and horizons of days to weeks, and three experiments limit the model: in one, access to AI raised unaided test scores; in another, it did not significantly lower them; in a third, the preregistered tests found no effect of prior AI use on later unaided reasoning, and only an exploratory estimate found a cost, when participants had no incentive. **For simulation and virtual reality against hands-on work, no claim in the wiki compares "complement" with "replace"**: the evidence is one within-subject comparison of 20 students' self-ratings, one reported quasi-experiment, and design reports. The default design below marks which steps rest on which.

## Default design

Steps 2, 3, 4 and 6 rest on claims that compare ways of using a generative AI tool on an unaided outcome; the rest are untested proposals, kept from the earlier page and the two pages folded into it. Each step says what stands behind it. Quantities are proposals to set locally and record.

1. **Name the process the learner must own, and what the tool may do.** Before the tool enters, write down the capability to be shown unaided (for example: write an argued paragraph; apply conservation of momentum to measured velocities; administer a radiopharmaceutical safely) and list which sub-processes the tool may perform (spelling, computing, rendering an invisible quantity) and which it may not (generating the argument, choosing the solution path, inventing data). Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("technology support must leave core cognitive processes in the hands of learners"). Learners can draw the same line themselves, so ask them where they would draw it: in one focus-group study of postgraduate research students, [generating research ideas, original arguments or whole papers was consistently rejected as violating authorship norms, while surface-level support such as language polishing was accepted](../claims/genai-authorship-boundary-rejected-substitution.md) [+W], and [AI-assisted ideation was acceptable while the researcher kept decision-making authority and problematic once it displaced their conceptual labour](../claims/conditional-ideation-dialogic-aid-vs-outsourcing.md) [+W] (one qualitative study, stated norms, no outcome measured). Where ownership of a written product matters, keep drafting with the learner: in two contrasts from one writing experiment, AI support during drafting lowered ownership more than support during planning or revision, and planning support preserved more than revision support ([AI support during drafting lowers ownership substantially more than support during planning or revision, and planning support preserves more ownership than revision support.](../claims/drafting-support-largest-ownership-decrease.md) [~M]; an ownership rating, not a learning outcome). In an essay study comparing LLM, search-engine and no-tool groups, [LLM-group participants reported low ownership of their essays and fell behind in their ability to quote from essays written minutes earlier](../claims/llm-group-low-ownership-poor-quoting-own-essays.md) [+W] (interviews, no test statistics).
2. **Learner attempts first, tool after.** Give an independent attempt before the tool opens: a complete first draft, a solution attempt with working shown, or a set of predictions before the simulation runs. As a default, at least 10 to 15 minutes, or one full attempt, before access (proposal). Evidence: a review reports that students who worked on a task independently before consulting an LLM produced stronger outputs ([Sequencing independent task engagement before LLM consultation is associated with stronger student outputs](../claims/independent-work-before-llm-stronger-outputs.md) [+W]; second-hand, an output measure, no effect size). One way to enforce it in software: a gate that releases guidance only after the learner has given a reasoned answer; in its validation, [the deterministic reflection gate coached filler answers and unlocked guidance only for substantive ones](../claims/reflection-gate-distinguishes-low-effort-defenses.md) [+W] (a design check of the gate, no learner data).
3. **Configure the tool to give hints, questions and comparison, not solutions.** Set the assistant's instructions (or choose a tool) so it asks what the learner has tried, gives the next hint rather than the answer, and compares the learner's answer with alternatives. Evidence: across the studies gathered under [How genAI is used, not who uses it, predicts whether independent performance declines or improves](../claims/persistence-costs-concentrated-among-direct-solution-users.md) [+M], learners who used AI for direct solutions lost independent performance while those who used it for hints improved; in [a randomised experiment on fraction problems, participants who used an AI assistant solved fewer unassisted test problems and skipped more](../claims/ai-assistance-impairs-unassisted-fraction-performance-exp1.md) [+M], and in the same research [most participants given the assistant reported using it mainly to get answers directly (61%), a report their chat logs bore out](../claims/majority-use-ai-for-direct-answers.md) [+W], so an unconfigured tool that can give answers is mostly used for them; telling learners about the risk is not a substitute for configuring the tool: in one physics course, after a lesson on LLM use, [the share of students using LLMs for solutions more often than they believed was ideal for learning rose from 53% to 75%](../claims/usage-exceeds-ideal-for-solution-generation.md) [+W] (pre/post survey, descriptive percentages, no comparison group); in a writing quasi-experiment, [bounded AI support gave the highest independent no-AI week-8 performance while open collaboration gave the highest supported quality](../claims/genai-supported-performance-independent-divergence.md) [+M]; and, as reported second-hand, [a guarded version of the same model that withheld answers erased the harm an answer-giving version did to unaided exam scores](../claims/gpt4-access-harms-learning-after-withdrawal.md) [+M] (high-school mathematics); the same experiment is reported by another review as [improving problem-solving during learning but harming durable independent learning once the AI was removed](../claims/gpt4-access-harms-learning-after-withdrawal.md) [+M] (second-hand, no effect size printed; one study, not a second one).
4. **Make AI feedback ask for the learner's revision rather than supply it.** When AI gives feedback on drafts, use a reflective form (questions about the learner's reasoning, the learner revises) or a hybrid of AI and teacher or peer feedback, not AI-written corrections. Evidence: [Reflective and hybrid feedback outperform direct GenAI feedback on delayed AI-free transfer](../claims/agentic-designs-delayed-ai-free-transfer.md) [+M] (higher education, scientific argumentation, multisite experiment, small effects). In EFL writing, [AI-peer integrated feedback produced higher posttest cognitive engagement than peer feedback alone](../claims/ai-peer-feedback-higher-cognitive-engagement.md) [~W] (one quasi-experiment; self-reported engagement, not an unaided outcome).
5. **Make checking the tool part of the task.** Before learners accept an AI answer, ask them to say how confident they are and how they would check it; have them compare their own analysis with the tool's; when the tool reports a value, ask where it came from. In a lab, the learners record measurements, never the tool. Evidence: [a brief metacognitive reflection prompt reduced acceptance of incorrect AI advice](../claims/reflection-reduces-incorrect-ai-advice-acceptance.md) [+M] (college students, decision tasks); in a text-linguistics course where students analysed texts by hand first, [comparing their own analyses with LLM outputs raised their sense of competence](../claims/llm-comparison-empowers-competence.md) [+W] (qualitative, reflections); experimental work reported second-hand finds that [AI assistance can reduce accuracy when the AI is faulty and inflate confidence even after errors](../claims/cognitive-surrender-inflates-confidence-after-errors.md) [~W], and a workshop paper reports two more studies in which [AI support raised task performance but left users overconfident about their accuracy, and physicians given an LLM that outperformed them did not reason better diagnostically](../claims/ai-performance-metacognition-dissociation.md) [+W] (second-hand, no effect size); and the limit that makes this step necessary: under student pressure an AI lab facilitator [produced mass values no one had measured](../claims/ai-facilitation-fragile-invents-data.md) [-M] (three student pairs, exploratory).
6. **Show learners their own pattern of use.** At the end of a session, show each learner how often they took the answer from the tool rather than working it, and what that predicts. Evidence: in a preregistered online experiment, [metacognitive feedback on learners' own LLM use reduced answer offloading and improved subsequent unaided test performance](../claims/metacognitive-feedback-reduces-llm-answer-offloading-improves-unaided-performance.md) [+M] (the confidence interval for the unaided gain includes no effect); in the same study [more answer offloading during learning went with lower unaided performance](../claims/answer-offloading-associated-lower-unaided-performance-exploratory.md) [+M] (exploratory, observational).
7. **Test without the tool, on a schedule.** End each session with a short unaided check (3 to 5 items, or one paragraph, in under 10 minutes), and give an unaided task in the form of the target every two to three weeks. Read unaided capability from these checks, not from assisted work. Untested proposal as a schedule; every comparison in steps 3 to 6 found its effect only because the outcome was measured with the tool removed, and a physics framework's authors name the same test as the decisive open question for their own design: [whether students retain representational competence when AI support is withdrawn](../claims/withdrawal-condition-ai-instruction-frameworks.md) [~W] (argument, no data). **The unaided check is added beside the brief's own assessment, never in place of it.** If the brief's rubric scores appropriate tool use, the learner's justification of it or the quality of the assisted product, keep those criteria exactly as given and grade them; the evidence on unaided performance says to include an unaided check, not to drop assisted assessment. A simple way to hold both: the learner submits the assisted work with a short note of what the tool did and why they accepted or changed it (scored on the brief's rubric), plus the short unaided check (reported separately).
8. **Use simulation and virtual reality for what the physical task cannot show, and keep the physical task.** Use the virtual version to make visible what cannot be seen (a radiation field, an idealised collision) and the physical version for handling, real measurement error and realism. Sequence for a procedural skill: hands-on procedural training first, then the virtual module for the concept, then back to hands-on. Evidence: in one within-subject comparison of 20 radiological technology students who already had procedural training, [VR training with radiation visualisation was rated much higher for radiation understanding than conventional training](../claims/vr-radiation-visualization-higher-understanding-ratings.md) [+M], [conventional training was rated higher for realism and immersion](../claims/conventional-training-higher-realism-immersion.md) [+M], and [operational skills and usability ratings did not differ](../claims/no-difference-operational-skills-usability.md) [~M] (self-ratings, immediate, fixed order; equivalence not tested); the same students also rated VR higher for [safety awareness and radiation protection](../claims/vr-training-higher-safety-awareness-ratings.md) [+W] and for [learning satisfaction and self-evaluation](../claims/vr-training-higher-satisfaction-self-evaluation.md) [+W]. A review states the same position as an argument: [immersive technologies such as VR should reinforce, not substitute, the original educational action](../claims/vr-reinforcement-not-substitute.md) [+W]. From the folded collision-carts page (untested proposal): after a simulation lesson, run the same collisions on real carts, if possible with video analysis (for example Tracker), so learners meet random measurement error and apply conservation of momentum to velocities just before and just after the collision, which the simulation does not model. Where real observational data exist beside a simulation (a research-based data lab), run the two in parallel rather than one in place of the other, have learners set their own criterion for locating the feature in both, compare their value with the one the simulation calculates, and include real data sets in which the feature is absent. Evidence: one design report of an undergraduate space-physics lab used over eleven years (instructor observations, no comparison group) finds that [students locate the boundary well in magnetic-field data but struggle with the messier particle data, and are unsettled by data sets that contain no crossing](../claims/magnetopause-lab-crossing-search-field-versus-particle-data.md) [~W]; that [their fits agree with each other but often differ from the simulation's own calculated location, through systematic differences in how they define it](../claims/magnetopause-lab-student-fits-agree-internally-but-differ-from-simulation-values.md) [~W]; and that overall [they reach reasonable agreement with model predictions](../claims/magnetopause-lab-reasonable-agreement-with-model-predictions.md) [+W]. The real data carry what the clean simulation lacks; the gap between the learners' value and the tool's is the thing to discuss, not to hide.
9. **Make the virtual activity ask for thinking, not copying.** Simulation worksheets should ask for a prediction before each run and an explanation after, not values to copy. Evidence: after a guided-inquiry simulation lesson, [students' self-reported knowledge rose and most rated it valuable](../claims/collision-carts-simulation-lesson-positive-student-feedback.md) [+M], but [some found the worksheet did not generate much thinking, only values to copy](../claims/students-criticize-inquiry-worksheets-low-thinking.md) [~W] (one lesson, self-report); students in the same lessons said [the virtual lab made them figure out concepts themselves](../claims/students-report-simulation-lesson-fun-and-concept-figuring.md) [+W]. Where the simulation is AI-generated, have learners check its output against the governing law before using it; its authors argue that [validating and refining AI-generated simulations promotes physics understanding](../claims/student-validation-of-ai-simulations-promotes-understanding.md) [+W] (argument, no data).
10. **Keep the teacher deciding.** The teacher reads the tool's output or scores and decides what to re-teach, to whom, and when the tool is used; the tool does not set the lesson. Evidence (qualitative, one study): teachers who [positioned an AI writing tool as a partner remained instructional leaders and used its score data to decide what to re-teach](../claims/partner-positioning-instructional-leadership.md) [+M], and [the focus of teacher–student talk was learning with partner teachers, the score with grading-assistant teachers and completion with substitute teachers](../claims/interaction-focus-varies-by-ai-positioning.md) [+M].
11. **Withdraw support across sessions, not only within one.** A default arc for a term (proposal): sessions 1 to 3, tool limited to hints after an independent attempt, a reflection prompt before any AI answer is accepted, and an unaided check at the end of each session; sessions 4 to 6, learners use the tool to critique or compare with their own work and see their own use pattern; later sessions, learners choose when to use it, with unaided target tasks every two to three weeks. Move a learner to freer use once their unaided checks on two consecutive occasions are close to their assisted work; move a learner back to hint-only use if unaided results fall while assisted results rise. For simulation and VR: hands-on first, virtual for the invisible or the impossible, hands-on again before assessment; in a data lab, simulation and real data side by side throughout, with real data sets carrying more of the work in later sessions (proposal). How support is withdrawn is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).

When a condition is not met: if the tool cannot be configured (a public chatbot learners use at home), move the independent attempt and the unaided check into class time, report them beside whatever the brief already grades, and ask learners to note how they used the tool at home; if no physical equipment exists, say plainly what the simulation omits (measurement error, handling) and add a recorded real-world video to analyse.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner can already do on the task without the tool.** Tool output that helps a learner with little prior knowledge can hinder one who could do it. If unstated, ask: can the learner do one representative item now, unaided, and explain it?
- **Whether the outcome that counts is measured with the tool removed, and when.** If the target is the product made with the tool, the trade-offs on this page matter less; if it is unaided performance or retention, they decide the design. If unstated, ask: what must the learner do without the tool, and how long after?
- **What the tool will do when asked, and who checks it.** An assistant that gives full solutions, invents data or writes feedback the learner simply accepts takes the process over; one limited to hints, or checked by a person, does not. If unstated, ask: can the tool be limited, and who reads its output before the learner relies on it?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: the learner's work with and without the tool, the tool's log (what was asked, what was returned, what was copied), the time between the tool's answer and the learner's submission, and the learner's own account of how they used it. "Has offloaded the thinking", "trusts the tool too much" and "owns the work" are inferred states. Assisted success shows what learner and tool did together. The learner's own account is worth asking for: in one experiment, [participants' self-reports of using the AI mainly for answers matched their chat logs](../claims/majority-use-ai-for-direct-answers.md) [+W]. The log alone is not enough, because the same request and the same returned text can be read actively or passively: a theoretical article on medical training argues that [within a task where AI help is appropriate, a learner passively taking in AI output looks the same as one engaging actively, and plausible errors pass unnoticed when the learner's knowledge is incomplete](../claims/passive-engagement-aid-subzone-mis-skilling.md) [~W] (argument, no data), so it proposes that an expert checks how the learner engaged, which is what the probes below do.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Strong assisted work, weak unaided work | The tool did the process; the unaided task differs from the assisted one; anxiety or time pressure without the tool | Give a matched unaided item without time limit; read the tool log for copied solutions; ask the learner to explain one step of their assisted work. |
| Accepts every AI answer without change, including wrong ones | Lacks the knowledge to judge; believes the tool is reliable; time pressure makes checking costly | Ask for a confidence rating and a one-line check before the learner accepts the tool's answer; review together one real case where the tool was wrong (from the learner's own log or a documented example), rather than planting wrong answers in learning materials, which is an untested and questionable probe. |
| Refuses to use the tool at all | Already competent; distrust; a rule or norm against it; does not know how to ask | Compare unaided performance with peers; ask what they think the tool would do; offer a hint-only mode. |
| High VR or simulation ratings, errors on the real equipment | The virtual version taught the concept, not the handling; no hands-on practice since; the virtual interface differs from the real one | Observe one real procedure after the virtual session; ask the learner to point out the invisible quantity on the real setup. |
| Low sense of ownership of a good product | The tool drafted it; the learner revised little; the learner is unsure where the ideas came from | Ask which ideas were theirs; compare their first independent draft with the final. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Each probe changes what it measures (asking for a check before acceptance changes how learners use the tool), so record each one.

## Evidence and qualifications

The claims below are the core of the model. They are mostly single studies, from 2026 preprints and journals, with adults; two print an effect size.

- [How genAI is used, not who uses it, predicts whether independent performance declines or improves](../claims/persistence-costs-concentrated-among-direct-solution-users.md) [+M]: its own entry is a theoretical article reporting a randomised trial in which participants who used AI mainly for direct solutions lost independent performance against their own pretest while those who used it for hints improved; its table gathers **9 distinct studies** (3 theoretical, 3 causal, 1 design, 1 review, 1 associational; `q1`–`q3`, one with an effect size coded), five narrower studies support it and three qualify it. It is the general claim behind steps 3 and 6.
- [In a randomized experiment on fraction problems, participants who used an AI assistant solved fewer unassisted test problems and skipped more than control participants](../claims/ai-assistance-impairs-unassisted-fraction-performance-exp1.md) [+M]: 307 online adults analysed; with a GPT-5 sidebar during 12 practice problems, then 3 unassisted problems, the AI group's solve rate was lower (d = −0.42) and skip rate higher (d = 0.25). `randomized`, `q3`, single session, `immediate-performance` and `persistence`.
- [Open AI collaboration yielded the highest supported writing quality, while bounded AI support yielded the highest independent no-AI Week 8 performance](../claims/genai-supported-performance-independent-divergence.md) [+M]: 168 students in six intact classes, three conditions (open, bounded, no AI); open collaboration gave the highest supported-writing means, bounded support the highest week-8 no-AI means, scored blind. Class-clustered quasi-experiment, `q2`, observed means only; the two entries are two outcomes of one study.
- [Reflective and hybrid feedback outperform direct GenAI feedback on delayed AI-free transfer](../claims/agentic-designs-delayed-ai-free-transfer.md) [+M]: multisite higher-education experiment on scientific argumentation; on a delayed, supervised task without AI, reflective (d = 0.39) and hybrid (d = 0.48) feedback beat direct AI feedback; direct AI feedback did not differ significantly from peer feedback. `q4`, small effects.
- [Learning outcomes differ by how students use AI: augmentation and mixed users show gains in both sessions, while automation users' Session One essay advantage is absent from their unaided essays](../claims/genai-outcomes-differ-by-use-type.md) [+M]: within the treated arm of a randomised experiment, 61% of AI users were classified as augmentation users and 9% as automation users; automation users' better first essays did not carry over to unaided essays a week later. Descriptive between-group comparisons; the use types were not assigned.
- [In one preregistered experiment, metacognitive feedback on learners' own LLM use lowered the odds of answer offloading and raised the odds of a correct unaided answer on one-sided tests, with 95% intervals reaching no effect](../claims/metacognitive-feedback-reduces-llm-answer-offloading-improves-unaided-performance.md) [+M]: preregistered online experiment; feedback lowered the odds of answer offloading (OR = 0.47) and raised the odds of a correct unaided answer (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 0.98 to 2.33, one-sided p = .030). The interval includes no effect.

**Claims that limit or qualify the model:**

- [One week later, the AI-access group's lead on unaided assessments shrinks to 3.9 pp (0.21 SD), short of statistical significance (p = 0.068)](../claims/genai-learning-gain-persists-one-week.md) [~M]: in a randomised experiment (`q4`), students allowed AI during a learning phase scored higher on a knowledge test taken without AI a week later, about half the first-session effect. So AI access as such need not cost unaided learning; in the same study the cost appears among automation users (above). The claim's title says the gain "persists", but its entry reports p = 0.068 for the week-later difference.
- [Access to an LLM assistant during practice did not significantly lower subsequent unaided test performance](../claims/llm-access-no-significant-lower-unaided-performance.md) [~M]: preregistered online experiment; AI-only participants did not differ significantly from no-AI controls on six unaided items (OR = 0.80, p = .244); equivalence was not tested. A non-significant difference is not evidence of no harm.
- [Prior genAI access shows only exploratory evidence of poorer subsequent unaided reasoning, within the no-incentive condition, and no preregistered main effect](../claims/prior-genai-use-poorer-unaided-reasoning.md) [~W]: a preregistered lab experiment (`q3`) in which participants either used generative AI on a first task or did not, then reasoned unaided; the preregistered tests found no significant effect of prior AI access, of incentives or of their interaction (partial η² ≤ .014), and only an exploratory item-level model found lower odds of a correct unaided answer after AI use when there was no incentive (OR = 0.68, p = .045). Read with the two studies above: a cost of AI use to later unaided work is not found every time, and where it appears here it appears only when the learner has no reason to try hard, which is consistent with the model's reading that the cost lies in how the tool is used rather than in access, but does not test it.
- [Descriptive user-study patterns suggest GPT output may scaffold lower-prior-knowledge IVDA students but reduce performance when prior knowledge is higher (CV)](../claims/gpt-output-effect-depends-on-prior-knowledge-and-domain.md) [~M]: descriptive percentages only, computer science multiple-choice exercises. It is the basis of the prior-knowledge rows.
- [AI-generated and prebuilt simulations produced higher immediate conceptual-assessment scores than physical laboratory work, but the advantage did not persist in later examinations](../claims/simulation-advantage-immediate-not-sustained.md) [~M]: one quasi-experiment (initial enrolment 187) reported in a systematic review. The simulations were better immediately; neither was better at the midterm and final. It neither supports nor refutes keeping the physical lab, but it warns against judging a replacement on an immediate test.
- The three VR claims in step 8 ([understanding](../claims/vr-radiation-visualization-higher-understanding-ratings.md) [+M], [realism](../claims/conventional-training-higher-realism-immersion.md) [+M], [no difference in operational skills](../claims/no-difference-operational-skills-usability.md) [~M]) and [the qualitative themes from the same study](../claims/qualitative-themes-visualization-versus-operability.md) [+W] (all 20 found radiation perceptible in VR; all 20 reported operability differences favouring conventional training, citing absent haptic feedback and distance judgement) come from one within-subject study of 20 students, fixed order, self-ratings, immediate only; so do its higher VR ratings for [safety awareness](../claims/vr-training-higher-safety-awareness-ratings.md) [+W] and [satisfaction and self-evaluation](../claims/vr-training-higher-satisfaction-self-evaluation.md) [+W]. The authors read the understanding rating as confirming the system works as intended, not as evidence of educational superiority. They support "complement" as a reading of the pattern of ratings; they do not compare complement with replacement.
- The three space-physics lab claims in step 8 ([crossing search](../claims/magnetopause-lab-crossing-search-field-versus-particle-data.md) [~W], [student fits against the simulation](../claims/magnetopause-lab-student-fits-agree-internally-but-differ-from-simulation-values.md) [~W], [agreement with model predictions](../claims/magnetopause-lab-reasonable-agreement-with-model-predictions.md) [+W]) are one design report (Crumley et al.), instructor observations across eleven years of sophomore and junior physics majors, no comparison group and no effect size. They describe what pairing a simulation with real spacecraft data shows learners (messier data, absent features, a gap between their definition and the tool's); they do not test pairing against simulation alone.
- [AI facilitation of ISLE inquiry is fragile: under student pressure the Gem crossed from scaffolding reasoning to inventing data](../claims/ai-facilitation-fragile-invents-data.md) [-M] and [An AI assistant configured on the ISLE approach can scaffold the epistemic core of inquiry, at the level of feasibility](../claims/ai-gem-scaffolds-isle-epistemic-core.md) [+M]: the same three-pair exploratory study shows a configured AI keeping students to the inquiry order and, under pressure, inventing measurements. Configuration (step 3) is not enough on its own; someone has to check (step 5). The same study notes that [a language-based facilitator sees only the verbal traces of embodied inquiry](../claims/language-facilitator-verbal-channel-limit.md) [~W], so in a hands-on lab it cannot see the handling it is meant to support.
- **Associational and second-hand evidence in the same direction.** In one course, [Low-Reliance LLM users outperformed High-Reliance users by roughly 7–8% on all three midterms](../claims/low-reliance-outperformed-high-reliance-midterms.md) [+W] (7 against 16 students, descriptive, no tests); a review reports [a negative correlation between frequent ChatGPT use and programming test performance](../claims/unstructured-chatgpt-use-risks-programming-education.md) [+W]; two articles report Gerlich's survey finding that [AI tool use correlates negatively with critical thinking, mediated by cognitive offloading](../claims/ai-reliance-correlated-lower-critical-thinking-offloading.md) [+W] (also in [this claim](../claims/cognitive-offloading-reduces-engagement-critical-thinking.md) [+W], which reports Stadler et al. as well); and two articles report Stadler et al.'s comparison in which [students using an LLM for an inquiry task reported lower effort and produced weaker arguments than web-search users](../claims/unstructured-llm-use-cognitive-offloading.md) [~W]. None separates how the tool was used from who chose to use it heavily, and the last is an outcome measured with the tool present, so they are consistent with the model without testing it.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (online adults on fractions, students writing essays, radiological technology students rating VR), so do not rank them by effect label. None follows learners beyond a term, and none compares a complementary simulation or VR design with a replacement design.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually unaided capability: the essay the learner can write, the problem they can solve, the procedure they can carry out without the tool, at a stated horizon. The learner may value something else: finishing the assignment, a better grade on assisted work, saving time, or learning to use the tool well because their job will require it. These diverge sharply here, because the tool can deliver the learner's goal (a finished product) while bypassing the designer's (unaided capability). Elicit both, and record them.

For example, a postgraduate student may want to use generative AI efficiently across the research process, because that is how they will work; the programme's objective is that they can judge a method and an argument themselves. Both can be served: assess the judgement unaided, and teach the tool as part of the work with explicit checks. Learners often already value keeping the core work: postgraduate research students in one study [rejected AI generation of ideas and arguments as violating authorship](../claims/genai-authorship-boundary-rejected-substitution.md) [+W], and in a survey at one university [high-achieving students described using AI selectively, verifying its outputs and keeping it subordinate to their own judgement](../claims/qualitative-selective-verification-intensive-use.md) [+W] (themes included "fear of cognitive substitution" and verification "as part of the work"; open-ended answers, one author's coding). Where a learner's stated value and their observed use diverge (for example, believing solution-getting is bad for learning and doing it anyway, as in the physics course in step 3), show them the divergence rather than assume either one. A nursing student who values passing quickly may take every AI answer; one who values being safe with patients may want the hands-on procedure repeated. Agreement and divergence are both observations.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if randomised comparisons with comparable learners show that solution-giving AI use produces unaided and delayed performance as good as hint-limited use; the three experiments in which AI access did not lower (or raised) unaided scores already point that way for unrestricted access in some tasks, and the model should then say which tasks. If metacognitive feedback or reflection prompts do not raise unaided performance in replications (the one estimate's interval includes no effect), drop step 6 to a proposal. If a comparison of simulation or VR used *instead of* hands-on practice shows equal real-world performance at a delay, "complement, not replace" becomes a cost decision rather than a learning one. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong kind of use" after the fact; classify use before seeing the outcome.

Assisted performance, unaided immediate performance, delayed retention, transfer, ownership and the learner's own goal are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no safe amount of AI use, no minimum length of independent work before access, and no sequence of hands-on and virtual training that has been tested against another.
