Distributed Cognition¶

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the division of a task's cognitive work across the learner, external representations, tools and other people, and the choice of which outcome that division is judged on. Salomon's distinction, as one claim page records it, separates two outcomes that are often run together: effects with the tool or group (how well the person-plus-system performs) and effects of it (the "cognitive residue": what the person can do alone afterwards) (Salomon distinguishes effects with the computer (system performance) from effects of the computer (cognitive residue on the solo performer) [+M]).

The relationship: for a task whose demands exceed what one learner can hold in mind or do alone (many interacting elements, several steps to track, expertise split across people), putting part of the work into a well-organised external representation, a tool or a partner is expected to raise performance with the system (immediate-performance with aids present). Whether it also builds what the learner can do alone (immediate-performance with aids removed, delayed-retention, near-transfer) is expected to depend on two things: whether the part the target says must be done unaided stays with the learner, and whether the learner's own decisions about when to rely on an aid are calibrated to how well they actually perform. Learners tend to rely on aids more than their performance warrants, and feedback on their own predictions is expected to correct that.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's task-specific starting response (novice learners lean on external structure that advanced learners may not need), on age (children and adolescents are reported to err in opposite directions), on whether the outcome counts the system or the solo performer, on how the external resources are organised and shared, and on the horizon.

What tests it in the wiki, and what does not. No study recorded in the wiki compares a task distributed across people, roles or shared representations with the same task done individually, on either outcome; one review reports such a comparison second-hand (collaborative better than individual learning on high-load tasks, Kirschner et al. 2009b, no effect size), and the wiki holds no record of that study itself. Three parts of the relationship are tested, each by few studies:

  • How much to hand to a tool. One classroom quasi-experiment (168 students in six intact classes, AI-assisted writing) found open collaboration with an AI gave the best writing while the AI was present and bounded AI support gave the best independent no-AI writing at week 8. That relationship, a tool that could do the learner's part, is owned by Technology Augments, Not Supplants, Learning Processes; this page uses its result and does not repeat its model.
  • Whether learners decide well when to offload, and whether feedback helps. A review and a pre-registered experiment (N = 164) report that people set more reminders than is optimal; in that experiment a short prediction-plus-feedback intervention reduced the bias (d = .347). A second pre-registered experiment with an LLM assistant found metacognitive feedback lowered answer offloading and raised the odds of a correct unaided test item, though that interval includes no effect.
  • What form the group's shared representation takes. One study with classes assigned to conditions (124 eleventh-graders in 39 groups, a two-position debate task) found that a graphical debate tool gave groups higher-quality shared representations than a textual one (large d), and better group essays on three of five quality measures, an advantage that disappeared on longer topics. It measured group products, not each learner alone.

How the converted siblings sit beside this page: Collaborative Learning holds joint work against individual work on individually measured understanding; this page holds the representations, roles and tools that carry the shared work, and the decision of what stays with each person. Chunking and Cognitive Load Theory hold how information is grouped and how load is managed inside one learner. Self-Monitoring and Self-Regulated Learning hold learners checking their own performance against criteria; the offloading decision is one such judgement. Scaffolding and Fading holds how support is withdrawn; external representations are faded the same way.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a default a designer can act on and revise. Each step says whether a claim bears on it; most are untested proposals. Quantities are proposals unless a claim gives them.

  1. Decide which outcome counts before dividing the work. Write down, for this task, whether success is the team's or system's performance with every aid present (a ward team using a checklist, a lab group with shared data), the individual's performance alone afterwards, or both. If both, name the parts each learner must be able to do unaided. Basis: Salomon distinguishes effects with the computer (system performance) from effects of the computer (cognitive residue on the solo performer) [+M] (a conceptual distinction, not a test).
  2. Map the task's load, then assign each part. List what has to be remembered, tracked, computed, decided and checked. Assign each to the learner's head, an external representation, a tool or a teammate. Keep with each learner what step 1 says must be done unaided; put the rest outside the head. Basis: external structure lowers the number of units to be held (Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [~S], carried from memory experiments to task design); for a tool that could do the learner's part, bounded support gave better unaided writing at week 8 than open collaboration (Bounded AI support outperformed open collaboration on all four Week 8 independent no-AI outcomes, though exact wild-cluster p values sat at the 0.050–0.063 boundary [+M]).
  3. Give the group one external representation that carries the shared memory. One shared document, diagram, checklist or board that everyone can see and edit, in a fixed layout: the goal at the top, who holds which part, the current state of the work, open questions. Keep any one view to a few groups of items (the chunking claim page reports three to five chunks as the limit for novel material), and ask for fewer, better entries rather than many: in one study, groups that put more items into their representation had lower average item quality (The number of items groups placed in their representations was negatively correlated with the average quality of those items. [~W], r = −.66, correlational). Where the work is an argument (positions, grounds, counter-arguments), lay it out graphically, as boxes and arrows, rather than as running text: in one study with classes assigned to conditions, a graphical debate tool gave higher-quality group representations than a textual one (Groups using the Graphical Debate-tool constructed representations of significantly higher quality than groups using the Textual Debate-tool, for both debate positions. [+M]), without any difference in how much students used the tool, and better group essays on three of five measures, with the advantage gone on longer topics (GD groups wrote essays significantly better in total grounds quality, total conceptual quality, and average conceptual quality, but did not cover more topics and were not better in average grounds quality. [~M]). Before dividing the work, have the group agree one name for each part or item, written on the representation: in one design study a group could split a building task and work in parallel only once three members' different names for the same item had merged into one (Merging different technical understandings of Minecraft items into shared nomenclature enabled parallel division of building work (Extract 2) [+W], one group, interaction analysis). The fixed layout itself is untested; check it with the learners, because representations that help experts externalise their reasoning are not always the ones novices can follow (Representation-alignment tension between expert externalization and novice comprehension [~M]).
  4. Use groups only where the task is too big for one person. If one learner could do the task alone in the time, make it individual work or make it bigger. Basis: Collaborative tasks should be complex enough that joint effort is worthwhile, engaging the collective working memory effect [+W] (a primer's report of theory); a review reports, second-hand and without an effect size, that learning from high-load tasks was more likely in a collaborative than an individual setting (Collaborative learning distributes cognitive load across multiple working memories, aiding learning of high-load tasks [+W]); joint work often fails to be productive when it is only "work together" (Collaborative situations often fail to be productive in practice, so orchestrating collaboration is not simple [~W]).
  5. Make roles and progress visible enough to monitor. Name each role on the shared representation (group roles: for example a recorder who keeps the representation current, a checker who compares the work with the criteria), and give a progress line a teacher and the group can read at a glance. Basis: Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] (reviews of individual self-regulation, carried to groups); roles as such are an untested proposal.
  6. Train the decision to rely on an aid. Before learners use a reminder, a calculator, a reference sheet or an AI assistant on a practice item, ask them to predict whether they would get it right unaided; after the item, show them how they actually did unaided and with the aid. Five such practice trials reduced over-reliance in one experiment. Basis: In Experiment 1, a five-trial metacognitive prediction-plus-feedback intervention significantly reduced reminder bias, producing more optimal reminder-setting behaviour [+M]; with an LLM assistant, In one preregistered experiment, metacognitive feedback on learners' own LLM use lowered the odds of answer offloading and raised the odds of a correct unaided answer on one-sided tests, with 95% intervals reaching no effect [+M]. A reward for effort did not do the same (An effort-based reward did not significantly reduce answer offloading or improve unaided performance [~M]), and framing the cost of an aid as a loss rather than a gain left adults still offloading more than was optimal (Participants still offload more than optimal under loss framing [~M], dz = 0.41 under loss framing against 0.82 under gain framing in the same experiment), so do not rely on wording alone.
  7. Check each learner with the aids removed. End each session with one or two items, or one step of the task, done alone with the shared representation and tools out of reach, if step 1 names an unaided outcome. Record what the learner did with and without the aids side by side, because the aid alone can account for a large gap: adults remembered nearly all intentions with reminders and about two-thirds without (Task accuracy is far higher with reminders than internal memory and is unaffected by reminder framing [+M], ηp² = 0.77, online memory task). Put the high-stakes steps in the unaided check first: a review reports that people offload higher-value intentions more readily, which leaves memory holding the low-value ones if the aid fails (Higher-value intentions are more likely to be offloaded than lower-value ones [~W], one study reported second-hand). Basis: the week-8 unaided result above; answer offloading during learning was associated with lower unaided test performance in an exploratory analysis (Greater answer offloading during learning is associated with lower subsequent unaided performance (exploratory) [~M]). The review distinction between offloading that replaces internal processing and offloading that duplicates it is a framework, not a finding (Substitutive offloaders show severe performance decline when external stores become unavailable, while duplicative offloaders maintain accuracy [~W]).
  8. Fade the external structure across sessions, and move learners on by the unaided check. A default arc for a unit of four to six sessions: early sessions, the teacher provides the shared representation already laid out, assigns roles, and bounds the tool (hints and checks, no finished answers); middle sessions, the group builds its own representation from a blank template, roles rotate each session so every learner holds each part once while group membership stays the same across the unit, and the prediction-and-feedback check of step 6 runs on two or three items per session; late sessions, the template is withdrawn, the tool is available only on request, and the unaided check covers the whole of what step 1 names. Move a learner or group to the next stage once they pass the unaided check in two consecutive sessions; return to the earlier stage after a failed one. If step 1 names only system performance (a team procedure where the checklist stays in use at work), do not fade the representation: fade the teacher's prompting and keep the artifact. A shared, visible representation may itself let the teacher withdraw: in one qualitative comparison of nursing problem-based-learning groups, the group working on an interactive whiteboard needed less teacher scaffolding in the harder late stage, while the traditional-whiteboard group needed more (The IW-supported group needed less teacher scaffolding in the late problem-solving stage, where nurse and instructor discourse decreased, while scaffolding increased from early to late stage in the TW condition [+W]). The arc and thresholds are untested proposals. Keeping groups together has one basis: in a 2×2 experiment with 240 secondary students in groups of three, groups with prior experience of collaborating outperformed new groups in the learning phase (a large multivariate effect, ηp² = .26, not interacting there with how densely the task information was split among members; In the learning phase, group experience and information distribution each produce significant multivariate effects on performance, mental effort, and efficiency, without interacting [+M]) and on an individual test a week later (Groups with prior collaborative experience on similar tasks outperform inexperienced groups on individual retention tests after collaborative learning of complex tasks [+M], no effect size printed for that contrast). Support that responds to the learner's last attempt is better supported than fixed support (Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M], one-to-one tutoring in most of its studies), and how to fade is modelled in Scaffolding and Fading.

A default 50-minute session (untested proposal): 5 minutes to set or check roles and open the shared representation; 30 minutes of joint work with the representation kept current; 5 minutes of prediction and comparison on the aid decision; 10 minutes for the unaided check and a note of what it showed. In a 25-minute session, halve the joint work and keep the unaided check to one item.

When a condition is not met: if the task fits in one head, drop the group and the shared artifact; if no one can see the shared representation during the work (no screen, no wall), give each learner a paper copy of the same layout and collect them at the end; if the brief wants system performance only, skip step 7 and evaluate the team's product with every aid in place.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which outcome counts: the system or the solo performer. A team that will always work with its checklist and its colleagues needs a well-designed system; a learner who will sit an unaided exam or work alone later needs the critical parts kept in their own head. If unstated, ask: when this matters later, which of these aids and people will the learner have with them?
  • How the learner currently uses aids on this task. Under-use (children, or learners who do not trust tools) and over-use (reaching for the tool before trying) need opposite moves. If unstated, ask: on one item now, does the learner try first or reach for the aid, and how accurate are they each way?
  • Whether the task is too large for one person. Distribution earns its cost only when one learner cannot hold or do the whole task in the time. If unstated, ask: could one capable learner do this alone in the time given, and what would they have to write down to do it?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: which aids each learner used on which item, whether they predicted their own unaided performance and how accurately, what the shared representation showed at the end, which role each person held, and how each learner did on the unaided check. "Relies on the group", "has internalised the procedure" and "offloads too much" are inferred states. Performance with the aids present shows what the system can do, not what any one member can do.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No study recorded in the wiki compares distributing a task across people, roles or shared representations with individual work; the one such comparison is reported second-hand in a review (below). The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship or limit it.

  • Salomon distinguishes effects with the computer (system performance) from effects of the computer (cognitive residue on the solo performer) [+M]: a 1999 conference paper's synthesis of the distributed-cognition literature, with Pea's critique that a low share of user contribution need not be alarming and that user–tool relationships evolve. A conceptual distinction; no data.
  • Bounded AI support outperformed open collaboration on all four Week 8 independent no-AI outcomes, though exact wild-cluster p values sat at the 0.050–0.063 boundary [+M] and Open AI collaboration yielded the highest supported writing quality, while bounded AI support yielded the highest independent no-AI Week 8 performance [+M]: one class-clustered quasi-experiment, 168 students in six intact classes, three conditions (no AI, bounded, open). Supported writing was highest under open collaboration; at the week-8 independent assessment the bounded condition was highest, with student-level g from 0.83 to 1.21, but class-clustered p values of 0.050–0.063 with six classes. The claim page's evidence line counts two studies; both entries are the same article. It is the clearest case in the wiki of effects with and effects of diverging, and it concerns an AI tool, not peers.
  • In Experiment 1, a five-trial metacognitive prediction-plus-feedback intervention significantly reduced reminder bias, producing more optimal reminder-setting behaviour [+M]: pre-registered online experiment, 164 adults, an optimal-offloading memory task with financial incentive; feedback reduced reminder bias against no feedback (d = .347, one-tailed). Both groups still set more reminders than optimal (In Experiment 1, participants in both groups were significantly biased towards excessive reminder use relative to the optimal strategy [+M]).
  • In one preregistered experiment, metacognitive feedback on learners' own LLM use lowered the odds of answer offloading and raised the odds of a correct unaided answer on one-sided tests, with 95% intervals reaching no effect [+M]: a pre-registered online experiment; feedback lowered the odds of answer offloading (OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.22 to 1.00) and raised the odds of a correct unaided test item (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 0.98 to 2.33). The second interval includes no effect, so the title's "improves" is stronger than the interval. In the same study feedback broke the run of offloading on consecutive items without changing whether learners used the assistant at all (Metacognitive feedback disrupted the tendency to repeat answer offloading across consecutive items without changing whether participants engaged with the assistant [+M]), and an effort-based reward did not significantly change offloading or unaided performance (An effort-based reward did not significantly reduce answer offloading or improve unaided performance [~M]; equivalence not tested).
  • Individuals systematically overuse reminders relative to the optimal offloading level, and reminder bias correlates with confidence and metacognitive bias [+M] and Feedback interventions show phase-specific effects: advice feedback reduces reminder bias, but ranking feedback alters beliefs without increasing offloading [~M]: one 2026 review reporting earlier studies second-hand. Advice feedback reduced reminder bias; ranking feedback shifted beliefs but did not increase offloading. Not every kind of feedback moves the decision.
  • Task accuracy is far higher with reminders than internal memory and is unaffected by reminder framing [+M] and Participants still offload more than optimal under loss framing [~M]: one article's online experiments on an intention-offloading task. With reminders forced, accuracy was near ceiling; forced to remember internally, participants recalled about two-thirds (ηp² = 0.77): a large effect with the aid. Framing the reminder cost as a loss left the over-reliance bias significant (Experiment 1, dz = 0.41; dz = 0.82 under gain framing). The article's comparison between the two framings is not on the claim page, so this page does not say loss framing helps.
  • Groups using the Graphical Debate-tool constructed representations of significantly higher quality than groups using the Textual Debate-tool, for both debate positions. [+M], GD groups wrote essays significantly better in total grounds quality, total conceptual quality, and average conceptual quality, but did not cover more topics and were not better in average grounds quality. [~M] and The number of items groups placed in their representations was negatively correlated with the average quality of those items. [~W]: one study (Janssen et al., 2008), 124 eleventh-graders in 39 groups, classes assigned to a graphical or a textual debate tool. Representation quality favoured the graphical tool with large d (values not on the claim page); group essays were better on three of five quality measures and no better on topic coverage or average grounds quality, and the advantage disappeared for longer topics; logged tool activity did not differ. More items went with lower average item quality (r = −.66, correlational). All are group products; whether individual understanding differed is not recorded in these claims.
  • In the learning phase, group experience and information distribution each produce significant multivariate effects on performance, mental effort, and efficiency, without interacting [+M] and Groups with prior collaborative experience on similar tasks outperform inexperienced groups on individual retention tests after collaborative learning of complex tasks [+M]: a 2×2 experiment, 240 secondary students in groups of three. Prior collaborative experience gave a large multivariate effect in the learning phase (ηp² = .26) and better individual retention a week later; how densely the task information was split among members had a smaller multivariate effect (ηp² = .10), not interacting with experience in that phase. It compares group with group, not group with individual.
  • Collaborative learning distributes cognitive load across multiple working memories, aiding learning of high-load tasks [+W]: a review's second-hand report of F. Kirschner et al. (2009b), that learning from high-load tasks was more likely in a collaborative than an individual setting. No effect size, design or sample is on the claim page; it is the only distributed-against-individual comparison the wiki reports, and it is not recorded at first hand.
  • Two people discussing perceptual decisions can outperform the better individual, via weighted confidence sharing [+W]: a review's report of a psychophysics experiment in which pairs who discussed disagreements beat the better partner alone. A system-performance result (effects with the partner) on a perception task with adults, not a learning outcome; it suggests that sharing confidence, not only answers, is part of what makes a pair better than its best member.
  • Limits. Cognitive offloading is positively, not negatively, associated with transformative learning experience, contrary to the hypothesized negative effect [+W]: in a 912-student survey, delegating tasks to generative AI positively predicted self-reported transformative learning (β = 0.333, f² = 0.140), against the authors' hypothesis. A self-report association, but it warns against treating all offloading as loss. Strategic cognitive offloading can support transformative learning only when the offloading decision is intentional [~W] reports (second-hand, no effect sizes) that the benefit depends on the decision being intentional, which is what step 6 trains. Review reports a developmental shift in cognitive offloading: children under-use external aids while adolescents over-rely on them [~W] means the direction of the correction depends on age. A tool's effectiveness results from the whole configuration of events, activities, and contexts in which it is used [~M] (Salomon, cited; Logo results varied by context) warns against expecting a tool or artifact alone to change much.
  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [~S]: reviews and experiments on immediate memory, gathered in its table from four distinct studies; the basis for external structure lowering load, carried here from memory tasks to shared representations, which no study in it tests.
  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] and Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M]: individual self-regulation reviews and mostly one-to-one tutoring studies (its table gathers 25 distinct studies across it and narrower claims); carried here to visible group progress and to fading the shared representation.

Learners, tasks and settings differ across these (online adults on memory tasks, students writing with AI in six classes, surveys), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a peer group with a shared representation against individuals.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do learners rely on external aids more, or less, than their own performance warrants? Individuals systematically overuse reminders relative to the optimal offloading level, and reminder bias correlates with confidence and metacognitive bias [+M]
  • Does feedback on their own performance make learners' decisions to offload better calibrated? Feedback interventions show phase-specific effects: advice feedback reduces reminder bias, but ranking feedback alters beliefs without increasing offloading [~M]
  • Does the form of a group's shared representation change what the group produces? Groups using the Graphical Debate-tool constructed representations of significantly higher quality than groups using the Textual Debate-tool, for both debate positions. [+M] (group products only)
  • Do groups that have worked together before learn more from a distributed task? Groups with prior collaborative experience on similar tasks outperform inexperienced groups on individual retention tests after collaborative learning of complex tasks [+M]
  • Is distributing a high-load task across a group better than individual work? Only a second-hand report in the wiki: Collaborative learning distributes cognitive load across multiple working memories, aiding learning of high-load tasks [+W]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is either a working system (a team or learner that performs well with its aids) or individual capability that survives removing them, at a stated horizon; say which. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the tools and the group: to finish the task, to look competent in front of peers, to spend less effort, to be able to do the job alone. One claim page reports an observation that many student computer users' goal seems to be avoiding cognitive load, at odds with the educational objective (Individual user goals, such as avoiding cognitive load, can contradict the objectives of the educational community [~M]). Agreement and divergence are both observations: a learner whose goal is to finish will offload whatever the design allows, so the design, not exhortation, decides what stays with them.

For example, a nursing student on placement may want to get through the medication round without mistakes, using the ward's checklist and a senior colleague; the designer's objective may be that the student can explain each check unaided. Both can be served: run the round with the checklist (the system), then ask for the reason behind two checks with the checklist put away (the residue). Record both, so that a clean round is not read as the unaided understanding.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing distributed and individual work on the same task find no difference in unaided outcomes when the critical parts are kept with each learner, or find that keeping them there costs system performance without any unaided gain. If calibration training (prediction plus feedback) does not carry from memory and LLM tasks to group work and shared representations, step 6 should be dropped for groups. If learners who offload heavily turn out to do as well alone at a delay, as the positive survey association hints, the unaided check matters less than this page says. If fading a shared representation harms performance in settings where the artifact stays in use on the job, the "keep the artifact" branch should become the default. If a graphical shared representation improves the group's product but not what each member can do alone, step 3's argument-map advice is about effects with only and should say so; if regrouping learners every session does no harm at a delay, the stable-groups default can go. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong part was offloaded" after the fact.

Performance with aids present, unaided performance at the end of a session, delayed retention, and the learner's use of the aids in their own setting are separate claims. The present evidence sets no rule for how much to distribute, no group size and no fading schedule.

Illustrative¶

Shared Documents — Learners co-construct an artifact that carries part of the group’s memory, coordination, and reasoning.

Checklists, diagrams, and dashboards in team-based work — External representations reduce memory burden and help groups coordinate complex tasks.

Studio or lab collaboration — Different learners, tools, and artifacts each carry part of the cognitive work, and success depends on how well those pieces fit together.

Ward or workplace job aids — A checklist or performance support tool stays part of the job; training judges the team with it in use and checks unaided only the steps the job requires from memory.
