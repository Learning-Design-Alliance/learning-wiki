# Mindfulness Activities

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 19 claims (8 for, 10 mixed, 1 against) · 11 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 1 causal, 1 design, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 11 report an effect size · 17 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **the short attention routine placed at a transition into learning**: one to a few minutes in which learners pause, slow their breathing or movement, notice what they feel and where their attention is, and then turn to a named task. For a learner whose attention or composure is unsettled at the start of demanding work (arriving distracted, after a stressful event, before an assessment or a presentation), a brief, optional routine tied to the next task is expected to shorten the time it takes to settle into that task and to support self-regulation during it (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`), and only through that to support performance on the task (`immediate-performance`). It is a readiness support, not instruction: it teaches no content, and any learning benefit runs through better use of the time that follows.

**No claim in the wiki tests the classroom routine itself** (a teacher-led pause before a lesson, compared with the same lesson without it). One classroom experiment tests a neighbouring configuration and did not find the expected benefit: seventh graders using an algebra tutor with added mindfulness interventions learned no more, and reported no less math anxiety, than peers using the same tutor without them, and they completed fewer practice steps ([Math learning gains did not differ between the Mindful and Cognitive conditions](../claims/no-condition-difference-math-learning.md) [-M]). Reviews report benefits of whole mindfulness *programmes* (several weeks, many sessions) for adolescents' regulation and stress, without effect sizes, and judge the school evidence too variable for definitive recommendations about specific practices ([The evidence base for school mindfulness interventions is promising but too variable for definitive practice recommendations](../claims/mbi-evidence-base-variability-limitation.md) [~M]). So the expectation for a one-minute routine before a task rests on the mechanism (noticing one's state is part of self-regulation) and on programme-level reviews carried down to a single routine, which is a step the evidence does not take.

How the neighbouring converted pages sit beside this one: [Check-ins](check-ins.md) owns the short report of a learner's state and what the teacher does with it; a mindfulness routine may end in a check-in but is not one. [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md) owns checking one's work against a criterion during the task; this page stops at the start of the task. [Positive Self-Talk](positive-self-talk.md) owns the words a learner uses at a hard moment. The [Social-Emotional Learning pattern](../patterns/social-emotional-learning.md) holds a multi-week programme that teaches regulation as a named skill; a school that wants regulation itself as the outcome is closer to that pattern than to a routine before a lesson.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name the task the routine prepares for, and put it right before that task.** Typical places: the opening of a lesson, the move from a heated discussion into focused work, the minute before a test, a performance or a presentation. Say in one sentence what it is for ("one minute to settle, then we start the proof"). Untested proposal, from the earlier page ("connect the routine to a concrete learning demand").
2. **Keep it short: one to three minutes**, and no more than about one-twentieth of the session (two to three minutes in a 50-minute lesson, under one minute in a 10-minute block). The earlier page's own example was "one or two minutes"; the share of session time is an untested proposal, set so that the routine does not crowd out instruction. In the one tutor experiment, learners given mindfulness interventions completed fewer practice steps in the same time ([Mindful-condition students completed fewer problem-solving steps and requested fewer hints per step than Cognitive-condition students](../claims/mindful-condition-fewer-steps-hints.md) [~M]), which is the cost to watch.
3. **Offer a small menu of plain practices.** Three slow breaths; noticing the feet on the floor and the sounds in the room; slow movement paired with breathing (arms up on the in-breath, down on the out-breath); a pause before speaking. The practices are those a school brief lists as common in school mindfulness work (deep breathing, body awareness, mindful movement, pausing before speaking, guided relaxation; [mbi-evidence-base-variability-limitation](../claims/mbi-evidence-base-variability-limitation.md) [~M]), which also says no specific practice can yet be recommended over another. Untested as a choice of practice.
4. **Make it an invitation, never a requirement, and ask for no disclosure.** Eyes may stay open; a learner may simply sit quietly or look over the task instead. No one is asked to say what they felt. Untested proposal, from the earlier page (low-pressure participation; inward focus can be dysregulating for some learners).
5. **End with one task-facing question the learner answers privately**: "What do I need for the next twenty minutes?" or "Where is my attention now, and where does it need to be?" This is the link to self-regulation the earlier page relied on ([Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M], a review and a theoretical synthesis about monitoring against a goal, carried here to noticing one's own attention; neither tests a mindfulness routine).
6. **Start the task at once.** The first item of work is ready on the desk or screen before the routine begins, so the settled minute is used. Untested proposal.
7. **The adult models the pause, including before reacting.** The teacher or facilitator does the routine with the group, and uses the same short pause visibly (for example three breaths) before answering something provocative. Untested proposal, from the schoolwide strategy page linked under Examples.
8. **Plan the hand-over across sessions, not only one session.** A default arc (all quantities untested proposals):
   - *Weeks 1–2:* the adult leads the same two-minute script daily at the same point in the lesson, names its purpose, and models it.
   - *Weeks 3–6:* learners choose one of two or three practices; the cue shortens to about thirty seconds ("settle"); learners take turns leading it.
   - *After about six weeks:* learners use the pause on their own before demanding moments (a test, a presentation, a disagreement), and the whole-class routine drops to the occasions that need it (before assessments, after disruptions).
   - *When to move on:* once most learners start the routine on the cue without instructions, and can say when they would use it on their own, shorten or fade the whole-class version. If the routine has become a chat or a struggle to start, shorten it to the breath cue alone or drop it.
9. **If regulation itself is the goal, plan a programme, not a routine.** Teaching learners to regulate stress or conflict (rather than settling them before a lesson) calls for a sequenced programme over weeks with practice in real situations; the reviews that report regulation benefits concern programmes of that kind ([A practice brief reviewing self-regulation interventions reports substantial benefits of mindfulness programs for cognitive and emotional regulation, stress and mental health in adolescents and young adults](../claims/mindfulness-programs-benefit-adolescent-regulation.md) [+M], a practice brief summarising a review, no effect size printed). See the [Social-Emotional Learning pattern](../patterns/social-emotional-learning.md) for that design.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time to spare, skip the routine and keep the instruction; if learners show distress during inward-focus practice, switch to an outward-focus one (eyes open, describe five things in the room) or drop it for them.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the routine is for.** Settling before a lesson, getting through an assessment, de-escalating after conflict and teaching regulation as a skill are different designs with different doses, and only the last has (review-level) evidence of its own. If unstated, ask: what should be different in the five minutes after the routine, and what over a term?
- **Who the learners are, and what they bring.** Age, prior experience of mindfulness, and especially stress or trauma history change which practices are safe and how much choice to give. If unstated, ask: are there learners for whom closing their eyes or attending to their body is likely to be distressing, and is there an adult who would notice?
- **Who leads it, and how much time they have.** A trained, willing facilitator with a stable group can run a daily routine; a teacher with one minute and no buy-in, a volunteer, or software cannot do the same thing. If unstated, ask: who will lead it, how confident are they, and how many minutes does the session have?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behaviour around the routine: whether the learner joined, sat out or disrupted it; how long the group took to start the task afterwards; time on task in the first minutes; any learner's own private rating of focus, if the brief asks for one. "Calmer", "more regulated" and "ready to learn" are inferred states. A quiet room after a routine shows compliance with a quiet routine; it does not show that attention or anxiety changed.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| The group settles faster after the routine than on days without it | The routine helped; the fixed start signal did the work (any consistent opening would); the days differed in other ways | Compare with days that open with a different consistent start signal (say, a do-now task) of the same length. |
| A learner refuses or sits out every time | Discomfort with inward focus (possibly trauma-related); belief or cultural objection; finds it pointless; wants to avoid being watched | Offer an eyes-open, outward-focus alternative privately and see whether they take it; ask privately what would help them start work. |
| Giggling, fidgeting or talk during the routine | Unfamiliarity and self-consciousness (common early on); the routine is too long for the age; it has become a break from work | Shorten to thirty seconds for a week and see whether settling improves; check whether the disruption fades after the first two weeks. |
| Learners report feeling calmer, but task performance does not change | The routine affects mood but not the task; the task's difficulty, not the learner's state, limited performance; the measure is not sensitive | Look at whether learners started and stayed on the task, not only at scores; compare with a session where support for the task itself was added. |
| Fewer practice items completed in sessions with the routine | Time taken by the routine; learners pacing themselves more slowly; less help-seeking | Count minutes of practice time with and without the routine; look at accuracy per item, which did not differ in the tutor study. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Asking about feelings is itself an intervention, so record what was asked and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares a short mindfulness routine before a lesson with the same lesson without it. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship or report programme-level findings.

- [Math learning gains did not differ between the Mindful and Cognitive conditions](../claims/no-condition-difference-math-learning.md) [-M]: 42 completing seventh graders randomly assigned within classes to two versions of an algebra intelligent tutoring system, one with added mindfulness interventions (`randomized`, `classroom`); both groups improved from pretest to posttest, with no group or interaction effect. It is the closest test in the wiki, and the mindfulness version gave no learning advantage. The claim page does not describe the mindfulness interventions themselves, the horizon is immediate, and equivalence was not tested, so the null does not show the routine is useless, only that no benefit was detected here.
- [The mindfulness interventions did not significantly change anticipatory or executive state-MA relative to cognitive support alone](../claims/no-condition-difference-state-ma.md) [~M]: in the same experiment, state math anxiety showed no group difference; executive state anxiety fell over time in both groups ([Using the ITS reduced executive state-math anxiety and improved algebra learning across conditions](../claims/its-reduces-executive-state-ma-improves-algebra.md) [~M]), so the drop cannot be credited to the mindfulness additions.
- [Mindful-condition students completed fewer problem-solving steps and requested fewer hints per step than Cognitive-condition students](../claims/mindful-condition-fewer-steps-hints.md) [~M]: exploratory log analysis from all participating students; the mindfulness group completed fewer steps and used fewer hints per step, while error rates per step did not differ ([Error rates per completed problem-solving step did not significantly differ between conditions](../claims/no-difference-error-rate-log.md) [~M]). It is the basis for treating time taken from practice as the routine's main cost.
- [Mindful-condition students rated the pedagogical agent as more supportive and more concerned about their well-being on single items](../claims/mindful-agent-single-item-support.md) [+W]: two single questionnaire items favoured the mindfulness version; single-item analyses, no effect size. A perception of support, not a learning outcome.
- [The evidence base for school mindfulness interventions is promising but too variable for definitive practice recommendations](../claims/mbi-evidence-base-variability-limitation.md) [~M]: a school practice brief (narrative review) noting wide variation in implementation, including programme length, and an evidence base with children and youths not developed enough for definitive recommendations about specific practices. It limits every row of the table above.
- [A practice brief reviewing self-regulation interventions reports substantial benefits of mindfulness programs for cognitive and emotional regulation, stress and mental health in adolescents and young adults](../claims/mindfulness-programs-benefit-adolescent-regulation.md) [+M]: a practice brief's summary of a review of preventive self-regulation interventions with high schoolers and young adults; programmes, not single routines; no effect size printed.
- [Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]: a review and a theoretical synthesis on monitoring performance against a goal. It supplies the mechanism the earlier page relied on, carried from checking one's work to noticing one's own attention; neither source studies mindfulness.
- [Implementation of co-regulation strategies was shaped by classroom context, facilitator experience and mindset, and varied in ease across strategies](../claims/co-regulation-implementation-factors-context-experience-strategy-ease.md) [~M]: implementation data from nine youth-programme sites; classroom size, session length and teacher buy-in shaped use, more experienced and open-minded facilitators used the strategies more, and breath to focus was the hardest to implement. A limit on who can run a breath-based routine well.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (seventh graders in a tutor, adolescents in programmes, youth programmes, the general self-regulation literature), and only one prints an effect size (for the time effect common to both tutor groups), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a delayed outcome of a mindfulness routine.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective for a routine is usually readiness for the next task: a quicker, calmer start and sustained attention, with any learning gain coming from the task itself. Elicit separately what learners want from it: a moment's rest, a way to manage nerves before a test or a performance, permission not to take part, or nothing at all. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the minute as rest may settle well and not use it for the task; a learner who finds it awkward may comply outwardly and gain nothing.

For example, a secondary teacher's objective may be a faster start to a mathematics lesson, while a student's own goal is to stop freezing in presentations. The opening routine serves the teacher's objective; offering the same one-minute grounding just before the student's next presentation, and asking afterwards whether it helped, tests the student's. Record both, so that a quiet room at the start of maths is not read as the help the student wanted.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken further if classroom comparisons of a short routine against the same lesson with an equally consistent, non-mindfulness opening show no difference in time to settle or time on task, as the tutor experiment showed no difference in learning or anxiety. It should strengthen if such comparisons show faster settling and more time on task, especially for learners who start unsettled, with no loss of practice time. If programme-level benefits for regulation turn out to come from multi-week teaching of the skill and not from the daily routine, the routine should be reframed as one part of a regulation programme rather than a stand-alone support. If reports of distress during inward-focus practice are frequent in a population, the outward-focus option should become the default there. Do not protect the model by calling every null "too short" or "not practised enough" after the fact.

Settling at the start of a task, sustained attention, lower anxiety, learning on the task, and a self-regulation habit used without a cue are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose, no best practice and no effect of a single routine on learning.

## Description
Mindfulness activities are short structured practices that help learners attend to the present moment, notice internal state, and regulate reaction before acting. In learning settings, these activities are often used to support focus, emotional regulation, transition into challenging work, or recovery from stress. Their instructional value is usually indirect: they do not teach content by themselves, but they can improve the conditions under which learners are ready to engage, persist, and monitor themselves.

## Implications
Mindfulness activities are best treated as readiness supports rather than as core instruction. Brief, bounded routines can help some learners settle attention, notice stress, and transition into demanding work with more self-control, which may improve later engagement and use of feedback by strengthening self-monitoring and regulation [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]. But the benefits are indirect and variable, so the design principle is modesty: make participation low-pressure, connect the routine to a concrete learning demand, and avoid treating mindfulness as a substitute for instruction, accommodation, or broader support.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A bounded, purposeful routine**: Activities should have a clear role such as settling attention, preparing for work, or recovering after stress.
- **Low-pressure participation**: Learners should not feel coerced into disclosure or into practices that conflict with their beliefs or comfort.
- **Developmentally and contextually appropriate framing**: The activity should be explained in practical, learning-related terms.
- **Connection to the task**: Mindfulness routines are strongest when they support transition into a specific learning activity.
#### Constraints
- **Overclaiming**: Mindfulness is not a cure-all for anxiety, trauma, or disengagement.
- **Trauma sensitivity**: Some inward-focus exercises can be dysregulating for some learners and should be optional or adapted.
- **Instructional displacement**: Mindfulness should not crowd out direct learning time without a clear purpose.
- **Indirect claim base in this repo**: Current linked claims support regulation and monitoring mechanisms more than mindfulness interventions directly.

### Target Learners
- **Learners who need support with transition and attention**: Brief routines can help learners shift into focused work.
- **Learners experiencing stress or overload**: Mindfulness may help some learners recover enough to re-engage.
- **Adult learners balancing competing demands**: Strong fit when learners arrive mentally fragmented or fatigued.
- **Instructors aiming to support self-regulation**: The routines are most defensible when framed as regulation supports rather than as universal wellness solutions.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Attention regulation**: Helping learners settle and sustain focus for the next task.
- **Self-awareness**: Increasing awareness of stress, distraction, or reactivity.
- **Improved task readiness**: Creating a more stable starting condition for difficult learning.
- **Response inhibition and pause**: Helping learners react less impulsively under challenge.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Self-regulated learning — mindfulness routines can support monitoring and regulation of attention and emotion.
- Cognitive load perspectives — reducing distraction and internal noise may improve readiness for demanding tasks.
- Attentional control perspectives — brief routines can act as transitions that help learners reset before work.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Benefits are variable and highly dependent on activity design, learner fit, and context.
- Mindfulness is supportive rather than sufficient; it does not replace instruction, accommodation, or mental-health care.

### Claims

- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — mindfulness routines are most defensible when they help learners notice and regulate attention or stress before engaging in work
