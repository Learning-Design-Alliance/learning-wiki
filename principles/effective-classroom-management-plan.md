# Effective Classroom Management Plan Criteria

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 24 claims (15 for, 8 mixed, 1 against) · 26 studies (10 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 6 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 26 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **classroom-wide management plan**: the routines, relationships and responses a teacher sets up for a whole group so that most of the time goes to learning. For a group of learners in a shared space (a `classroom`, a workshop, a live online class), a plan that a teacher puts in place before problems arise and keeps consistently is expected to produce less disruptive behavior and more time engaged in learning (`behavioural`) than ordinary, improvised practice. The plan has six parts, from the earlier page: positive relationships with every learner; prevention of attention-seeking and work-avoidance through the room, taught rules and taught routines; quick, unobtrusive redirection of minor misbehavior; a few simple, consistently used responses to persistent misbehavior; teaching self-control, with any external controls planned to fade; and respect for cultural differences in what a correction or a reward means.

The relationship is conditional on the plan being **taught and kept**, not only written (a rule nobody rehearsed is not a routine, and inconsistently applied responses are what the plan replaces); on the problem being **group-wide or ordinary** (a single learner whose behavior keeps recurring despite a working plan needs an individual assessment); on the teacher being **able to see and reach every learner** (in person or online); and on the responses being simple enough to use on a busy day. The expected change is in disruptive behavior and engaged time over `days-weeks` to `term-plus`. A gain in `general-achievement` is not established by anything recorded here, and neither is lasting self-control once external supports are withdrawn.

**What is tested, and what is not.** One meta-analysis of 12 studies (Oliver, Wehby & Reschly 2011) compares universal, whole-classroom management programmes with usual practice in K–12 classrooms and finds less disruptive and aggressive behavior in the treated classrooms in all 12 studies. That tests the *core* of this page: a planned, universal approach against improvisation. **No claim tests the six-part plan as a whole, compares one tradition (humanist, behavior-analytic, prevention-oriented) with another or with a blend, or tests the self-control or cultural parts as parts of a management plan.** The same meta-analysis found no moderators and no difference between a manualised programme and the others, so it cannot say which parts carry the effect. The other claims test single moves: greeting each learner at the door, an empathic rather than punitive stance on discipline, students recording their own on-task behavior, taught social-emotional skills, and the use of rewards.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page. [Functional Behavior Assessment](functional-behavior-assessment.md) owns the individual learner whose behavior recurs despite a working classroom plan; this page is its precondition ("check universal practice first") and does not repeat its model. [Culturally Responsive Classroom Norms](culturally-responsive-classroom-norms.md) owns the interaction and correction norms themselves (wait time, public questioning, eye contact, how behavior is corrected) and is how this page's sixth part is carried out. [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md) owns learners checking their own work during a task; here self-recording is used only for on-task behavior. [Clear Structure](clear-structure.md) owns how lesson content is organized; [Check-Ins](check-ins.md) holds the short private routines for asking a learner how things are going.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's six criteria, made concrete as a plan a teacher can put in place and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it; quantities are untested proposals unless a claim is cited.

1. **Before the first session: room, rules and routines on one page.** Arrange the space so you can see every learner and reach any of them in a few steps, with the main area open. Write three to five rules, stated as what to do ("Listen while someone is speaking"), and a procedure for each recurring task: entering and starting work, the attention signal, getting and returning materials, moving between activities, asking for help, what to do when finished early, and leaving. Untested as a set; from the earlier page (criterion 2) and [Classroom Space and Procedural Design](classroom-space-and-procedural-design.md). Planned, universal management against usual practice is supported: [universal classroom management](../claims/universal-classroom-management-reduces-problem-behavior.md) [+M] (12 studies, K–12; which components matter is not known).
2. **Weeks 1 to 3: teach the routines as you would teach content.** For each procedure: say why it exists, show it, have the class rehearse it, give feedback, and rehearse again the next day. Spend about five to ten minutes a lesson on this in week 1, falling to a quick reminder before each use by week 3. Re-teach after holidays and whenever a routine starts to slip, rather than punishing the slip. Untested proposal; from the earlier page and the prevention tradition ([Kounin's Classroom Management Research](../theories/kounins-classroom-management-research.md), not read here as a claim).
3. **Every lesson: build the relationship deliberately.** Greet each learner by name as they arrive, with something positive and personal, not a correction. Make more positive contacts than corrections in each lesson (count them for a week and set your own ratio). Use learners' ideas in the lesson. Evidence: [positive greetings at the door](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M] (middle-school teachers randomly assigned to greetings against an attention control: more engaged time, less disruption; no effect size reported). The wider link between relationships and engagement is correlational: [teacher–student relationships](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [~M].
4. **Prevent attention-seeking and work-avoidance in the lesson itself.** Pitch tasks so most learners can start them; give a "what to do when stuck" routine (reread the instructions, ask a partner, signal for help, start the next item) so that being stuck does not become disruption; keep transitions short and signalled; move around the room and scan it while you work with one learner or group. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (criterion 2) and the prevention tradition (withitness and overlapping).
5. **Redirect minor misbehavior with the least intrusive move that works.** In order: move closer, make eye contact, say the learner's name in the flow of the lesson, give a quiet private prompt naming the expected behavior, acknowledge a nearby learner doing it, then wait a few seconds for the learner to correct themselves. The redirection should cause less disruption than the behavior did. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (criterion 3) and [Low-Profile Classroom Control](../strategies/low-profile-classroom-control.md).
6. **Persistent misbehavior: one short ladder, used the same way every time.** Decide in advance a sequence of no more than three or four steps (a specific private warning, a logical consequence tied to the behavior, such as finishing work at another time or repairing damage, a short conversation after the lesson, then contact with the family or a colleague) and use it consistently. Do not respond to every single act, and do not raise the severity when frustrated. Consequences should repair and restore, not shame ([Natural and Logical Consequences](natural-and-logical-consequences.md)). Untested as a ladder. A related finding on the teacher's stance: [empathic discipline](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M] (a brief exercise turning middle-school teachers from a punitive to an empathic mindset; in the preregistered replication the overall effect on suspensions was small and marginal, with larger reductions for previously suspended students and a narrower racial disparity). If one learner keeps climbing the ladder for two to three weeks, move to [Functional Behavior Assessment](functional-behavior-assessment.md).
7. **Teach self-control, and plan the fade of every external control.** Teach the skills the rules assume (how to ask for help, wait a turn, disagree, calm down) as short lessons, and from about grade 4 up let learners record their own on-task behavior at prompted moments. Evidence: [taught social-emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [+M] (universal school SEL programmes improve behavior; the achievement effect is small or negligible); [self-monitoring of on-task behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M] (single-case studies of students with or at risk for disabilities; on-task behavior, not learning). Prefer specific praise and the natural payoff of the activity to tangible prizes, and if a points or token system is used, tie it to a stated standard and write down when it will be thinned and withdrawn: [expected tangible rewards](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M] (two meta-analyses of mostly laboratory experiments disagree on size; praise does not undermine).
8. **Check the plan against cultural differences before using it.** Ask whether each redirection signal (a direct look, a raised voice, standing close, calling a name in public) and each reward or consequence (public praise, competition, removal from the group) means the same to your learners as it does to you, and widen the options where it does not. Read a departure from a classroom norm as a possible difference of convention before reading it as defiance. Carried out through [Culturally Responsive Classroom Norms](culturally-responsive-classroom-norms.md); untested as part of a management plan, apart from the narrowing of the suspension disparity in the empathic-discipline replication above.
9. **Review every two weeks against a simple record.** Keep a tally of disruptions in two chosen lessons a week, or sample engagement (every five minutes, how many learners are on task), and look at who receives corrections and consequences, by group. Change one part of the plan at a time. Untested proposal.

**Dose and progression across a term (untested proposal):** weeks 1 to 3, routines taught and rehearsed daily, frequent teacher prompts and praise, the ladder used rarely and explained each time; weeks 4 to 8, prompts reduced to the attention signal and a word, praise thinned from every instance to intermittent, learners take on class roles (materials, timekeeping, greeting visitors); from about week 8, learners help review the rules in a class meeting ([Class Meetings](../strategies/class-meetings.md)), self-recording replaces teacher prompts for those who need it, and any token system is thinned towards withdrawal. **Move on** from teaching a routine when it runs on one signal, without re-teaching, for two consecutive weeks; reopen it when the tally rises.

When a condition is not met: if the whole group is out of control, return to step 2 for every routine before adding consequences; if one or two learners account for most incidents, keep the plan for the class and start an individual assessment for them; if you cannot see the whole room, change the seating before anything else.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the problem is the group or one learner, and how serious it is.** Group-wide low-level disruption is what a classroom plan addresses; one learner's recurring behavior needs an individual assessment; dangerous behavior needs a safety plan first. If unstated, ask: how many learners are involved, how often, and has anyone been hurt?
- **Who the learners are and where they meet.** Age, whether they meet one teacher or many in a day, and whether they are in a room, online or both change what a routine, a greeting and a redirection look like. If unstated, ask: how old are they, how often do they meet this teacher, and in what setting?
- **What the plan has to fit into.** A school behavior policy, an existing points system or rubric, a fixed number of sessions, or a few minutes of adult time each set limits a plan must work within. If unstated, ask: is there a school or programme policy or routine already in place, and how much time is there to set the plan up?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behavior in a setting: how often learners are off task or disruptive, when (start of lesson, transitions, independent work), who, what the teacher did just before and just after, and how long it took to get the group back to work. "Out of control", "attention-seeking", "disrespectful" and "a weak manager" are inferences, and the same tally can come from different causes.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Disruption clusters at the start of lessons and at transitions | Routines for entry and transitions were never taught or have slipped; too much unstructured time; a trigger outside the room (after break, after another lesson) | Tally by time in the lesson for a week; re-teach the entry routine for three days and tally again; compare lessons after break with others. |
| Rules are known (learners can recite them) but not followed | Rules were posted, not rehearsed; consequences are applied inconsistently; following them costs the learner something they value (face with peers) | Watch whether the same act gets the same response each time; ask two or three learners privately what happens when someone breaks a rule. |
| Most incidents come from a few learners | An individual function (attention, escape from hard work); a skill those learners lack; the plan's tasks are not pitched for them | Give those learners an easier start to the task for a week; if the rate does not change, start an individual assessment. |
| Behavior improved, then slid back after a few weeks | Prompts and praise were thinned too fast; routines were not re-taught after a break; the teacher's responses drifted | Check the record of the teacher's own responses against the plan; restore daily rehearsal for one week. |
| Compliance is high but learners do not work when the teacher leaves or steps back | Control is external; self-control has not been taught or the fade was never planned | Leave the group with a task and a self-recording sheet for ten minutes and compare with teacher-present work. |
| One group of learners receives most corrections and consequences | A difference in classroom conventions read as defiance; bias in who is noticed; a real difference in behavior | Sample behavior by an observer blind to the tally; compare what was done with what was corrected; ask learners what a correction meant to them. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Changing a routine to test an explanation also changes the behavior, so record each change and its dates, and keep two explanations open while both still fit the record.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim compares the six-part plan with another plan or with improvisation, or compares the humanist, behavior-analytic and prevention traditions. The claims below test a planned universal approach as a whole and single moves within it.

- [Teachers' universal classroom management practices significantly reduce problem classroom behavior](../claims/universal-classroom-management-reduces-problem-behavior.md) [+M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 12 studies of universal, whole-classroom management interventions in K–12 classrooms (Oliver, Wehby & Reschly 2011, `q3 i2`), against usual practice, reports a mean classroom effect of 0.80 (ICC = .05) or .71 (ICC = .10), with study effects from −0.05 to 1.74. It supports planning management universally, ahead of problems. It does not say which practices carry the effect, and the outcome is behavior, not learning.
- [Students in treatment classrooms showed less disruptive behavior than control classrooms in all 12 studies](../claims/treatment-classrooms-less-disruptive-than-control.md) [+M]: the same review's conclusion that every included study favoured the treated classrooms, most of them randomised. Same study as above, not a second one.
- [Manualized COMP programs and other classroom management approaches do not differ significantly in effectiveness](../claims/comp-no-different-from-other-classroom-management.md) [~M] and [Effect sizes across classroom management studies are homogeneous](../claims/homogeneous-effect-sizes-classroom-management.md) [~M]: in the same review, seven studies of one manualised programme did not differ significantly from the other five, and the homogeneity test found no variation to explain, so no moderators were analysed. **These limit the model**: they leave no basis for preferring one package or tradition, and a non-significant difference across 12 studies does not establish that they are equivalent.
- [Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: middle-school teachers randomly assigned to greet students individually at the door, against an attention control, saw more academic engaged time and less disruption (Cook et al. 2018, `q3`); in a three-student single-case study, on-task behavior rose from a mean of 45% to 72% (`q1`). Neither reports a standardised effect size. It supports the relationship part of the plan at the start of a lesson.
- [Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [~M]: two meta-analyses of mostly correlational studies (mean r = .31 across 119 studies; associations with engagement stronger than with achievement in 99 studies). The direction is not shown: engaged learners may draw warmer relationships.
- [Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]: a brief online exercise for middle-school math teachers halved year-long suspensions in one randomised field experiment (9.6% to 4.8%); a preregistered, active-controlled replication with 66 randomised teachers found a small, marginal overall effect, and cut the Black/Hispanic-versus-other suspension disparity from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points. It bears on the persistent-misbehavior and cultural parts of the plan through the teacher's stance, and the replication's modest overall result should set expectations.
- [Self Monitoring Improves On Task Behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of single-case studies with students with or at risk for disabilities (grades 4–12 in one) found on-task behavior rose when students recorded their own behavior at prompted intervals, in one similarly with or without added reinforcement. It supports a self-control step for those learners; it is not a test with whole classes, and the outcome is on-task behavior.
- [Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [+M]: across 213 universal school programmes, conduct problems fell and positive social behavior rose by small amounts (ES 0.22 and 0.24) at post-test; achievement gains are small (0.27) in that synthesis and negligible (g = 0.08) in a later one restricted to rigorous designs. It supports teaching the skills the rules assume, not a promise of achievement gains.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]: two competing meta-analyses of mostly laboratory experiments (128 and 96 studies) disagree on the size of the undermining effect, but agree that verbal praise does not undermine intrinsic motivation; one found tangible rewards more detrimental for children. It qualifies the use of tangible rewards in a management plan and is the reason the plan writes down when any reward system fades. Classroom studies are not recorded.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (whole K–12 classrooms, middle-school doorways, suspension records, students with disabilities in single-case designs, laboratory free-choice tasks), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures whether a gain in classroom behavior lasts after the plan's supports are withdrawn, or whether it raises achievement.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The teacher's or school's objective is usually fewer disruptions and more engaged time, measured against a baseline over weeks, and sometimes fewer exclusions. Elicit separately what the learners want from the room: to be treated fairly and the same as others, not to be shown up in front of peers, to get help without asking out loud, to work with friends, to be able to concentrate. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being seen to fail may disrupt to avoid a public task; a plan that removes public failure serves both the objective and that goal, while a plan that only adds consequences serves the objective and works against the goal.

For example, in an adult evening class the tutor's objective is that sessions start on time and phones stay away; the learners, coming from work, value a few minutes to settle and a way to take an urgent call. A working agreement with a five-minute settling routine and a "step outside for calls" rule serves both. Record both, so that a quiet room is not read as learning.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if further comparisons of planned universal management against usual practice, with independent observers, show smaller or no reductions, or reductions that disappear once the programme's support ends. If studies separating components show that one part (for example taught routines, or relationship moves) carries most of the effect, the plan should lead with it and drop the rest to options. If self-control taught in a management plan does not outlast the external supports, the fifth part is a hope, not a design. If correction and exclusion records show the plan widening a gap between groups, the cultural part must change before anything else.

Fewer disruptions, more engaged time, learning gains, lasting self-control and fair treatment across groups are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no best package, no number of rules, no length of the teaching period for routines and no point at which supports can be faded.

## Description
Approaches to classroom management have historically grouped into three traditions: a **humanist** tradition emphasizing communication and problem-solving between teacher and student ([Ginott's Congruent Communication](../theories/ginotts-congruent-communication.md); [Glasser's Choice Theory](../theories/glassers-choice-theory-and-cooperative-learning.md)); an **applied behavior analysis** tradition applying behaviorist principles to the classroom (see [Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence Model](../elements/antecedent-behavior-consequence-model.md) and [Reinforcement Theory](../theories/behaviorism.md)); and a newer **classroom-management/prevention** tradition emphasizing the teaching skills involved in organizing instruction and preventing misbehavior before it starts ([Kounin's Classroom Management Research](../theories/kounins-classroom-management-research.md)). Each tradition has genuine strengths and limitations, and effective managers in practice blend elements of all three rather than relying on just one.

A comprehensive classroom management plan should incorporate strategies that accomplish six things:

1. **Establish positive relationships between all classroom participants.** A positive, supportive environment meeting students' needs for belonging and acceptance is a necessary foundation for an orderly classroom.
2. **Prevent attention-seeking and work-avoidance behaviors.** Management time is best spent engaging students in learning and preventing behaviors that interfere with it — through both physical-space arrangements and taught rules and routines for working in that space.
3. **Redirect misbehavior quickly and unobtrusively once it occurs.** Most classroom problems are minor off-task or attention-seeking events; techniques for handling them should not themselves cause more disruption than the original behavior.
4. **Stop persistent and chronic misbehavior with strategies simple enough to use consistently.** Management systems requiring a response to every single act of positive or negative behavior are rarely practical in a busy classroom.
5. **Teach self-control.** Students should have the opportunity to exercise internal control before external control is imposed, and any external controls that are imposed should come with an explicit plan for fading them out.
6. **Respect cultural differences.** Verbal and nonverbal techniques for redirecting disruptive behavior do not mean the same thing to every cultural group, and reward/consequence systems can unintentionally violate cultural norms (see [Culturally Responsive Classroom Norms](culturally-responsive-classroom-norms.md)).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A plan that draws on more than one tradition — purely reactive approaches (humanist, behavior-analytic) address misbehavior once it has occurred, but neither is inherently prevention-oriented the way the Kounin tradition is
- Explicit attention to cultural variation in how management techniques are received, not just to whether a technique "works" on average
#### Constraints
- Systems requiring a response to every instance of behavior are not practical at classroom scale and tend to break down under the ordinary busyness of a school day
- A plan built entirely around external control, without any fading toward student self-control, works against criterion 5 even if it succeeds at criteria 2-4 in the short term

### Target Learners
- All students in a K-12 classroom setting; the "respect cultural differences" criterion specifically foregrounds students whose cultural background diverges from mainstream classroom-management assumptions

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained engagement in learning tasks and minimized disruption, achieved through a durable management structure rather than moment-to-moment improvisation
