# Spaced Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 53 claims (28 for, 23 mixed, 1 against, 1 unmarked) · 39 studies (22 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 8 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 11 of 39 report an effect size · 46 claims rest on one study

## Description

A conditional model relating learner state, activity and subsequent observation; the linked pattern specifies a reusable design policy.

## Conditional relationship

When later retrieval is the target, distributing comparable learning opportunities can improve delayed performance relative to massing; gap, prior learning, retrieval conditions and assessment horizon constrain the relationship. This is a conditional explanatory model, not a universal individual forecast. The [spaced learning pattern](../patterns/spaced-learning.md) supplies a reusable observation and design policy. Specific local examples below illustrate it rather than define its scope.

**The relationship is tested, at the grain of spaced against massed practice of the same amount.** A synthesis of the verbal-recall literature found spaced presentation of the same study time raised final-test recall from 36.7% to 47.3% correct across 271 comparisons, with only 12 showing no benefit or a reversal; a meta-analysis across verbal, cognitive and motor tasks put the effect at d = 0.46, moderated by task type and larger in less rigorous studies; and a randomised experiment with 1,354 adults found the best gap beat a same-session review by d = 1.1 on recall tested up to a year later: [Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Spacing out retrieval practice beat massing it in a meta-analysis of 29 studies (g = 0.74), a comparison of spaced with massed retrieval, not of retrieval with rereading: [Spacing out retrieval practice beats massing it](../claims/spaced-retrieval-outperforms-restudy.md) [+M]. In a field experiment after a credit workshop, spaced quizzes beat massed quizzes (d = .66) and no quizzes (d = .39) about five months later, while massed quizzes did not differ detectably from none: [Spaced beat massed retrieval and no practice](../claims/spaced-beats-massed-and-control-financial-knowledge.md) [+M]. Weaker designs point the same way but do not isolate spacing: a dissertation experiment compared structured computer-assisted instruction with spaced practice and spaced review against unstructured instruction, so structure and spacing changed together ([Structured CAI with spaced practice and review beat unstructured CAI](../claims/structured-cai-spacing-improves-recall-and-retention.md) [+W]); and a 51-student ESL quasi-experiment with monthly audio repetition of target vocabulary scored 84.1 against 80.6 ([Spaced listening outperformed comparison students](../claims/svas-spaced-listening-outperforms-comparison-end-of-experiment.md) [+W]). In a one-group revision-podcast study, the centre whose learners kept their gains at 30 days and the centre whose scores decayed differed in how they used the podcasts; reading that as distributed against massed listening is the authors' interpretation of usage nobody assigned, with no control group: [Retention diverged by centre](../claims/podcast-retention-divergence-massed-vs-distributed.md) [~W]. A review of classroom-oriented spacing research states the same relationship for final tests generally, without a printed effect size: [Spaced sessions improve final-test performance](../claims/spacing-improves-final-test-performance.md) [+M].

**The relationship has a developmental boundary in the wiki's evidence.** In two small within-subject experiments, nursery-school children (mean age about 4) recalled massed and spaced repetitions of pictures equally well (46.7% each in one, 34.7% against 32.0% in the other), although repeating a picture did help them: [Preschoolers showed no spacing effect in free recall](../claims/preschoolers-no-spacing-effect-free-recall.md) [~M]. First-graders given the same procedure recalled spaced repetitions better (54.2% against 37.5%): [First-graders showed the spacing effect](../claims/first-graders-spacing-effect-free-recall.md) [+M]. The spacing there was lag within one list, tested at once, not gaps of days; the wiki holds no claim testing day-scale spacing with children under six.

## Learner state, objective and valued goal

This is a reusable design model. A study result supplies a bounded observation; it does not reveal the state or future outcome of a new learner.

Before selecting a configuration, record the target task and representation, task-specific prior experience, access and language constraints, time available, observed responses and the exact help provided. Preserve an unknown rather than replacing it with a generic label such as novice. Ask the learner what they want to accomplish and why it matters. Record that **valued goal** separately from the designer's **learning objective**; agreement is an observation to elicit, not an assumption. If they diverge, negotiate the task or purpose and retain the unresolved difference.

Define the intended capability, a representative assessment task and scoring instrument, the criterion chosen for this design, and the assessment horizon. Agree what response would warrant changing support and how to check later independent performance. Criteria are local decisions, not universal mastery thresholds. If the brief only asks for better learning, elicit these fields before selecting an exact dose or forecasting a gain.

Keep the record in this form: **response under stated conditions → uncertain state hypotheses → discriminating observation → next activity → response at the stated horizon**. Difficulty, accuracy, speed, confidence and engagement are different observations. None alone proves learning. Record a learner's changed purpose or constraints as well as a changed performance.

## Observation and adaptation

This diagnostic policy is a **design proposal, not a validated scheduling algorithm**. Start with an unassisted sample of the target items, record item-level errors and the time since last exposure, then separate cued recognition, recall and meaningful use. Record restudy, corrective feedback, retrieval opportunities and actual gaps; changing them together changes the intervention.

| Observation | Rival interpretations and discriminating observation | Proposed next action |
|---|---|---|
| An item is not recalled | Weak initial encoding and loss of access across the gap are different possibilities. Check comprehension/recognition with a cue and whether the learner can explain the association; retest unassisted after relearning and an interval. | If the association is not understood, clarify and re-encode it. If understood but inaccessible, use corrective feedback and another separated retrieval. Do not optimize the gap from a single failure. |
| Immediate recall is fluent | Recent repetition and durable retrieval both predict this. Use an unassisted check after the target delay. | Maintain the delayed check rather than count fluency as retained learning; revise the schedule only after collecting later evidence. |
| The learner rates items they just went over several times in a row as well known | The recent run of exposures makes items feel familiar whether or not they will be recalled later; in one free-recall experiment learners rated massed items as more likely to be recalled than spaced ones while recalling them worse ([Learners judged massed items more memorable though recall was worse](../claims/judgments-overconfidence-massed-items.md) [~M]). Compare the rating with an unassisted check after a gap. | Do not shorten or drop spaced reviews on the learner's sense of knowing; use the delayed check to decide. |
| Words are recalled but not used appropriately | Item retrieval and contextual language use are different capabilities; ask for meaning and use in a new conversational context. | Add contextual practice if recall succeeds but use fails. Do not claim that a word-pair recall study validates conversation transfer. |

If six of ten words are recalled today, that is a sample response, not a calibrated probability of recall next week. For a learner valuing conversation, record the designer's recall criterion (for example eight of ten is a local choice), the sampled prompts and the one-week horizon; also agree how appropriate use will be judged in a representative conversation. Do not silently substitute recall for that valued goal. If these fields are missing, elicit them before choosing a calendar schedule.

## Evidence-bounded expectation

A word-pair experiment separated total retrieval spacing from its relative arrangement: after first correct recall, repeated retrievals with intervening trials improved one-week recall relative to massed retrieval. At matched total nominal spacing, expanding, equal and contracting arrangements did not differ detectably: [absolute versus relative spacing observation](../claims/total-retrieval-spacing-beats-massed-but-relative-schedule-not-distinguished.md). The full source was checked. This does not establish equivalence or an advantage for expanding gaps, a universal calendar gap, or conversational use. Retain total practice, corrective feedback, initial learning criterion, actual gaps and final-test horizon when comparing schedules.

The same pattern holds at synthesis grain: across 54 effect sizes, expanding and uniform schedules of retrieval practice did not differ reliably (g = 0.034), with the number of retrieval exposures per item moderating that null ([Spacing out retrieval practice beats massing it](../claims/spaced-retrieval-outperforms-restudy.md) [~M]). An equal schedule may be selected for practical reasons while this exact comparison remains unresolved; do not predict a gain from changing only equal to expanding gaps on this evidence. Calendar timing and the dose needed for a particular learner remain local questions. The gap that helps depends on the delay to the test, and not monotonically: at a 1-day retention interval a 1-day gap beat a 1–15 minute gap, while one study suggested performance drops once the gap grows beyond a day ([ISI effects are nonmonotonic and depend on retention interval](../claims/isi-effect-nonmonotonic-joint-retention-interval.md) [~M]); in the 1,354-person experiment the best gap grew with the test delay but shrank as a proportion of it, from about 20–40% of a one-week delay to about 5–10% of a one-year delay ([Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]). Lower practice fluency can coexist with better delayed performance, but difficulty alone does not identify the cause or predict the outcome.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: **how long until the knowledge is needed** (the test, the exam, the job), **how many sessions the learners will have**, and **whether each return to the material can be a recall attempt rather than a reread**.

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| The goal is retention of facts, terms, vocabulary or procedures over weeks or a course | Split the same total study time into several sessions on different days instead of one block; when comparing schedules, hold total practice constant | [Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]; [Spaced repetition improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+M]; [Spaced sessions improve final-test performance](../claims/spacing-improves-final-test-performance.md) [+M] |
| The learners are preschool children (about age 4) | Rely on repetition itself and do not expect spreading the repetitions within a session to add to it; from about age six (first grade) the spacing benefit appeared under the same procedure. Untested at day-scale gaps for this age in the wiki, so if a programme for young children spaces sessions across days, check recall at a delay before claiming a spacing gain | [Preschoolers showed no spacing effect in free recall](../claims/preschoolers-no-spacing-effect-free-recall.md) [~M]; [First-graders showed the spacing effect](../claims/first-graders-spacing-effect-free-recall.md) [+M] |
| The test or use that matters is weeks or months away | Lengthen the gap between study and review as the delay grows; in one classroom simulation a review eight days after the lecture beat one after a day on a test five weeks later. Do not treat any single gap as universal | [The optimal gap grows with the retention interval](../claims/optimal-isi-increases-with-retention-interval.md) [+M]; [Eight-day review beat one-day review](../claims/eight-day-review-improves-five-week-science-test-versus-one-day-review.md) [+M] |
| The test is the same day or the next morning | Short within-day gaps still beat massing, but expect a smaller gain; add a later check if retention beyond the test matters | [The spacing benefit holds at very short retention intervals](../claims/spacing-benefit-short-retention-no-peterson-paradox.md) [+M] |
| The programme runs a year or more (a language course, a certificate) | Revisit each item on a schedule matched to when it will next be used; do not stretch gaps to many months just because the calendar allows it. Monthly repetition of target vocabulary through short audio lessons is one tested arrangement, in a small quasi-experiment | [One review argues spreading over years can be worse than over months](../claims/distributed-practice-limits-l2.md) [~W]; [Spaced listening outperformed comparison ESL students](../claims/svas-spaced-listening-outperforms-comparison-end-of-experiment.md) [+W] |

A credit workshop followed by spaced quizzes retained more at about five months than massed quizzes or none, and massed quizzes did not differ detectably from none | [Spaced retrieval after a financial workshop improved retention at five months](../claims/spaced-beats-massed-and-control-financial-knowledge.md) [+M]; [Spaced beat massed retrieval and no practice](../claims/spaced-beats-massed-and-control-financial-knowledge.md) [+M] |

In one flashcard study spacing beat massing for 90% of participants, yet 72% judged massing more effective after their first session; in a free-recall experiment learners rated massed items as more memorable while recalling them worse | [Learners misjudge spacing benefits](../claims/learners-misjudge-spacing-benefits.md) [~M]; [Learners judged massed items more memorable though recall was worse](../claims/judgments-overconfidence-massed-items.md) [~M] |

# Spaced Practice

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 9 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 4 causal), `q3`–`q4` · 4 of 9 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
Spaced practice distributes study sessions or practice opportunities for the same material across time, with gaps between encounters, rather than concentrating them in a single massed session. The recommendation is to schedule repeated engagement with content at expanding or fixed intervals — typically separated by days — so that learners must partially reconstruct or retrieve prior learning at each encounter. Spacing is one of the most robust findings in learning science, with benefits documented across ages, materials, and retention intervals.

## Implications

Spaced practice trades short-term performance for long-term retention: massed study feels more effective in the moment but produces substantially worse delayed recall and transfer [Distributed practice yields durable long-term retention advantages over massed practice.](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. A meta-analysis covering hundreds of comparisons found spacing benefits across verbal, skill, and academic tasks, with effects persisting at retention intervals of weeks to months [Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) [+S]. Spacing works because each spaced encounter occurs after partial forgetting, forcing effortful retrieval or reconstruction that strengthens the memory trace — the same mechanism that makes [Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) powerful, and the reason spacing and retrieval combine multiplicatively [Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [+S]. Because learners misjudge their own learning, relying on fluency during massed study, spacing should often be built into course structure rather than left to learner choice [Learners prefer massed study despite its inferior outcomes.](../claims/learners-misjudge-spacing-benefits.md) [~M].
