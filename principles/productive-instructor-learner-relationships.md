# Build productive instructor-learner relationships so adults feel connected and take an active role in learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 18 claims (12 for, 6 mixed) · 24 studies (7 causal, 5 associational, 4 review, 3 qualitative, 3 theoretical, 2 quant-synthesis), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 24 report an effect size · 14 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **working relationship between an instructor and each learner**: whether the learner believes the instructor knows them, takes them seriously, holds them to a clear standard and will not shame them, and so is willing to say what they need, show unfinished work, voice a view that differs from the instructor's or a peer's, and take an active part. For `adult` learners (`postsecondary` or `adult-workplace`), who come with varied ages, histories of schooling and responsibilities and who, the earlier page notes, are slow to reveal anything until they are comfortable with the person and the group, deliberate relationship work early in a course (learning about each learner, greeting and naming each one, stating expectations and how feedback will work, a brief one-to-one contact, low-exposure participation before public participation, and non-punitive responses to conflict) is expected to raise willingness to participate and to raise needs, a sense of community, and openness to opposing arguments (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`, `other`: participation), and through these to support engagement with the course work. Any gain in achievement (`general-achievement`, `skill-performance`) is indirect: it runs through what the learner then does in the course.

The relationship is conditional on the relationship being a *working* one (warmth together with clear expectations, not warmth in place of them), on the instructor's other conduct matching what the relationship work promises, on how much time the instructor actually has with each learner, on the setting giving learners real chances to take part, and on the learners' own reasons to mistrust instructors (earlier schooling, discrimination, care systems), which make the early work slower and more important.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship for adult learners.** Nothing here compares adults taught with and without deliberate relationship work on participation, community, openness or learning. What the wiki holds:

- **Associations, mostly in schools.** Two meta-analyses of correlational and longitudinal studies, preschool to high school, find warm teacher–student relationships go with engagement (medium to large) and, more weakly, with achievement ([Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]). Among university students in service-learning courses, positive professor relationship qualities correlate with civic outcomes and conflict and antagonism correlate negatively ([Professor-student relationship quality correlates with multiple civic engagement outcomes in both directions](../claims/professor-relationship-quality-civic-engagement-bidirectional.md) [+M]). Neither shows direction.
- **Randomised tests of single relationship moves, all with middle-school students.** Greeting each student positively at the door raised engaged time and reduced disruption against an attention control ([Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]); a brief online empathic-mindset exercise for teachers lowered suspensions, halving them in one trial and, in a preregistered replication, narrowing group disparities while the overall effect was small and marginal ([A brief online empathic-discipline exercise for teachers halved suspensions in one randomized field experiment, while its preregistered replication found a small, marginal overall effect but narrowed group disparities](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~S]).
- **Reports about adults on the receiving end of a relationship**: teachers interviewed about their evaluators and coaches, describing trust as what made them open to feedback ([Trusting teacher-evaluator relationships lessened evaluation anxiety and encouraged teachers to welcome and use REACH feedback](../claims/trusting-teacher-evaluator-relationships-reduce-anxiety.md) [+W]; [Four relational components—positive feedback, rapport, mutual respect, and reciprocal trust—form the relational framework of coaching](../claims/four-relational-components-coaching-partnership.md) [+W]). These are qualitative and have no comparison.

And one review states plainly that there is not yet much rigorous evidence to guide instructional and support strategies for adult learners ([There is not yet much rigorous evidence to guide decision making around instructional and support strategies for adult learners](../claims/little-rigorous-evidence-adult-learner-strategies.md) [~M]). So the default design below is a set of labelled proposals, carried from school studies where it cites one, and says so.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (relate to different personalities and backgrounds; use the relationship to convey expectations and feedback and to let adults express needs; give adults an active role so a community forms; expect adults to hold back until comfortable), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. Quantities are for a course meeting weekly for about 2 hours over 8–12 weeks; scale them to the brief.

1. **Learn about each learner before or in the first session, and find what you share.** A short intake (about 10 minutes, on paper or a form): the name they want used and how to say it, why they enrolled and what they want to be able to do, work and caring commitments that affect attendance, and how earlier schooling went for them, with every item optional. Read every answer before the second session and note one thing you have in common with each learner, or one thing you will ask them about. In one school study reported in a policy brief, students and teachers who learned their commonalities reported better relationships, students earned higher grades and gaps for Black and Latinx students narrowed ([Getting-to-know-you surveys that surface student-teacher commonalities improved relationships and grades, and closed achievement gaps for Black and Latinx students by more than 60% in one study](../claims/getting-to-know-you-surveys-commonalities-gap-closure.md) [+W]; secondary students, second-hand, carried to adults). Adult groups vary more in age and experience than children's groups, which is a reason to ask rather than assume ([Adult learner groups are more heterogeneous than child groups, calling for individualized instruction and varied learning strategies](../claims/adult-learner-groups-more-heterogeneous.md) [+W]; an essay, no data).
2. **Greet each learner by name as they arrive, and use names from the second session on.** Two to three minutes at the door or at the start of a live online session, a short individual and positive word (not a correction). Greeting middle-school students individually at the door raised engaged time and cut disruption against an attention control ([Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]; middle school, outcomes behavioural, carried to adults). The recipes are on [Positive Greetings at the Door](../strategies/positive-greetings-at-the-door.md) and [Learning Students' Names](../strategies/learning-students-names.md).
3. **State the working terms in the first session.** In about 10 minutes: what the course expects of learners and of you, how and when feedback will come and what it is for, how to raise a need privately, and that disagreement with you or with peers is welcome when it gives reasons. The earlier page says the relationship exists partly to convey expectations and feedback; the claim pages on relationships say warmth without expectations is not the profile that goes with engagement ([Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M], correlational). Untested as a step.
4. **Hold one short one-to-one contact with every learner in the first three weeks.** Five minutes each, before or after a session, during group work, or by phone: ask what they want from the course and what might get in the way, and agree one thing you will do (a reading in their field, a quieter role at first, a reminder before a deadline). Untested proposal; how to keep the route open afterwards is on [Instructor Accessibility](instructor-accessibility.md).
5. **Ask for low-exposure participation first, public participation later.** Sessions 1–2: written responses, pairs, and choice of whether to share; nobody is called on cold for a personal view. Sessions 3–5: small groups that report back through one spokesperson the group chooses. From about session 5: whole-group discussion, and structured exchanges in which learners must state and answer an opposing argument. Move a group to the next stage when most learners are speaking at the current one (say, at least three in four contributing in a session, by your own tally); move an individual on when they volunteer. The earlier page's constraint that adults hold back until comfortable is the reason for the order; the stages and thresholds are untested proposals.
6. **Respond to conflict and misconduct to keep the relationship, not to punish.** When a learner is late, disruptive or hostile, talk privately, ask what is going on before deciding, and restate the standard without sarcasm. A brief empathic-mindset exercise for middle-school teachers lowered suspensions, and previously suspended students reported more respect from their teachers ([A brief online empathic-discipline exercise for teachers halved suspensions in one randomized field experiment, while its preregistered replication found a small, marginal overall effect but narrowed group disparities](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~S]; three studies, two randomised; the preregistered overall effect was small and marginal, larger for groups at high risk; carried to adults). Conflict and antagonism with professors correlated with weaker social-justice attitudes among university students ([Professor-student relationship quality correlates with multiple civic engagement outcomes in both directions](../claims/professor-relationship-quality-civic-engagement-bidirectional.md) [+M]; correlational).
7. **Hand learners active roles once the group is speaking.** From about the middle of the course, rotate roles: a learner leads part of a discussion, brings a case or problem from their own work or life, or chooses between two topics for the next session. The earlier page says a sense of community forms when adults believe they have an active role. Untested proposal; choice as a motivational condition is on [Autonomy](autonomy.md).
8. **Check the relationship from the learner's side every three to four weeks, and act on it.** One or two anonymous questions at the end of a session ("Could you raise a question or a disagreement here if you wanted to?", "Is there anything you need that you have not asked for?"), answered with one visible change at the next session. Untested; the check-in routine is modelled on [Check-ins](check-ins.md).
9. **Keep the instruction intact.** Relationship time comes out of the arrival window, the first minutes of group work and short exchanges, not out of the teaching. Budget about 15 minutes of each 2-hour session for relational work in the first three sessions (intake, greetings, working terms, one-to-ones), falling to about 5 minutes (greetings, a word with whoever is quiet) from session 4 on; untested quantities. In one tutoring programme, students in larger groups reported weaker tutor relationships yet showed the largest score gains ([Exploratory analyses: students ending the year in larger tutoring groups showed the largest math score increases but reported weaker tutor relationships than peers in smaller groups](../claims/blueprint-larger-groups-scores-up-relationships-weaker.md) [~W]; exploratory, pre/post), so relationship quality and learning can come apart.

**Across sessions**, the source of initiative moves from the instructor to the learners. Early (first three sessions): the instructor makes every first move (intake, greeting, one-to-one, low-exposure tasks). Middle: learners speak in small groups and then the whole group; the instructor names and answers differing views and handles conflict privately. Late (last third): learners hold roles, bring material, and challenge each other's arguments; the instructor's relational work narrows to greetings, the periodic check and whoever has gone quiet. A learner who has stopped attending or speaking goes back to the early stage individually: a private message naming a time to talk.

When a condition is not met: if there is no time for one-to-ones (a single session, a very large group), keep the intake, the greeting by name where possible and the working terms, and offer a time learners can book; if the instructor cannot keep a promise made in the working terms, make a smaller promise, because the relationship rests on what is done, not what is said.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learners bring to the relationship.** Adults who have been failed, judged or mistreated by schools or other institutions, or who belong to groups that report fewer caring adults at school, need slower, more private early steps and more consistency; experienced professionals may want a peer relationship and little personal disclosure. If unstated, ask: how did schooling go for these learners, and what do they expect of an instructor?
- **How much contact time the instructor has with each learner.** A cohort of 15 over a term allows one-to-ones and names by the second week; 200 learners or a single workshop does not. If unstated, ask: how many learners, how many sessions, and how many instructor hours outside sessions?
- **What the relationship is for in this course.** Raising needs and staying enrolled, taking part in discussion, being open to opposing arguments, or accepting critical feedback on work need different first moves. If unstated, ask: what should learners be doing by the middle of the course that they would not do with a stranger?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: who speaks, posts or volunteers in a session and in what format (written, pair, whole group); who raises a need or a disagreement, and through which channel; attendance and lateness; responses to the periodic anonymous check; what a learner says in the one-to-one. "Feels connected", "trusts the instructor" and "is comfortable" are inferred states, and a learner's report that the relationship is good is an observation of that report, not of what they will do.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A learner never speaks in the whole group by mid-course | Not yet comfortable with the group or with you; language or literacy load; a cultural norm against disagreeing with a teacher; disengagement from the content | Compare their contributions in writing and in pairs with their whole-group silence; ask in a one-to-one which format they prefer. |
| Warm in one-to-ones but never raises a need until it is a crisis | Asking feels like an imposition; does not know what counts as a need here; past help came with judgement | Name a specific route and time ("bring the draft on Thursday"), and see whether a specific invitation is used where a general one was not. |
| Agrees with everything you say, and never with a peer who disagrees | Deference to the instructor's role; fear of being marked down; genuinely agrees | Ask for the strongest argument against your view in writing, anonymously, and see whether it appears there. |
| Attendance falls after a critical comment or a disciplinary exchange | The exchange read as rejection; outside pressures coincided; the critique showed a gap the learner cannot see how to close | Ask privately what happened; restate the standard with an assurance they can reach it; see whether they return. |
| The anonymous check says the group feels comfortable, but few speak | Comfort is real but participation is limited by the task, the time, or one dominant voice | Change the participation format for one session (roles, turns) and compare who speaks. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe (a one-to-one, a check) is itself relationship work and changes what it measures, so record each one and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares adult learners taught with and without deliberate relationship work. The claims below test or describe neighbouring parts of the model.

- [Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses, Cornelius-White (2007; 119 studies, 355,325 students, mean r = .31 for learner-centred teacher variables, higher for affective and behavioural outcomes than cognitive) and Roorda et al. (2011; 99 studies, preschool to high school, medium-to-large associations with engagement, small-to-medium with achievement, stronger in higher grades). Both pool correlational or longitudinal studies, and the claim page states neither tests whether improving relationships raises engagement. It supports the expected association; it does not establish direction, and none of its studies are adults outside school or university.
- [Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: a randomised experiment with middle-school teachers assigned to greet or to an attention control (engaged time up, disruption down) and a three-student single-case design (on-task from 45% to 72%). No standardised effect sizes; outcomes are behaviour, not learning; middle school only.
- [A brief online empathic-discipline exercise for teachers halved suspensions in one randomized field experiment, while its preregistered replication found a small, marginal overall effect but narrowed group disparities](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~S]: the claim's table counts 3 distinct studies (2 causal, 1 review). A first randomised field experiment halved suspensions (9.6% to 4.8%) and raised the respect previously suspended students perceived; the preregistered replication (66 randomised teachers, 5,822 students) found a small, marginal overall effect but narrowed the Black/Hispanic disparity from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points. It shows that changing a teacher's stance toward students can change a hard outcome, and limits the expected size: confidence should rest on the replication.
- [Professor-student relationship quality correlates with multiple civic engagement outcomes in both directions](../claims/professor-relationship-quality-civic-engagement-bidirectional.md) [+M]: 125 university students in critical service-learning; guidance, satisfaction, nurturance and reassurance of competence correlated with civic action (r = .28 to .46), while conflict and antagonism correlated negatively with social-justice attitudes (r = -.27, -.42). The closest population to this page's adults, and correlational.
- [Displaced students in receiving schools with high student-teacher trust and teacher personal attention showed larger reading and math gains than those in low-support schools](../claims/teacher-relationships-moderate-displaced-student-gains.md) [+M]: Chicago students moved by school closures did better in reading and maths in receiving schools with high student–teacher trust and personal attention than in low-trust ones (more than three months behind in reading in low-trust schools). School-level survey measures, a moderator analysis; it shows trust mattering most at a disruptive transition, for school students.
- [Getting-to-know-you surveys that surface student-teacher commonalities improved relationships and grades, and closed achievement gaps for Black and Latinx students by more than 60% in one study](../claims/getting-to-know-you-surveys-commonalities-gap-closure.md) [+W]: one study reported in a policy brief, not described in detail; the basis for the intake step, carried to adults.
- **The limiting claims.** [Exploratory analyses: students ending the year in larger tutoring groups showed the largest math score increases but reported weaker tutor relationships than peers in smaller groups](../claims/blueprint-larger-groups-scores-up-relationships-weaker.md) [~W] shows reported relationship quality and score gains moving apart. [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~S] (two randomised trials with college entrants) found a brief belonging message worked only where the institution gave students' groups real opportunities to belong, which is the condition that relational promises must be kept. [There is not yet much rigorous evidence to guide decision making around instructional and support strategies for adult learners](../claims/little-rigorous-evidence-adult-learner-strategies.md) [~M] (a systematic review for adult education) is why every step for adults here is labelled.

Populations, outcomes and designs differ across these claims (preschool to high school, middle-school behaviour, university students' attitudes, Chicago school records), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures adult participation, openness to opposing arguments or sense of community, which are the earlier page's objectives.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective in the earlier page is comfort and willingness to participate, a sense of community, and openness to opposing arguments, usually in service of the course's own learning goals. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the relationship: some adults want to be known and encouraged, some want a competent instructor and to be left alone, some want a credential with as little exposure as possible. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values privacy may read the intake and one-to-ones as intrusion; make them optional and keep the course workable without them.

For example, a working parent in an evening certificate course may want only to pass and get home, while the designer's objective is open discussion of opposing arguments. Relationship work that serves the learner here is a quick greeting, clear expectations and a route to raise a clash with childcare; asking them to share personal stories serves the designer. Record both, so that a quiet learner who passes is not read as a failure of the relationship.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of adult courses with and without early relationship work, with comparable learners, show no difference in participation, persistence or openness to opposing views; if the school findings (greetings, empathic responses) do not carry to adults; or if, as in the tutoring finding, relationship quality and learning move apart so that time spent on relationships costs learning. It should be narrowed if the effect appears only for learners with reason to mistrust instructors, as the belonging and empathic-discipline findings suggest for their groups. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough rapport" after the fact.

A good reported relationship, participation, persistence, openness to opposing arguments and achievement are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose of relationship time, no order of steps and no effect on adult learning.
