# Engagement

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (6 for, 8 mixed, 2 against) · 28 studies (11 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 3 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 28 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

"Engagement" names three different things, and this page keeps them apart because they are observed differently and move independently: **behavioural engagement** (is the learner on task, starting, finishing, coming back), **emotional engagement** (interest, enjoyment, willingness to stay with the work) and **cognitive engagement** (what the learner is thinking with). Only the first can be observed directly. The last is the least well defined of the three and is usually measured by self-report ([Cognitive engagement is the least well-defined engagement perspective and conflates with motivational constructs](../claims/cognitive-engagement-least-well-defined.md) [~M]).

The relationship this page states has two links, and they are tested separately:

1. **Conditions → behavioural and emotional engagement.** For a learner whose observed participation in a task is low (does not start, drifts off task, does not finish, does not come back), changing the conditions around the task is expected to raise on-task time, completion and voluntary persistence (`persistence`, `motivation-affect`, measured as `behavioural` observation or `self-report`) in the near term. The conditions are: a personal, positive contact with the teacher; a reason for the task the learner can see and connect to their own goals; a challenge the learner believes is reachable, with progress they can see; and choices that matter within a stated structure.
2. **Engagement → learning, only through the task.** More time on task, more interest or more participation is expected to raise learning of the target (`immediate-performance`, `conceptual-understanding`, `general-achievement`) **only when the task the learner is engaged in requires thinking with the target content**. Engagement raised by material that is interesting but irrelevant to the target, or by rewards for merely doing the task, can rise while learning stays flat or falls, and tangible rewards can lower later voluntary engagement.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's starting state (why participation is low: no reason, no belief in success, no relationship, an attention difficulty, or a task that is too hard), on which outcome is measured (on-task behaviour, interest, completion and learning are separate claims), and on the horizon (most evidence below is single-session or a few weeks; durable interest is not shown). **No claim in the wiki tests engagement as a whole**: none manipulates "engagement" and measures learning, and none shows that a rise in engagement causes a rise in learning. What the wiki has are tests of single conditions on single outcomes (a greeting routine on engaged time, self-monitoring on on-task behaviour, rewards on free-choice persistence, seductive details on comprehension), associations between relationships and engagement, and reviews.

[Cognitive Activation](cognitive-activation.md) owns the second link above, the cognitive demand of the task, and this page does not repeat it. ## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default and labelled. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name the engagement you want to change as an observation, and pair it with a learning probe.** Choose one behavioural indicator (starts within the first 3 minutes of work time; on-task at 5 spot checks per lesson; tasks completed; returns to an optional task) and one short probe of the target, and record both for 2–3 sessions before changing anything. Untested proposal; the numbers are illustrative. The pairing is the point: a rise in the indicator with no change in the probe is the commonest failure this page warns against.
2. **Open every session with a brief personal, positive contact.** Greet each learner by name as they arrive (1–2 minutes at the door); online, a short named message or a first-minute check-in. Evidence: [Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M] (middle school, observed academic engaged time and disruption, a randomized evaluation against attention control and a three-student single-case study; no effect size, no learning outcome). The broader warmth of the relationship is associated with engagement ([Teacher Student Relationships Improve Engagement And Achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement-and-achievement.md) [+M], correlational).
3. **Make the reason for the task visible, and let learners make the connection themselves.** At the start of a unit, say what the work is for, who it matters to and what success looks like; then ask each learner to write two or three sentences on how today's material connects to something they want (5 minutes). Repeat at each new unit, roughly every two to three weeks (an untested proposal). Evidence: [Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M] (two reviews read without source text; the writing exercise is reported second-hand).
4. **Make the task require thinking with the target, not only activity.** Ask learners to respond, decide, explain, predict or compare, so that being on task means processing the target. Evidence: [Constructive learning beats active and passive learning](../claims/constructive-learning-beats-active-passive.md) [~M] (only the end points, passive lowest and interactive highest, held in a university field study). How to set the demand is in [Cognitive Activation](cognitive-activation.md).
5. **Set a goal that is challenging but reachable, about the process for beginners.** For a learner who is a `novice` on the task, name the steps to carry out ("check each sentence for a verb") rather than a score; move to an outcome goal once the steps are under control. Evidence: [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M] (high-school writing revision and a review); [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~M] (one review; conditional on commitment and ability). What the goal should be is modelled in [Goal Setting and Monitoring](goal-setting-monitoring.md).
6. **Make progress visible, and let learners record it.** A progress chart, a checklist of completed steps, or a three-minute end-of-session record of what was done against the goal. For a learner who drifts off task, a cued self-record (a tone or prompt every few minutes, "was I on task?") during work blocks of 10–20 minutes. Evidence: [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] (a review and a theoretical synthesis); [Self Monitoring Improves On Task Behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M] (single-case studies of students with or at risk for disabilities; on-task behaviour, not achievement). Block lengths are untested proposals.
7. **Keep success reachable.** Plan for most attempts to succeed with the help available; when a learner stops after failing, shorten the step or give a worked example before the next attempt rather than more encouragement. Untested proposal, from the earlier page ("engagement is fragile when success feels unattainable").
8. **Use hooks only if they carry the target.** A surprising fact, image, story or video prompt may open a lesson if it shows the idea to be learned; cut or move to the end anything whose job is only to entertain, especially for learners who are struggling. Evidence: [Interesting but irrelevant details can impair learning, but the recorded effects are small and depend on the material and the learner](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [-M].
9. **Do not pay learners for doing a task they already find interesting.** Prefer informational feedback and specific praise to tangible rewards given for taking part; treat points and badges as a small, uncertain addition, not the engine. Evidence: [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S] (two competing meta-analyses agree on expected tangible rewards for doing a task); [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [~M] (the motivational and behavioural effects were not significant in the more rigorous studies).
10. **Offer choices that matter, inside a structure.** Two or three options for topic, product or order, all aligned to the same goal. Untested here; modelled in [Autonomy](autonomy.md) and [Learner Choice](learner-choice.md).

**Across sessions** (an untested arc; adjust from the indicator and probe in step 1):

- **Weeks 1–2:** the teacher runs the contact routine, states the reason for each task, sets process goals and checks progress during work time (every 10–15 minutes); hooks and short varied tasks are allowed, all tied to the target.
- **Weeks 3–6:** learners write their own process goals and keep their own progress record; teacher checks drop to once per work block; first choices are offered.
- **After that:** learners move to outcome goals, choose more of the task, and the teacher's prompts are withdrawn one at a time. Novelty devices are not relied on to carry the work; the reason for the task and visible progress are.
- **Moving on:** withdraw a support when the indicator has held at the level set in step 1 for three consecutive sessions **and** the probe has not fallen; reinstate it if the indicator drops for two sessions running. Thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no teacher is present (self-paced online), replace the greeting with a named welcome message and a first-minute check-in question, build the progress record into the platform, and probe learning with automatically checked items; if the probe shows no learning while engagement is high, change the task (step 4), not the engagement supports.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Why participation is low, for this learner, on this task.** No visible reason, doubt about success, a weak relationship, an attention difficulty, or a task that is too hard each call for a different move; raising interest does nothing for a learner who believes they cannot succeed. If unstated, ask: what does the learner do when the task starts, and what do they say when asked why they stopped?
- **Which outcome counts.** On-task time, interest, completion, voluntary return and learning of the target are separate outcomes and can move in opposite directions. If unstated, ask: if engagement rose and test scores did not change, would the design have succeeded?
- **Who can see participation, and how often.** A teacher at the door, a teacher with thirty learners, and an unattended platform can each observe different signals. If unstated, ask: during work time, what can anyone see of each learner's activity, and how soon can they respond?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: whether and when a learner starts, what they are doing at a spot check, what they produce, whether they finish, whether they return to an optional task, what they say about the work, and their answer on a learning probe. "Engaged", "motivated", "bored" and "disengaged" are inferences. Busy is not engaged with the target, and quiet is not disengaged.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| On task and enjoying it, but the learning probe does not move | The task can be done without thinking with the target; attention went to an attractive but irrelevant feature; the probe is not aligned to the task | Ask the learner to explain what the task showed; remove the attractive feature for one session and compare the probe; check that the probe asks for what the task practised. |
| Does not start, or stops after the first attempt | Does not believe success is possible; does not see why the task matters; the task is too hard; does not understand the instructions | Give an easier first item and see whether the learner then continues; ask what the task is for; ask the learner to restate the instructions. |
| Participates only when points or prizes are on offer | The reward is the reason for taking part; the task has no other visible purpose; the learner doubts they can succeed without the reward's structure | Remove the reward for one optional task and record voluntary return; ask what the learner would do with the time instead. |
| Engaged in class, never returns to optional or home tasks | Engagement depends on the teacher or peers being present; home time is constrained; the task has no value to the learner outside class | Ask about time and setting outside class; offer the same task in class time and compare; ask what the learner would use the skill for. |
| Reports high interest but is often off task | Interest in the topic, not the task; an attention difficulty; the work blocks are too long | Try shorter blocks with cued self-recording for one week and compare spot checks; ask which part of the topic interests them and set a task on it. |
| High self-reported engagement on a survey, low observed participation | The survey measures something else (attitude toward the subject); social desirability; the learner is engaged in a way the observation misses (thinking before writing) | Compare survey answers with spot checks and with what the learner produces; ask the learner what they were doing at a recorded off-task moment. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking about engagement changes it, so record each probe, and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim tests engagement as a design principle, and none shows that raising engagement raises learning. The claims below test single conditions or single links.

- [Positive Greetings At The Door Improve Engagement](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: middle-school teachers randomly assigned to greet students positively at the door, against an attention control, produced more observed academic engaged time and less disruption; a single-case study of three middle-school students with problem behaviours found on-task behaviour in the first 10 minutes rose from a mean of 45% to 72%. Both read as abstracts, no standardised effect size, no learning outcome. The claim page's Discussion still says the claim "lacks evidence entries", which is stale.
- [Teacher Student Relationships Improve Engagement And Achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement-and-achievement.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of mostly correlational studies. Learner-centred teacher variables correlate with student outcomes at a mean r = .31 (119 studies), higher for affective and behavioural than for cognitive outcomes; in 99 studies from preschool to high school, relationship quality was associated more strongly with engagement (medium to large) than with achievement (small to medium). It shows association, not that improving relationships raises engagement; the title's causal wording is not tested, as the claim page says.
- [Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]: two reviews of expectancy-value research. Expectancy and task value predict academic choices and effort beyond prior performance; brief utility-value writing interventions are reported to raise engagement and grades, especially for students from underrepresented groups. Read without source text; no effect sizes. It supports making the reason for a task visible; the writing-exercise result is second-hand.
- [Self Monitoring Improves On Task Behavior](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of single-case designs with K-12 students with or at risk for disabilities; self-monitoring of on-task behaviour produced consistent, often large increases in on-task behaviour, with or without added reinforcement. The outcome is on-task behaviour, not achievement, and the claim page says so.
- [Constructive learning beats active and passive learning](../claims/constructive-learning-beats-active-passive.md) [~M]: a qualification on the second link. A theoretical review with a lab study reports learning rising from passive to active to constructive to interactive engagement; a field study of 3,820 student assessments in university courses supported only the end points and not the constructive-over-active ordering. Which kind of engagement matters is more settled at the extremes than in the middle.
- [Interesting but irrelevant details can impair learning, but the recorded effects are small and depend on the material and the learner](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [-M]: the limiting claim. A meta-analysis (abstract) concludes that interesting but irrelevant additions can hinder learning, depending on presentation and other features; decorative pictures with 7th and 8th graders neither helped nor harmed overall but weakened the benefit of instructional pictures for low-prior-knowledge learners; in an e-learning course, decorative images lowered comprehension slightly (η² = .04), and in a harder follow-up only for students with low attentional inhibition. Raising interest with material off the target can cost learning.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]: a second limiting claim. One meta-analysis of 128 experiments found tangible rewards contingent on engagement, completion or performance reduced later free-choice engagement (d = −0.28 to −0.40) and found positive verbal feedback raised it; a competing meta-analysis of 96 found no overall undermining, with a negative effect confined to expected tangible rewards for merely doing a task. Both agree on that case.
- [Gamification has small positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes](../claims/gamification-has-small-positive-effects-on-learning-outcomes.md) [~M]: across 40 experiments, game elements in ordinary instruction had small pooled effects on cognitive (g = .49), motivational (g = .36) and behavioural (g = .25) outcomes; in the more rigorous subset only the cognitive effect stayed significant. The motivational case for game elements is the weakest-founded of the three.
- [Cognitive engagement is the least well-defined engagement perspective and conflates with motivational constructs](../claims/cognitive-engagement-least-well-defined.md) [~M]: a limit on measurement. A review in one article concludes cognitive engagement is the least well defined of the three perspectives, is mostly measured by survey and self-report, and is not always linked to achievement. A survey score is a weak stand-in for cognitive engagement.
- The three claims the earlier page cited, on goals and progress, bear on the first link through goal commitment and visible progress; the full model for them is in [Goal Setting and Monitoring](goal-setting-monitoring.md). [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~M] rests on one narrative review read without source text (the earlier page's `[~S]` was above the claim's recorded evidence and is lowered); [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] is a review and a theoretical synthesis; [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M] rests on one experiment with high-school writers and a review chapter. None measures engagement directly.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (middle-school classrooms, students with disabilities, college laboratories, university courses), and most report no standardised effect size, so do not rank them. None measures whether engagement raised by these conditions lasts beyond a few weeks, and none tests the second link directly.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a learning outcome (a capability on a stated measure at a stated time), with engagement as the route to it; sometimes engagement is the objective (continued study, voluntary practice). Say which. Then ask the learner, separately, what they want from the work: to pass, to be left alone, to be seen as capable by peers, to use the skill in their own life. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner whose goal is to avoid being seen failing may disengage from public tasks they would do in private; a learner whose goal is a qualification may engage fully with anything that is assessed and nothing that is not.

For example, a Year 9 student in a science class may value time with friends and avoiding embarrassment, while the teacher's objective is that students can explain why the seasons occur. A lively group game raises participation and serves the student's goal; it serves the teacher's only if winning the game requires explaining the seasons, which an individual written explanation afterwards can check. Record both, so that a lively lesson is not read as the understanding the teacher wanted.

## What would revise this model?

The first link should weaken if controlled comparisons of these conditions (a contact routine, visible reasons, visible progress) show no change in observed on-task time, completion or voluntary return, or a change that disappears within a few weeks. The second link should change if engagement raised by off-target means (decorative hooks, rewards for taking part) turns out to raise learning on aligned measures as much as engagement in a demanding task does; then "engagement through the task" should become "engagement in general". If self-report measures of cognitive engagement come to predict learning as well as observation of what learners produce, the caution about surveys should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not really engaged" after the fact.

On-task behaviour, interest, completion, voluntary persistence and learning of the target are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how much engagement is enough, how long a raised level lasts, or that engagement causes learning.

## Description
Engagement is the principle of designing learning so that learners attend, participate, and sustain effort in cognitively meaningful ways. It matters when attention, persistence, and active involvement affect whether instruction actually results in learning.

## Implications

Engagement matters because even strong content design has little effect if learners do not attend, participate, or persist long enough to benefit from it. The principle is broader than “interest.” It includes behavioral participation, cognitive investment, and emotional willingness to stay with the work. Engagement can be supported through relevance, challenge, autonomy, interaction, and visible progress, and learners are often more likely to persist when goals are challenging but intelligible [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~S], when progress is visible enough to monitor [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M], and when novices can focus on actionable process rather than distant outcomes [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M]. It is still easy to mistake surface activity for meaningful involvement. The core design question is not whether learners are busy; it is whether they are invested in thinking and continuing.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A clear reason for learners to invest attention** — relevance, curiosity, challenge, or social purpose usually needs to be visible
- **Tasks that require participation rather than passive exposure alone** — engagement grows when learners must respond, decide, explain, or create
#### Constraints
- **Surface attention-grabbers do not guarantee deep engagement**
- **Engagement is fragile when success feels unattainable** — learners who see no path to competence often withdraw
- **Novelty fades** — designs that rely only on surprise or entertainment often lose power over time

### Target Learners
- Learners at risk of withdrawal, passivity, or low persistence
- Learners whose effort depends strongly on relevance, autonomy, or visible success
- Learners in discussion, project, or technology-rich environments where participation is a key mediator of learning

### Target Learning Objectives
- Increase participation, persistence, and active involvement in learning
- Improve willingness to attend to difficult tasks and sustain effort
- Strengthen cognitive and emotional investment in meaningful learning work

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Motivation](motivation.md) — engagement is often the visible behavioral and cognitive expression of motivated learning
- [Active Learning](active-learning.md) — participation can increase engagement when the activity requires meaningful thought
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) — autonomy, competence, and relatedness help explain when engagement rises or falls

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — tasks can appear engaging yet still fail if they overload learners or split attention

### Claims
- [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~S] — appropriately challenging goals can increase effort and persistence when learners are committed and supported
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — visible progress can help sustain engagement by showing learners that effort is going somewhere
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M] — engagement may be easier to sustain when novices can focus on actionable process rather than distant outcomes

### Illustrative
