# Signaling Principle

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 37 claims (19 for, 17 mixed, 1 against) · 31 studies (13 causal, 11 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 9 of 31 report an effect size · 33 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one decision inside [Multimedia Learning](multimedia-learning.md): **whether, and how heavily, to cue the essential material and its organisation**. When learners study a lesson whose essential elements and structure they cannot yet pick out for themselves (a `novice` on this material), adding cues that point to those elements and to how they connect is expected to improve learning over the same lesson without the cues. Cues include headings, an overview sentence or outline, highlighting or bold, arrows, colour that links a word to the part of the picture it names, and spoken emphasis. Signals add no new content; they change where attention goes.

The claims in this wiki test the relationship directly, in two meta-analyses, an overview of meta-analyses and a series of experiments. They also report where it bends:

- **The size.** Across 29 experiments (2,726 participants) signaling was associated with better learning, d = 0.38 ([Alpizar et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7)). The largest signaling meta-analysis, as reported in Noetel et al.'s (2022) overview, gives g = 0.43 (k = 209) on learning and g = 0.25 on reduced cognitive load. Mayer's (2008) programme found signaled lessons better on transfer in 6 of 6 experiments, median d = 0.52.
- **Prior knowledge.** In an eighth-grade field study, signals linking text to pictures helped learners with low prior knowledge and harmed those with high prior knowledge, with higher extraneous load ([Richter et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000220)). In an eye-tracking study with middle-school students, revised visuals (labels, colour coding, call-out boxes) reduced processing for low-prior-knowledge students and increased it for middle-prior-knowledge students, with no difference on the post-lesson test ([Clinton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.4018/978-1-5225-1005-5.ch010)). Noetel et al. found no consistent prior-knowledge moderation across multimedia principles as a whole, so the reversal is recorded for signaling in two studies, not as a general rule.
- **Pacing and complexity.** Good multimedia design, signaling among it, mattered more for complex material and for system-paced lessons ([Noetel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)). Alpizar et al. report that pacing and other presentation features moderated the effect.
- **What is cued.** Cues move attention to what is marked, whether or not it matters: underlining low-relevance text raised scores on low-relevance questions ([Nist & Hogrebe 1985](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED265520)), and learners shown a cued object noticed less elsewhere on the screen ([Mutlu-Bayraktar & Bayram 2018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1205386)). A signal helps the outcome it points at.
- **Outcome.** In expository text, organizational signals often changed *what* college readers recalled more than *how much* ([Lorch & Lorch 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.38)). Most of the effects were measured soon after a short lesson.
- **Representation and age.** In a middle-school electricity study, signaling the written text helped with an abstract animation (d = .35) but not significantly with a concrete one (Kutbay & Akpınar 2020).

How this sits beside its neighbours: [Multimedia Learning](multimedia-learning.md) holds the umbrella model (words with relevant pictures, and the principles that qualify it) and its [pattern](../patterns/multimedia-learning.md) the reusable design; this page goes deeper on the cueing decision only and does not repeat that model. [Clear Structure](clear-structure.md) covers making a lesson's or course's organisation explicit more broadly (organizers, graphic organizers, course-level structure); signaling is its cue-level instance within a presentation, and the two share the meta-analysis behind [Clear structure improves learning](../claims/clear-structure-improves-learning.md) [+M]. [Annotating](annotating.md) is about marks the *learner* makes; signals are made by the designer. [Cognitive Load Management](cognitive-load-management.md) gives the shared explanation: a cue saves a novice the search for what matters.

## Default design, while the dose is untested

The claims establish the direction, the size on average and the prior-knowledge boundary. None in this wiki sets a dose (how many cues per screen, which kind of cue, when to fade them). This default is a **labelled proposal**: the kinds of cue and the fade follow the claims named, the numbers are untested choices.

1. **Check the condition.** Is the material complex enough that a newcomer could miss what matters (a busy diagram, an animation, a long expository text, a system-paced video)? Are the learners new to this content? If both are no, signal lightly or not at all ([Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [~M], the Richter et al. reversal).
2. **List the essential elements first**, from the learning goal and the assessment the brief uses: the three to five ideas, parts or steps the test or task will ask about. Cue only these. Cues move attention to whatever is marked ([Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]). Three to five is an untested proposal.
3. **Signal the organisation.** Open with a one- or two-sentence overview of the steps or parts, give each section a heading that names its idea, and close with a summary ([Signaling principle, Mayer 2008](../claims/signaling-principle-mayer-2008.md) [+M]: overview sentences, headings and vocal emphasis).
4. **Signal within the display.** Mark the one element each screen, slide or paragraph is about (bold, highlight, an arrow), and use the same colour for a term and the part of the picture it names. In narration, stress the key words. On a busy screen, pair a highlight with a pointing cue such as an arrow ([The signalled and cued change was most detected](../claims/signalled-and-cued-object-change-most-detected.md) [+W]). One cued element per screen is an untested proposal.
5. **Early sessions (first one or two on a topic):** full signals as above, including the colour or label links between text and picture.
6. **Later sessions on the same topic, once a short probe shows learners can name the parts and steps unaided:** drop the text–picture link cues first, keep headings and the overview. Added link cues harmed learners with high prior knowledge and raised processing for middle prior knowledge ([Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [~M]; [Revised visuals reduce processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle](../claims/revised-visuals-processing-text-prior-knowledge-interaction.md) [~M]). The probe (two or three "name the parts" items) and the order of fading are untested proposals.
7. **Move on** when the learner explains the material without the cues. If performance drops with cues removed, restore them for one more session (untested proposal).
8. **Judge it on the brief's own test**, and check that the test asks about the cued elements; a cue that points at what the test does not ask about cannot show a gain.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: **how much do the learners already know about this material?** (a newcomer or someone who could explain it already); **how complex is the display and who sets the pace?** (a busy system-paced animation, or a short learner-paced text); and **what will the learners be tested or asked to do?** (so the cues point at it). Most of the evidence is from `lab` lessons with `adult` (`postsecondary`) learners on immediate tests; rows below that carry it to children, other media or longer horizons say so.

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are new to the content (`novice`) and the material is a complex multimedia lesson | Use the full default design: overview, headings, one cued element per screen, colour links between words and picture | [Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+S] (d = 0.38 over 29 experiments; g = 0.43, k = 209) |
| Learners already know much of the material (`advanced`) | Drop the text–picture link cues; keep at most headings; let learners turn highlighting off | [Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [~M] (signals harmed high-prior-knowledge eighth graders in one field study) |
| A class with mixed prior knowledge | Give a short prior-knowledge probe and offer the cued version to those who score low; expect no difference on the test for the rest | [Revised visuals reduce processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle](../claims/revised-visuals-processing-text-prior-knowledge-interaction.md) [~M]; [Revisions to visuals had no effect on post-lesson test scores](../claims/revised-visuals-no-learning-effect.md) [~M] |
| Learners are children in middle school learning from animation | Signal the written text where the animation is abstract (symbols, schematic); a concrete, realistic animation may need less | [The signaling effect holds only for abstract animation](../claims/signaling-effect-abstract-only.md) [~M] (one quasi-experiment, d = .35 abstract; not significant concrete) |
| Pre-readers or early readers with a read-aloud e-book | Highlight or track each word as the narration reads it, to bring eyes to the print; expect attention to drift once the animation stops, and do not expect a reliable comprehension gain | [Narration with finger-tracking animation directs preschoolers' attention to print](../claims/enhancing-features-direct-attention-dual-language-e-books.md) [+M]; [Attention drops after the animation ends](../claims/animation-phase-dependent-attention-to-print.md) [~M]; [Comprehension benefit only marginal](../claims/comprehension-benefit-dual-language-only.md) [~M] |
| Adults in a short system-paced video | Signal heavily: spoken overview, on-screen headings for each segment, a pointer or highlight on the part being described | [Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+S] (design mattered more for system-paced lessons) |
| A learner-paced text or print reading | Use headings, a preview and a summary; test what is recalled as well as how much | [Clear structure improves learning](../claims/clear-structure-improves-learning.md) [~S] (signals shifted what readers recalled more than the amount) |
| Slides for a lecture | One heading that states the slide's point, one highlighted element per slide, and an agenda slide at the start | [Signaling principle, Mayer 2008](../claims/signaling-principle-mayer-2008.md) [+M]; the one-per-slide dose is untested |
| The brief chooses between spoken narration and on-screen text for young learners | Signaled written text did as well as spoken text in one middle-school study; if text must stay, signal it | [Spoken text does not differ from signaled written text](../claims/spoken-versus-signaled-written-null.md) [~M] (a null, not a test of equivalence) |
| A VR scene, simulation or busy animated display | Pair each highlight with a pointing cue (arrow), keep few objects on screen, and expect learners to miss changes outside the cued area | [The signalled and cued change was most detected](../claims/signalled-and-cued-object-change-most-detected.md) [+W]; [Signalling focuses attention and reduces change detection elsewhere](../claims/signalling-focuses-attention-reduces-change-detection-elsewhere.md) [~W]; carrying it to VR is untested |
| Phone-only, small screen | Fewer cues fit: keep the heading and one highlighted element per screen, and put the overview first | untested |
| A single session | Use the early-session design only; there is no later session to fade in | untested proposal |
| A fixed short time budget (for example ten minutes a day) | Signals add no content, so keep them; spend the minutes on the cued elements, not on extra decoration | [Signaling (cueing) increases learning and reduces cognitive load](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+M] (reduced load g = 0.25); its time cost is untested |
| One teacher for many learners | Build the cues into the shared materials once (slides, handouts, video) rather than relying on the teacher to point in the moment | untested proposal |
| Low literacy, or learners reading in a second language | Label illustrations with short text and use colour or arrows to link label and part; pair the written key word with narration for young bilingual readers | [Signaling with labeled illustrations is associated with increased learning (review for emergent bilinguals)](../claims/signaling-principle-increased-learning-outcomes.md) [+W]; [Narration with finger-tracking directs attention to the target-language print](../claims/enhancing-features-direct-attention-dual-language-e-books.md) [+M] |
| Learners who are deaf or hard of hearing | Carry every cue visually (captions, on-screen headings, highlight, arrow); do not rely on vocal emphasis | untested |
| Learners who are blind or have low vision | Carry cues in structure a screen reader announces (real headings, a spoken overview, stressed key words); colour alone signals nothing | untested |
| The brief supplies its own materials, routine or rubric | Keep them as given; add cues to them (headings, highlight, an overview) and point the cues at what the rubric scores; run the brief's own assessment, and add no test it did not ask for | [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]; keeping the materials is untested |
| Private or sensitive material | No change: signals need no learner data; a prior-knowledge probe, if used, stays with the teacher | untested |
| The task must be kept whole (a full case, a whole procedure) | Keep it whole and cue its parts and their order inside it, rather than splitting it | untested proposal |
| High-stakes assessment follows | Cue only what the assessment covers; marking the wrong material raises scores on that material instead | [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M] |
| The plan is to have learners highlight their own copies instead | Supply designer cues; learner highlighting alone has low utility, and in one experiment learners' own underlining did no better than supplied underlining | [Highlighting shows low utility](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-M]; [Students' own underlining did not beat experimenter underlining](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M] |
| The test that matters is delayed, or is use on a real task | Keep the cues, but do not count on the immediate-test effects to carry; check at the delay | untested; the recorded effects are mostly immediate |

## Observation, state and explanation

Record what the learner did with the cued material under stated conditions: which cues were present, who set the pace, and what they were asked to do afterwards. Where a learner looked, how long they took and what they rated as effort are observations; an organised representation of the material is an inferred state.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Recalls the cued items but not how they connect | Cues marked elements but not their organisation; the test asked only for the items | Add an overview and connecting cues; ask for an explanation of how the parts act on each other. |
| Learner who knows the topic slows down or does worse with cues | Cues are redundant with what they already know and add material to process | Give the uncued version and compare on the same test; probe prior knowledge first. |
| Scores well on cued content and poorly on the rest | Attention went to the cues, as intended or too much; the cues missed something the test asks | Check the cue list against the assessment; ask about an uncued but essential item. |
| No difference with or without cues | Material simple enough to need none; learners already knew it; cues too many to stand out | Count the cued elements per screen; try a version with one cue per screen; check prior knowledge. |
| Looks at the cued print during narration, then drifts | The cue holds attention only while it moves | Check whether the drift matters for the outcome; repeat the cue at the point that matters. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A single contrast shifts confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Signaling improves learning](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+S]: Noetel et al. (2022), an overview of 29 reviews, ranks signaling among the multimedia principles with the largest benefits and gives g = 0.43 (k = 209) from the largest signaling meta-analysis; Alpizar et al. (2020), a meta-analysis of 29 studies (44 effect sizes, 2,726 participants), d = 0.38, with study, participant, presentation and method features moderating it and clearer benefits in higher-quality studies. The third entry qualifies it: Richter et al. (2018), a quasi-experimental field study with eighth graders, found text–picture integration signals helped low-prior-knowledge learners and harmed high-prior-knowledge ones. All three were read from abstracts or a preprint.
- [Signaling principle: highlighting essential words improves transfer from multimedia lessons](../claims/signaling-principle-mayer-2008.md) [+M]: 6 of 6 experiments (computer lessons on airplanes, paper lessons on lightning and biology), median d = 0.52 on transfer. One research programme, reported in a review; read its median as that programme's result.
- [Signaling (cueing) increases learning and reduces cognitive load in multimedia](../claims/signaling-improves-learning.md) [+M]: the same Noetel et al. overview, adding that signaling reduced cognitive load (g = 0.25, k = 27), increased learning-relevant fixations (g = 0.39, k = 14) and slightly raised motivation (g = 0.13, k = 13).
- [Clear structure improves learning](../claims/clear-structure-improves-learning.md) [~S]: Alpizar et al. again, and Lorch & Lorch (1996), two experiments with 139 college readers in which organizational signals often did not raise the amount recalled but shifted what was recalled.
- [Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]: 67 college freshmen; underlining high- or low-relevance text raised scores on questions about whichever was underlined. One experiment, immediate test.
- [The signaling effect holds only for abstract animation representations](../claims/signaling-effect-abstract-only.md) [~M]: middle-school students learning electricity; signaled written text beat unsignaled written text with abstract animation (d = .35) but not significantly with concrete animation (p = .074). The entry calls the design a quasi-experiment.
- [Revised visuals reduce cognitive processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle prior knowledge students](../claims/revised-visuals-processing-text-prior-knowledge-interaction.md) [~M] and [Revisions to visuals had no effect on post-lesson test scores](../claims/revised-visuals-no-learning-effect.md) [~M]: Clinton et al. (2017), 62 middle-school students reading an algebra lesson with original or revised visuals (labels, colour coding, call-outs, applying signaling, contiguity and coherence together). The eye-tracking measures moved in opposite directions by prior knowledge; the test score did not move. A non-significant difference is not equivalence.
- [The signalled and cued change was the most detected change](../claims/signalled-and-cued-object-change-most-detected.md) [+W] and [Signalling focuses attention and reduces change detection elsewhere](../claims/signalling-focuses-attention-reduces-change-detection-elsewhere.md) [~W]: Mutlu-Bayraktar & Bayram (2018), an eye-tracking case study with a small group of undergraduates (12 in the detection counts); 10 of 12 detected the change on the signalled and cued object. Descriptive counts, no comparison group.
- [Narration with finger-tracking animation directs bilingual preschoolers' attention to the target-language print](../claims/enhancing-features-direct-attention-dual-language-e-books.md) [+M], [Attention drops after the animation ends](../claims/animation-phase-dependent-attention-to-print.md) [~M] and [Comprehension benefit only marginal](../claims/comprehension-benefit-dual-language-only.md) [~M]: Yow & Priyashri (2019), eye-tracking with preschoolers; the cue moved looking to the print while it ran (partial η² = .74), less after, and comprehension rose only marginally (p = .060) in dual-language books and not at all in single-language ones.
- [Spoken text modality does not significantly differ from signaled written text modality](../claims/spoken-versus-signaled-written-null.md) [~M]: Kutbay & Akpınar again; a null result, not a test of equivalence.
- [Signaling in multimedia (labeled text with illustrations) is associated with increased learning outcomes](../claims/signaling-principle-increased-learning-outcomes.md) [+W]: a narrative review for dual-language instruction, attributing the result to three cited studies; no effect sizes.
- [Highlighting shows low utility for improving learning outcomes](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-M]: Dunlosky et al. (2013); learner-made highlighting, a different act from designer signals, gave little benefit over reading.

Taken together: designer-made cues that point at the essential material improve learning from complex multimedia lessons by roughly a third to a half of a standard deviation on average, mostly on immediate tests with university students. Cues help what they point at; for learners who already know the material they can add load, and in two studies they did. No claim in this wiki tests the number of cues, a fading schedule, or delayed outcomes.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is that learners select the essential material and see how it fits together. Ask separately what the learner wants from it: a learner revising for a test values cues that match the test; a learner using the material on a job values cues on what the job needs, which may differ. Cues that serve the designer's list and not the learner's purpose will move attention, and the learner may still miss what they came for. Record both aims and point the cues at the outcome that will be checked.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with comparable novice learners, holding content and time equal, find no learning advantage for cued over uncued material on aligned tests, or if the advantage seen on an immediate test is absent at a delay. It should be narrowed if more studies like Richter et al. and Clinton et al. find cues harming learners with moderate prior knowledge, which would make a prior-knowledge probe a requirement rather than a refinement. If the gains turn out to come from the cues matching the test items rather than from organising the material, the explanation should shift from organisation to attention alone. Do not protect the model by calling every null result a case of too many cues after the fact.
