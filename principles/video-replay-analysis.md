# Video Replay Analysis

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 25 claims (15 for, 10 mixed) · 28 studies (10 qualitative, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 3 causal, 3 theoretical, 2 associational, 2 design), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 28 report an effect size · 21 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **reviewing a recording of a performance to change the next one**: a teacher or teacher candidate watching their own lesson, a trainee clinician reviewing a recorded consultation, a learner replaying their own presentation, interview or physical skill. For a learner who is building a `complex-skill` performed in real time (teaching, consulting, presenting, a motor or technical routine), usually an `adult` in the `postsecondary` or `adult-workplace` band, and whose starting response is that they cannot say accurately what they did at the moments that mattered, reviewing a recording **against one stated focus, with pauses at chosen moments, an explanation of each moment (what happened, why, what else was possible), a debrief in which someone else asks before they tell, and one change carried into a recorded next attempt** is expected to improve what the learner notices and can explain about their own performance (`conceptual-understanding` of its cues and decisions) and the next comparable performance (`skill-performance`, `near-transfer`). Rewatching without a focus or a follow-up attempt is expected to give little: a novice watching a complex scene sees less than an expert does, and a recording on its own changes nothing until something is tried differently.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as stated.** No recorded study compares reviewing one's own recording with an alternative of matched time (a debrief from memory, an observer's written notes, practice without review), or compares focused with unfocused replay. What the wiki holds is: teachers' reports, in one qualitative study of web-mediated coaching, that watching their own lessons was among the most powerful parts of coaching; a review's second-hand report that, in a thirteen-school coaching study in England, positive outcomes were mediated by using video to analyse teacher–student interactions; one design-based study in which preschool teacher candidates' observation assignments scored higher after workshops on authentic classroom video than on a virtual simulation, with analytic gains levelling off after the third workshop; and the general findings on self-explanation prompts and contingent support that this page carries to replay. Two findings limit it: in a meta-analysis of coaching, programmes that supplied a video library were associated with smaller effects on instruction, and novice teachers watching classroom video monitored less and made contradictory observations where experts did not.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this one. [Observation and Shadowing](observationshadowing.md) owns watching **someone else's** practice, live or recorded, as a newcomer; this page uses that page's model for the early sessions in which learners analyse another person's recording before their own, and does not repeat it. [Reflection](reflection.md) owns examining one's own performance against a criterion and committing to a change, with or without a recording; this page adds what the recording changes (exact moments instead of recall, pausing, a second viewing of the next attempt). [Debriefing](debriefing.md) owns the facilitated conversation after a shared experience; the conversation after a replay is one such debrief. [Self-monitoring](self-monitoring.md) owns checks of one's own work during a task; replay is a check made afterwards, on a record. The [Professional Development pattern](../patterns/professional-development.md) builds coached cycles for teachers in which the teacher films a lesson and, in late cycles, chooses the segment; this page is the principle behind that step.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (a clear analytic focus, pause-and-discuss, a feedback or debrief structure, psychological safety, others' video before one's own), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it.

1. **Set one focus before anything is recorded.** Name one to three things to look for, tied to what the learner is trying to improve (wait time after questions, the opening two minutes of a consultation, eye contact during a talk), and how each will show on screen. Basis: learners notice gaps and adjust when they compare performance with a stated criterion, [Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M] (a synthesis and a theoretical account, not a replay study). Where the brief already has criteria, a rubric or an observation tool, the focus comes from it.
2. **Record a short, real performance and cut it to the moments that matter.** Record the whole performance if possible but review two or three clips of 2–5 minutes each, chosen for the focus. Untested proposal; the duration is a proposal. Coded data from a recording can show a pattern the performer did not notice: a first-grade teacher found from a coded lesson video that she had called on fewer than half her students, [EQUIP data reports surfaced participation inequities that teachers were unaware of and motivated instructional change](../claims/equip-data-surfaces-unseen-participation-inequities.md) [+W] (one case report).
3. **Start with someone else's recording.** In the first one or two sessions, learners analyse a peer's or an anonymous practitioner's clip with the same focus, before their own. Untested in the wiki as a sequence; from the earlier page and its source (Nagro 2020, which has no claim page). Analysing authentic classroom video built teacher candidates' analysis and support scores more than a simulation did, in a fixed order, [Authentic video outperformed virtual simulation on the higher-order analysis and support dimensions of observation assignments](../claims/av-advantage-analysis-support-dimensions.md) [+M]; how to run these sessions is modelled in [Observation and Shadowing](observationshadowing.md).
4. **Watch once through, then pause at moments tied to the focus.** On the second viewing the learner stops at each moment, time-stamps it, and writes what happened (what was said or done, what the other people did) apart from what it means. Untested proposal. A novice sees less in a busy scene, so the focus and the time-stamps do the selecting: [Expert teachers interpret complex classroom phenomena more accurately and fluidly than novices and postulants](../claims/expert-teachers-interpret-classroom-phenomena-better.md) [~M] (one video study reported in a review).
5. **Explain each paused moment.** For each moment: what was I trying to do, what happened, why do I think it happened, what else could I have done. Basis: [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S], carried from studying examples and texts to one's own performance, which none of its studies tested.
6. **Debrief with someone who asks before telling.** A coach, mentor or peer opens with questions about the learner's own moments, listens, then adds what the learner did not see and one suggestion. Keep the learner talking for at least half the time (a proposal). Basis: support that responds to the learner's own response beat fixed or no support in one-to-one tutoring, [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] (children, long division; carried to coaching); in one practicum case a mentor's routine of open questions, listening and suggestions supported the mentee's reflection, [A structured feedback-and-reflection cycle (pre-lesson plan review, immediate verbal comments, written feedback, take-up time, and open-ended questioning) supported the mentee's reflective development](../claims/structured-feedback-reflection-cycle-mentoring.md) [+W], while in the same case the mentor out-talked the mentee in every recorded dialogue, [Talk-time analysis showed the mentor spoke more than the mentee in video-recorded dialogues, with classroom management talk ranging from 3% to 39% of total talk time](../claims/mentor-mentee-talk-time-classroom-management-dialogues.md) [~W]. Coaching in which video was used to analyse interactions is reported as the route to positive outcomes, [Video-based analysis of teacher-student interactions mediates positive coaching outcomes, and challenging teachers supports reflection but is uncommon](../claims/video-analysis-mediation-coaching-outcomes.md) [+W] (second-hand, no effect size); the same report says challenging the teacher supported reflection but was uncommon, so the debrief should name a gap, not only praise.
7. **End with one change and record the next attempt.** The learner names one change to try, tries it in the next comparable performance, records it, and compares the two clips at the same moments. Untested proposal. The account offered for why self-review motivates change is the gap a person sees between their aims and what the tape shows, [Perceived discrepancy between actual teaching performance and goals motivates teachers to change their teaching](../claims/performance-goal-discrepancy-motivates-teacher-change.md) [+W] (a 1970 programme's theoretical argument, untested); a recording without a next attempt leaves the gap unused.
8. **Keep it safe to be seen.** The learner owns their recording: they decide who watches it, it is deleted when the cycle ends unless they choose to keep it, and everyone else in frame (pupils, patients, clients) has consented or is cropped out. Critique is about moments on screen, not the person. Untested; from the earlier page. Teachers in one coaching study described moving past initial self-consciousness to find watching their own lessons among the most useful parts of coaching, [Video-based self-observation of teaching was among the most powerful parts of the coaching process for teachers](../claims/video-self-observation-powerful-coaching-component.md) [+W] (self-report).
9. **Plan the arc across sessions and hand over the choices.** A default for a placement, term or coaching programme of five to eight cycles, one every one to two weeks (proposals): **sessions 1–2**, another person's clips, focus set by the coach, group or pair analysis, 30–45 minutes; **sessions 3–4**, the learner's own clips, focus agreed with the coach, coach chooses the moments; **sessions 5 onward**, the learner chooses the focus and the clip and leads the analysis, and the coach only asks and adds. Within a 40-minute session: 5 minutes to restate the focus, 10 minutes of viewing, 15 minutes of paused analysis, 10 minutes of debrief and one change. Move the learner on to choosing their own clip once, in two consecutive cycles, the moments they pick and explain match those a coach would pick (a proposed criterion). In the one sequential study, teacher candidates' analysis scores rose between the second and third authentic-video workshops and then levelled off, [Analytical competence showed a leap-plateau trajectory across sequential authentic-video workshops, improving mainly between Workshops 2 and 3](../claims/analysis-leap-plateau-trajectory.md) [+M], while their suggestions for supporting children kept improving to the fourth, [Supportive competence grew continuously through Workshop 4 while basic observation ability remained stable across sequential AV workshops](../claims/support-growth-observation-stable-longitudinal.md) [+M]: change the focus or the task after three or four sessions on the same one, rather than adding more of the same.

When a condition is not met: if no one can debrief, give the learner a written prompt card for steps 4, 5 and 7 and a worked example of a completed analysis; if recording is not allowed, use a short audio recording or an observer's time-stamped notes and say that the evidence for video does not transfer to them; if the learner already notices and explains their moments accurately, skip the early sessions on others' clips.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **How accurately the learner already sees their own performance.** A novice needs a narrow focus, others' clips first and a coach choosing the moments; a practitioner who already names the right moments needs only the recording, their own focus and a check. If unstated, ask: after one viewing of a two-minute clip, which moments does the learner pick out, and would an experienced colleague pick the same ones?
- **Who will see the recording, and who is in it.** Self-review, review with one trusted coach, and review in a group are different levels of exposure, and other people on screen need consent. If unstated, ask: who may watch this clip, where is it kept, and has everyone in it agreed?
- **What the next performance is, and when.** Replay changes performance only through the next attempt. If unstated, ask: when does the learner next do this task, and can that attempt be recorded too?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what the learner does with a recording under stated conditions: which moments they stop at, what they write at each (description, interpretation, alternative), how long they talk in the debrief, the one change they name, and what they do in the next recorded attempt at the same moment. "Sees their own practice accurately", "is defensive" and "has changed their practice" are inferred states. A fluent analysis of a clip shows what the learner can say about a recording; it does not show what they will do in the next performance.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Comments only on appearance, voice or mannerisms | Self-consciousness on first viewing; no focus given; the learner cannot yet see the decisions in the scene | Give a clip of someone else with the same focus; if the comments move to decisions there, the issue is exposure, not seeing. |
| Picks moments a coach would not pick | A different, legitimate aim; the focus was unclear; a novice's narrower view of the scene | Ask what they were trying to do at their moment; show a coach's moment and ask what they see in it. |
| Names a change but does the same thing in the next recording | The change was not practicable in the moment; the cue that should trigger it was not recognised; the change was not the learner's own | Ask what happened just before the moment in the new clip; rehearse the change once on its cue before the next attempt. |
| Describes the clip accurately but cannot explain why things happened | Description and interpretation are separate skills; little knowledge of the domain to explain with | Prompt with two possible explanations and ask which the clip supports, and what would distinguish them. |
| Declines to record or to show a clip | Fear of judgement; a real privacy or consent problem; no value seen in it | Ask which of these it is; offer private self-review, audio only, or others' clips first. |
| Analysis improves over sessions, the performance does not | Clip analysis is being practised, not the performance; too few attempts between reviews | Count the attempts between reviews; check whether the next performance is recorded at the same moment. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A review is itself an intervention and changes what it measures, so record each one, and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares reviewing one's own recording with another way of reflecting on the same performance, or varies the focus, the number of reviews or who leads the debrief. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

- [Video-based self-observation of teaching was among the most powerful parts of the coaching process for teachers](../claims/video-self-observation-powerful-coaching-component.md) [+W]: in one qualitative study of web-mediated coaching, teachers recorded their lessons and watched them before coaching meetings, reported noticing student reactions they had missed, and called self-observation among the most powerful parts of coaching. Teachers' reports; no comparison and no measured outcome.
- [Video-based analysis of teacher-student interactions mediates positive coaching outcomes, and challenging teachers supports reflection but is uncommon](../claims/video-analysis-mediation-coaching-outcomes.md) [+W]: a review reports, second-hand, that in a coaching study in thirteen schools in four regions of England positive outcomes were mediated by video analysis of teacher–student interactions, and that challenging teachers supported dissonance between planning and enacting but was uncommon. No effect sizes; the original study is not recorded in the wiki.
- [Authentic video outperformed virtual simulation on the higher-order analysis and support dimensions of observation assignments](../claims/av-advantage-analysis-support-dimensions.md) [+M], with [Analytical competence showed a leap-plateau trajectory across sequential authentic-video workshops, improving mainly between Workshops 2 and 3](../claims/analysis-leap-plateau-trajectory.md) [+M] and [Supportive competence grew continuously through Workshop 4 while basic observation ability remained stable across sequential AV workshops](../claims/support-growth-observation-stable-longitudinal.md) [+M]: one design-based study (Zhang et al. 2026) with preschool teacher candidates in 8 groups. Group observation assignments scored higher on analysis and on support after an authentic-video workshop than after a virtual-simulation workshop (Cohen's d on the claim pages about 1.2 and 0.9), analysis rose from the second to the third workshop and then levelled, and support kept rising to the fourth. The learners analysed **others'** recorded practice, the video workshop always followed the simulation, and outcomes were researcher-scored group assignments, so these bear on the early sessions and the dose, not on self-review. The same study found students reported more purposeful reflection after the simulation, [Virtual simulation stimulated higher self-reported purposefulness (active reflection) than authentic video](../claims/vs-higher-purposefulness-reflection.md) [~M], which the authors attribute to its simpler scene: a reason to keep early clips short and the focus narrow.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S]: four studies including two meta-analyses (one reports g = .55 across 69 effect sizes); prompted explanation of worked examples and texts helps on immediate tests, with little evidence on delayed or classroom gains. Carried here to explaining moments of one's own recorded performance, which none of its studies tested.
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]: fully contingent one-to-one tutoring of fourth and fifth graders on long division beat fixed or no support at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition); its `## Studies bearing on this claim` table gathers 20 distinct studies across it and 17 narrower claims, most from tutoring, AI tutors and feedback. Carried to the debrief after a replay; the claim notes most evidence is one-to-one.
- [Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]: a synthesis and a theoretical account (two sources, no effect size) that comparing current performance with a goal or criterion is what lets learners detect a gap and change strategy. It supports setting a focus before review; it does not test replay.
- **Limiting:** [Pairing coaching with group trainings is associated with larger effects on instruction and achievement; instructional materials with larger, and video libraries with smaller, effects on instruction](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [~M]: in a meta-analysis of 60 causal studies of teacher coaching, programmes that provided a video library were associated with effects on instruction 0.27 SD smaller; the authors stress these regressions are descriptive, not causal. It cautions against treating access to video as the active ingredient. The pooled effect of coaching on instruction in the same meta-analysis, [Teacher coaching programs improve teachers' instructional practice by a pooled 0.49 SD across 43 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-instruction-effect-0-49-sd.md) [~M], is for coaching as a whole, with wide variation between programmes, and does not isolate video review.
- **Limiting:** [Expert teachers interpret complex classroom phenomena more accurately and fluidly than novices and postulants](../claims/expert-teachers-interpret-classroom-phenomena-better.md) [~M]: in a three-screen video study reported by Berliner (1988), experts monitored all screens and commented more, and in more detail, while novices made contradictory statements. A novice reviewing a recording without a focus is likely to see less than the recording shows.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (in-service teachers' reports, teacher candidates' group assignments, children tutored in arithmetic, learners studying texts), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a change in the learner's own later performance after reviewing their own recording.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a change in a specific part of the next comparable performance (`skill-performance`), with the learner able to say why. Elicit separately what the learner wants from watching themselves: to stop a habit they have been told about, to see whether a new technique works, to prepare for an assessed observation, or to get through the requirement with as little exposure as possible. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A teacher candidate who values a good placement grade may pick flattering clips; a practitioner who values a particular class going better may choose a focus the designer did not plan, and that focus is usually the one that will be acted on.

For example, a new nurse's programme may set "closed-loop communication during handover" as the focus, while the nurse wants to stop being interrupted by a senior colleague. Review one handover clip for each: the designer's focus tests the programme's objective, the nurse's tests what they came for, and both are recorded so that one is not reported as the other.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing review of one's own recording with a matched-time debrief from memory or an observer's notes, with comparable learners and an outcome measured on the next real performance, show no advantage; if focused replay does no better than unfocused rewatching; or if gains in clip analysis do not appear in later performance. If others' clips alone produce the same change as one's own, the self-review steps are a preference, not a requirement. If a debrief led by the learner does no better than one led by the coach, drop the "ask before telling" step. If the analytic plateau after three or four sessions does not recur in other populations, drop the proposal to change the focus at that point. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong focus" or "not enough psychological safety" after the fact.

Noticing in a recording, explaining it, the next performance and lasting change in practice are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of review cycles, no clip length and no criterion for handing the choice of clip to the learner.

## Description
Video replay analysis uses recorded performance, instruction, or interaction as an object for review. Learners or instructors pause, revisit, and analyze moments that would otherwise pass too quickly to examine. This makes it possible to notice decisions, patterns, cues, and missed opportunities with much more precision than relying on memory alone.

The principle is especially valuable for performance domains where timing, communication, physical execution, or situational judgment matter. Replay creates distance from the original event, which can support more accurate reflection and discussion. Its usefulness depends on focus: simply watching video again is less powerful than analyzing it against a question, standard, or goal.

## Implications
Video replay analysis is powerful because it gives learners a stable record of performance instead of relying on memory alone. Replay can strengthen self-monitoring by making discrepancies between intention and action more visible [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M], and it becomes especially useful when learners have to explain what happened and why [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~S]. Replay rarely teaches on its own, though; insight depends on analytic focus, pause-and-discuss structure, and responsive coaching that helps learners interpret what they are seeing [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A clear analytic focus**: Learners need to know what they are looking for in the replay.
- **Pause-and-discuss opportunities**: Video is most useful when learners can stop, annotate, compare, or explain key moments.
- **Feedback or debrief structure**: Replay analysis works best when paired with coaching, self-explanation, or guided reflection.
- **Psychological safety**: Reviewing one's own performance requires norms that keep critique specific and constructive.
#### Constraints
- **Replay without criteria**: Watching video repeatedly without a lens often produces shallow commentary.
- **Overemphasis on surface features**: Learners may focus on visible details and miss underlying reasoning.
- **Performance anxiety**: Self-video can feel exposing or threatening without careful framing.
- **Technical and privacy barriers**: Recording, storage, and access need to be handled responsibly.

### Target Learners
- **Learners in performance-heavy domains**: Strong fit for teaching, speaking, clinical, technical, athletic, and workplace practice.
- **Learners developing metacognitive awareness**: Replay can help learners notice habits they miss in the moment.
- **Learners benefiting from concrete evidence**: Video gives a shared object for discussion instead of relying on vague recall.
- **Adult and professional learners**: Useful when improvement depends on analyzing authentic practice.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Self-assessment and reflection**: Noticing strengths, errors, and alternatives in one's own performance.
- **Performance improvement**: Identifying specific moments to revise in later attempts.
- **Reasoning about practice**: Connecting visible action to intention, decision making, and outcomes.
- **Feedback literacy**: Learning to interpret evidence and use critique productively.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Metacognitive and self-regulated learning perspectives — replay supports monitoring, evaluation, and revision.
- Multimedia learning perspectives — video can preserve multiple cues that learners can revisit and integrate.
- Reflective practice traditions — practitioners improve when they examine concrete records of action.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Replay analysis needs focus and facilitation; video by itself does not guarantee insight.
- Learners may need to analyze others before analyzing themselves if self-review is too cognitively or emotionally demanding at first.
