# Modeling

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 21 claims (15 for, 6 mixed) · 29 studies (9 causal, 7 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 design, 3 theoretical, 2 associational, 2 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 29 report an effect size · 13 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **making the process of a performance visible before and alongside the learner's own attempts**: showing the decisions behind a piece of work (what the performer notices, chooses, checks and corrects, and why) rather than only the finished product. For a learner whose first response on a task shows they cannot yet produce the performance, or can produce a surface imitation of the product without its decisions (a `novice` for this task, on a `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill` or `metacognitive-strategy` whose key moves are not visible in the product), a model that externalises those decisions, studied actively and followed at once by the learner's own supported attempt, is expected to produce better unaided performance on comparable tasks (`immediate-performance`, `near-transfer`) than either the finished product alone, a silent demonstration, or being left to work out the process unaided. Watching alone is not the expected active ingredient: the expectation is stronger when the learner has to explain or predict the modelled decisions, and it weakens as the learner's task-specific expertise grows. Whether the gain holds at `delayed-retention` or `far-transfer` is not settled here.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as stated.** No recorded study compares a narrated (think-aloud) model with a silent demonstration or with the product alone, or varies the kind of model (expert, peer, a model who makes and repairs mistakes). The nearest tested evidence is about worked examples, a written form of modelling: studying solved examples before solving beats solving alone for novices, prompting learners to explain what they study helps on immediate tests, and the advantage of full examples shrinks with expertise. Claims about live modelling itself are single studies, mostly qualitative or design reports. The model below carries the worked-example findings to live, spoken and video modelling, and says so wherever it does.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this one: [Worked Examples](worked-examples.md) owns studying a written solution before a comparable problem, and is the best-evidenced special case of modelling. [Direct Instruction](direct-instruction.md) owns the whole explain–model–guide–hand-over sequence and its comparison with discovery; modelling is one of its moves, treated here on its own and for tasks (writing, discussion, professional conduct, play, motor and social skills) that have no written solution. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) owns the attempts that follow the model, and [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) owns how support is withdrawn; this page hands over to both and does not repeat their models. The [Cognitive Apprenticeship pattern](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) is a reusable policy that opens with modelling; its branches are untested proposals.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (a visible process, narrated reasoning, an immediate link to practice; slow down, segment and annotate for novices; reduce explicit modelling as expertise grows), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step says what evidence it rests on.

1. **Find the decisions the product hides.** Before modelling, list the three to six decision points where a novice's attempt goes wrong or stalls (what to notice first, which option to choose, what to check, when to stop and revise). Model those, not every keystroke. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("what to notice, what to prioritize, why a step happens now instead of later, and how errors are handled").
2. **For a learner new to the task, model before the first attempt.** Show the model first, then the attempt; do not open with an unaided attempt and use the model as feedback. Evidence carried from written worked examples: example-first sequences beat problem-first ones and problems alone for novices on an immediate test ([Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+M]; [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]). Learners with some experience are different (step 10 and the situation table).
3. **Narrate the reasoning, not only the action.** Think aloud at each decision point: "I'm checking X first because…", "I could do A or B; I choose B because…". Keep each narrated segment short: about 3–5 minutes, covering one to three decision points, then pause. The narration and the segment length are untested proposals; from the earlier page's warning that silent demonstration hides the cognition and expert performance moves too fast for novices.
4. **Make the learner do something with each segment.** At each pause, ask learners to predict the next move, or to say (or write in a line) why the move just made was made. Evidence: prompting learners to explain what they study helps on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, across task types ([Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]); constructive activity beating merely watching is supported in the lab but was not confirmed in one large university field study ([Constructive learning beats active and passive learning](../claims/constructive-learning-beats-active-passive.md) [~M]).
5. **Include a mistake and its repair.** In at least one segment, make (or show) a typical error, notice it aloud, and fix it, then ask learners what gave it away. Evidence carried from tutoring with fourth and fifth graders on decimals: comparing correct with incorrect examples beat comparing correct examples only ([Erroneous examples improve conceptual understanding by forcing comparison with correct models.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]); untested for live modelling.
6. **Show a second, different instance.** Model the same decisions on a second task whose surface differs, so learners can see what stays the same; for open tasks (writing, design), show two acceptable products and name what they share. Untested proposal; a single learner comparing his writing to one model paragraph noticed only vocabulary ([Comparing original texts to model paragraphs prompted noticing that was again focused on lexical features](../claims/model-comparison-noticing-lexical.md) [~W]).
7. **Hand over at once.** Follow each model within the same session with the learner's own attempt on a comparable item, with help still available, then an unaided item. Evidence: example-first sequences that included problems did as well as examples only and better than problems only on an immediate test ([Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+M]); how to run the attempts is in [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md).
8. **Re-model at the decision the errors show.** When several learners make the same error, model again only that decision, narrating what you check; do not repeat the whole demonstration. Untested proposal.
9. **Plan the withdrawal of the model across sessions.** A default arc for a unit of four to six sessions: sessions 1–2, a full narrated model of a whole task before practice; sessions 3–4, a partial model (the teacher starts, learners finish and narrate the rest), with the full model kept available for reference; sessions 5–6, learners model for each other (one narrates while a partner checks against a criterion list), and the teacher models only at decision points the errors show. Move a learner to the next stage once they can do the modelled decisions unaided, and say why, on two consecutive items. Evidence for fading from complete to incomplete examples, on near transfer ([Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]); the arc, the session counts and the threshold are untested proposals. How to fade is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).
10. **Keep full models for those who need them; shorten them for those who do not.** Learners with low prior knowledge on this task keep complete models longer; learners who succeed on the first unaided item get a brief model of only the new decision, or attempt first and then compare with the model. Evidence: guidance that helps novices can become redundant as expertise grows ([Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]; [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]); in one algebra experiment with college students, lower-prior-knowledge learners did better after full than completion examples ([Lower-prior-knowledge learners scored higher on an algebra posttest after full-worked than completion-worked examples, while higher-prior-knowledge learners' non-significant advantage ran the other way](../claims/lower-prior-knowledge-learners-score-higher-with-full-than-completion-worked-examples.md) [~M]). Neither gives a crossover point.

When a condition is not met: if no one can watch learners' attempts after the model (self-paced video), embed the predict-or-explain pauses in the recording and follow it with automatically checked items; if the task has no hidden decisions (the product shows them all), a worked product with annotations may be enough and live modelling adds time without a recorded gain.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner can already do on this task.** A learner who cannot begin needs a full, slowed, narrated model first; one who succeeds unaided needs at most a brief model of the new decision, and a full model may be redundant. If unstated, ask: can they produce one example of this performance now, unaided, and say why they made its main choices?
- **Which decisions the finished product hides.** If the product shows every step (a solved equation), an annotated product may do; if the decisions are invisible (why a writer cut a paragraph, why a nurse checked a line first, how a mentor defused a classroom incident), they have to be narrated or they are not modelled. If unstated, ask: where do novices' attempts go wrong, and can that choice be seen in the finished work?
- **What the model is for: the performance, or the learner's belief they can do it.** Accurate performance calls for an expert's clear, narrated model; confidence and persistence may call for a model the learner sees as similar to themselves, who struggles and recovers. If unstated, ask: is the problem that learners cannot do it, or that they do not try?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what the learner does and says after a model under recorded conditions: which model they saw (live or recorded, narrated or silent, expert or peer, with or without an error), what they predicted or explained at each pause, their next attempt, and whether that attempt reproduces the model's decisions or only its surface. "Has understood the process" and "is copying" are inferred states. A correct imitation right after the model shows what the learner can do with the model fresh in mind; it does not show what they can do on a different task or a week later.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| The learner's product closely copies the model's wording or layout, but the decisions are wrong on a new task | Copying the surface; only one model seen, so its features and its decisions cannot be told apart; the decisions were not narrated, or not attended to | Give a task where the model's surface would be wrong, and ask the learner to say which of the model's choices they kept and why. |
| Watched attentively, predicted well at the pauses, then cannot start alone | The prediction relied on the model being in view; the first decision was never modelled (the model began after it); low confidence rather than missing skill | Ask for the first move only, with the model hidden, then with only the decision list; ask how sure they are before they start. |
| Can perform the steps but cannot say why | A procedure learned without its reasons; a language barrier to explaining; the narration gave actions, not reasons | Pose an item where the usual step is wrong, and allow a non-verbal explanation (point, draw, act). |
| Finds the model too slow, or tunes out | The learner already has the decisions (redundancy); the segment is too long; the task or model is not one they value | Give one unaided item at once: if it is right, shorten or skip the model; if wrong, keep the model and shorten its segments. |
| Avoids trying after an expert model, though the attempts show partial skill | The expert's ease made the task look out of reach; fear of being seen to fail; the learner does not see themselves in the model | Show a peer or coping model of the same task, or the teacher's own early attempt, and compare willingness and first-attempt quality. |
| Performs well right after the model, much worse a week later | Little consolidation; the attempts were too close to the model; no practice between | Re-test with a new item at the delay, and record what practice and exposure happened in between. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself practice and changes what it measures, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares narrated with silent modelling, process with product, or one kind of model with another. The claims below test neighbouring relationships, mostly through written worked examples.

- [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]: Sweller & Cooper (1985), algebra learners who studied worked examples performed better than those who spent the same time solving conventional problems (a peer-reviewed experiment, `q3`, no effect size in the abstract); the authors' explanation is that examples cut means–ends search. The claim's own Discussion limits it to novices. It supports showing a solved model before a novice's first attempt; it does not test live or narrated modelling. The claim page's "Primary evidence link" (`10.1037/0022-0663.77.1.59`) differs from the DOI in its citation (`10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3`).
- [Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+M]: van Gog et al. (2011), novices in electrical-circuit troubleshooting (103 randomised, 96 analysed): examples only and example–problem pairs beat problem–example pairs and problems only on an immediate test, and did not differ from each other; Renkl & Atkinson (2003) argue for fading. It supports model-first for novices in a short task; it does not show that following a model with practice beats the model alone, nor delayed transfer.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports (g = .55) across task types, subjects and levels; a mathematics meta-analysis finding small-to-moderate immediate gains, with much less evidence after a delay or in classrooms; and small studies with eighth graders and physics students. It supports asking learners to explain the modelled decisions; the evidence is strongest for immediate outcomes, and the claim's Discussion warns that learners without the background may explain wrongly.
- [Erroneous examples improve conceptual understanding by forcing comparison with correct models.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]: in a brief tutoring session, 74 fourth and fifth graders learning decimal magnitude who compared correct with incorrect examples gained more in correct procedures and key concepts than those comparing correct examples only; a mathematics meta-analysis (abstract not available to the wiki) highlights erroneous examples. It supports including an error and its repair; it was not tested with live modelling, and the claim page's own correction says the study did not measure transfer.
- [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 580 comparisons favours explicit instruction over unassisted discovery (d = 0.38), while discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations did better than other instruction; an experiment with 112 third and fourth graders found many more learned experimental design by direct instruction. The claim names modelling among the explicit supports but does not isolate it. It is evidence for showing novices how, not for one form of showing.
- [Constructive learning beats active and passive learning](../claims/constructive-learning-beats-active-passive.md) [~M]: a lab study in the ICAP review found learning rising from reading only, to highlighting, to interpreting a graph, to interpreting it jointly; a field study of 3,820 student assessments in 170 university sessions supported only the ends (passive lowest, interactive highest) and not constructive over active. **It limits this page**: watching a model is a passive mode, and the hoped-for gain from making learners explain or predict is confirmed in the lab but not, in one large study, in real classes.
- [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M] and [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: reviews (Kalyuga et al. 2003; Renkl & Atkinson 2003) that guidance useful for novices can become redundant for learners who already hold the schema, and can depress their performance. **They limit this page**: the full narrated model is for novices on the task; neither gives a point at which to stop.
- [Lower-prior-knowledge learners scored higher on an algebra posttest after full-worked than completion-worked examples, while higher-prior-knowledge learners' non-significant advantage ran the other way](../claims/lower-prior-knowledge-learners-score-higher-with-full-than-completion-worked-examples.md) [~M]: one factorial experiment with college students solving simultaneous equations (t(1,26) = 1.98, p = .05 for lower-prior-knowledge learners; the higher-prior-knowledge difference was not significant and equivalence was not tested). It supports keeping complete models longer for those who know least.
- [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]: three experiments (20 ninth graders, then 54 and 45 college students) in which fading from complete to increasingly incomplete examples favoured near transfer over static example–problem arrangements, with removing the last steps first more favourable; and a review naming fading as essential to scaffolding. It supports withdrawing the model across sessions; it sets no fading rate.
- [Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]: two narrative reviews (Bandura 1997; Pajares 1996) that self-efficacy predicts choice, effort and persistence, and that watching similar others succeed is one of its four sources. It supports choosing a model learners identify with when confidence is the problem; it compares no model types and prints no effect size.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (algebra novices, circuit troubleshooters, children with a tutor, university classes), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a modelled skill at `delayed-retention` against a condition without the model.

**Single studies of modelling itself**, each weak and cited in the situation table only for the setting it observed:

- [Teacher modeling with worked examples helped EFL students internalize reading comprehension strategies](../claims/teacher-modeling-internalizes-reading-strategies.md) [+W]: the experimental group's questionnaire comments in one EFL study; the claim rests on students' reflections, not on a comparison of modelling with no modelling.
- [Mentor modelling and a school-wide behaviour program's strategies transferred to the mentee's classroom management practices](../claims/mentor-modelling-strategies-transfer-mentee-behaviour-management.md) [+W]: one preservice teacher's mentoring, a qualitative case (`r3`); modelled strategies appeared in the mentee's teaching, alongside a school-wide programme, so modelling's own share is not separable.
- [Modeling training improved imaginative play in disadvantaged kindergartners, with gains in imaginativeness, verbal communication, spontaneity, and attention span persisting two months](../claims/modeling-training-improves-imaginative-play.md) [+W]: 80 kindergartners, eight small-group sessions of modelling and direct teaching against a puzzle-play control that showed no change; gains held at two months. Reported second-hand in a 1973 seminar report; modelling and direct teaching are not separated.
- [Students are more attentive to the teacher's model track when they hear their own recording first](../claims/student-recording-first-increases-attention-to-model.md) [+W]: classroom tests of a language-lab video sequencer; attention was reported, learning was not measured, and no effect size is printed.
- [Comparing original texts to model paragraphs prompted noticing that was again focused on lexical features](../claims/model-comparison-noticing-lexical.md) [~W]: one EFL learner comparing his writing to a model paragraph noticed only vocabulary. It qualifies the assumption that seeing a model shows learners its structure.
- [Vicarious experiences are positively related to agriculture teachers' general teaching efficacy, with observing a first year teacher explaining 11% of variance](../claims/vicarious-experiences-positive-teaching-efficacy.md) [+W]: correlations, reported in a review, between student teachers' observing other teachers and their teaching efficacy; associational, so it does not show that observing caused the efficacy.
- [Proximal goals, ability attributional feedback, and peer models raise self-efficacy for learning more than distant goals, effort feedback, and teacher models](../claims/proximal-goals-feedback-peer-models-build-learning-efficacy.md) [~W]: a review chapter whose two entries report proximal goals and ability feedback; **neither entry reports the peer-versus-teacher model finding the title states**, so it gives no recorded support for choosing peer models.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually unaided performance on a representative task, with stated aids, at a stated horizon, and a model is a means to it. Elicit separately what the learner wants from watching: to see that the task is possible for someone like them, to get a template they can copy for tomorrow's assignment, to understand how an expert thinks, or simply to get the session over. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants a template will attend to the model's surface and copy it, which serves a deadline and not the objective; one who doubts they can do the task may need a model they identify with before a clearer expert model helps.

For example, a new nurse may want above all not to look incompetent in front of a patient, while the designer's objective is a correct sequence of checks before giving a medication. A model at full speed by a senior nurse shows what competence looks like to the patient; a slowed, narrated rehearsal of the checks, away from the patient, serves the objective. Record both, so that a calm performance on the ward is not read as the checks having been learned, or the reverse.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of narrated with silent modelling, or of a process model with an annotated product, with comparable novices and aligned unaided outcomes, show no advantage, or an advantage only at the end of the session. If asking learners to predict or explain during a model works no better in real classes than watching, as one large field study of activity modes suggests for constructive activity in general, the pause prompts are a lab finding only. If peer or coping models raise performance or persistence no more than expert models, drop the model-choice row. If learners who attempt first and then study a model do as well as those modelled first, even as novices, the order in step 2 should be relaxed. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the model was too fast" or "not enough narration" after the fact.

Imitation right after the model, unaided immediate performance, delayed retention, transfer, confidence and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no segment length, no number of models, no kind of model and no point at which to stop modelling.
