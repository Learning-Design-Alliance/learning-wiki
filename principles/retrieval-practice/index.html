Retrieval Practice¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 88 claims (46 for, 39 mixed, 2 against, 1 unmarked) · 39 studies (15 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 8 review, 3 associational, 3 theoretical, 2 qualitative), q1–q4 · 12 of 39 report an effect size · 80 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner who has already studied some material, recalling it from memory, rather than restudying it or not being tested, tends to raise performance on a delayed test of that material. The best-supported cases are adults and university students learning prose, word pairs and facts (verbal-association, concept), compared with restudy at a horizon of a day or more (delayed-retention). Two conditions bound the relationship. The initial retrieval has to mostly succeed, or be followed by corrective feedback; repeated failure with no feedback has shown about no benefit. And the horizon matters: on a test minutes after study, restudy has been ahead. Transfer to new questions (near-transfer) is smaller and depends on how the retrieval and the later task are configured. Whether the benefit holds for material high in element interactivity is contested. This is a bounded expectation for groups under stated conditions, not a forecast for one learner.

The Team-Based Learning pattern is one reusable design that puts this relationship to work: its individual readiness test is a retrieval attempt, and its team retest supplies immediate feedback. The retrieval evidence bears on those steps, not on the team formation, application exercises or peer evaluation around them. The pattern's response-dependent branches are design proposals, untested with learners.

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record a response under stated conditions: the item format (free recall, cued recall, short answer, multiple choice), cues and notes allowed, the time since study, the stakes, and whether feedback followed. A score on a quiz is an observation. "Has a durable, retrievable memory" is an inferred state, and so is "studied the reading". Correct recognition of an option is a different performance from producing the answer, and an answer given two minutes after reading says little about one given a week later. Confidence is a separate observation; learners who restudied in the cited experiment were more confident and did worse at a week, so confidence alone does not settle the state.

Strengthened memory access is the explanation the claim pages give for the effect. It is a mechanism hypothesis; no observation in a single quiz measures it. Two rival explanations have been tested and did not account for it. In word-pair experiments, attempting recall before each re-presentation beat study-only presentations of equal number (nonsense pairs [+M]); the advantage remained when only the last presentation differed, so a different way of encoding later presentations does not explain it (not an encoding strategy [+M]), and when each list was all one condition, so learners rehearsing the study items more does not explain it (not differential rehearsal [+M]). One laboratory study (Carrier & Pashler, 1992) with no effect sizes printed; it rules out alternatives, it does not show the mechanism.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Recalls well right after studying, poorly a week later Fluent restudy created short-lived access; the material was never well encoded; later learning interfered Compare an unaided recall probe at the delay with the immediate one, on matched items; ask for a prediction of later recall first, and record whether any retrieval or feedback came between.
Fails most items on a first unaided attempt The material was not studied or not encoded; the cues on the probe do not match how it was learned; the stakes or anxiety suppressed responding Re-present the material, then probe again after a short gap; compare a cued with a free-recall version; compare a low-stakes attempt with a graded one.
Correct on multiple choice, wrong on short answer for the same content Recognition without recall; a lure picked earlier has been learned as the answer; unfamiliarity with the response format Give a short-answer version of the items; check whether wrong answers repeat options the learner selected before; note whether feedback followed the earlier test.
Recalls the fact, cannot use it on an application question The retrieved item is not connected to the principle the question needs; the question demands knowledge that was never practised; reading or language load Ask the application question with the fact supplied and without it; ask the learner to explain the relation; vary the wording of the question.
Scores rise across repeated quizzes but not on the later exam Item-specific memory of the quiz questions; the exam measures something else; the gap before the exam was longer than any practised one Probe with new items on the quizzed topics, at a delay like the exam's; compare the quiz and exam blueprints.

These are proposals for telling explanations apart, untested with learners. A probe is itself a retrieval attempt and can change what it measures; record it as an exposure. One contrast shifts confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]: two meta-analyses. The first, of 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, compared tested with restudied material (g = 0.50, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.58, heterogeneity high); the effect was larger at retention intervals of a day or more (0.69) than below a day (0.41), with feedback (0.73 against 0.39) and with cued recall rather than recognition (0.61 against 0.29), and about zero (0.03) with no feedback and initial success at or below 50%. Published studies showed larger effects than unpublished ones (0.58 against 0.25). The second, of practice testing across education levels, found g = 0.51, with multiple-choice and constructed-response practice tests both effective; its comparators mix rereading, filler tasks and no treatment, which is not the same contrast as testing against restudy. An experiment with undergraduates reading science passages found restudy ahead at five minutes (81% against 75%) and testing ahead at two days and one week; the claim page records it with two sample sizes (n=180, and 120 + 180 across two experiments), so read the full entry before quoting one. Not settled: the abstracts available could not confirm the entries. It does not predict an individual's gain or the size of an effect on complex skills.
  • Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S]: in a review of classroom studies (222 in the published abstract; 48,478 students), quizzing beat restudy and other controls overall (g = 0.499), and quizzes with corrective feedback produced g = 0.537 against 0.374 without. That is a between-study comparison, and another meta-analysis found no moderation (0.63 against 0.60). A word-pair experiment (258 participants, Luganda–English, final test one week later) found that supplying the answer after an error raised retention, while feedback after a correct response made little difference. One of two entries passes the judge; the other could not be confirmed. On timing, one multiple-choice experiment (72 participants) found immediate and delayed feedback equally effective at removing lure intrusions, bringing them down to the level of a group never shown the lures (d = .65 and .81 against no feedback) (lure intrusions [+M]); that is one outcome of one study, and the wiki records no comparison of timing on correct recall. Feedback after a correct answer has a cost when time is fixed: in a Swahili–English word-pair experiment, letting learners skip it bought more recall attempts (5.88 against 4 per item) and higher recall after 24–48 hours (.72 against .63) (skipping feedback [+M]; more attempts [+M]). Elaborated against answer-only feedback is not tested here. Errors made with high confidence are corrected more durably than low-confidence ones (hypercorrection [+M]; a narrative review and one experiment, no effect size printed, not confirmed against the sources). Without feedback, multiple choice carries a specific risk. The same 72-participant experiment found a multiple-choice test without feedback still beat no test on later cued recall (d = .97), but for items the learners had not studied first, lure intrusions (.32) outnumbered correct answers (.18), so testing unstudied material without feedback did harm (net harm boundary [~M]). And students later produced lures they had selected (lures [-M]), and more alternatives helped retention when initial success was high but hurt it when success was low (alternatives [~M]); both are reported second-hand in one review chapter.
  • Retrieval Failure Reduces Benefit [~M]: in the same 61-study meta-analysis, no-feedback studies whose initial recall was at or below 50% showed g = 0.03, and those above 75% showed g = 0.56. This is a moderator analysis across groups of studies, not a manipulation of success, so it gives no threshold for one learner. Not settled on the abstract. Pretesting studies, where failure followed by feedback helps, are a counterpoint the page records. The same review chapter reports that effects grow more robust as initial success rises, especially above 75% (success [+M]), and also that less support during practice helps when learners can still retrieve: free recall beat recognition although 89% of words were recognised and 69% recalled, and fewer letter cues beat more (less support [~M]). Read together, success and effort pull in opposite directions; the chapter gives no point at which they balance, and none of these were manipulations of success within one study.
  • Spacing the retrieval: a meta-analysis of 29 studies found spaced retrieval ahead of massed retrieval (g = 0.74) and no reliable difference between expanding and uniform schedules (spaced retrieval [+M]; not yet checked against its source). Experiments reported in the review chapter found that a small spacing cut initial recall by about 25% yet raised final retention, and that massed repeated retrieval gave no benefit at one week over dropping an item after one recall (spaced against massed [+M]). In one study, adaptive spaced retrieval beat learner-directed generative-AI study on a seven-week posttest (partial η² = .128), while a fixed spaced schedule did not differ reliably from either (adaptive retrieval [~M]); the comparator is learner-directed study, not restudy, and the study is a single preprint.
  • Settings and learners: classroom quizzing by clickers, software or paper improved classroom exams in eighth-grade science, college statistics and introductory psychology, including on new questions in one study (classroom quizzing [+M]); benefits are reported for children aged 9–11, adults aged 55–65 and memory-impaired patient groups, more evident at about age 8 than age 6 (learners [+M]); and higher trait or induced anxiety went with smaller, still positive effects (anxiety [~M]). All three are second-hand from one review chapter, with no effect sizes printed. In two undergraduate courses with four voluntary online practice tests, each test taken went with a higher exam score after prior knowledge was controlled (β = .27 without feedback, N = 201; β = .28 with corrective feedback, n = 111) (voluntary practice tests [+W]); students chose how many to take, so motivation or study time may carry part of that association. A monograph's narrative synthesis rates practice testing high utility across ages, abilities, tasks and contexts (utility [+W]), a summary judgment rather than a new comparison.
  • Format matching: the review chapter concludes that the benefit does not depend on an exact match between practice and final-test format (cue direction, map matching to route recall) (format matching [~M]), and that short answer beats multiple choice mainly when feedback follows, with multiple choice ahead without feedback in Kang et al.'s first experiment (86% against 54% initially correct) (feedback and format [~M]); across four experiments the format difference was d = 0.07 (format evidence mixed [~M]). Breadth is a separate choice from format: in a within-subject study of 54 university students reading two expository texts, short-answer practice on specific sections retained those sections better a week later on both posttest types (η² = .195 on the short-answer test), while free recall of the whole text retained the untargeted sections better only on a free-recall posttest (η² = .084) (targeted [+M]; holistic [~M]); overall scores did not differ (no overall difference [~W]).
  • Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer [~S]: a meta-analysis of 122 experiments (192 effect sizes, N = 10,382) found transfer from practice testing against non-testing re-exposure (d = 0.40, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.50), strongest to application and inference questions and across test formats, weakest to rearranged items, untested material and worked-example problems; bias corrections often left no positive transfer when the favourable moderators were absent. Four prose-passage experiments found repeated testing ahead of repeated study on new inferential questions a week later. Both entries pass the judge (abstracts). It does not establish far transfer to new problem structures, and one large field experiment points against far transfer from test practice: within PISA 2012, students randomly given forms with more mathematics content gained about 2% of a standard deviation per hour on a later mathematics task (b = 2.29, SE = 0.84; N = 19,355) and nothing measurable on non-curricular problem-solving tasks (b = 0.36, SE = 1.81; N = 19,481) (mathematics gain [+M]; no general problem-solving gain [-M]). The comparator was less mathematics within the same two-hour test, and the claim pages record nothing about feedback, so it measures more practice testing in a subject against less, not testing against restudy.
  • The argument that retrieval practice effects do not occur with materials high in element interactivity is contested [~M]: second-hand, from one review chapter: one study found effects with a randomly ordered text but not an intact one, another found them for both, and the chapter judges the limiting argument not convincing. Not yet checked against its sources. For high element-interactivity material or a complex-skill goal, treat the relationship as unsettled.

Retain the comparator (restudy, rereading, filler or nothing), the item format, feedback, initial success, the horizon and the learners when transporting these results. The pooled values come from different comparisons and should not be ranked against one another or read as an individual's expected gain.

The evidence by question¶

Each question below links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it into one table, one row per distinct study, with each study's design and effect as recorded and no averaged verdict (eval/synthesis/retrieval-practice.json, scripts/synthesize_claim.py; a pilot, 2026-10-13). The count is of distinct studies across the claim and the narrower claims that bear on it.

  • Does retrieving material (quizzes, practice tests, recall) improve later retention more than restudying it? Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]: 12 distinct studies.
  • How does the benefit depend on the delay before the final test? Retention interval moderates the testing effect: effects are larger after a day or more than at shorter intervals, where individual studies vary from reliable to null or reversed [~M]: 5 distinct studies.
  • Does the benefit carry to new questions, inferences and applications, or only to the items practised? Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer [+S]: 6 distinct studies.
  • Does feedback after retrieval add to the benefit? Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S]: 6 distinct studies.
  • Which practice format: short answer, multiple choice, or matching the final test? Evidence on whether initial short-answer questions produce more learning than initial multiple-choice questions is mixed, with recent studies finding little or no difference [~M]: 5 distinct studies.
  • How much should retrieval succeed during practice, and how much support should it get? Retrieval Failure Reduces Benefit [~M]: 4 distinct studies.
  • How many practice tests, and how should they be scheduled? Spaced Retrieval Improves Retention [+M]: 8 distinct studies.
  • For whom, and with what materials, does it work? Test-enhanced learning generalizes across education levels, from elementary school (g = 0.328) through university/college (g = 0.486). [+M]: 3 distinct studies.
  • Will learners choose to retrieve on their own, and can they be taught to? Learners underuse effective learning strategies such as spacing and retrieval practice [~M]: 6 distinct studies.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

None at present. (2010) in a new fixed-time row and the feedback decision; Endres et al. (2020) in the format row and decision; Naujoks et al. (2022) in the classroom row and the settings qualification; Avvisati & Borgonovi (2020) in the transfer row, qualification and decision.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner wants to be able to do with the material and why, and record it apart from the designer's objective. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations, not assumptions. Then fix the target: the capability (recall a term, explain a relation, apply it to a case), a representative instrument, a criterion chosen locally, the aids permitted, and the horizon. Retrieval evidence is mostly about delayed recall of studied material; if the target is application or a whole skill, say so, because the expectation is weaker there.

For example, a nursing student may value being able to answer a patient's question about a medication on placement, while the designer's objective is one-week cued recall of drug classes and their actions. A recall quiz serves the designer's objective directly and the learner's only in part: answering a patient is an application to a new question, which is where the transfer evidence is conditional. Keep both aims, and assess the application at its own horizon rather than inferring it from quiz scores.

What would revise this model?¶

The delayed-retention expectation should weaken if comparable learners, at equal time, on aligned delayed outcomes, show no advantage for retrieval over restudy in defensible comparisons, including unpublished ones; the publication-bias gap already recorded is a reason to watch for this. If within-study manipulations of feedback or of initial success fail to reproduce the between-study moderators, the conditions above should be restated. If retrieval on high element-interactivity material or complex-skill goals is shown, under controlled comparisons, to help or not to help, replace the "contested" line with that finding. Do not protect the model by relabelling every null as too little feedback or too low success after the fact.

A within-session quiz score, delayed recall, transfer to new questions and valued use are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal success rate for an individual, an optimal schedule, or a benefit across all domains.

Design Decisions¶

Should learners retrieve, or restudy?¶

  • Default: replace some restudy with recall from memory; two meta-analyses put testing against restudy or rereading at g = 0.50 and g = 0.51, and repeated testing beat repeated study one week later (61% against 40%) at equal time — Retrieval Practice Improves Long Term Retention [+S], Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]
  • Changes when: only performance minutes after study matters → restudy was ahead on a five-minute test (81% against 75%) and testing won only at two days and one week — Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [~S]
  • Changes when: the setting is a classroom → quizzing still beat restudy and other controls across 222 classroom studies (g = 0.499) — Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S], Classroom quizzing delivered by clickers, computer software, or paper improves student performance on classroom exams in middle school and college courses [+M]
  • Tested with: undergraduates reading science prose; 61 studies of tested against restudied material; classroom studies from middle school to college.
  • Not settled: Rowland's published studies showed larger effects than unpublished ones (0.58 against 0.25), and the author advises caution about publication bias.

Should feedback follow retrieval?¶

  • Default: give corrective feedback; the testing effect was g = 0.73 with feedback against 0.39 without in one meta-analysis, and g = 0.537 against 0.374 across classroom studies — Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S], Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S]
  • Changes when: the learner answered correctly → feedback after correct responses made little difference, even at low confidence; supplying the answer after an error is what raised one-week retention — Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [~S]
  • Changes when: the error was made with high confidence → corrected high-confidence errors are remembered better than low-confidence ones, so correct them clearly — High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors. [+M]
  • Changes when: practice time is fixed and many answers are correct → let learners skip feedback after a correct answer and use the time for more attempts; that gave about six attempts per item instead of four and higher recall after 24–48 hours — When total learning time is fixed, permitting learners to skip feedback after retrieval improves delayed final recall compared with mandatory feedback [+M], Permitting feedback skipping increases the number of retrieval attempts completed within a fixed learning time [+M]
  • Changes when: the quiz is multiple choice → give feedback, immediately or later in the session; both schedules removed lure intrusions in one experiment — Feedback on a multiple-choice test sharply reduces lure intrusions on a later cued recall test, with immediate and delayed schedules equally effective [+M]
  • Tested with: Luganda–English word pairs (258 participants); Swahili–English word pairs (68 undergraduates); multiple-choice tests on prose (72 participants); classroom quizzing studies.
  • Not settled: the feedback comparisons are across studies, and Adesope et al. (2017) found no moderation by feedback (g = 0.63 against 0.60); feedback timing is recorded only for lure intrusions after multiple choice, and elaborated against answer-only feedback after retrieval has no wiki evidence.

How hard should retrieval be?¶

  • Default: pitch retrieval so it usually succeeds, or follow it with feedback; with no feedback and initial success at or below 50% the effect was about zero (g = 0.03), and effects were more robust above 75% success — Retrieval Failure Reduces Benefit [+M], A review reports that retrieval practice effects grow more robust as initial retrieval success rises, especially above 75%, and separately that giving more retrieval cues yields smaller effects [+M]
  • Changes when: retrieval is made easy with many cues → more initial cues gave smaller effects; free recall beat recognition and fewer letter cues beat more, despite lower success during practice — Initial retrieval conditions that provide less cue support, such as free recall rather than recognition or fewer letter cues, tend to produce better retention despite lower initial success [~M]
  • Tested with: lab word lists and word pairs reported in one review chapter; the Rowland moderator analysis.
  • Not settled: the success threshold comes from a between-study moderator analysis, not a manipulation; where "effortful but achievable" lies for a given learner is not given.

Which question format: multiple choice or short answer?¶

  • Default: either format works when feedback follows; studies disagree and one set of four experiments found d = 0.07 between them — Evidence on whether initial short-answer questions produce more learning than initial multiple-choice questions is mixed, with recent studies finding little or no difference [~M], Retrieval Practice Improves Long Term Retention [+S]
  • Changes when: there is no feedback → short answer's advantage appears mainly with feedback; without it the higher success of multiple choice can favour multiple choice — Initial short-answer tests outperform initial multiple-choice tests mainly when feedback follows them; without feedback, the higher initial success of multiple-choice tests can favor multiple-choice [~M]
  • Changes when: multiple choice is used without feedback → students later produced the wrong lures they had chosen; give feedback after multiple-choice quizzes — Taking initial multiple-choice tests without feedback can lead students to later produce the incorrect lure answers they selected, even when an overall retrieval practice benefit occurs [-M]
  • Changes when: specific points must be kept → ask short-answer questions on those points; free recall of a whole text helped the untargeted parts only on a free-recall test — Targeted retrieval via specific short-answer tasks improves retention of directly targeted information more than holistic retrieval via free-recall tasks [+M], Holistic retrieval via free-recall improves retention of non-targeted information, but only on the free-recall posttest [~M]
  • Tested with: college students on lectures and SAT II questions, online college courses, seventh-grade science; university students reading two expository texts.
  • Not settled: whether matching practice format to the final exam matters is answered only for exact matching (it is not required) — The benefits of retrieval practice do not depend on an exact match between initial retrieval practice conditions and the final test format [+M].

When should retrieval happen?¶

  • Default: space retrieval across sessions after a delay rather than straight after study; spaced beat massed retrieval at g = 0.74, and massed retrieval immediately after study produced very little learning — Spaced Retrieval Improves Retention [+M], A review reports that retrieval practice effects grow more robust as initial retrieval success rises, especially above 75%, and separately that giving more retrieval cues yields smaller effects [+M]
  • Changes when: the outcome is measured within a day → the effect is smaller (g = 0.41) than at intervals over a day (g = 0.69) — Retrieval practice effects are larger at retention intervals greater than 1 day (g = 0.69) than at intervals less than 1 day (g = 0.41) in Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis [~M]
  • Tested with: 29 studies of spaced against massed retrieval; the Rowland meta-analysis.
  • Not settled: gap lengths for retrieval are not given on these pages; see Spaced Repetition for gap decisions.

Will it help learners apply knowledge, not only recall it?¶

  • Default: expect some transfer (d = 0.40 across 192 effect sizes), strongest to application and inference questions and across test formats, with elaborated retrieval practice and initial test performance among the strong moderators — Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer [~S]
  • Changes when: the transfer is to rearranged items, untested material or worked-example problems → transfer was weakest there, and bias-corrected estimates often showed none when the favourable moderators were absent — Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer [-S]
  • Changes when: the hoped-for gain is general problem-solving ability → do not expect it from subject practice tests; more mathematics practice in PISA 2012 gave a small mathematics gain and no change on non-curricular problem solving — Mathematics test practice does not improve domain-general problem-solving ability [-M], Greater mathematics content in a practice test improves subsequent mathematics performance by a small amount (~2% of a standard deviation per hour of practice) [+M]
  • Tested with: 122 experiments (N = 10,382); prose passages with inferential questions one week later; a randomised field experiment with about 19,000 15-year-olds in 32 countries.
  • Not settled: far transfer to novel problem structures within a subject.

For whom, and with what material?¶

  • Default: use it beyond college students; benefits have been reported for children aged 9–11, adults aged 55–65 and memory-impaired patients — Retrieval practice benefits have been observed in children, healthy older adults, and memory-impaired patient groups, not only college students [+M]
  • Changes when: learners are anxious or the quiz feels high-stakes → benefits held for high and low trait anxiety, but effects were smaller with higher trait anxiety and when anxiety was induced; keep it low-stakes — Retrieval practice benefits learners regardless of trait anxiety level, but higher trait or induced anxiety is associated with smaller testing effects [~M]
  • Changes when: learners are younger children → a few studies suggest benefits are clearer at age 8 than at 6 — Retrieval practice benefits have been observed in children, healthy older adults, and memory-impaired patient groups, not only college students [~M]
  • Tested with: evidence reported second-hand in one review chapter (Karpicke 2017).
  • Not settled: whether retrieval practice works for material high in element interactivity is contested — one study found effects with randomly ordered but not intact text, another found them for both — The argument that retrieval practice effects do not occur with materials high in element interactivity is contested, and the chapter judges its research base not convincing [~M].

Claims¶

  • Classroom pretesting improves high-stakes exam performance on pretested content by 8–9% over a multi-week course [+W]
  • Whether initial retrieval practice enhances semantic organization in final free recall is unclear, with results that may depend on how retrieval practice is implemented [~W]
  • Whether prompting students to construct explanations during retrieval adds benefit beyond initial free recall is mixed across studies [~W]
  • Retrieval practice instructions led students to recall items to a criterion of about three correct retrievals before dropping them [+W]
  • Quizzing outperforms no/filler activity, fewer-question testing and restudying, with a larger advantage over no/filler activity (low reexposure) than over restudying (high reexposure); against other elaborative strategies its advantage is small and not significant. [+W]
  • Repeated successful retrieval during learning predicted final recall in both experiments [+W]
  • Both preclass (g = 0.186) and postclass (g = 0.536) quizzes enhance learning, with postclass quizzes more effective; benefits increase with treatment duration. [~W]
  • LLM-generated retrieval practice was practical to deploy and well received, but high correctness rates (about 70% of items at p=1.0) restricted score variance [~W]
  • Retrieval practice instructions improved final recall of translations in Experiment 1 [+W]
  • Benefits of retrieval practice have persisted for 6 months in medical students and 9 months in middle-school students [+W]
  • The effect of retrieval practice format on reading comprehension does not differ significantly between lower-order and higher-order thinking questions [~W]
  • Practicing retrieval of some portions of an educational text can enhance retention of related nontested portions (retrieval-induced facilitation), but not when relational encoding is disrupted [~W]
  • Retrieval practice produces reliable positive effects at short delays, although some forgetting must set in so that the final test is not at ceiling [+W]
  • The effect of retrieval practice format on reading comprehension does not differ significantly between an immediate and a three-week-delayed final test [~W]
  • Retrieval practice enhances learning across domains and the lifespan in applied educational settings [+W]
  • A math teacher whose students had typically prepared for tests by rereading notes implemented three retrieval-practice strategies after learning that rereading is not effective for long-term retention [+W]
  • Retrieval practice enhances performance on final inference and transfer questions relative to restudying, although a single-word fill-in-the-blank task did not [~W]
  • Retrieval practice format, levels of thinking and retention interval show no significant three-way interaction on undergraduates' reading comprehension [~W]
  • Spaced retrieval practice (successive relearning) produced multi-year retention of foreign language vocabulary in adults, as reported by the review [+W]
  • Transfer of test-enhanced learning is greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, to medical diagnosis problems, and to mediator and related word cues [+W]
  • Transfer of test-enhanced learning is weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested materials seen during initial study, and to problems involving worked examples [~W]
  • Testing benefits retention over a conservative restudy control, establishing the testing effect as robust [+W]
  • Pretesting benefits for paired associates depend on semantic association between pair members, with cued-recall benefits absent for unrelated pairs [~W]
  • The meta-analysis supports retrieval effort as a contributor to the testing effect [~M]
  • Offering corrective feedback after class quizzes significantly increases learning gains over withholding feedback (g = 0.537 vs. g = 0.374). [+W]
  • Offloading work to AI costs learning: students given answers did well in practice but scored lower on an unassisted exam [+W]
  • Effects of instructions on some measures were inconsistent across experiments, and instructed students did not recall all items to criterion [~W]
  • Learners underuse effective learning strategies such as spacing and retrieval practice [+W]
  • Test-enhanced learning generalizes across education levels, from elementary school (g = 0.328) through university/college (g = 0.486). [+W]
  • Overall posttest performance does not differ between targeted and holistic retrieval conditions [~W]
  • Holistic retrieval increases situational interest, with better recall performance predicting greater interest, contrary to the knowledge-deprivation hypothesis [~W]
  • The retention benefit of spaced recall quizzes holds regardless of instructor teaching experience [+W]
  • Spaced recall quizzing significantly improves overall post-test performance on fundamental mathematics without harming learning of current material [+W]
  • Spaced recall quizzing improves long-term retention even when immediate feedback is not provided [+W]
  • The retention benefit of spaced recall quizzes holds regardless of the hour of the day the class meets [+W]
  • Without feedback, more practice-test participation predicts lower specificity (worse detection of incorrect answers); with corrective feedback this negative relation disappears [~W]
  • Feedback neutralizes the effect of prior study on final cued recall performance [~W]
  • Incorrect information learned from a multiple-choice test is applied and reasoned with on conceptually related transfer questions [~W]
  • Taking a multiple-choice test boosts performance on later tests compared with nontested control conditions, including with complex SAT II materials [+W]
  • Multiple-choice testing improves later cued recall of prose facts, a benefit that persists though reduced after a one-week delay [+W]
  • The retrieval benefit occurs at the level of individual items, not through general practice at retrieval [+W]
  • The testing effect is largest at a retention interval of 1–6 days (g = 0.82) and robust across all intervals [+W]
  • Testing effects are stronger when practice and final test formats are identical (g = 0.63) than dissimilar (g = 0.53), supporting transfer-appropriate processing [~W]
  • Participants' willingness to skip feedback after correct responses increases substantially across retrieval-practice cycles [~W]
  • Repeated test-restudy trials produced higher final cued recall than study-only or single test-restudy controls across both experiments [+M]
  • The retrieval (TTST) advantage appeared at both short (5 min) and long (24 h) retention delays, with no reliable evidence that it differed between them [+W]
  • The TTST advantage replicates with meaningful Eskimo/English word pairs (Experiment 2) [+W]
  • A systematic review of 50 classroom experiments found retrieval practice yields medium-to-large benefits in most cases, but only 6% were conducted in non-WEIRD countries [~W]
  • Correct-answer feedback alone was insufficient for generalization: significantly worse than lecture in Study 1, with the difference not significant in Study 2 [~W]

Related Principles¶

  • Pretesting (Prequestions Before Study)
  • Spaced Learning
  • Immediate Feedback
  • Retrieval practice and spaced retrieval improve achievement and retention by medium amounts, while interleaving helps category learning only for some materials and blocking beats it for words

Examples¶

  • A biology course opens each lesson with short no-notes prompts that ask learners to explain last week’s concepts before new content begins.
  • A language app revisits previously learned vocabulary with delayed recall items and immediate corrective feedback rather than only offering rereading.
  • EISEL
  • Use frequent, low-stakes, cumulative tests in varied formats, including pretests, and encourage student self-testing
