# Fostering Creative Thinking

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 12 claims (8 for, 2 mixed, 2 against) · 17 studies (5 review, 4 causal, 3 theoretical, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 design, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 17 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

## Description
Creativity is the ability to make or do something new that is also useful or valued by others (Gardner, 1993) — the "something" can be an object, a skill, or an action, but to count as creative it cannot simply be bizarre, nor merely accidental (a poem produced by typing letters at random is not creative even if the result happens to be pleasing). This experience is not restricted to a few geniuses or to specific fields like art or music; most people have had it at some point (Kaufman & Baer, 2006). **Creative thinking** specifically is the generation of ideas that are new as well as useful, productive, and appropriate — and it is a form of thinking teachers can deliberately stimulate.

### Divergent and Convergent Thinking
Teachers can encourage **divergent thinking** — open-ended ideas that lead in many directions — by posing open-ended questions with many possible answers (e.g., "How many uses can you think of for a cup?"; "What is the most unusual use you can think of for a shoe?") (Torrance, 1992; Kim, 2006). Answering such questions creatively still depends partly on prior knowledge of the objects involved. Divergent thinking's converse is **convergent thinking** — focused, logical reasoning toward a specific answer. Developing convergent thinking, which much ordinary schoolwork emphasizes through mastery of content, indirectly facilitates divergent thinking and creativity up to a point (Sternberg, 2003; Runco, 2004; Cropley, 2006) — but excessive emphasis on convergent thinking, carried to an extreme, can discourage creativity rather than support it.

### Intrinsic Motivation and Evaluation
Creativity, in or out of school, tends to flourish best when the creative activity is its own intrinsic reward and the person is relatively unconcerned with how others will judge the result — whether composing a song, writing an essay, or organizing an event, the activity is more likely to be creative when the creator focuses on and enjoys it for its own sake rather than anticipating evaluation (Brophy, 2004).

### Balancing Assessment and Creative Exploration
This creates a genuine tension for teachers, who must evaluate students' learning of particular content within the fixed time limits of a course, even though encouraging students to disregard others' evaluation is part of what supports creativity. The tension is manageable, not fatal: creativity can still be encouraged in classrooms at least some of the time (Claxton, Edwards, & Scale-Constantinou, 2006). Assessment does not have to happen constantly — if students must be assessed on understanding of specific vocabulary, for instance, testing that understanding may itself limit creative thinking in the moment, but there can also be separate times for experimenting or playing with the same content (writing poems, inventing word games) that are themselves potentially creative. Learning content and playing with content are not mutually exclusive; one often supports the other.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Open-ended prompts with genuinely many possible answers, to elicit divergent thinking
- Deliberately separated times or activities where evaluation is relaxed or absent, alongside the necessary times for assessing specific content mastery
- Assessment structures such as portfolios should document thinking and growth over time rather than only the final artifact
- Instructors must refrain from judging ideas during ideation and critical-comment phases, since most STEM students are unfamiliar with this type of pedagogy
- At least two distinct model types (e.g., a physical model with real material constraints, and a computational model that can be freely manipulated) built in parallel for the same phenomenon, so students can compare what each affords
- Open-ended facilitation prompts that ask what a model reveals or what students value about it, rather than prompts aimed at a single disciplinary criterion
- A nonlinear, student-led activity sequence responsive to emergent student questions, rather than a fixed sequence culminating in one final model
#### Constraints
- Constant, high-stakes evaluation of an activity suppresses the intrinsic focus that supports creativity in that same activity
- Convergent-thinking instruction (content mastery) is not opposed to creativity, but excessive emphasis on it can crowd out divergent thinking if never balanced with more open-ended activity
- Especially emphasized for young children when fostering creativity and innovation is the primary goal
- The authors note it is difficult for instructors to avoid making judgments about ideas, particularly positive and negative critical comments
- Requires a topic that genuinely supports multiple non-redundant model types (e.g., an ecosystem, where physical, computational, and diagrammatic models each foreground different aspects); not every topic affords this
- Without a comparison-oriented whole-class discussion structure, multiple models risk becoming parallel busywork rather than mutually informing representations

### Target Learners
- Learners in content areas where instruction is dominated by convergent, single-right-answer tasks, for whom deliberately introduced divergent-thinking activities restore balance
- K-12 students, especially young children, in maker education programs
- undergraduate engineering and other STEM students
- Middle-school students (studied at grade 6) in ecosystem/energy-flow science units

### Target Learning Objectives
- Generating ideas that are new, useful, and appropriate (not merely unusual), within a domain the learner already has some working knowledge of
- Creativity, innovation, and growth as a maker
- willingness to generate unrestrained ideas
- evaluating existing heuristics and models

# Heterogeneity-Seeking Modeling Curriculum

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Most modeling curricula aim for convergence: students revise their models until the class agrees on one shared, canonical representation. This pattern instead treats multiple, non-reconciled model types as the intended end state — students build a physical model (e.g., a sealed biosphere jar), a computational model (a simulation), a diagrammatic model (a food web), and sometimes an embodied model (role-playing organisms) of the *same* phenomenon, and instruction treats each model as revealing something the others don't, rather than ranking them against one canonical standard. Teacher facilitation prompts are correspondingly open-ended ("what can you learn from each model?" "why are we doing both at the same time?") rather than discipline-directed toward a single correct answer.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Select a phenomenon that supports multiple, genuinely non-redundant model types (e.g., an ecosystem supporting physical, computational, diagrammatic, and embodied models).
2. Introduce model types in parallel or in loose sequence, without designating any one as the "real" or final model.
3. Facilitate whole-class and small-group discussion with open-ended prompts ("what can you learn from each model?" "why are we doing both at once?") rather than prompts aimed at a single correct answer.
4. When students' models diverge (e.g., two different food-web formats), discuss what each reveals rather than ranking them.
5. Follow emergent student questions (e.g., a caring-driven question about an organism's wellbeing) even when they extend beyond the unit's original learning objectives, treating them as legitimate modeling work.
