# Fostering Creative Thinking

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 22 claims (14 for, 6 mixed, 2 against) · 23 studies (8 causal, 5 review, 4 theoretical, 3 design, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 5 of 23 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page is about **producing ideas and products that are new and useful in a domain the learner already partly knows**: many uses for an object, alternative solutions to an open problem, a poem or game made from this week's vocabulary, a model of a phenomenon, a maker project. For a learner whose output on such a task is few ideas, ideas close to the first one, or the answer they expect the teacher to want, a design that (a) poses prompts with many acceptable answers, (b) keeps generating separate in time from judging, grading and reward, and (c) has the learner produce their own ideas before turning to a tool, model answer or AI, is expected to yield more and more varied ideas during the activity (`skill-performance`, measured as fluency and originality), to keep the learner's interest in the activity (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`), and to leave the learner more able to generate ideas **unaided** on a later task (`near-transfer`). Judging, convergent practice and content mastery are not the enemy: they come before generation (knowledge of the objects is what ideas are made from) and after it (choosing, combining, refining). The claim is that they should not occupy the same minutes as generation.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's knowledge of the content (generation from nothing produces little; the earlier page notes creative answers depend partly on knowing the objects involved), on whether evaluation is expected during the activity, on what any reward is contingent on, and on whether the outcome is measured with or without the help that was available while practising.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as a whole.** None compares protected, unevaluated generation time with the same activity under evaluation and measures creative output. The claims below test parts of it, mostly from outside creative tasks:

- Expected tangible rewards for an already-interesting task lower later free-choice engagement with it, while verbal praise does not (two competing meta-analyses and laboratory work, `q3`–`q4`). This is the strongest evidence near the page, and it measures motivation, not creativity.
- Paying children a penny per idea **raised** the number of ideas they produced in the session (one randomized experiment with fifth graders). Reward contingent on producing ideas is not, on this evidence, a brake on idea output while the reward is in force; the undermining finding concerns what learners do once the reward is gone.
- University students who used ChatGPT freely on a creative task were more original on that task, but not on a later task without it; a "think first, ChatGPT later" group was more original on the later unaided task (one randomized experiment).
- A short structured creativity program was followed by higher creativity scores (one-group pretest–posttest studies, no control group).

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Teach the content first, then play with it.** Run creative work on content learners have already met: the vocabulary they have been taught, the materials they have handled, the model they have built. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (creative answers depend partly on knowledge of the objects; convergent content mastery supports creativity "up to a point").
2. **Pose an open prompt with many acceptable answers.** "How many uses can you think of for a cup?", "Write a poem or invent a word game with these ten words", "Which ways could this circuit fail?". Give 5–8 minutes of individual, silent generation with a goal of quantity ("as many as you can, including odd ones") before anyone shares. Untested proposal; the prompts are the earlier page's; the minutes are a proposal.
3. **Keep judging out of the generating minutes.** During generation the teacher and peers do not comment on whether ideas are good, right or wrong, positively or negatively; every idea is recorded. The earlier page's source notes that instructors find this hard: say aloud at the start that ideas will not be judged until the next step, and hold yourself to it. Where learners fear embarrassment, collect ideas anonymously (cards, a shared board or a poll that hides names from peers but not from the teacher). Basis: the authors' account in one undergraduate physics course ([Anonymous real-time sharing mitigates evaluation apprehension](../claims/anonymity-mitigates-evaluation-apprehension.md) [+W], no test reported); otherwise untested.
4. **When learners stall, ask what they are assuming.** If ideas dry up or all share one function, ask "what have you assumed this object, word or rule is for?" or "what does the task not actually say?" before offering more strategies. Basis: [Functional fixedness blocks solutions that require reinterpreting an object's function](../claims/functional-fixedness-limits-problem-solving.md) [+M] (one experiment cited second-hand and a classroom anecdote; it shows the obstacle, not that the question removes it).
5. **Then converge, as a separate step.** After generation, spend about as long again choosing, combining and improving: learners group ideas, apply criteria (stated in advance, or theirs, see the table), pick one or two and develop them. This is where judgement belongs. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (learning content and playing with content "are not mutually exclusive").
6. **Own ideas before AI.** If generative AI is available, learners write their own ideas first and use the tool afterwards to extend or critique them, not to start. Basis: [the regulated "think first, ChatGPT later" group was more original on the later unassisted task](../claims/regulated-ai-outperforms-independent-originality.md) [+M] and [free ChatGPT use raised originality on the assisted task, an advantage that disappeared without it](../claims/chatgpt-free-use-originality-gains-vanish-unassisted.md) [+M] (one randomized experiment, university students).
7. **Separate assessment time from creative time.** Assess the content (the vocabulary test, the energy-flow quiz) at its own time; do not grade the ideas produced in a generation session. Where creative work must be assessed, assess the process over time (a portfolio of drafts, sketches, failures and reflections; a rubric that scores process, understanding and product separately) rather than the final artifact alone. Do not promise tangible rewards for taking part in creative activities learners already enjoy; feedback that names what worked is safe. Basis: [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S] (measured on interest and free-choice persistence, not creativity, mostly in laboratory tasks); [Grading practices can diminish interest, encourage easier paths and reduce quality of thinking in maker education](../claims/grading-harms-maker-education-outcomes.md) [+W] (an argument citing Kohn). The portfolio and rubric come from the earlier page and the maker-assessment framework; untested.
8. **Plan the arc across sessions.** A default for a unit of several weeks: early sessions give one 15–20 minute creative segment a week on familiar objects or just-taught content, teacher-posed prompts, ungraded, ideas shared anonymously; middle sessions move prompts onto the unit's own content and let learners help set the convergence criteria; late sessions have learners pose the problem themselves and develop one idea into a product, with critique rounds only after each generation round and a portfolio entry each session. Move a learner from teacher-posed to self-posed problems once they produce ideas in more than one category (not only variants of one use) on two prompts running. All quantities are untested proposals; the three-week duration of the CREACT studies ([CREACT improves 5th-grade students' creative thinking performance in drawing arts](../claims/creact-improves-creative-thinking-drawing-arts.md) [+W], no control group) shows only that a program of this length was followed by higher scores.
9. **For modeling units in science, keep several model types and do not force them into one.** Build at least two non-redundant model types of the same phenomenon (a physical model, a computational one, a diagram), ask "what can you learn from each model?" rather than "which is right?", and follow learner questions that arise even when they leave the unit's objective. Basis: [Heterogeneity-seeking modeling curricula surface epistemic commitments in most students](../claims/heterogeneity-seeking-curricula-surface-diverse-epistemic-commitments.md) [+W] (sixth graders, one design study, no comparison curriculum and no learning outcome measured); the topic must support several models (an ecosystem does), and without a whole-class comparison the models risk becoming parallel busywork (the earlier pattern's constraint, untested).

When a condition is not met: if learners know little of the content yet, teach it first and keep the creative segment short; if every activity in the course is graded, mark the creative segment explicitly as ungraded and say when the graded part comes.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the creative work is for, and how it will be judged.** Generating ideas as practice, a product for a real audience, and a graded creative assignment need different handling of evaluation. If unstated, ask: will anyone grade, rank or publish what learners produce, and when?
- **What the learner already knows of the content and of open tasks.** Novices in the domain have little to recombine; learners used only to single-answer tasks may freeze or wait for the "right" answer. If unstated, ask: can they name several features or uses of the objects involved, and have they done an open-ended task before?
- **What help is available while generating.** Generative AI, model answers and a teacher's suggestions all raise output during the task; the question is what the learner can do without them later. If unstated, ask: will learners have AI or examples during the task, and will they have them when it counts?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what a learner produces under stated conditions: the prompt, the time, who could see the ideas, whether evaluation, reward or a tool was present, the number of ideas, how many distinct categories they fall into, and how far each is from the commonest answers in the group. "Is creative", "is afraid of being judged" and "is fixated" are inferred states. A short list from one session says little about capacity.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Very few ideas | Little knowledge of the objects; fear of being judged; unused to open tasks and waiting for the right answer; tired or disengaged | Repeat with an everyday object and anonymous collection; if output rises, knowledge or fear was the limit, not willingness. Ask what they would need to know to say more. |
| Many ideas, all variants of one use or one category | Functional fixedness or a set framing; the prompt's wording suggests one category | Ask what they assumed the object or rule is for, then count categories in a second round. |
| Ideas stop when the teacher walks over or classmates look | Evaluation apprehension; the learner reads attention as judgement | Compare output in anonymous and named rounds on matched prompts. |
| Highly original work with AI, ordinary work without it | The tool supplied the novelty; the learner has not built the habit of generating first | Give a matched task without the tool after a think-first round. |
| Many ideas when rewarded, little interest afterwards | The reward carried the activity | Offer the activity again as a free choice with no reward and record who takes it up. |
| Ideas that are odd but do not answer the task | Novelty without usefulness; the learner thinks "creative" means "strange" | Ask how the idea would work for its user, and show that the convergence step judges usefulness. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe changes what it measures (a second round is also practice), so record each one and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares creative work done with and without evaluation in the same minutes, or a generate-then-judge sequence with an unseparated one. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]: a 128-experiment meta-analysis found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement (d = −0.28 to −0.40), while positive verbal feedback enhanced it; a competing 96-experiment meta-analysis found no overall undermining and a minimal negative effect only for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Its `## Studies bearing on this claim` table counts **8 distinct studies**. It measures interest and free-choice persistence on mostly laboratory tasks, not creativity, and the two meta-analyses disagree on size.
- [Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]: a review and the same 128-experiment meta-analysis (d = −0.4 for rewards on already-interesting tasks), with a conceptual framework proposing that "cognitive" autonomy support (evaluating work against one's own standard) fosters the most enduring investment in deep thinking. Its table counts **8 distinct studies**, one with an effect size coded. It supports letting learners set convergence criteria; it is not a creativity measure.
- [Free ChatGPT use boosts originality on the assisted creative task but the advantage disappears when assistance is removed](../claims/chatgpt-free-use-originality-gains-vanish-unassisted.md) [+M] and [The regulated 'think first, ChatGPT later' group outperformed both other groups in independent originality on the unassisted task](../claims/regulated-ai-outperforms-independent-originality.md) [+M]: one study, 196 university students randomly assigned to human-only, free-AI and regulated-AI groups. Free AI raised originality on the assisted product-improvement task (d = 0.79 over human-only) but not on the later unassisted invention task (d = 0.04); the think-first group did not differ on the assisted task (d = 0.18) and was more original on the unassisted one (d = 0.52 over human-only, 0.48 over free AI). The delay between tasks is not stated on the claim pages. A second-hand account of the same study is [listed below](../claims/chatgpt-free-use-originality-gains-vanish-unassisted.md) [+M]; it is not a second study.
- [Concrete reward increases children's mean ideational fluency on creativity tasks relative to control conditions](../claims/concrete-reward-increases-mean-ideational-fluency.md) [~M]: 191 fifth graders randomly assigned to control, immediate or delayed penny-per-idea reward produced more ideas under both reward conditions; per task, immediate reward raised ideas on all four tasks and delayed reward on three ([Reward effects hold across individual creativity tasks](../claims/reward-effects-hold-across-individual-tasks.md) [~M]); pennies for "good" ideas only showed no incentive effect, in a condition the authors set aside as confounded ([Rewarding only 'good' ideas produced no incentive effect](../claims/good-idea-reward-no-incentive-effect.md) [~M]). One study, 1970, no effect size printed. **This limits the model**: a reward contingent on producing ideas raised output while it was in force; the page's caution about rewards rests on later free-choice interest, which this study did not measure.
- [Stepwise constructive-conflict guidance reduced new idea generation, while the antagonistic agent played a more balanced role](../claims/stepwise-conflict-guidance-reduces-idea-addition.md) [-M]: novice interaction designers given step-by-step critique guidance added fewer new ideas in their second iteration than those who reflected on their own (Hedges' g = −1.03); an interactive AI-agent condition fell between, not significantly different from either. One study. It qualifies the convergence step: heavy structure in critique can crowd out new ideas.
- [Functional fixedness — treating an object's or idea's function as fixed — blocks solutions that require reinterpreting it.](../claims/functional-fixedness-limits-problem-solving.md) [+M]: an experiment (cited second-hand, sample not reported) found functional fixedness in a technologically sparse culture, and a classroom anecdote shows a teacher's repeated question about assumptions unblocking a student. It supports the assumption question as a move; it does not test it.
- [Twenty-three 5th graders' creativity scores rise after nine CREACT drawing-arts lessons in a one-group pretest-posttest study without a control group (effect size .71)](../claims/creact-improves-creative-thinking-drawing-arts.md) [+W]: 23 fifth graders, nine lessons over three weeks, total creativity rose from 52.32 to 71.95 (an effect size of .71 the article calls large; coded `i2` medium). One group, no control, so practice on the test and maturation are not ruled out. Marked W here for the missing comparison.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these (laboratory interest measures, children's idea counts, university originality ratings, a drawing test), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures creative output months later.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually stated as a capability ("generates several useful alternatives to an open problem in this domain, unaided") or a product. Elicit separately what the learner wants from creative work: to make something they are proud of, to have fun with the content, to get a good mark, to avoid looking foolish, to make something for someone. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants a good mark will steer towards the safe idea the teacher seems to want; one who wants to make something for a friend may care more about usefulness than novelty; one who wants not to look foolish will produce little in a named round.

For example, a teacher's objective may be fluent use of ten new vocabulary words, while a learner wants to write a funny poem to read to a younger sibling. The poem session serves both if the words are required and the poem is not graded; a vocabulary test at another time checks the teacher's objective. Record both, so that a good poem is not read as mastery of the words, nor a weak test score as a failure of the creative session.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing creative activities with and without evaluation in the same session, on matched learners and tasks, find no difference in idea count, originality or later unaided output; or if reward per idea raises output without lowering later voluntary engagement, in which case the caution about rewards in step 7 is too broad for idea generation. If think-first sequencing with AI does not replicate beyond one university sample, or the unaided advantage disappears at a longer delay, step 6 becomes a preference. If one-group gains from short creativity programs vanish against a control group that takes the same tests, the arc's dose has no support at all. Do not protect the model by blaming every null result on hidden evaluation after the fact.

Output during an activity, originality judged by raters, interest afterwards, unaided performance on a new task, and creative work the learner values are separate outcomes, and the present evidence ties none of them to the separation of generating from judging.

## Description
Creativity is the ability to make or do something new that is also useful or valued by others (Gardner, 1993) — the "something" can be an object, a skill, or an action, but to count as creative it cannot simply be bizarre, nor merely accidental (a poem produced by typing letters at random is not creative even if the result happens to be pleasing). This experience is not restricted to a few geniuses or to specific fields like art or music; most people have had it at some point (Kaufman & Baer, 2006). **Creative thinking** specifically is the generation of ideas that are new as well as useful, productive, and appropriate — and it is a form of thinking teachers can deliberately stimulate.

### Divergent and Convergent Thinking
Teachers can encourage **divergent thinking** — open-ended ideas that lead in many directions — by posing open-ended questions with many possible answers (e.g., "How many uses can you think of for a cup?"; "What is the most unusual use you can think of for a shoe?") (Torrance, 1992; Kim, 2006). Answering such questions creatively still depends partly on prior knowledge of the objects involved. Divergent thinking's converse is **convergent thinking** — focused, logical reasoning toward a specific answer. Developing convergent thinking, which much ordinary schoolwork emphasizes through mastery of content, indirectly facilitates divergent thinking and creativity up to a point (Sternberg, 2003; Runco, 2004; Cropley, 2006) — but excessive emphasis on convergent thinking, carried to an extreme, can discourage creativity rather than support it.

### Intrinsic Motivation and Evaluation
Creativity, in or out of school, tends to flourish best when the creative activity is its own intrinsic reward and the person is relatively unconcerned with how others will judge the result — whether composing a song, writing an essay, or organizing an event, the activity is more likely to be creative when the creator focuses on and enjoys it for its own sake rather than anticipating evaluation (Brophy, 2004).

### Balancing Assessment and Creative Exploration
This creates a genuine tension for teachers, who must evaluate students' learning of particular content within the fixed time limits of a course, even though encouraging students to disregard others' evaluation is part of what supports creativity. The tension is manageable, not fatal: creativity can still be encouraged in classrooms at least some of the time (Claxton, Edwards, & Scale-Constantinou, 2006). Assessment does not have to happen constantly — if students must be assessed on understanding of specific vocabulary, for instance, testing that understanding may itself limit creative thinking in the moment, but there can also be separate times for experimenting or playing with the same content (writing poems, inventing word games) that are themselves potentially creative. Learning content and playing with content are not mutually exclusive; one often supports the other.

## Description
Most modeling curricula aim for convergence: students revise their models until the class agrees on one shared, canonical representation. This pattern instead treats multiple, non-reconciled model types as the intended end state — students build a physical model (e.g., a sealed biosphere jar), a computational model (a simulation), a diagrammatic model (a food web), and sometimes an embodied model (role-playing organisms) of the *same* phenomenon, and instruction treats each model as revealing something the others don't, rather than ranking them against one canonical standard. Teacher facilitation prompts are correspondingly open-ended ("what can you learn from each model?" "why are we doing both at the same time?") rather than discipline-directed toward a single correct answer.
