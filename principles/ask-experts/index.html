    Ask Experts¶

    Description¶

    Give learners structured access to people with deeper domain expertise so they can ask questions, hear expert reasoning, receive targeted feedback, and compare their own thinking to more mature practice. Expert access is most valuable when it is interactive rather than performative: learners bring a real task, problem, or uncertainty, and the expert responds with explanation, modeling, questioning, and calibrated support.

    Implications¶

    Asking experts works best when the interaction helps learners move from vague confusion to more precise reasoning. The expert's role is not only to provide answers, but to make disciplinary judgment visible, surface key vocabulary and distinctions, and redirect learners toward productive next steps. This is strongest when support is contingent on what the learner actually understands claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning [+M], when the expert prompts learners to explain their own reasoning instead of merely listening to answers claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding [+S], and when the expert gradually hands responsibility back to the learner rather than remaining the permanent problem-solver claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility [~S]. In practice this often overlaps with Coaching, Feedback, Demonstration, and Conceptual Questioning.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Access to a relevant expert, mentor, instructor, practitioner, or more knowledgeable peer who can explain decisions, not just outcomes
    • A concrete learner question, artifact, case, or task; unguided "ask me anything" formats are usually less productive than interaction around real work
    • Psychological safety so learners can reveal uncertainty, partial understanding, or misconceptions without social penalty
    • A mechanism for follow-through such as revision, practice, or reflection; expert advice that is not applied quickly is easily forgotten

    Constraints¶

    • Hard to scale with fidelity in large-group settings; responsive expert interaction is time-intensive and depends on scarce human attention
    • Quality varies with the expert's instructional skill; deep content knowledge alone does not guarantee clear explanation or effective questioning
    • Can produce dependency if experts solve problems for learners instead of diagnosing understanding and fading support claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility [~S]
    • Less effective when learners lack enough background knowledge to ask meaningful questions or interpret expert answers without additional scaffolding
    • Can intimidate learners with low confidence, low status, or limited language proficiency unless the interaction is deliberately structured and supportive

    Target Learners¶

    • Novices or near-novices who need access to expert vocabulary, heuristics, and standards of quality
    • Learners entering a profession, discipline, or authentic practice community where tacit knowledge matters
    • Learners developing confidence in help-seeking, discussion, and disciplinary communication
    • Learners working on complex or ambiguous tasks where feedback on judgment is more useful than answer keys alone
    • Less necessary for advanced learners who can already self-diagnose many errors and may benefit more from independent performance or peer critique

    Target Learning Objectives¶

    • Clarify concepts, terminology, and decision criteria in a domain
    • Improve problem formulation by helping learners ask better, more specific questions
    • Develop metacognitive awareness by comparing novice reasoning to expert reasoning
    • Support transfer on complex tasks through contingent hints, questioning, and feedback rather than answer-giving
    • Build professional or disciplinary identity through contact with authentic practice and standards

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Social constructivist accounts (Vygotsky, Bruner) support expert interaction as a way to extend learner performance through guided dialogue and shared meaning-making
    • Social learning theory (Bandura) supports learning through observation, modeling, and guided imitation of more capable others
    • Cognitive Apprenticeship operationalizes the principle by making expert thinking visible and combining modeling, coaching, scaffolding, and fading
    • Cognitive Load Theory qualifies expert support as most useful when it reduces unproductive search and clarifies decisions for novices

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • Overly directive expert help can suppress generative processing if learners are positioned as passive recipients rather than active explainers
    • The value of expert access depends on contingency; interactive questioning and hints outperform simple explanation for transfer in tutoring studies claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning [+M]
    • Support should fade as competence grows; keeping experts as constant problem-solvers can interfere with independence claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility [~S]

    Claims¶

    • claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning [+M] — expert interaction is more effective when support is responsive to learner understanding, especially through hints and questioning rather than explanation alone
    • claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding [+S] — expert questioning is especially valuable when it prompts learners to explain their own reasoning instead of only listening to answers
    • claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility [~S] — expert involvement should decrease as competence increases so learners assume more responsibility for performance
    • claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices [+M] — experts can improve novice learning by redirecting attention from "getting the right answer" to strategies, criteria, and process

    Related Principles¶

    • Mentoring/Coaching — a broader relationship structure in which expert guidance is sustained over time rather than episodic
    • Communities of Practice — extends expert access into sustained participation in a practice community
    • Guided Practice — turns expert explanation into coached performance on real tasks
    • Explaining Their Thinking — experts are most useful when they elicit learner reasoning rather than replacing it
    • Purposeful Reflection — follow-up reflection helps consolidate what was learned from the interaction and what remains uncertain

    Examples¶

    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — learners observe expert reasoning, attempt similar work, and receive coaching with gradually fading support
    • Leveraging Instructor Office Hours — scheduled access to disciplinary experts for question-driven clarification, feedback, and advice
    • Coaching — one-to-one or small-group expert guidance during performance
    • Demonstration — experts make tacit processes visible before learners attempt the task themselves
    • Clinical rounds, design critiques, code reviews, and studio conferences are common real-world forms of this principle when experts respond directly to learner work
