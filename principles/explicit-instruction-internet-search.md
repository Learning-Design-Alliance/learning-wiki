# Explicit Instruction: Internet Search

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 13 claims (7 for, 6 mixed) · 29 studies (13 causal, 6 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 theoretical, 2 associational, 2 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 29 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one capability: **turning an information need into searches that find relevant material, and revising them when they do not.** For an `adolescent` or `adult` learner who is a `novice` searcher on this kind of task, and whose observed starting response is to type the whole question or one vague word, take the first results, and give up or repeat the same query when results are poor, explicit modelling of a short search routine (frame the question, pull out key terms and alternatives, build the query, scan the results, revise for a stated reason), followed by practice on real inquiry questions with feedback on the queries and revisions themselves, is expected to produce more relevant sources found, more deliberate revisions and less abandonment on new search tasks (`skill-performance`, `near-transfer`) than leaving learners to explore on their own. The target is a `procedure` used with judgment; the old page's reason for teaching it explicitly is that unguided exploration does not show learners what to change, which frustrates them.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship directly.** No claim here compares taught search routines with unguided exploration, or varies how query formulation is taught. The nearest evidence is one single-group study inside a claim about fact-checking instruction: eleventh graders given eight lessons on fact-checkers' strategies improved on a task of finding reliable sources in an open internet search, with no control group ([Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]). The other claims used below are carried from other tasks (writing revision, mathematics and science learning, one-to-one tutoring) and say so where they are used.

**The relationship is conditional on** the kind of search task (a routine look-up needs little; an open, complex question involves a longer process of forming the question as the learner searches, [Task complexity moderates whether users experience the constructive information search process](../claims/task-complexity-moderates-information-search-process.md) [~M]); on the learner's background knowledge of the topic, which supplies the vocabulary for terms and synonyms; on whether the learner's searches can be seen by anyone who can respond to them; and on the tool (a web engine, a library database, a site search, or a generative AI chatbot that learners may wrongly believe is searching the web).

**How this sits beside the converted siblings.** [Evaluating Sources](evaluating-sources.md) owns the next judgment, whether to trust what a search returns, including the off-page (lateral reading) move, its dose and its evidence; this page hands over to it at the point of choosing which results to open and does not repeat its model. [Inquiry-based Learning](inquiry-based-learning.md) holds the investigation in which searching sits. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) holds the practice phase between a model and independent work, and [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) how support is withdrawn; the default design below uses both. [Direct Instruction](direct-instruction.md) holds the general explain–model–guide–hand-over relationship, of which this is one application to a digital skill.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

Each step is a design proposal built from the earlier page's guidance (model turning questions into terms, practise on real inquiry tasks, teach construction and result-checking together, normalise revising a failed search). The evidence status is stated beside each step; none is tested for search instruction as such.

**The routine learners carry (a checklist card).** Four steps, taught in this order and kept on a card or the first lines of a search log; the same card is the checklist for any assessment of the skill. Untested as a routine; built from the earlier page's guidance.

- [ ] **1. Ask:** write what you actually need to know, in one sentence.
- [ ] **2. Pick words:** underline two to four key ideas, drop filler words, and list another word for each (a synonym, a narrower or broader term, the word an expert source would use).
- [ ] **3. Search and scan:** build the query (add quotation marks for a phrase, OR for alternatives, a minus sign to exclude a meaning), then read titles, snippets and who is behind each result before clicking; skip sponsored results.
- [ ] **4. Check and revise:** open the two or three most promising results; if they do not answer the question, say why (too broad, too narrow, wrong words, wrong kind of source), change one thing and search again; keep a source only if you can say why it is trustworthy.

Teaching the steps as the goal, rather than "find a good source", follows [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M], carried from writing revision. Assessment against the card: for a new question, score each step from the learner's log (a stated need; key terms with at least one alternative; a query and a note on the results scanned; at least one revision with a reason, and a reason for each source kept).

**Teaching each step.** Where a brief names its own instruction sequence (for example short explanation, modelling with think-aloud, guided practice, immediate corrective feedback, independent practice), keep that sequence and run each step of the card through it; the steps below are the default when no sequence is named, and map onto that one (steps 2–4 are explanation and modelling, 5–6 guided practice with corrective feedback, 8–10 the release to independent work).

1. **Watch one search before teaching (5–10 minutes).** Give a real question from the learners' current work and ask each learner to find two useful sources for it while logging every query typed, which results they opened, and when they gave up or changed course. This records the starting response. Untested proposal.
2. **Model the routine as a few named steps, thinking aloud (10–15 minutes).** On a shared screen, work one question through: (a) say what you actually need to know; (b) underline the two to four key ideas and drop filler words; (c) list synonyms, narrower and broader terms, and the words an expert source would use; (d) build the query; (e) scan titles, snippets and who is behind each result before clicking, noting sponsored results; (f) say what was wrong with the results and change one thing. Teaching the steps of the process as the goal, rather than "find a good source", follows [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M], carried from high-school writing revision and a theoretical chapter, untested for searching.
3. **Teach a few operators only after the terms (5–10 minutes).** Quotation marks for an exact phrase, an asterisk for word endings where the tool supports it, OR for synonyms, a minus sign or NOT to exclude a meaning, and a site or domain limit, each shown fixing a problem the class has just seen. Do not teach operators as a list on their own: interfaces change, and question quality and vocabulary matter more (the earlier page). Untested proposal.
4. **Model a failed search and its repair.** Run a query that returns poor results, say why (too broad, too narrow, everyday words where the field uses others, the wrong kind of source), change one thing, and compare. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's example of normalising iteration.
5. **Practise on real questions with a search log (20–30 minutes a session).** Learners search for questions from their own subject or inquiry work, keeping a short log: question, terms and alternatives, each query, what was wrong with the results, what they changed and why. Asking for the reason behind each revision draws on [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], carried from explaining worked examples and texts in mathematics and science; untested for searching.
6. **Respond to where the search broke down.** Read the log, not only the sources found: a learner whose terms were everyday words needs help finding the field's vocabulary; one whose query was a whole sentence needs to see the key-term step again; one who never revised needs a failed search modelled; one who found good results but opened only sponsored or first-listed links needs the scanning step. Give the least help that makes the next search work. Basis: [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M], carried from one-to-one tutoring of mathematics and science; untested for searching and at class scale.
7. **Join searching to judging what it finds.** Every practice search ends with a choice of which sources to use and why, so that a fast query that returns misleading sources does not count as success. How to teach the trust judgment is modelled in [Evaluating Sources](evaluating-sources.md); in two one-session comparisons, learners kept relying on domain names and design after training ([One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction in source evaluation did no better than the alternative methods it was compared with in two quasi-experiments](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [~M]), so do not treat a URL ending as the test.
8. **Spread it over several sessions and fade the teacher's part.** A default arc, untested as a whole: **sessions 1–2**, the teacher models the full routine on a new question each time and learners practise with a printed planning sheet (question, key ideas, alternatives, query, what changed) on teacher-chosen questions with known good answers; **sessions 3–4**, pairs search their own inquiry questions with only the revision log, and the teacher reads logs and gives one targeted comment each; **sessions 5 and later**, individual searches on new questions, at least one in a different tool (a library database, another engine, a site search), without the sheet and with help only on request. Plan four to six sessions of 30–50 minutes across two to four weeks, inside a subject task rather than as a stand-alone skills lesson. The only doses on a claim page here are for fact-checking instruction (two 75-minute lessons at university, six 50-minute lessons in high school, eight lessons in eleventh grade; [Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]); the counts above are proposals of the same order.
9. **Move a learner on when the process, not only the result, is sound.** A learner works without the sheet once they find usable sources for two or three consecutive new questions within the time set, with at least one revision they can give a reason for. A learner whose first observed search (step 1) already shows this skips to step 10 or to the evaluation work. Untested threshold; skipping guidance for those who already succeed follows [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M], carried from cognitive-load studies.
10. **Check later, in a new tool.** Two or more weeks after the last session, set a new question in a tool not used in practice and score the log (terms chosen, revisions and reasons) as well as the sources found. Untested proposal.

When a condition is not met: with one session only, teach steps 2 and 4 on one question and give one logged practice search, and say that no lasting change is expected; with no one able to read logs, use worked logs (a model search for the same question, with its revisions and reasons) that learners compare with their own; without open web access, use a library database or a saved set of result pages and teach the same steps on them.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner does now when searching.** A learner who types a whole sentence and takes the first result needs the routine modelled; one who already picks terms and revises needs harder questions and less teaching. If unstated, ask: given a question from their own work, what do they type first, and what do they do when the results are poor?
- **What kind of searching the task needs, and with what tool.** A quick fact, an open research question, a professional database and a chatbot each need different moves. If unstated, ask: what will learners search for after this teaching, with which tool, and what will they do with what they find?
- **Who can see a learner's searches, and how soon.** Feedback on where a search broke down needs someone, or something, to see the queries. If unstated, ask: during practice, who will look at a learner's queries before their next search, and how?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a search under stated conditions: the question given, each query typed, the time between queries, the results opened, the reason given for each change, the sources chosen, and when the learner stopped. "Is information literate", "has search skills" or "is frustrated by searching" are inferred states. A good source found does not show a sound process: it can come from luck, a helpful autocomplete or a single well-known site.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Types the whole question as a sentence and takes the first results | Knows no other way; the engine usually answers sentences well enough; did not see a reason to do more | Give a question where the sentence query returns poor results, then ask for key words; if they pick good terms at once, the routine was absent, not the skill. |
| Repeats the same query, or near-identical ones, then gives up | Lacks alternative vocabulary for the topic; does not know which part of the query to change; frustration or low confidence | Ask for three other words for each key idea; if they cannot, the issue is topic vocabulary, so supply a source of terms; if they can, model changing one part. |
| Uses operators correctly but finds little that is relevant | Operators learned as tricks; the underlying question is unclear; terms are everyday rather than the field's | Ask the learner to say in one sentence what they need to know, then compare their terms with those in a known good source. |
| Finds relevant pages fast but uses an unreliable or sponsored one | Search worked, judgment did not; takes ranking as a sign of quality | Give two results on the same topic with unequal backing and ask which to use and why; route to the evaluation work if the reasons rest on rank or design. |
| Searches well in class, poorly in a new tool or on their own assignment | The routine is tied to the practice tool or the planning sheet; low stakes made it seem not worth the time | Set a matched question in a new tool with and without a one-line prompt to use the routine; compare. |

These rows are untested proposals. Each probe is itself practice in searching, so record what was asked and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares explicit search instruction with unguided exploration, or tests any step of the default design for searching. The claims below test neighbouring relationships and are carried to this one.

- [Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]: three studies of teaching fact-checkers' strategies (a high-school field study with matched controls, a randomised pilot in four university sections, and a single-group study of 68 eleventh graders), none with an effect size. The one result that touches searching is in the last study: after eight lessons, scores rose on three of four tasks, one of which was finding reliable sources in an open internet search; with no comparison group the gain cannot be separated from practice or maturation. The claim is about judging credibility, so it bears on step 7 and on the doses in step 8, not on query formulation. Its recorded evidence (three studies, one at `q3`) would allow a strong marker; it is cited here at moderate because only one uncontrolled study touches searching.
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]: high-school students learning writing revision did better with goals about the strategy than with goals about the final score, and a theoretical chapter synthesises studies (including dart-throwing) to the same effect; the experiment also suggested shifting to outcome goals once the skill is mastered. No effect size printed. It is the basis, carried, for making the search steps the goal early and the result the goal later; it is not a study of searching.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports found a moderate average benefit of self-explanation prompts (g = .55) across task types, subjects and levels; a mathematics meta-analysis finds evidence after a delay or in classrooms much more limited. It is the basis, carried, for asking a reason for each revision; no study here tests reason-giving during searching, and a learner without the topic vocabulary may explain a poor revision confidently.
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]: fully contingent one-to-one tutoring of fourth and fifth graders in long division beat fixed, partly contingent and no support at immediate and one-month tests (8 per condition); a meta-analysis ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching; a review notes most evidence is one-to-one. Its studies table gathers 20 distinct studies across the claim and 17 narrower ones. It supports responding to where a learner's search broke down, carried from mathematics and science; its ranking of explicit strategy training first is consistent with modelling the routine before coaching it.
- [Task complexity moderates whether users experience the constructive information search process](../claims/task-complexity-moderates-information-search-process.md) [~M]: a theoretical paper reports that workers sought information straightforwardly in routine tasks but went through a process of construction and formulation in tasks they saw as complex, which took longer. It qualifies the relationship: a full routine with a log suits open questions, and for routine look-ups it may be overhead. Workers, not learners; no instruction tested.
- [One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction in source evaluation did no better than the alternative methods it was compared with in two quasi-experiments](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [~M]: **limits the model.** In one-shot library sessions with first-year business and nursing students, a checklist and an alternative method did not differ on quizzes or accuracy, both gave modest gains, and learners in both groups kept leaning on domain names and design. It concerns evaluation rather than searching, but it is the only evidence here on one-session information-skills teaching, and it suggests that a single session of explicit teaching changes habits only partly, whatever is taught. It also warns that search results judged by surface cues stay misleading however good the query.

Learners, tasks and settings differ across these claims (writing revision, mathematics tutoring, workers' information seeking, credibility judgments), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures search performance, and none follows learners beyond a month for searching.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually that, given a new question in an unfamiliar tool, the learner can find relevant, usable sources in reasonable time and say why each revision was made, weeks after teaching. Elicit separately what the learner wants from searching: an answer to a question that matters to them now, an assignment finished with acceptable sources, less time wasted, or not feeling lost in front of others. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values a fast answer may stop at the first plausible result however well they can search; that is a choice to discuss, not a missing skill.

For example, an adult in a returning-to-study course may want to find out whether they qualify for a childcare grant, while the course objective is locating academic sources for an essay. Using the grant question as the first modelled search serves the learner's goal and teaches the routine; the essay search in a library database is then the check of transfer to the course objective. Record both, so that success on the grant search is not read as the capability the essay needs.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of taught search routines with unguided exploration, on matched learners and new search tasks, show no difference in sources found, revisions or abandonment, or a gain that disappears within weeks or in a new tool. If teaching operators alone matches the full routine, the routine is overhead. If asking for reasons slows learners without improving their searches, drop the log for all but the hardest tasks. If generative tools that answer directly make query revision rare in learners' own work, the target should move from building queries to checking what an answer rests on. Do not protect the model by calling every failure too little practice or too little background knowledge after the fact.

Finding relevant sources on a practice question, choosing trustworthy ones, searching well in a new tool, and searching well weeks later in the learner's own work are separate claims. The evidence here reaches none of them directly for searching; it establishes no number of sessions, no routine and no hand-over criterion.

## Description
Teaching learners how to effectively search the internet is critical for helping them learn how to find accurate and relevant information and aids in developing information literacy. Learners need to learn to draw upon their Background Knowledge to frame a good research question, generate associated vocabulary, and create a search string that will bring up relevant information. Since an internet search requires sorting through extensive pieces of information, this task can be heavy on learners' Working Memory. Teaching adult learners search skills such as utilizing quotation marks, asterisks, and connecting words can help alleviate that load, as well as build a Learner Mindset for research. This skill is especially important to teach explicitly since just letting the learners explore does not help them make the mental connections necessary to search effectively, and this can be frustrating for them.

## Implications
Explicit instruction in internet search is valuable because searching is a compound process, not a single skill. Learners have to translate a question into terms, judge results, revise queries, and manage credibility signals all at once, which is why novice searchers benefit from visible routines for query design and revision [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M] and chunked decision-making rather than one opaque “search better” demand [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. The design goal is not to teach search tricks in isolation, but to help learners search more deliberately and reflect on why a search succeeded or failed [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] so the skill transfers across tools and interfaces. Search instruction also improves when feedback responds to where the learner’s search logic actually broke down [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit modeling of how to turn questions into search terms, refine queries, and interpret results
- Practice tasks where learners actually search, compare results, and revise strategies
- Search goals tied to real inquiry tasks so learners can judge relevance, not just mechanics
- Guidance on both search construction and result evaluation; the two are inseparable in practice

#### Constraints
- Search instruction can become tool-fragmented if it focuses on operator trivia without linking to question quality and source evaluation
- Interfaces change over time, so durable principles matter more than memorizing one platform layout
- Learners may overtrust the first result or sponsored results unless this is explicitly addressed
- Searching itself imposes cognitive load because learners must juggle terms, intent, and credibility at once

### Target Learners
- Learners with limited search experience or weak digital-literacy foundations
- Adult learners returning to formal study, online work, or information-heavy environments
- Learners who can state a need in everyday language but struggle to translate it into effective search behavior
- Learners doing research, problem solving, or civic information tasks online

### Target Learning Objectives
- Formulate better search queries from information needs
- Refine searches iteratively when initial results are weak or off-target
- Distinguish relevant from irrelevant or low-quality results
- Reduce frustration and search abandonment by giving learners concrete routines for improvement

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Information-processing perspectives support breaking search into manageable sub-processes: question framing, term selection, result scanning, and refinement
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) supports explicit routines and chunked instruction because search requires juggling many competing signals
- Self-regulated-learning perspectives support planning, monitoring, and revising search behavior rather than treating search as a one-shot act
- Instructional models that emphasize guided demonstration and practice fit well because search expertise is often tacit unless modeled

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Learners do need exploratory searching, but exploration is more productive after explicit modeling of useful search moves
- Search skill without source evaluation is incomplete; good queries can still return misleading sources
- Operators and tricks matter less than question quality, vocabulary choice, and iterative revision
