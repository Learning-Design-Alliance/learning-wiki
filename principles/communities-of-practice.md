# Communities of Practice

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 32 claims (24 for, 7 mixed, 1 against) · 28 studies (6 causal, 6 qualitative, 5 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 3 theoretical, 2 design), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 28 report an effect size · 29 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For learners who are entering a shared practice (mostly `adult`, in the `adult-workplace` or `postsecondary` band: teachers in a professional learning community, students in an advanced laboratory, members of an online professional community), and whose starting response is limited or peripheral participation in that practice, sustained participation alongside more experienced members is proposed to change three things: how they take part (the norms of talk and contribution they use), their understanding of the practice's content, and their ability to carry out the practice itself. The knowledge type is a `complex-skill` together with the practice's tacit norms and vocabulary; the setting is `workplace-clinical`, `classroom` or `online-instructor-led`; and the duration is `term-plus`. Membership alone is not the activity: what is expected to matter is active contribution to shared work, norms that make critique safe, and a shared object the community is working on.

Three conditions limit this. Norms take time to form, so a short assignment is unlikely to reproduce the relationship. A community's stated aim and what its members actually learn often diverge, so the outcome must be observed rather than assumed from the design. And the model's newcomer-to-centre trajectory can mark learners who do not or will not move toward the centre as failures, which is a harm to design against, not a learner deficit. No pattern page instantiates this principle as a conditional model yet; [Fostering Communities of Learning](../patterns/fostering-communities-of-learning-fcl.md) is the nearest pattern. The withdrawal of expert support as members gain competence is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) and the cognitive apprenticeship pattern it links; this page does not repeat it. [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) holds the conditional model for structured group work within a course, which is shorter and more bounded than the practice this page concerns.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The page's earlier guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and none of it has been tested as written.

1. **Name a shared object of practice.** Give the group a real problem space, profession or recurring task to work on, not a topic to discuss. Without one the group becomes a discussion forum. Evidence: the mathematics professional-learning-community review describes directing attention towards an object as one category of productive norms (see below); otherwise untested, from the earlier page.
2. **Schedule recurring interaction over a term or more.** One-off discussion does not build norms. Evidence: the same review reports that productive norms develop over time, and one laboratory case describes exchanges deepening between week 1 and week 8; both descriptive.
3. **Build trust norms before asking for critique.** Make explicit that criticism is directed at practices, not people, and establish open communication before inquiry and disagreement. Evidence: the review's report that trust norms act as prerequisites for critical inquiry; descriptive, not a tested sequence.
4. **Design entry points at manageable levels of participation.** Give newcomers specific contributions (reporting a case, reviewing an artifact, asking a briefing question) before more central roles, and coach them responsively. Untested here; from the earlier page. Online communities in particular are argued to need deliberate design and scaffolding through teaching and assessment activities rather than emerging on their own: [Online learning communities do not simply emerge; they must be designed for and scaffolded through teaching and assessment activities](../claims/online-communities-must-be-designed-and-scaffolded.md) [+W] (one case study, the authors' interpretive conclusion; not yet checked).
5. **Ask for reasoning, not only resources.** Prompt members to articulate and compare how they solved a problem, and to apply, test and revise ideas in their own practice. Untested here; from the earlier page.
6. **Make non-central participation legitimate.** Decide in advance what a valued contribution from someone who stays peripheral looks like, so the community does not read every member who does not move to the centre as failing. Argued, not tested (see the limiting claim below).
7. **Observe the outcome you intended, and the ones you did not.** Record changes in practice and understanding against the community's stated aim, since the two often diverge.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is participation under stated conditions: who posts, speaks or contributes, how long and how detailed exchanges are, what artifacts are shared, what members say they changed in their own practice, and what the practice itself looks like afterwards. "Has become a full participant", "holds the community's tacit knowledge" and "belongs" are inferred states. Silence, brevity and deference can each reflect several different states, and a self-report of learning is not an observation of changed practice.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A newcomer attends but rarely contributes | Lacks the practice's vocabulary or a manageable entry point; norms do not yet make contribution safe; values the community for observation alone; time or workload limits | Offer a specific bounded contribution (report one case, review one artifact) and, separately, ask what the member wants from the community; see which, if either, changes the response. |
| Exchanges stay brief and superficial | Norms for how long an enquiry may last are not yet negotiated; no shared object to discuss; members lack expertise to offer each other | Track exchange length and depth over several weeks on the same interaction type; give a shared problem and see whether depth changes. |
| Members trade resources but rarely critique one another | Trust norms not yet established; critique read as personal; no expectation of articulating reasoning | Ask for reasoning on a common case, with critique framed at the practice, and record whether disagreement appears and is taken up. |
| Members report learning, but practice outside the community looks unchanged | Learning of a different kind than intended (norms, not practice); the self-report reflects belonging, not change; the change is real but not yet transferred | Compare the community's stated object with an observation of practice (a lesson, a review, a work product) before and after, recording unintended outcomes. |
| Experienced members dominate | Expertise is concentrated; norms reward speed or status; newcomers' contributions are not recognised as legitimate | Record who speaks and whose contributions are taken up; rotate roles and see whether participation spreads. |

These are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence between explanations; it does not identify a cause, and probing a community changes it.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as stated: none compares learners who take part in a community of practice with comparable learners who do not, on a measure of practice or understanding. Every claim below is a review of descriptive studies, a single qualitative case, a perception survey or an argument, and none has yet been checked against its source.

- [Participation in mathematics PLC changed teachers' collaboration norms, understanding of mathematics and its teaching, and ability to design and carry out teaching](../claims/plc-participation-changes-teacher-norms-understanding-practice.md) [+W]: a systematic review of mathematics professional learning communities (`synthesis-mixed`, `review · r2`, `q2`) synthesises outcome descriptions across its studies: teachers changed collaboration norms, understanding of mathematics and its teaching, and their ability to design and carry out teaching, including leaving more of the talking space to their students. No effect size and no comparison group are reported on the claim page; it describes what participants changed, not what participation caused. Teachers only, mathematics only.
- [Productive collaboration norms in mathematics PLC fall into three categories, with trust norms acting as prerequisites for critical inquiry, and develop over time](../claims/plc-norms-three-categories-trust-prerequisite.md) [~M]: the same review, in schools where collaboration norms were established, groups productive norms as supporting participation and critical inquiry, supporting open communication and trust, and directing attention towards an object, with trust norms as prerequisites for critical inquiry; several studies concluded that productive norms develop over time. It qualifies the relationship: the community's effect is conditional on norms that take time to build. Descriptive synthesis, not a tested order.
- [Reported PLC outcomes frequently do not correspond with the stated object; complete object–outcome correspondence appeared in only three studies](../claims/plc-object-outcome-mismatch.md) [~M]: across the review's 32 studies, only three showed complete correspondence between what the community set out to work on and what was reported as its outcome; six had non-corresponding outcomes and eight had additional unintended outcomes. It limits the relationship: a community's design does not predict which learning it produces, so the outcome must be observed.
- [Active participation in a community has a more significant impact on learning than mere membership](../claims/active-participation-beats-mere-membership-for-learning.md) [+W]: a mixed-methods perception survey of 28 honours students (`correlational`, `associational · r1`, `q2`); the contrast between active participation and membership is the author's interpretation of perception data, not a tested comparison between groups. It supports the page's emphasis on contribution over membership only as students' perceptions.
- [The quality of inter-group interactions shifted from brief, superficial exchanges to long, detailed, collaborative discussions as community norms were negotiated](../claims/adlab-interaction-quality-shifted-from-superficial-to-collaborative.md) [+W]: a micro-ethnographic analysis (`qualitative · r3`, `q2`) of an advanced physics laboratory course compares a week-1 briefing exchange with a week-8 exchange of the same type, which had become long and detailed with expertise shared. One course, one pair of episodes; it shows that norms of interaction changed over a semester, not that learning outcomes did.
- [Within the CoP model, learners who do not progress toward the centre through nonparticipation are relegated to the fringes with invalidated identities](../claims/cop-nonparticipation-invalidates-learner-identities.md) [-W]: a theoretical critique (`argument`, `theoretical · r2`, `q1`) of the model as applied in second-language acquisition, arguing that its newcomer/oldtimer binary limits the identities available, and that learners who may not, cannot or choose not to move toward the centre are negatively interpreted. No data. It bears on how a designer reads non-central participation, not on whether communities produce learning.

Taken together: the wiki's evidence describes what members of established communities report changing, that productive norms take time and rest on trust, and that outcomes often differ from aims; it does not predict an effect size, a horizon at which change appears, or that a community outperforms other professional learning. How far each may be carried: the three review claims are mathematics teachers in professional learning communities, so they transport to other teacher communities more readily than to students or to online networks; the laboratory case is one physics course; the survey is one honours community's perceptions. None is evidence about online communities of practice in particular, which the page's key source (a systematic review of adult learning in online communities of practice) concerns but which no claim page yet records.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective names the practice the community is meant to change and how that change will be observed: a teaching move, a diagnostic routine, a code review standard, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what each member wants from the community: help with an immediate problem, colleagues, a credential, a professional identity, or simply to watch how others work. Agreement and divergence are both observations, and a member who values observation is not thereby failing.

For example, in a teacher inquiry group the designer's objective may be that teachers leave more talking space to students in mathematics lessons, while a newly qualified teacher may value the group mainly for reassurance and practical lesson resources. Attendance and a positive self-report serve the teacher's goal; only an observation of lessons, compared with the group's stated aim, tests the designer's. Recording both keeps a sense of belonging from being read as a change in practice, and keeps a member who wanted resources from being read as disengaged.

## What would revise this model?

The model should weaken if comparisons of comparable learners who do and do not take part in a structured community show no difference in observed practice or understanding at a term-or-longer horizon, or if structured entry points and trust norms make no difference to newcomers' participation. If outcomes keep diverging from stated aims, the model should say plainly that a community's learning cannot be designed toward a target, only observed. If non-central members turn out to learn as much as central ones by other measures, the trajectory toward the centre should stop being treated as the indicator of learning. Do not protect the model by calling every unchanged practice a sign that norms have not yet formed.

Changed participation norms, changed understanding, changed practice and a member's sense of professional identity are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how long a community needs, what size or mix of expertise works, whether online communities behave like co-located ones, or that communities outperform other forms of professional learning.

## Description
Communities of practice are groups that deepen learning through sustained participation around a shared domain, problem space, or profession. Members do not just exchange information; they build shared language, norms, tools, and standards of practice over time. Learning happens through contribution, observation, feedback, and increasingly central participation in the work of the community.

## Implications
Communities of practice support learning because people often learn complex work by participating with others who are already doing it, not by studying rules in isolation. Newcomers gain access to tacit knowledge, language, tools, and standards through recurring contribution, explanation, and feedback, and communities become more educative when members articulate and compare reasoning rather than only exchange resources [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. They also become more capable as responsibility gradually shifts toward fuller participation [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S], ideally through authentic shared work that stays tied to integrated performance rather than isolated drills [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+S]. The design challenge is that communities can easily become exclusionary or vague, so entry points, contribution norms, and authentic shared work have to be deliberately structured, with responsive guidance for newcomers as they enter the practice [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared domain of interest or practice that gives the group a real reason to convene
- Recurring interaction over time, not one-off discussion
- Norms and structures that support contribution, feedback, and knowledge sharing
- Opportunities for newcomers to participate at manageable levels before taking on more central roles

#### Constraints
- Communities can become exclusionary or dominated by experts unless participation norms are intentionally designed
- Without a shared purpose or recurring activity, the group becomes a discussion forum rather than a practice community
- Informal knowledge sharing alone is insufficient if there is no mechanism for applying, testing, or revising ideas in practice
- Healthy communities take time to build; they are difficult to force through short-term assignments alone

### Target Learners
- Adult and professional learners building identity in a field, trade, or role
- Learners who benefit from ongoing peer and expert interaction rather than isolated coursework
- Newcomers to a practice who need access to tacit norms, vocabulary, tools, and examples
- Learners whose motivation and persistence increase when they feel they belong to a meaningful community

### Target Learning Objectives
- Develop participation in authentic disciplinary or workplace practices
- Build shared language, standards, and repertoires for solving recurring problems
- Increase help-seeking, peer teaching, and collaborative problem-solving
- Support professional identity formation and long-term learning beyond a single course

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Situated-learning theory strongly supports communities of practice: learning occurs through participation in authentic social practice, including legitimate peripheral participation
- Social constructivist perspectives support knowledge development through dialogue, shared tools, and co-construction of meaning
- Apprenticeship-oriented perspectives support the movement from novice observation to guided participation to fuller contribution
