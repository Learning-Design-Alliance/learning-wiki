Understanding must be cultivated through exploration, not described or told¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 21 claims (12 for, 5 mixed, 4 against) · 25 studies (7 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 4 design, 3 qualitative, 3 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 25 report an effect size · 16 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns how a teacher guides while learners explore a new idea: by clues, questions and critique that work on the learners' own attempts, or by stating the principle and explaining it. For a learner meeting a concept or principle (in the source studio, design principles such as balance, rhythm or hierarchy; elsewhere, an explanatory idea in a discussion) who already knows enough to make and talk about attempts, the proposed relationship is: a period of exploration in which the teacher sets a problem with a clue, asks questions that sharpen the learner's own partial idea, and leads a critique of everyone's work, followed by naming the principle with the learners' own works as the cases, is expected to produce better conceptual-understanding and transfer to new cases (near-transfer) than either being told the principle first and then practising it, or exploring with no guidance. It is not expected to help with facts to be remembered, with procedures the learner has never seen, or with domain-general skills such as designing a fair test.

No claim in the wiki tests this page's relationship as such: none compares indirect, clue-and-question guidance with direct explanation of the same principle in a studio or discussion, on an outcome measured afterwards. What is recorded is (a) qualitative episodes in which a teacher's guiding question let students integrate ideas while a teacher's interjected explanation shut their talk down, (b) one small tutoring study in which prompting without explaining taught as well as explaining, and (c) meta-analyses showing that exploration with guidance beats other instruction, unguided exploration loses to explicit instruction, and problem solving before instruction helps conceptual learning only when instruction follows. That last finding revises the earlier page: the evidence recorded here supports exploring before being told and being guided by questions, not never being told. The principle is still named, explicitly, after the exploration and from the learners' work.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's prior knowledge of the target (guidance matters most for novice learners and its advantage recedes with high prior knowledge), on the kind of knowledge (concepts and principles, not domain-general skills or facts), on age (problem-first sequences reversed for second to fifth graders), on whether anyone can ask the questions during the exploration, and on the horizon.

How the converted siblings sit beside this page: Productive Failure owns whether a new concept is attempted or taught first; this page assumes the attempt comes first where that page's conditions hold and adds how the teacher talks during it. Inquiry-Based Learning owns guided against unguided inquiry. Direct Instruction owns explanation, modelling and guided practice, which is the right default where this page's conditions fail. Error Analysis owns using errors as the object of study, which this page's critique step draws on. Peer Discussion owns discussion after an individual commitment. Technology should augment, not supplant, learning processes applies the same division to a tool that could give the answer.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (an architecture basic-design studio running six hours a week over a 14-week term), kept as a concrete default and corrected where the evidence disagrees. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Open with a problem and a clue, not the principle. Each week, give a clue drawn from another discipline (a musical rhythm, a biological pattern, a typographic grid) and a design problem it bears on; learners first analyse the clue (what is it made of, how are its parts organised) and then make their own composition. In a six-hour studio: about 30 minutes on the clue, in pairs. Untested proposal, from the earlier page and its clues strategy; the one observation recorded is that students later used the clues they found when explaining their designs (Students exemplified discovered clues when explaining their designs orally [+W]).
  2. Give real time to make attempts. About two to two and a half hours of the six-hour session for making, with at least two versions of the composition. Exploration with guidance is supported in general (Enhanced discovery learning produces greater learning than other forms of instruction [+M]); the minutes are an untested proposal.
  3. Guide by questions on the learner's own idea, not by explanation. Circulate during making; wait until a learner has a position, then ask one specific question that sharpens something the learner has already half-said ("what is holding the left side down?"), and confirm the right term when a learner reaches for it, without adding the explanation. Do not interrupt a group's reasoning to state the principle. Basis: in recorded small-group episodes a teacher's guiding question let students' own partial idea become the shared concept, while a teacher who interjected disciplinary content was resisted and the talk stopped being dialogic (Subtle teacher guidance, not imposition, enables interdisciplinary integration in small-group deliberation [+W]); in one tutoring study, prompting without explaining taught as well as explaining (Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback [+M]). Avoid closing remarks ("fine", "OK, next") that end a learner's line of thought before it is finished (A 1970 teacher self-analysis programme asserts, without data, that teacher responses including verbal rewards can close off pupil thinking [+W]).
  4. Critique every work, strengths and weaknesses together. Pin up all compositions; for each, the class says what works and what does not, against the week's principle, without sorting works into good and bad and without a single "right" answer. Treat a weak composition as data about the principle. About 90 minutes for a studio of 20 to 25. Untested as a studio routine, from the earlier page; comparing correct with incorrect examples is supported for concepts in mathematics (Erroneous examples improve conceptual understanding by forcing comparison with correct models. [+M], carried here from decimal magnitude with children to design critique with adults). See Error Analysis.
  5. Each learner explains their own work aloud. Two or three minutes each during the critique: what the clue was, what they did with it, and why. Untested; from the earlier page; the observation in step 1 is the only record ([+W] above). See Self-Explanation.
  6. Then name the principle, from their work. In the last 20 to 30 minutes, state the principle explicitly and its usual name, pointing to the pinned-up works that show it and to those that break it. This replaces the earlier page's "avoid direct narration": in the evidence recorded here, problem solving before instruction helps conceptual learning when instruction follows and builds on the learners' solutions (Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]; PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features (contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions) are implemented [~M]), and exploration with no guidance learns less than explicit instruction (Explicit instruction produces greater learning than unassisted discovery learning under most conditions [-M]).
  7. Check on a new case, without the clue. End the week, or open the next, with a short task the clue does not cover: a new composition to judge or adjust, explained in a sentence or a sketch. Untested proposal; it shows whether the principle transfers beyond the clue that carried it.
  8. Hand over the questioning across the term. Weeks 1 to 4: a strong clue close to the principle, the teacher asks most questions, and names the principle at the end of every session. Weeks 5 to 9: looser clues; learners lead the critique with a short question card ("what holds this together? what breaks it?") and the teacher adds one question per work. Weeks 10 to 14: learners choose or bring their own clues, critique one another's work, and name the principle themselves; the teacher corrects only what is wrong. A learner moves to the looser stage when they can name and use the week's principle on a new case unaided in two consecutive weeks. The arc and its threshold are untested proposals; that guidance matters less as prior knowledge grows is a review's statement (The advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge to provide internal guidance [~M]).
  9. Tell first when the conditions fail. If the target is a procedure learners have never seen (a drawing convention, software operation), a domain-general skill, or the learners are young children, explain and model first and let them explore afterwards (For domain-general skills, one meta-analysis finds a negligible effect (g = −0.17) in favour of I-PS over PS-I [-M]; the age reversal in the productive-failure claim above). See Direct Instruction.

When a condition is not met: if nobody can ask questions during the exploration (self-paced online work), build the questions into the task as prompts, and show contrasting example works with a short explanation after the learner's attempt; if the session is short, keep the order (attempt, critique, name, check) and shrink each part.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learner already knows about this target, and what kind of target it is. Clue-led exploration needs learners who can make attempts and talk about them, and a concept or principle to find; a procedure never seen, a domain-general skill or a set of facts is better told first. If unstated, ask: can learners already produce something, however rough, in this domain, and is the target an idea to understand or a step sequence to follow?
  • Who can ask the questions during exploration, and how often. The evidence for guiding by questions comes from teachers sitting with small groups and from one-to-one tutoring; with one teacher for many groups, or none, the questions must be prepared in advance or built into the task. If unstated, ask: during making, who will see each learner's or group's attempt and talk with them, and for how long?
  • Whether the principle will be named after the exploration, and what outcome counts. Exploring before being told helps conceptual understanding when instruction follows; it does not help procedural fluency. If unstated, ask: what must the learner be able to do at the end (judge a new case, explain why, produce work), and when?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: the attempt (each version of the work), what the learner says about it when asked, which clue or term they use, what question the teacher asked and what the learner did next, and the learner's handling of a new case. "Has understood the principle" and "is copying the clue" are inferred states. A fluent oral explanation shows the learner can talk about this work; it does not show they can use the principle on a case the clue did not carry.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares clue-and-question guidance with direct explanation of the same principle on a later outcome. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

  • Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]: its table gathers 5 distinct studies (q2–q4). A meta-analysis of 164 studies found explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (580 comparisons) and enhanced discovery (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) beat other instruction (360 comparisons); an experiment with 112 third- and fourth-graders found more learned the control-of-variables strategy from direct instruction, and those taught directly transferred as well as the few who discovered it. It qualifies the earlier page: exploration helps when guided, and being told is not worse for transfer in that experiment. The claim page notes that no entry compares guided with pure discovery head to head.
  • Enhanced discovery learning produces greater learning than other forms of instruction [+M] and Explicit instruction produces greater learning than unassisted discovery learning under most conditions [-M]: the two halves of that meta-analysis on their own pages (d = 0.30 for enhanced discovery; d = −0.38 against unassisted discovery), both with high heterogeneity. The second is the limiting claim: exploration with no guidance is the configuration that fails.
  • Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]: its table gathers 4 distinct studies (q2–q4). Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction followed by problem solving on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), not on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03); the advantage reversed for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. It supports exploring before being told, and it is why the default design ends with explicit naming: in every comparison the instruction follows.
  • PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features (contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions) are implemented [~M]: a review concluding that problem solving before instruction helps only with contrasting cases or instruction built on the students' own solutions. It is the basis for naming the principle from the pinned-up works rather than in the abstract.
  • Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback [+M]: eighth graders, circulatory system, 11 tutor–student pairs per study; tutors told to withhold explanations and only prompt produced learning as good as ordinary tutoring. The nearest test of "asking instead of telling", but one-to-one, small and quasi-experimental, and it shows equal, not better, learning.
  • Subtle teacher guidance, not imposition, enables interdisciplinary integration in small-group deliberation [+W]: 150 Grade 7 students in four Israeli schools; narrative analysis of recorded small-group discussions. A guiding question on a student's own partial recall led to integration; a teacher's interjected explanation was resisted and the talk stopped being dialogic. The authors report the hindrance pattern as the more frequent, without a count, and no comparison condition.
  • A 1970 teacher self-analysis programme asserts, without data, that teacher responses including verbal rewards can close off pupil thinking [+W] and Dialogic teacher support cultivates all four aspects of statistical modeling practice over a multi-week unit [+W]: the first is a 1970 teacher-training document's assertion with no data; the second a single sixth-grade classroom (28 students, 15 lessons) in which nearly every episode of the modelling practice included explicit dialogic teacher support. The second is a reminder that dialogic guidance is still explicit support, not withdrawal.
  • Students exemplified discovered clues when explaining their designs orally [+W]: the source studio's only observation (14-week trial, 113 students): students used the clues they had found when explaining their designs. It shows the clues were used, not that understanding was deeper than under explanation. The earlier page marked it [+M]; it is lowered here because the claim reports an observation with no outcome measure.

Learners, domains and outcomes differ across these claims (children learning a science strategy, adolescents in deliberation, eighth graders with tutors, meta-analyses across domains), so do not rank them by effect label. None is from a design studio except the last, and none measures delayed retention of understanding gained by exploring against understanding gained by being told.

The evidence by question¶

  • Should learners explore a new idea with guidance, rather than be told it or left to find it alone? Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]
  • Should learners attempt a problem before the idea is taught, and what must the teaching afterwards do? Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Student Centered Environments Assumptions Framework [+W]: the theory page for the source of the earlier page's central statement (Land & Hannafin 1996, "Wisdom cannot be 'told'"). It is a theoretical synthesis (q1), not a test; the earlier page marked it [+M], lowered here to its recorded evidence. It bears on the page as its origin, not as evidence for it.
  • Positioning personal experience as an epistemic resource supports expansion from inward to outward critical orientations [+W]: one design-based study, two 12th-grade English classes (69 students), no comparison condition. It bears on the page only as a way of starting exploration from learners' own experience (used in the adult row above), not on clue-led guidance.
  • Invention Tasks Prepare Future Learning [+M]: ninth graders who invented a statistics procedure before a worked example transferred better than tell-and-practice students only when a learning resource was present at test. Bears on exploring before being told; a narrower member of the productive-failure question.
  • Age moderates discovery-learning effects: the explicit-instruction advantage over unassisted discovery is larger for adolescents than adults, and the enhanced-discovery benefit larger for adults than children [~M]: a moderator analysis of the discovery meta-analysis; used in the age rows above.
  • Critical Temper Weakest On Basic Prejudices [+W]
  • Objections Stage Most Difficult Crito [+W]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually that learners can see and use a principle in new work, explain why, and later work without the clue. Elicit separately what learners want: to produce a work they are proud of, to pass the jury, to be told what the teacher wants, to avoid being exposed in critique. A learner who values a good mark may ask for the "right answer" and read questions as withholding; one who values their own voice may resist the closing explanation. Agreement and divergence are both observations, and a request to "just tell us" is information about the learner's goal, not only about their understanding.

For example, a first-year architecture student may want to make a striking model for the end-of-term jury, while the studio's objective is that they can explain the principle of hierarchy in any composition. A question-led critique serves the objective; letting them name what made their model striking, and then naming the principle from it, serves both. Record both, so that a good jury result is not read as the understanding the course was aiming at.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of question-led against explanation-led guidance during the same exploration, with comparable learners and an outcome on new cases, show no advantage, or show that the advantage disappears at a delay. If teachers prepared to ask questions still mostly interject explanations (as the recorded episodes suggest unprepared teachers do), the model becomes a teacher-preparation problem before it is a learner one. If naming the principle at the end adds nothing beyond critique, drop step 6; if naming it first and then critiquing matches the default, the order matters less than the critique. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too much telling" after the fact.

Understanding shown in an oral explanation of one's own work, judgement of a new case, independent design work later in the course, and lasting understanding are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no amount of exploration time, no number of clues and no hand-over criterion.
