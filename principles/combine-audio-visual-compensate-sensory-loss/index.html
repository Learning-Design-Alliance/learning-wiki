Compensate for age-related sensory impairments by combining audio input with visual presentation¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 20 claims (9 for, 11 mixed) · 14 studies (5 review, 3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 associational), q1–q3 · 3 of 14 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one access decision: how to present new material so that a learner who hears or sees less well than the teaching assumes still receives it. For an adult learner, often an older one, whose hearing or eyesight has declined (speech is hard to follow in background noise, small or low-contrast print is hard to read), presenting each new item through both channels at once (spoken, and also written or shown), in a room with good light on faces and materials and little outside noise, with the learner able to replay or ask for repetition, is expected to let the learner take in more of the new material (immediate-performance, conceptual-understanding) than speech alone in an ordinary room. The relationship comes from Schleppegrell's 1987 digest on older language learners, which recommends compensating for visual or auditory impairments "by combining audio input with visual presentation of new material, good lighting, and elimination of outside noise", and notes that hearing and vision problems are not restricted to older learners.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship with older learners or with learners who have a hearing or vision loss. The digest is a recommendation, not a study. What the wiki holds is evidence on neighbouring comparisons with other learners: the same words spoken and written against spoken only, mostly with postsecondary students (a meta-analysis of verbal redundancy); captions on second-language video; how pacing and pictures change those results; and the modality effect, which favours spoken over printed words beside a picture for learners who can hear. The closest of these supports the core move: spoken–written presentations beat spoken-only ones, and the authors explain the advantage under system pacing as the chance to recover information misperceived from one source. That is the situation of a learner who misses words in noise, but it was not measured with such learners. The design below is therefore a labelled default.

The relationship is conditional on four things a page cannot settle in advance:

  • Which channel the learner can rely on. Adding print helps a learner who can read it; for a learner with low vision, the visual half must be enlarged or replaced, and for a learner with low literacy, pictures, objects and demonstration carry the visual half.
  • Whether a picture also needs the eyes. With no picture, spoken plus written words did no harm and helped over speech alone; with images or animation in the presentation the advantage disappeared, and for hearing learners narration beside a picture beats printed words.
  • Who controls the pace. The written-plus-spoken advantage appeared in system-paced material and not in learner-paced material, where a learner can relisten or reread.
  • Whether listening is the goal. If the goal is to understand speech without print (conversation, a second-language listening skill), print is a support to be reduced, not a permanent route.

How the converted siblings sit beside this page: Redundancy owns the general decision whether to present the same words twice and when it hurts; this page applies its no-picture finding to learners who miss part of one channel, and does not repeat its model. Universal Design for Learning holds the planning audit that finds a sensory demand outside the goal; this page is one route that audit can call for. Multimodal Instruction holds the wider choice of modes and an alternative mode where one is inaccessible; Text-to-Speech holds the reverse route (speech added to print for a learner whose barrier is reading).

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Find out privately what each learner hears and sees, in the first session. Take two or three minutes, offered to everyone so no one is singled out: can you read this handout and the board from your seat; can you follow me when I speak at a normal voice from the front, and when I turn away; do you use glasses or a hearing aid, and does it work in this room? Record the answers and ask again after a few sessions. Untested proposal.
  2. Fix the room before teaching. Light the teacher's face and the materials from the front, without glare on the board or screen; close doors and windows to outside noise and turn off fans or machines that are not needed; seat learners who report difficulty near the front with a clear view of the speaker's face; the teacher faces the group when speaking and does not talk while writing on the board. From the earlier page ("good lighting, and elimination of outside noise"); untested for hearing. A school-facilities review reports that lighting and the physical environment affect learning (Physical environment elements affect learning [+W]), not for this population.
  3. Every new item spoken is also shown. Write each new word, phrase, name, number and instruction on the board or a handout as you say it, in large, high-contrast print (a proposal: at least 18-point on handouts, letters several centimetres high on the board, dark on light); for audio or video, turn on captions or give a transcript. Basis: spoken–written presentations outperform spoken-only presentations, though not written-only ones [+M] (g = 0.29 against spoken-only, g = −0.04 against written-only; mostly postsecondary students without a recorded sensory loss); captioning second-language videos [+M] (second-language learners; g = 0.99 comprehension, 0.87 vocabulary).
  4. When the learner must also study a picture, show key words, not a full script. With a diagram, map or demonstration in view, put only the key terms beside it and give the full text on paper to read afterwards, so the eyes are not split between the picture and a block of print. Basis: the spoken–written advantage disappears when images or animation are included [~M]; duplicated on-screen text can hurt learning beside a diagram [~S]. Neither was tested with learners who hear poorly, for whom the printed key words may be the only reliable verbal channel.
  5. Give the learner control of the pace. Speak a little slower, pause after each new item, repeat learners' questions aloud for the group, and offer recordings and printed copies the learner can replay or reread at home. Basis: the spoken–written advantage appears for system-paced but not learner-paced material [~M], which the authors explain as learners in system-paced conditions having no time to recover misperceived information; for hearing learners, the modality effect disappears under self-paced conditions [~M].
  6. Do not depend on fast, listening-only practice. Replace fast oral drills, rote repetition and competitive speed games with practice that has the written form in view and time to respond; let learners connect new material to what they know. From the same digest: oral drills, memorisation and fast-paced competitive activities disadvantage older learners [~M] (a digest's assertion, no study).
  7. Plan the arc across sessions (all quantities untested proposals):
  8. Weeks 1–2: full written support. Every new item written as it is spoken; a handout of the full script or dialogue before any listening; spoken segments of no more than two to three minutes, each followed by a written recap of its key points.
  9. Weeks 3–6: key-word support. Show only the key terms while speaking; the full script stays available on paper after the first listening. Keep the room fixes throughout.
  10. Later sessions, when listening itself is a goal: a first listening with no print, then the same passage with captions or script to check, then a third listening without. Bimodal captioned input improved second-language listening [+W] (reported second-hand) supports using captions to build listening, not only to bypass it.
  11. When to reduce support: a learner who follows a short passage at normal speed, in the room's usual noise, with key words only, on two consecutive sessions moves to the next stage; one who misses much of it stays, and the first thing to check is the room and the hearing aid, not the learner's effort. When the goal is the content rather than listening, keep the written support for the whole course; it costs nothing for learners who do not need it.

When a condition is not met: if the room cannot be made quiet (a shared hall, a busy workplace), increase the written share and move key explanations to a quieter moment or a recording the learner can play with headphones; if the learner cannot read the print, carry the visual half with pictures, real objects and demonstration (a "show and do" demonstration with hearing-impaired students [+W], a practitioner's report).

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which channel is unreliable, and how much. Hearing loss, low vision, both, or neither but a noisy room each calls for a different visual or auditory half. If unstated, ask: in this room, can each learner read the materials from their seat and follow speech at a normal voice?
  • Whether learners also need to look at a picture, object or demonstration while words are spoken. If they do, a full printed script competes with it; key words do not. If unstated, ask: while you speak, what else must learners be looking at?
  • Whether understanding speech without print is part of the goal. If it is, print fades across sessions; if not, it stays. If unstated, ask: at the end, must learners follow spoken language without any written support?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: whether the learner responds to a spoken question, a written one, or both; what they write down when asked to note new words; whether they ask for repetition; where they sit and whether they watch the speaker's face; how their responses change when the room is quieter or the print larger. "Has a hearing loss", "did not understand" and "did not know" are inferred states, and a missed answer can come from any of them.

The evidence by question¶

Each question links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it (a synthesis table, one row per distinct study, no averaged verdict).

  • Without a competing picture, do the same words spoken and written help or hurt, and for whom? (Here: for learners who may miss part of what is said.) On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions [~S]. Its table counts 7 distinct studies (3 causal, 2 quantitative syntheses, 2 reviews); the spoken–written moderator claims used above all come from one of them, Adesope and Nesbit's (2012) meta-analysis of 57 effect sizes, mostly with postsecondary students.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim compares combined audio and visual presentation with audio alone for older learners or for learners with a hearing or vision loss, and none tests lighting or noise reduction on learning. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

  • Spoken–written presentations do not outperform written-only presentations but do outperform spoken-only presentations [+M]: one meta-analysis (Adesope & Nesbit 2012); against spoken-only controls g = 0.29 (k = 34), against written-only g = −0.04 (k = 23). It supports adding the written form to speech; it also says that for learners who read well, written-only does as well as both. Learners were not selected for sensory loss.
  • Verbal redundancy yields a small overall learning benefit with large between-study heterogeneity [~M]: the same meta-analysis, overall g = 0.15 across 57 effect sizes with I² = .82. The average is small and depends on conditions, which is why the moderators below matter.
  • The spoken–written advantage over spoken-only presentation appears for system-paced but not learner-paced materials [~M]: g = 0.34 system-paced (k = 24) against g = 0.12 learner-paced (k = 10); the authors' explanation, recovery of misperceived information, is the mechanism this page relies on. Their explanation, not a tested one.
  • Spoken–written advantages are larger for second-language and nonfluent first-language readers and for low prior knowledge learners [~M]: g = 0.82 nonfluent first-language, 0.58 second-language, 0.24 fluent readers; low prior knowledge 0.29, high −0.05 (marginal). Many older language learners are second-language learners, so this is the subgroup closest to the digest's population, but age and hearing were not moderators.
  • Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+M]: a meta-meta-analysis reporting Montero Perez et al. (2013), g = 0.99 (k = 15) for comprehension and 0.87 (k = 10) for vocabulary. Second-language learners, not learners with hearing loss.
  • Limiting: Verbal redundancy does not facilitate learning when materials include images or animation [~M]: g = 0.45 without images against g = 0.06 with images or animation. Written-plus-spoken words help over speech when nothing else needs the eyes; with a picture, the gain vanished for these learners.
  • Limiting: Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning [~S]: four studies, including Ginns's (2005) meta-analysis (d = 0.72 across 39 between-subjects experiments) and a meta-meta-analysis (g = 0.38, 0.20 after publication-bias correction). For hearing learners, words beside a picture are better heard than read. The claim's own Discussion says it does not apply to learners with hearing impairments, for whom on-screen text is the better choice; this page's moves for such learners rest on that exclusion, not on a test. The old page cited it as support [+M]; here it qualifies.
  • Presentation pacing moderates the modality effect [~M]: d = 0.93 system-paced against −0.14 self-paced (Ginns 2005). When learners control the pace, printed words lose nothing against spoken ones, which makes printed support cheap in self-paced materials.
  • Common language teaching methods such as oral drills, memorization, and fast-paced competitive activities disadvantage older learners [~M]: the same 1987 digest, an assertion with no study or effect size (review r1). It supports avoiding speed- and listening-dependent drills; it does not show what replaces them.
  • Learning ability does not decline with age in healthy older adults [+W]: the same digest, citing other authors; no data printed. It is why a slow or missed response should be checked for hearing and room conditions before being read as low ability.

Learners, outcomes and settings differ across these claims (postsecondary students in short multimedia lessons, second-language video viewers, a digest's assertions), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures delayed retention, and none includes learners selected for age-related sensory loss.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims bearing on the page that the model above uses only lightly or not at all; each says how far it bears.

  • Physical environment elements — sunlight, nature views, temperature, and lighting — affect learning [+W]: a learning-sciences synthesis reporting that sunlight and nature views boost achievement and well-being; it bears on "good lighting" only in general, not on visual access for learners with low vision.
  • Natural lighting and green window views benefit mood, sleep, cognition, and test performance [+W]: a school-facilities brief, second-hand; bears on lighting only loosely.
  • Bimodal captioned input improved L2 listening skills, generalizing to unfamiliar sentences and speakers [+W]: reported second-hand in a pronunciation article; used above for the late-session listening stage.
  • Hearing-impaired students learn library skills quickly via the "show and do" demonstration method [+W]: a practitioner's assertion about library instruction; used above for learners who cannot use print.
  • Older adults can succeed in learning another language, though adults need more deliberate learning effort than children [+W] (link text shortened from the claim's title): a digest on elderly refugees; bears on the expectation set for older learners, not on presentation.
  • Adults learn languages more quickly than children in the early stages of second language acquisition [~W]: from the same 1987 digest; does not bear on sensory compensation.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually that the learner takes in and can use the new material (words, procedures, information) at the end of the session. Elicit separately what the learner wants. An older language learner may want to follow a conversation with a grandchild, a doctor or a neighbour, which is speech without print in an ordinary noisy room; written support that the designer keeps for the whole course then serves the objective and not the learner's goal, and the listening stage of the arc becomes the point. Another learner may most want not to be seen struggling, and will decline a front seat offered in public but use a handout given to everyone. Record both the objective and the goal; agreement and divergence are both observations that change the plan.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies with older learners, or with learners who have a hearing loss, find that adding the written form to speech does not improve comprehension over speech alone in a typical room, or that captions draw attention from the speaker's face and lower understanding of a live conversation. If lighting and noise fixes make no difference to these learners' comprehension, they are comfort measures, not learning measures. If written support kept across a course leaves learners less able to follow speech without it at the end, the fading arc should start sooner. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough support"; check the room and the learner's report before adding more.

Speech comprehension in the room, use of new material, listening without print, and use in the setting the learner cares about are separate outcomes, and nothing here establishes a print size, a segment length or a criterion for reducing support.

Claims¶

  • K-DTVP-2 visual perception scores decline significantly with age in Korean adults, dropping significantly from the 50s compared to the 20s [+W]
  • When light is well distributed and diffuse with no extremes of surface brightness, the eye is practically independent of illumination intensity within ordinary lighting ranges [~W]
  • Indirect systems and semi-indirect systems with high-density reflectors produced the best results for sustaining clear seeing among the artificial lighting systems tested [~W]
