Cognitive Load Management¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 60 claims (24 for, 33 mixed, 3 against) · 51 studies (23 causal, 12 review, 9 quant-synthesis, 6 theoretical, 1 design), q1–q4 · 19 of 51 report an effect size · 52 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner whose current capacity on the particular task is limited (a novice on that task, whatever their standing in the wider domain), material whose interacting elements must be processed together (complex-skill, principle or multi-step procedure goals) is expected to be learned better when the design distributes processing demand over the sequence instead of presenting it all at once: avoidable processing removed from the presentation, component knowledge introduced before the whole (pretraining, segmenting, isolated parts first), and support withdrawn or effort reintroduced as the learner's task-specific capacity grows. Compared with an unmanaged version of the same material, this is expected to change performance, transfer or efficiency; which of them changes, and whether the change holds at a delayed horizon, differs between the claims below and must be stated for each design.

The relationship has two edges. Too much simultaneous demand for this learner is expected to impede learning; but less effort is not the objective. Conditions that slow performance during learning (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, problem-solving before instruction) can improve delayed-retention, conceptual-understanding or transfer, and support that helps at the start can become redundant later. Deferring intrinsic demand is a default for tasks learners cannot yet coordinate, not a rule for every task: for a procedural skill that could be attempted whole, one experiment found full complexity from the start beat a gradual ramp at two days (see Evidence and qualifications), so a ramp should be checked against a delayed probe rather than assumed. Managing load therefore means deciding, for a stated outcome and horizon, which demand to remove, which to defer and which to keep.

Cognitive Load Theory, the converted sibling principle, holds the narrower model that sits inside this one: for task-specific novices on high-element-interactivity material, removing avoidable processing (integrated sources, segmented transient media, isolated elements, full examples) improves immediate performance or efficiency, a relationship that weakens with expertise. This page adds the sequence-level decisions around that lever: when to defer intrinsic demand, when to restore effortful processing, and how to tell which a response calls for. Working-memory limits and schema construction are the explanatory hypotheses of the theory and of the expertise reversal effect; they are not observations. The 4C/ID pattern is a reusable whole-task policy that applies both; its response-dependent branches remain proposals.

Two studies in the wiki test a whole load-reducing design rather than one lever, and they disagree on what it buys. In one small randomized study (37 first-year students, thermodynamics, eight sessions), a design built on cognitive load theory scored far higher than lecturer-designed instruction on the session retention and transfer tests, but not significantly higher on the achievement post-test or lower on the load scale (CLT design raised retention and transfer [+M]; no difference on the post-test or load scale [~M]). Revising a middle-school mathematics lesson's visuals by signaling, contiguity and coherence changed no post-lesson test score, while eye-tracking indices of processing fell for low-prior-knowledge students and rose for middle-prior-knowledge ones (revised visuals had no learning effect [~M]). Neither withdraws support or restores effort over the sequence, so the relationship as a whole stays untested here; the first supports its removal half, the second warns that removal can change processing without changing learning.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: how much do these learners know about this particular task?, how many elements must be handled together?, is the outcome immediate performance, transfer or retention at a delay?, and the learners' age and language of instruction. Ask also whether learners will work alone or in groups, and what tools (an AI chatbot, a search engine) they will use.

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record a response under stated conditions: the task and its representation, how many elements must be handled together as judged for this learner, the supports present (integrated labels, segments, a primer, worked steps, hints, aids), the pacing, the time taken and the response mode. "Overloaded", "under-challenged" and "novice" are inferred states. Accuracy, time, a rated mental effort, errors and confidence are observations; none measures working-memory load or a schema directly. A synthesis below found that integrated designs improved learning without reliably changing load ratings, so a rating and a learning outcome can move apart, and a low rating can also mean disengagement or an easy-looking task. The same split appears in eye-tracking: revised mathematics visuals shortened fixations on the text for low-prior-knowledge students and lengthened them for middle-prior-knowledge ones, with no difference in test scores or in time spent on the lesson (on the text [~M]; on the mathematics visuals [~M]; no difference in lesson time [~M]). In the other direction, rated mental effort and study time across eight sessions ranked topics almost exactly as experts had classified their element interactivity, so the two together are a usable observation of how demanding a topic is for a group (Mental effort and study time indicate element interactivity [+M]).

A rating also depends on when it is taken and what produced it. Example–problem pairs lowered rated effort during learning but raised it on the near-transfer test, against problem–example pairs (effort reversed between phases [~W]). Presenting principles before a case comparison raised rated germane load and lowered rated extraneous load by large amounts, yet timing made no difference to the knowledge test (ratings moved [~W]; knowledge did not [~W]). Expert example standards raised rated effort and difficulty while making learners' judgements of their own examples more accurate (standards raised effort and difficulty [~W]; in a second experiment too [~W]). A review argues that effort ratings blend task demand with feelings and motivational beliefs, and that high effort can mean either more productive activity or less prior knowledge, so a high rating is not by itself a sign of good or bad design (effort self-reports are not a valid measure of a task's cognitive demand [~W]; high effort is not necessarily better learning [~W]). Read an effort rating beside a learning outcome at the horizon that matters, never alone.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Managing load through segmenting and worked examples improved learning, with qualifications [+M]: two meta-analyses. Segmented multimedia (56 investigations, 88 comparisons) had small to medium effects on retention and transfer against continuous presentation, lowered overall rated load and lengthened learning time; learners with high prior knowledge gained more on retention than those with little, against what the expertise reversal effect predicts. Worked examples in mathematics (55 studies, elementary to postsecondary) gave g = 0.48, and adding self-explanation prompts produced a negative effect against examples without them, so an added effort-inducing activity did not help there. Horizons are not separated on the claim page. Not yet checked against its sources.
  • Reducing split attention improves learning, though load measures do not show why [+M]: integrated rather than separated text and diagrams, g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons (2,426 participants), holding across many moderators; a second meta-analysis of 50 studies reports the benefit for novices largest with complex materials. A systematic review of 41 comparisons found integrated designs did not reliably change any cognitive-load measure, so the benefit stands while its explanation by reduced load is not established. Not settled: the abstracts available could not confirm the entries.
  • A pretraining primer before a complex lesson improved transfer [+M]: one randomized experiment, n=93; a video naming a micropipette's parts before an immersive VR procedure lesson, against the same lesson with no primer. The primed group scored higher on a knowledge test, made fewer errors on a real-life transfer task and reported lower load; errors inside VR did not differ. No standardized effect size, delayed horizon or prior-knowledge moderation is reported. It supports deferring component knowledge out of a complex task, not a primer for learners who already know the parts. Not yet checked against its sources.
  • Guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [~M]: a synthesis of cognitive load studies, read from its abstract, reporting that integrated explanations, worked examples and step-by-step guidance help novices and can depress performance relative to leaner conditions as prior knowledge increases. No effect sizes, threshold or horizon are recorded. It argues for changing support over time; it does not say when, and the segmenting result above runs the other way on retention. Not settled against its sources.
  • Effortful conditions can slow learning-phase performance while improving delayed retention [~M]: three meta-analyses of classroom quizzing (222 studies, g = 0.499 against restudy and other strategies), spaced against massed retrieval (29 studies, g = 0.74) and interleaving for category learning (59 studies, g = 0.42, but blocking beat interleaving for words, g = -0.39). It limits this principle: minimizing effort is not the goal when the outcome is delayed retention. The claim page itself says the difficulty must be surmountable, and none of these studies manipulated presentation load, so it does not say how the two interact. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries pass, the rest could not be confirmed.
  • A CLT-based design raised retention and transfer in thermodynamics [+M]: one randomized study, 37 first-year students (18 and 19), eight sessions, against lecturer-designed instruction, with a memory-span test and the achievement pre-test as covariates. Session retention test means were 77.07 against 38.48 (ηp2 = .72) and transfer 62.96 against 29.67 (ηp2 = .71). The achievement post-test and the load scale did not differ significantly (both ηp2 = .09, p = .07 and .09), which is not equivalence (no group difference [~M]); combining performance and effort, the design's effective learning scores were higher at retention and transfer and about equal on the post-test, because its students also reported more effort (effective learning scores [+M]). Session scores fell in both groups as the topics' element interactivity rose (retention and transfer fell with interactivity [+W]), a descriptive pattern. Rigour is coded r1 on the main entries; the claim pages do not say what the design changed session by session, so it tests a load-reducing package, not this page's sequence of withdrawal and restored effort.
  • Revising visuals by signaling, contiguity and coherence did not change learning [~M]: one experiment, 62 middle-school students reading a mathematics lesson with original or revised visuals. Condition did not affect the post-lesson test (F(1, 56) = .00, p = .96) and did not interact with prior knowledge; prior knowledge itself predicted scores (d = 1.30, low against high). Eye-tracking measures of processing showed condition-by-prior-knowledge interactions, reduced for low and increased for middle prior knowledge (text processing [~M]; mathematics visuals [~M]). It qualifies the removal lever: applied together to existing material, the principles changed how learners processed it without a measured learning gain, and in opposite directions for neighbouring prior-knowledge groups.
  • Problem-solving before instruction improved conceptual learning and transfer, not procedure [~M]: a meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons), problem-solving first against the same instruction first: g = 0.36 on conceptual knowledge and transfer, g = −0.03 on procedural knowledge; the advantage reversed for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. One more randomized study pair in mathematics. It limits the load-reduction default for conceptual goals in older learners; it does not license unguided problem-solving for novices, since instruction follows in every comparison. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries pass.
  • Constantly high task complexity improved delayed posttest performance against gradually increasing complexity [~M]: one 2×2 experiment, 132 participants learning Italian verb conjugation, complexity (constant high against gradually rising) crossed with a desirable difficulty (interleaving). Two days later the constant-high groups needed less time and fewer trials (η² = 0.09); complexity did not interact with the desirable difficulty (no interaction [~W]), and the constant-high groups reported lower intrinsic load and less metacognitive strategy use (lower rated load and strategy use [~W]). It runs against this page's default of deferring intrinsic demand, for one procedural skill that can be attempted whole; it does not test isolated parts for a task novices cannot yet coordinate, and the learners' prior knowledge is not given on the claim page. Rigour not established from the text.
  • Students who researched with web search wrote better justifications than students using an AI chatbot [+M]: one randomized experiment, n = 91, a science inquiry task. The chatbot group reported lower intrinsic, extraneous and especially germane load (lower germane load [+W]) and gave fewer relevant aspects in its justifications (η² = 0.11); an exploratory mediation found the germane-load difference accounted for the quality difference (mediation [+W]). It supports the "keep productive effort" edge: a tool that removes effort can remove the processing the outcome needed. Outcome was immediate; no delayed horizon.
  • Collaborative cognitive load: a review reports earlier experiments in which groups learned more efficiently than individuals on high-complexity tasks and less efficiently on simple ones (collective working memory effect [+W]; collaboration aids high-load tasks [+W]), and argues that coordination costs can cancel the benefit (transactive costs [+W]); one experiment found groups with prior experience together more efficient only when the information to share was dense (Zambrano 2019 [~W]). The first findings are second-hand, without sample sizes or effect sizes, and efficiency combines performance with rated effort.

Keep learners, task, comparator, outcome and horizon with each result when transporting it. Effort ratings, efficiency, immediate performance, delayed retention and transfer are different outcomes; a non-significant difference is not equivalence; pooled effects do not forecast one learner's response, and these unlike comparisons are not ranked here. No claim in the wiki tests the general relationship as stated, a sequence-level policy of removing, deferring and restoring demand against a fixed design; most claims test one lever each, and the two whole-design comparisons above test the removal half only, with a large gain on session tests in one and no learning difference in the other.

The evidence by question¶

  • Should component knowledge come before the whole task? Pretraining learners on a system's key parts and terms before the main lesson improved knowledge and real-world transfer in one VR experiment, and is reported second-hand to improve learning in multimedia studies [+M]
  • When should support be withdrawn as the learner improves? Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]
  • Should complexity rise gradually, or can a whole task be given at full complexity from the start? For Italian verb conjugation, constantly high complexity beat a gradual increase two days later [~M]; no claim in the wiki compares the two for a task novices cannot yet coordinate.
  • Does a tool that makes the task easier help? An AI chatbot lowered rated load and produced weaker justifications than web search [+M]; deliberately disfluent text did not improve outcomes [~W], so neither easing nor arbitrary hardening is the lever.
  • When the outcome is retention at a delay, should effortful practice be reintroduced once the material is understood, and for which material? Retrieval practice and spaced retrieval improve achievement and retention by medium amounts, while interleaving helps category learning only for some materials and blocking beats it for words [~M]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner wants to accomplish and why, separately from the designer's objective. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations; do not infer motivation from compliance or from a low effort rating. The designer chooses the representative task, the permitted aids, the criterion and the horizon. Load management is a means to that objective, and the objective decides which effort is avoidable: an aid that removes effort is a support if the objective allows it and an obstacle to the target if it does not. How much effort a learner chooses to spend also matters: a review argues that load-based designs tested in the laboratory, such as worked examples, may not deliver their effect when learners are not motivated to study the material carefully, and that authentic-setting studies seldom check this (optimal task design may depend on effort allocation [~W]).

For example, a laboratory trainee may value getting through a pipetting session without the embarrassment of handling equipment they cannot name, while the designer's objective is accurate unaided pipetting a week later. A primer and step-by-step guidance can serve the first and raise in-session accuracy, yet the delayed, unaided performance remains untested until it is probed. Record both aims, say when the guidance is withdrawn, and check the delayed task.

What would revise this model?¶

The model should weaken if comparable learners on comparable material learn as well from the unmanaged design under defensible comparisons with aligned outcomes, or if deferring component knowledge (primers, segments, parts first) repeatedly fails to help on tasks with many interacting elements; the Italian conjugation result, where a gradual ramp lost to constant high complexity, is one such signal for a simpler procedural task. The expertise qualification should weaken if, as in the segmenting synthesis, higher prior knowledge keeps predicting larger rather than smaller benefits. The "keep productive effort" edge should weaken if effortful conditions fail to improve delayed outcomes for the learners and materials in question, as interleaving did for word lists and problem-first designs did for younger children. If rated effort falls without any gain in performance, transfer or retention, the design reduced discomfort, not demand on learning. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure after the fact as "too much load" or "too little challenge".

A learning-phase efficiency gain, immediate performance, near transfer, delayed retention and unaided use in the learner's setting are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish a threshold of expertise at which to remove support, an optimal amount of difficulty, an ordering of load-management techniques, or that changes in load cause the gains they accompany.

Examples¶

  • A math lesson introduces multi-step equation solving in short worked chunks, with each step visually separated and narrated before learners attempt a full problem.
  • A science simulation hides advanced controls for novices, then gradually reveals more variables once learners can explain the core system.
  • Consider a focused more is more approach combining minimized extraneous processing with prompts for generative processing
  • Step One: a free age-friendly pre-beginning ESL and citizenship curriculum for older immigrants

  • Allowing Think-Time and Re-Reading
