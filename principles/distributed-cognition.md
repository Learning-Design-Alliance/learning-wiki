# Distributed Cognition

## Description
Distributed cognition is the principle that thinking and problem solving are often spread across people, tools, representations, and environments rather than residing entirely inside one learner. It is useful when instruction relies on collaboration, external representations, or shared expertise.

## Implications

Distributed cognition matters when successful performance depends on how learners coordinate with tools, artifacts, and other people rather than only on what they can hold in mind individually. The principle reframes instruction away from the isolated learner and toward the whole cognitive system: people, prompts, diagrams, checklists, platforms, and social routines. In design terms, this means that external supports are not just aids for weak learners. They are often part of the normal architecture of expert reasoning, especially when external representations reduce internal memory burden [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [~S]. The risk is fragmentation: if the distributed system is poorly coordinated, learners may experience scattered resources instead of support, which is why roles, progress, and use of supports have to be visible enough to monitor [[claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation]] [~M] and responsive enough to match actual need rather than adding generic complexity [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Tasks where tools or peers meaningfully extend reasoning** — the problem should actually benefit from external representation, division of expertise, or coordination
- **Design that makes distributed resources usable and visible** — learners need to know what tools, people, or artifacts to rely on and how
#### Constraints
- **Poor coordination can make distributed work feel fragmented rather than supportive**
- **External supports can become crutches if learners never understand how to use them strategically**
- **Shared cognition still requires accountability** — distribution does not eliminate the need for individual understanding and contribution

### Target Learners
- Learners working in collaborative, tool-rich, or professional-like environments
- Learners solving problems that benefit from diagrams, shared documents, checklists, or specialized roles
- Learners preparing for real work settings where cognition is routinely distributed across systems

### Target Learning Objectives
- Improve shared reasoning and effective use of external cognitive supports
- Help learners coordinate with tools, representations, and collaborators productively
- Build understanding of how reasoning is supported by environments, not just minds in isolation

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Situated and sociocultural perspectives — cognition is shaped by participation, tools, and context
- [[theories/situated-learning|Situated Learning]] — distributed cognition often appears within authentic social practice
- [[principles/collaborative-learning|Collaborative Learning]] — shared reasoning can depend on how work and representation are distributed

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [[theories/information-processing-theory|Information Processing Theory]] — qualifies that individual attention, memory, and processing limits still matter inside distributed systems

### Claims
- [[claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]] [~S] — external representations can reduce internal memory burden when they are organized and interpretable
- [[claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation]] [~M] — distributed systems become stronger when progress and roles are visible enough to monitor
- [[claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning]] [~M] — tools and collaborators are most helpful when support is responsive rather than generic

## Related Principles
- [[principles/social-learning|Social Learning]] — distributed cognition often depends on learning through interaction with others
- [[principles/community-of-inquiry|Community of Inquiry]] — one context where cognition is distributed across discourse, facilitation, and shared artifacts
- [[principles/situated-learning|Situated Learning]] — provides the contextual account of why cognition is often distributed in real practice

## Examples

### Illustrative

**[[elements/shared-documents|Shared Documents]]** — Learners co-construct an artifact that carries part of the group’s memory, coordination, and reasoning.

**Checklists, diagrams, and dashboards in team-based work** — External representations reduce memory burden and help groups coordinate complex tasks.

**Studio or lab collaboration** — Different learners, tools, and artifacts each carry part of the cognitive work, and success depends on how well those pieces fit together.
