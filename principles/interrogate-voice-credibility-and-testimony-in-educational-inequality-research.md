# Interrogate Voice, Credibility and Testimony When Studying Inequality in Education

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study, `q1` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article's four suggestions for further research. Inequality literature "can communicate forms of epistemic injustice" through guiding questions such as "Who has voice and who doesn’t?"; it should build a responsive approach to testimony, credibility and recognition; it "should question and problematize the structural inequalities in society"; and education literature should problematize its own epistemic practices and the credibility of the knowledge institutions it references.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Raising the guiding questions the article lists: who has voice, whether voices interact with equal agency and power, in whose terms they communicate, who is understood, who is believed, and who is acknowledged and engaged with
- Asking how to understand the institutions of knowledge referenced by educational studies and how to address the structural causes of those resources' credibility
#### Constraints
- These are offered as suggestions for further research, not as tested interventions

### Target Learners
- Education researchers and practitioners examining social and cultural inequalities in school settings

### Target Learning Objectives
- Identifying the epistemic agency and authority of actors in a school setting when studying inequality
