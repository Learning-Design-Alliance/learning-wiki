# Phonics

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 22 claims (12 for, 10 mixed) · 37 studies (11 quant-synthesis, 9 causal, 7 review, 5 theoretical, 4 associational, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 10 of 37 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner who cannot yet turn print into speech (who names few letter sounds, cannot blend sounds into a word, or reads only words already memorised and guesses at the rest), explicit teaching of letter–sound correspondences in a planned sequence, applied at once to reading and spelling words, is expected to produce more accurate decoding of unfamiliar regular words and pseudowords, and better word reading, than unsystematic phonics or none. The capability is a `procedure` (blending and segmenting) built on `verbal-association` knowledge (which grapheme stands for which phoneme); the outcomes it predicts are `immediate-performance` and `near-transfer` on decoding and word reading, with spelling close behind. Comprehension is a separate and weaker claim.

**A claim does test this relationship.** The National Reading Panel meta-analysis compared systematic phonics with unsystematic or no phonics across 38 experiments from kindergarten to grade 6 and found a moderate advantage (d = 0.41), largest on decoding regular words and pseudowords and for kindergarten and first-grade children ([Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S]). The relationship is conditional on four things the evidence names: the learner's starting point (gains are smaller for older readers with difficulties, and further phonics adds little once decoding is accurate), the comparison (the advantage over whole-language teaching is smaller and contested, d = 0.31 in 12 non-randomised studies), the outcome (decoding gains are larger than comprehension gains), and the reader's language. The detailed design below (pace, lesson length, move-on criteria, how support changes over a year) is not what the studies compared, and is labelled as a proposal wherever no claim gives it.

## Default design

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Including systematic phonics at all is tested; the steps, quantities and thresholds below are proposals unless a claim is linked, and should be set locally and recorded.

1. **Find where each learner starts before teaching.** In about five minutes per learner: name the sounds of the letters taught so far, blend three spoken sounds into a word (/k/ /a/ /t/), segment a spoken word into its sounds, and read ten short pseudowords using the correspondences already taught. Pseudowords show decoding without memorised wholes. Place the learner at the first correspondence they do not know. Untested proposal; pseudoword decoding is the outcome on which the phonics effect was largest ([Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S]), and letter knowledge and phoneme sensitivity are the earliest skills that predict later word recognition ([Phoneme awareness is a stronger predictor of reading development than rhyme awareness](../claims/phoneme-awareness-stronger-predictor-than-rhyme.md) [+M]).
2. **Follow a written scope and sequence, not the order words happen to appear in texts.** Systematic against unsystematic or no phonics is the comparison the meta-analysis tested ([Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S]). A default pace: three to five new correspondences a week in the first weeks, starting with frequent consonants and short vowels so that whole words can be built within the first week. The pace is an untested proposal.
3. **Teach in a short daily lesson of 20–30 minutes** (untested proposal on length):
   - *Review* (2–3 minutes): earlier correspondences, quickly, from cards.
   - *Model the new correspondence* (3–5 minutes): say the sound, show the grapheme, show how the mouth makes it, then blend it into two or three words aloud ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
   - *Oral blending and segmenting with letters in view* (about 5 minutes), working on one or two skills at a time. Phonemic-awareness training gave larger reading gains when letters were included, when one or two skills were taught rather than several, and in small groups ([Phonemic Awareness Training Improves Reading](../claims/phonemic-awareness-training-improves-reading.md) [+M]; children from preschool to grade 1).
   - *Read 8–12 words* that use the new and earlier correspondences, first together and then one learner at a time, correcting each error at once ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)). Untested as a number.
   - *Spell 3–5 of those words* from dictation, so the same knowledge is used for encoding. The meta-analysis reported spelling among its outcomes; spelling gains were not reliable for older readers with difficulties.
   - *Read a short decodable text* (5–10 minutes) containing only taught correspondences plus the few irregular words already taught. Untested proposal from the earlier page.
4. **Teach common irregular words by their regular parts.** Two or three a week: read the word, mark the part that does not follow the taught code, practise it in sentences. Untested; from the earlier page's note on English spelling.
5. **When a learner misreads, send them back to the letters.** Point to the grapheme they skipped and ask for its sound, then have them blend again. Do not prompt them to guess from the picture or the sentence. Untested as a correction rule. The rationale on the earlier page: weaker readers lean on context as a compensation, a pattern one review reports second-hand from a study of eleven-year-old remedial readers ([Poorly fluent readers use context clues more heavily than fluent readers, as a compensatory device](../claims/struggling-readers-use-context-clues-compensatorily.md) [~W]).
6. **Check each week and move on by accuracy, not by the calendar.** Ten words and pseudowords on the week's correspondences, read aloud one-to-one in about two minutes. Move on when a learner reads at least nine of ten correctly on two successive checks; reteach in a small group otherwise. The thresholds are untested proposals.
7. **Keep meaning and oral language running alongside, not after.** A daily 10–15 minute read-aloud of a richer book than the learner can decode, with the adult prompting the child to talk about it, kept separate from decodable reading. Interactive (dialogic) reading raised expressive vocabulary more than ordinary shared reading in a meta-analysis (d = 0.59), less so for four- and five-year-olds and children at risk of language impairment ([Dialogic Reading Improves Expressive Language](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]); shared reading at home is associated with later reading, mostly in correlational studies ([Joint Book Reading Predicts Literacy Success](../claims/joint-book-reading-predicts-literacy-success.md) [+M]).
8. **Plan the year as an arc, and end phonics lessons for each learner when decoding is secure.** A default for beginning readers (an untested proposal): *first term*, single-letter correspondences and blending of three-sound words; every new item modelled by the teacher; all practice text fully decodable; phonemic-awareness work concentrated here (the training that worked lasted 5–18 hours in all, which the meta-analysis found more effective than longer programmes: [Phonemic Awareness Training Improves Reading](../claims/phonemic-awareness-training-improves-reading.md) [+M]). *Second term*, consonant digraphs and blends and the first vowel teams; learners blend silently before reading aloud, and the teacher models only after an error; texts still mostly decodable, with ordinary books read with an adult. *Third term*, the remaining vowel spellings and two-syllable words; decodable texts give way to ordinary books, and phonics time shrinks as weekly checks are passed. A learner who reads pseudowords across the taught code accurately and without laboured sounding-out leaves the phonics group for fluency, vocabulary and comprehension work; extra guidance for a learner who already has the skill can become redundant ([Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]; a cognitive-load review, not a reading study).

When a condition is not met: if learners already decode accurately, skip to fluency and comprehension and teach only the gaps the screening shows; if the reader is older and struggling, an adult, or learning in a second language, see the table below.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner can already do with print.** A child who knows no letter sounds, an eight-year-old who decodes single syllables but stalls on longer words, and an adult who reads familiar words but cannot attempt new ones need different starting points and different amounts of phonics. If the brief omits it, ask: can the learner say the sounds of the letters, blend three spoken sounds into a word, and read a short invented word?
- **The learner's age and the reason they are behind.** Effects are largest in kindergarten and first grade and smaller, mainly on decoding, for older readers with difficulties; a diagnosed reading disability changes the dose and the expected gain. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, how long have they been taught to read, and has a reading difficulty been identified?
- **Which outcome counts.** Decoding unfamiliar words, reading taught words, spelling, reading fluently and understanding text respond differently; phonics predicts the first most strongly. If unstated, ask: what must the learner be able to read or write, and how will it be checked?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a reading or spelling attempt under recorded conditions: the item (a taught word, a pseudoword, a word in a sentence with or without a picture), what the learner said or wrote, whether they sounded it out, how long it took, and what help was given. "Knows the code", "has not grasped blending" and "is guessing" are inferred states. Reading a taught word correctly does not show decoding, because the word may be remembered whole.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Reads taught words in a list, fails pseudowords with the same correspondences | Words memorised as wholes; the learner knows the sounds but does not blend them; one vowel is confused | Give pseudowords one correspondence at a time, and ask the learner to say each sound aloud before blending; note where the attempt breaks. |
| Says a word that starts with the right letter but is otherwise wrong, often one that fits the picture or sentence | Later correspondences in the word not yet known; a habit of predicting from context; the text is above the taught code | Show the same words alone with no picture or sentence; check them against the correspondences taught so far. |
| Accurate but slow, sounding out every word | Correspondences known but not yet automatic; weak phonological processing; tiredness or anxiety | Re-read the same passage on three days and time it; compare with a new passage of the same code. Gains on the repeated passage only suggest practice volume; no gain on either suggests a processing difficulty worth assessing. |
| Decodes accurately, cannot say what the text was about | Word meanings unknown (vocabulary or second language); attention used up by decoding; the passage is about something unfamiliar | Read the same passage aloud to the learner and ask the same questions: good listening comprehension points to decoding load; poor listening comprehension points to language or knowledge. |
| Cannot blend or segment spoken words even without print | Phonemic awareness not yet developed; the sound does not exist in the learner's first language; a hearing difficulty | Give oral blending of two-sound and three-sound words, a minimal-pair discrimination task (ship / sheep), and ask whether hearing has been checked. |
| Falls behind the group while others advance at the same pace | Fewer lessons attended; the pace is too fast for this learner; weaker letter knowledge at the start, which in one intervention study was partly offset by higher cognitive flexibility ([Higher pretest cognitive flexibility predicted greater decoding gains and compensated for lower pretest alphabet knowledge](../claims/pretest-cognitive-flexibility-predicts-decoding-gains-compensates-alphabet.md) [~M]) | Check attendance; re-screen the taught correspondences one-to-one; compare progress after two weeks at a slower pace in a smaller group. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A probe is also practice, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain rather than settling on the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S]: the National Reading Panel meta-analysis (Ehri et al. 2001) pooled 38 experimental and quasi-experimental studies (66 treatment–control comparisons) of systematic phonics against unsystematic or no phonics, kindergarten to grade 6; overall d = 0.41, held at the end of instruction and at the end of the first year, largest on decoding regular words and pseudowords, and larger in kindergarten (d = 0.46), grade 1 (d = 0.54) and grade 2 (d = 0.43) than for older readers with difficulties, whose decoding gains remained but whose comprehension and spelling gains were not reliably above zero. Coded `q4 i2`; rigour not settled (`r?`). The same claim records Bowers (2020), which argues the advantage over whole language is not shown (below).
- [Systematic phonics outperforms unsystematic or no-phonics instruction on children's reading overall, but its advantage over whole-word teaching on novel words is untested in children and contested](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [~S]: its three entries pull in different directions. The meta-analysis abstract reports phonics ahead of every control type, whole language included, larger when it began early (d = 0.55) than after first grade (d = 0.27), and not helping low-achieving readers with cognitive limitations; it gives no separate effect for reading untrained words, which is the claim's subject. An artificial-script experiment with 24 adults found print-to-sound training made untrained words faster to read but not more accurate (both about 80% correct). Bowers (2020), a systematic review of 12 meta-analyses (`review · r3`), reports that the National Reading Panel's direct comparison with whole language used 12 non-randomised studies, d = 0.31, largest for decoding (d = 0.55) and smallest for comprehension (d = 0.19), and concludes there is little evidence that systematic phonics beats common alternatives, while not arguing for whole language. **This is the limiting claim**: the advantage over no or unsystematic phonics is tested; the advantage over other structured reading methods is smaller and disputed.
- [Phonics instruction has a small but statistically confirmed effect for children with reading disabilities, while Orton-Gillingham structured-literacy programs show positive but non-significant effects](../claims/structured-literacy-interventions-help-struggling-readers.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials with children and adolescents below the 25th percentile in reading found phonics the only treatment with a statistically confirmed effect on reading and spelling, small after adjustment for publication bias (g′ = 0.198, 95% CI 0.039–0.357); coloured overlays, auditory training and other categories did not reach significance. A meta-analysis of Orton-Gillingham programmes found positive but non-significant effects (0.22 on foundational skills, 0.14 on vocabulary and comprehension). It supports phonics for struggling readers and qualifies the size of the gain and the value of one branded programme.
- [Phonemic Awareness Training Improves Reading](../claims/phonemic-awareness-training-improves-reading.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 52 studies of phonemic-awareness training from preschool to grade 1 found a large effect on phonemic awareness (d = 0.86), a moderate effect on reading (d = 0.53) and on spelling (d = 0.59, though not for disabled readers); reading gains were larger with letters included, one or two skills taught, small groups, and 5–18 hours of instruction; classroom teachers were as effective as researchers. One study (`q4 · r2`). It supports the oral-sound part of the lesson, not phonics as such.
- [Phoneme awareness is a stronger predictor of reading development than rhyme awareness](../claims/phoneme-awareness-stronger-predictor-than-rhyme.md) [+M]: three correlational and longitudinal studies of children aged about 5 to 9 found phoneme awareness and letter knowledge predicted word recognition, and rhyme skills did not add once those were counted. It supports screening letter sounds and phoneme blending rather than rhyme; it is associational and does not test teaching.
- [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+W]: a theoretical model (LaBerge & Samuels 1974, `q1`) and a review (Perfetti 2007, `q2`) proposing that word recognition which runs without attention leaves capacity for comprehension, reframed by Perfetti as depending on the quality of word representations. The claim page says neither source shows that training recognition to automaticity produces a comprehension gain. The earlier page cited it `[+S]`, above its cap.
- [Dialogic Reading Improves Expressive Language](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M] and [Joint Book Reading Predicts Literacy Success](../claims/joint-book-reading-predicts-literacy-success.md) [+M]: interactive shared reading beat ordinary shared reading on expressive vocabulary (d = 0.59, 9 studies, 322 children), less for older preschoolers and children at risk; trained educators' effects did not replicate when children's own teachers read in ordinary classrooms. Shared reading frequency is associated with later language and literacy (d = .59 across mostly correlational studies), and the joint-reading page notes weaker effects on decoding-related skills, which depend more on explicit code-focused teaching. Both support running oral language alongside phonics; neither tests phonics.
- [Morphological Instruction Improves Literacy](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses (preschool to grade 8) found benefits of teaching word parts, largest on decoding and morphological knowledge, with no reliable effect on comprehension or fluency in one, and particular benefit for less-able readers in the other. It supports the move to word parts for older readers.
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies, not of reading. It is the reason to end phonics lessons for learners who already decode; it gives no criterion for when.

Learners, comparisons and outcomes differ across these (kindergarten classes, struggling adolescents, adults learning an invented script), so do not rank them by effect size. None tests the pace, lesson length, move-on thresholds or year-long arc in the default design, and none tests phonics with adults learning to read their own language.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually accurate decoding of unfamiliar words using the taught code, checked with pseudowords and new words, and in time fluent reading of connected text. Elicit separately what the learner wants from reading. A five-year-old may want to read a favourite book alone or keep up with a sibling; a ten-year-old who has struggled may want above all not to read aloud in front of classmates; an adult may want to read a payslip, a text message from a child's school, or the labels at work. Agreement and divergence are both observations: a learner who values the book may lose patience with decodable texts, and one who values not being seen to fail may avoid sounding out and guess instead.

For example, an adult in a workplace literacy course may want to read safety signs and shift notes, while the session's objective is the vowel team *ea*. Teach the correspondence through words from those notes (*clean*, *leave*, *team*), then have the learner read one real note. Record both, so that success on the word list is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if randomised comparisons of systematic phonics against other structured reading methods, with comparable time and aligned outcomes, show no advantage on decoding, as Bowers argues the current evidence fails to show. If the pseudoword advantage did not carry into reading connected text at a delay, the model should claim decoding only. If older struggling readers or adults gained as much from phonics as beginning readers, the age conditioning would go; if they gained nothing, phonics for them would be a design preference. If dialogic or other meaning-focused reading built decoding as well as phonics did, the parallel strand would become a substitute. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "unsystematic" or "too little phonics" after the fact.

Decoding taught words, decoding new words, spelling, fluency, comprehension and reading what the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence supports the first two most strongly for beginning readers, and sets no pace, lesson length or move-on threshold.

## Description
Phonics instruction teaches the systematic relationships between written symbols (graphemes) and speech sounds (phonemes), and trains learners to apply these relationships to decode words. It recommends explicit, sequenced teaching of letter–sound correspondences followed by immediate application to reading and spelling real words. Systematic phonics — teaching correspondences in a planned progression rather than incidentally — is one of the most consistently supported findings in reading research.

## Implications

Phonics works because skilled reading depends on building automatic orthographic–phonological mappings; guessing from context or pictures is a poor substitute and can entrench bad habits. The National Reading Panel's meta-analysis found systematic phonics instruction produces significant advantages in word reading, decoding, and comprehension across grade levels, with the largest effects in kindergarten and first grade [Ehri et al. (2001) meta-analysis of systematic phonics instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.36.3.5) [+S]. Once word recognition becomes automatic, attentional resources are freed for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Phonics is most effective when embedded in explicit teaching sequences with immediate practice and feedback, consistent with [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) and [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — decoding is a skill that must be automatized through many successful repetitions, not discovered through immersion alone. However, phonics is necessary but not sufficient: comprehension, vocabulary, and oral language must be developed in parallel, and instruction that overweights decoding at the expense of meaning can narrow what reading is for.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An explicit [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) element — learners need to see and hear how a grapheme maps to a phoneme and how to blend sounds into words
- High-frequency [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with decodable text — newly taught correspondences must be applied immediately in connected text, not just isolated drills
- Cumulative sequencing ([Chunking](chunking.md)) — correspondences introduced in small sets, reviewed, and built upon; a scope and sequence is essential
- Ongoing [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of decoding accuracy — instruction should be adjusted based on which correspondences learners have and have not mastered

#### Constraints
- Effects are strongest in kindergarten through first grade; phonics interventions show smaller benefits for older struggling readers, who often need additional phonological and fluency work [Ehri et al. - English's irregular orthography means some high-frequency words must be taught as partially irregular; pure phonics-only programs leave gaps
- Phonics alone does not build vocabulary, background knowledge, or comprehension strategies; overemphasis can reduce time for meaning-making [~M]
- Whole-language approaches that rely on context cues and memorizing words as wholes have been shown to produce weaker decoding outcomes [Castles, Rastle & Nation (2018) review of the reading wars.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100618772271) [-S]

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers in kindergarten and grade 1, where effects are largest [Ehri et al. (2001) meta-analysis of systematic phonics instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.36.3.5) [+S]

- English learners, who benefit from explicit grapheme–phoneme mapping, especially when paired with oral language development
- Learners who already decode fluently gain little from further phonics instruction

### Target Learning Objectives

- Accurate, automatic decoding of regular words and common irregular (high-frequency) words

### Theory

# Explicit Instruction: Phonics

## Description
Adult learners who struggle with foundational reading skills, including decoding and phonemic awareness, can benefit from explicit phonics instruction. Phonics instruction specifically addresses word-level reading by connecting letters to sounds, aiding learners in reading and comprehending text. Direct instruction that resembles early reading instruction assists adult literacy learning, particularly as adult readers may need to unlearn ineffective reading and writing habits. This process requires explicit teaching and sufficient practice to ensure retention of skills for transfer into long-term memory.

## Implications
Explicit phonics instruction is appropriate when word-level reading is the bottleneck preventing broader comprehension. Systematic teaching of sound-symbol patterns, combined with guided practice and adult-appropriate materials, can reduce the cognitive effort required for decoding and make later reading more fluent and less effortful [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Beginning readers benefit from explicit process routines for decoding rather than being pushed directly to independent outcome performance [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M], and decoding support is strongest when instructors respond to the specific breakdown in sound-symbol mapping rather than giving generic correction [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The instructional caution is that phonics should function as a bridge into meaningful reading, not as an isolated drill system, so transfer into connected text and real literacy goals needs to happen quickly and deliberately [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Direct, systematic teaching of grapheme-phoneme correspondences, decoding patterns, and blending or segmenting routines
- Ample guided practice reading and spelling words that use the taught patterns
- Instruction matched to learners’ current decoding profile rather than assumed from age or grade level
- Respectful adult-appropriate materials and framing so foundational-skill instruction is not infantilizing

#### Constraints
- Adults may carry shame or avoidance around foundational reading work, so pacing and tone matter
- Phonics instruction alone does not build full reading comprehension; it must connect to meaningful text and vocabulary work
- Learners with very weak phonological awareness or highly inconsistent attendance may need intensive repetition to consolidate patterns
- Instruction is less effective when it is unsystematic, purely incidental, or detached from practice in actual reading and writing

### Target Learners
- Adult learners with persistent decoding difficulties or incomplete foundational reading development
- Multilingual learners who need explicit support mapping English sounds to print
- Learners re-entering literacy instruction after negative prior schooling experiences
- Learners whose reading comprehension is constrained by word-level reading, not only by language knowledge

### Target Learning Objectives
- Strengthen decoding accuracy and efficiency
- Improve transfer from isolated sound-symbol work to reading and spelling connected text
- Build confidence with foundational reading tasks that adults may have long avoided
- Support later growth in fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension by stabilizing word recognition

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Information-processing perspectives support explicit, sequenced instruction for foundational decoding routines
- Systematic skill-building models fit phonics well because patterns build cumulatively and require repeated practice
- Adult-learning perspectives qualify delivery: adults still need explicit foundational instruction, but the materials and rationale must respect their maturity and goals
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) supports reducing the burden of word-level processing so attention can later be freed for comprehension

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Phonics is most important when decoding is the bottleneck; it is not the whole reading curriculum
- Adults need transfer into meaningful reading quickly, or foundational drills may feel disconnected and demotivating
- Support should move gradually from highly guided decoding to more independent reading and writing [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]
