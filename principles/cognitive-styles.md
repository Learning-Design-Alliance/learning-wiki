# Cognitive Styles

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 22 claims (2 for, 15 mixed, 5 against) · 14 studies (6 review, 4 causal, 3 theoretical, 1 associational), `q1`–`q3` · 4 of 14 report an effect size · 20 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the question of **what to do with a learner's characteristic way of approaching a task**: seeing a whole before its parts or analysing parts separately (field dependence and independence), answering quickly or deliberately (impulsivity and reflectivity). Two uses were proposed on the earlier page: give learners tasks that suit their style, or deliberately build the approach a learner lacks. The model here is conditional on the task, not the person. For a learner whose attempts at a task stall in a way that looks like a habit of approach rather than missing knowledge (a holistic learner who cannot organise a lab report, a quick responder who makes careless errors in multi-step problems, a deliberate learner who freezes in a brainstorm), explicit modelling and supported practice of the approach **the task requires**, given to every learner who needs it and faded across sessions, is expected to produce better `immediate-performance` on that kind of task than assigning tasks or formats to match a measured style. Whether the habit itself changes, and whether a gain lasts to `delayed-retention`, are separate questions the evidence here does not settle.

**No claim in the wiki tests either use of cognitive styles with learners.** The nearest evidence is against the first: matching the format of instruction to a measured *learning* style (visual, auditory, Kolb's assimilator and accommodator) has failed to produce the predicted style-by-instruction interaction ([Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]; [No significant interaction between learning mode and learning style on gain scores, while the learning-mode main effect was significant](../claims/no-mode-by-style-interaction-vr-gains.md) [-M]). Those studies tested learning styles, not field dependence or reflectivity, so carrying them here is a transfer the earlier page's own distinction between the two constructs invites caution about. The second use, building balance, rests on theoretical argument only: styles are argued to be partly socialised and so modifiable ([Intellectual styles are partly socialized and therefore modifiable to some degree](../claims/styles-socialized-and-modifiable.md) [+W]), and heavy reliance on one style is argued to make the opposite harder to adopt ([More stylistic information processing makes individuals' viewpoints narrower and more rigid, reducing ability to adopt opposite styles](../claims/stylistic-cognitive-rigidity-reduces-adoption-of-opposites.md) [~W]). A review reports that a Kolb style measure did not change over a year of varied instruction ([Learning styles measured by Kolb's inventory showed no significant change over one year of varied instruction](../claims/kolb-learning-style-stable-over-one-year.md) [~M]), which limits how much change to expect in the measured style, though it says nothing about task performance.

The relationship is also conditional on what a style measure is measuring. The embedded figures test, the common measure of field independence, correlated relatively highly with an intelligence test in a reviewed study ([Embedded figures test performance differed by sex and correlated relatively highly with intelligence](../claims/embedded-figures-test-sex-difference-intelligence-correlation.md) [~M]), and measured styles differ by cultural and linguistic group, partly because tests are given in English ([Cultural and linguistic background influences measured learning and cognitive styles, and should be considered in style assessment](../claims/cultural-background-influences-learning-style-measurement.md) [~M]). So a low "field-independence" score can be a low score on an ability or language test, and a design that sorts on it can sort on ability or background.

This page sits beside [Personalization](personalization.md), which owns what to tailor to each learner and on what basis, and [Adaptive Learning](adaptive-learning.md), which owns choosing the next task from responses; both already advise against tailoring on learning styles, and this page does not repeat their models. [Cognitive Flexibility](cognitive-flexibility.md) owns revisiting content across cases and perspectives; building the approach a learner lacks is a narrower move about one learner's habit on one kind of task.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name what each task demands, not what each learner is.** For each main task, write which approach it needs: part-by-part analysis (a lab report, a multi-step `procedure`), a holistic overview (summarising a text's argument, reading a map), deliberate checking (multi-step mathematics, proof-reading), or quick spontaneous response (brainstorming, cooperative turn-taking, team sport). Untested proposal, from the earlier page's account of which tasks suit which style.
2. **Give every learner the format the content needs.** Choose diagrams for spatial structure, speech for pronunciation, text for an argument, for everyone; do not sort learners into parallel materials by a style inventory. Against matching: [Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]; in one virtual-reality experiment both Kolb style groups gained most from the same guided mode ([Assimilator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode than in VR (non-guided exploration) or Non VR modes](../claims/assimilators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [-M]; [Accommodator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode than in VR (non-guided exploration) or Non VR modes](../claims/accommodators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [-M]).
3. **Observe before describing a habit, and describe the habit on a task.** Before deciding that a learner has a characteristic approach, look at how they tackle at least three tasks with different demands over two or three weeks (an untested proposal for the earlier page's "multiple contexts"). Record "on the lab report, listed observations without grouping them", not "field-dependent". Do not use a style test to decide this alone: its score overlaps with general ability and with language and cultural background ([embedded figures and intelligence](../claims/embedded-figures-test-sex-difference-intelligence-correlation.md) [~M]; [cultural and linguistic background](../claims/cultural-background-influences-learning-style-measurement.md) [~M]).
4. **Where a learner stalls on an analytic task, teach the structure explicitly and fade it over four to six sessions.** Untested proposal; the earlier page's "explicit help organising and analysing academic tasks". Session 1: model the structure for 5–10 minutes on a worked case (for a lab report: question, variables, method, result, claim), thinking aloud about where each observation goes. Sessions 2–3: the learner fills a printed frame with the headings, with a check-in at the end of each section. Sessions 4–5: the frame becomes a checklist the learner ticks after writing. Session 6 on: a blank page, with the checklist used only to self-check. Move on when two consecutive tasks are organised correctly without the frame. Modifiability is argued, not tested: [Intellectual styles are partly socialized and therefore modifiable to some degree](../claims/styles-socialized-and-modifiable.md) [+W].
5. **Where quick answers produce avoidable errors, add a stop-and-check step and fade it.** Untested proposal, from the earlier page's account of impulsivity in mathematics and reading. Before submitting each multi-step problem, the learner spends 1–2 minutes estimating the answer or checking it by a second route, and marks the check on the page. Keep the visible check for three or four sessions; drop the written mark once the learner's error rate on unaided problems has fallen on two consecutive sets, and keep the check itself.
6. **Where deliberate learners hold back on a task that needs spontaneity, give short timed rounds with judgement deferred.** Untested proposal, from the earlier page (creative writing, cooperative partnering). Two or three rounds of 2–3 minutes per session in which ideas are written or said without evaluation, followed by a separate round that selects among them; repeat for three or four sessions, then let the learner set the round length. Do not grade the generating rounds.
7. **Keep more than one route of expression open where the goal allows it, and let the learner choose.** Where the objective does not fix the form (a diagram, a written explanation or an oral account of the same concept), allow any of them against the same criterion. Untested proposal, from the merged page "Teach students to make the best of their intellectual styles rather than forcing one mandated mode". The merged page's concern that schools reward one way of working is argued, not tested: [Schools overwhelmingly reward executive styles and confuse style with level of intellect](../claims/schools-reward-executive-styles.md) [~W].
8. **Do not keep a learner in their preferred approach.** Pair step 7 with at least one task per unit that needs the approach the learner uses least, supported as in steps 4–6. Untested; argued in [More stylistic information processing makes individuals' viewpoints narrower and more rigid, reducing ability to adopt opposite styles](../claims/stylistic-cognitive-rigidity-reduces-adoption-of-opposites.md) [~W].
9. **Where the habit does not shift, compensate rather than remediate.** If after six sessions the learner still needs the frame or checklist, let them keep it as a permanent job aid on assessed work where the goal is the product, not the unaided structuring. Untested proposal, from the merged page's "compensation mechanisms may be needed instead". A review reports a measured style unchanged after a year of varied instruction: [Kolb's inventory stable over one year](../claims/kolb-learning-style-stable-over-one-year.md) [~M].

**Across a term:** weeks 1–3, observe approaches on varied tasks with everyone given the content's format; weeks 3–8, explicit structure or check routines for the learners who stall, faded as in steps 4–6; around week 8, re-observe on a fresh task of the kind that stalled, with no frame, and record what changed. When a condition is not met: if nobody can observe a learner across tasks, build the structure or check into the task for everyone and let those who do not need it skip it.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the task demands, and whether the learner's difficulty is about approach or knowledge.** A learner who cannot organise a lab report may lack the structure, or may not understand the content. If unstated, ask: can the learner explain the content aloud when the organisation is not required, and do they stall on the same kind of task across subjects?
- **What the style information is and where it came from.** An inventory score, a teacher's impression and a record of several task attempts are different evidence; a test score can reflect ability or language. If unstated, ask: what was the learner asked to do, in what language, and was it compared with their performance on the actual tasks?
- **How long and how often you will see the learner.** Building an approach needs several sessions with fading; a single session can only give everyone the structure the task needs. If unstated, ask: how many sessions, how long, and who looks at each attempt?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is an attempt on a task under stated conditions: the task, the support available (a frame, a checklist, a time limit), what the learner produced, how long it took, and which errors appeared. "Field-dependent", "impulsive" and "reflective" are inferred states, and so is "lacks the knowledge". A style label is an interpretation of several such attempts, not an observation.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Contributes well in group work, produces a disorganised solo lab report | A holistic approach without a structure for analysis; weak grasp of the content; reading or writing load; prefers working with others | Ask the learner to explain the experiment aloud; give the same task with a frame; give an equivalent task with less writing. |
| Fast answers with many errors on multi-step problems | A habit of quick response; does not know the procedure; time pressure or anxiety; low interest in the task | Give an untimed problem with a required check and ask the learner to explain each step; compare errors with and without the check. |
| Slow and accurate on analytic work, produces few ideas in a brainstorm | A deliberate approach; fear of being judged; little content knowledge to draw on | Run a round where ideas are anonymous and ungraded; give a topic the learner knows well. |
| Low score on an embedded-figures or style test | An analytic approach not habitually used; lower general ability; unfamiliar test format or language | Compare with the learner's attempts on real analytic tasks; repeat with non-verbal instructions. |
| The learner says "I'm a visual learner" and disengages from text | A stated preference; a label taught earlier; a real difficulty with reading | Offer text and diagram versions of matched content and compare learning on matched items; check reading separately. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is also practice and can change what it measures, so record each one and keep the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests building a cognitive style, or matching tasks to field dependence or reflectivity. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

- [Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]: a systematic review (Pashler et al. 2008, `q3`) found only one study even potentially using the crossover design needed to test matching instruction to learning style, and judged it unconvincing; a randomised study of 125 fifth graders (Rogowsky et al. 2020, `q3`) found no style-by-modality interaction, with self-reported visual learners scoring higher on both listening and reading; a third, second-hand review (`q2`) reports the same. No effect size is printed. Its table counts 4 distinct studies. It bears against the "build on strengths by matching" use, for learning styles; it does not test field dependence or reflectivity.
- [No significant interaction between learning mode and learning style on gain scores, while the learning-mode main effect was significant](../claims/no-mode-by-style-interaction-vr-gains.md) [-M]: 184 learners (Chen et al. 2005, `q3`, ANCOVA with pretest) across three virtual-reality modes; learning mode mattered (η² = 0.199), Kolb learning style did not, and the interaction was not significant. Within the guided mode, assimilators and accommodators did not differ ([guided VR no style difference](../claims/guided-vr-no-learning-style-difference.md) [~M]; equivalence not tested), and both gained most from guided exploration ([assimilators](../claims/assimilators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [-M], [accommodators](../claims/accommodators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [-M]). The design choice, not the style, carried the difference. One study.
- [Learning style (visual-verbal) shows no significant correlation with the effectiveness of pictorial elucidation for adolescent EFL learners](../claims/learning-style-no-correlation-pictorial-elucidation-effect.md) [-M]: in the pictorial group, visual-verbal style scores correlated r = .08 with immediate and r = .01 with delayed posttest (Lee 2017, `q3`). A visual format did not help visual-style learners more.
- [Intellectual styles are partly socialized and therefore modifiable to some degree](../claims/styles-socialized-and-modifiable.md) [+W]: a theoretical argument in Sternberg's chapter (Presseisen 1990, `q1`) that styles shaped by culture, gender, age, parenting and schooling are modifiable to some degree. It is the only claim behind the "build balance" use, and it is argument, not data.
- [More stylistic information processing makes individuals' viewpoints narrower and more rigid, reducing ability to adopt opposite styles](../claims/stylistic-cognitive-rigidity-reduces-adoption-of-opposites.md) [~W]: a theoretical chapter (Cameron 1984, `q1`) argues that reliance on one way of thinking makes the opposite harder to adopt. It supports practising the less-used approach; no data.
- [Learning styles measured by Kolb's inventory showed no significant change over one year of varied instruction](../claims/kolb-learning-style-stable-over-one-year.md) [~M]: a review reports occupational therapy students showing no change on two style inventories after four quarters of varied formats. It limits the expectation that teaching changes a measured style; it does not measure task performance, and the instruction was not aimed at changing styles.
- [Embedded figures test performance differed by sex and correlated relatively highly with intelligence](../claims/embedded-figures-test-sex-difference-intelligence-correlation.md) [~M] and [Cultural and linguistic background influences measured learning and cognitive styles, and should be considered in style assessment](../claims/cultural-background-influences-learning-style-measurement.md) [~M]: both from one 1985 review (Tamaoka, `q2`); the first reports a relatively high correlation between the embedded figures test and an intelligence test, the second that cultural groups differ in measured style and that tests in English limit conclusions. They qualify any use of a style score: it may be partly an ability or language score. Neither prints an effect size.
- [Schools overwhelmingly reward executive styles and confuse style with level of intellect](../claims/schools-reward-executive-styles.md) [~W]: an assertion in Sternberg's chapter (`q1`), no data. It is the reason to check that an assessment rewards the goal and not one way of working.

Learners, measures and settings differ across these claims (fifth graders on comprehension tests, adults in virtual reality, adolescents learning English vocabulary, reviews of style inventories), so do not rank them by effect label. None measured delayed task performance after teaching an approach.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a capability on a kind of task (an organised lab report, accurate multi-step problem solving, a set of ideas in a brainstorm), unaided or with a stated aid, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to work with friends, to finish quickly, to avoid being wrong in public, to be good at a subject they already like. A learner who values working with others may resist solo analytic tasks for that reason, not for want of an approach; a learner who has been told they are "a hands-on learner" may value keeping that identity. Agreement and divergence are both observations.

For example, a field-dependent-seeming secondary student may want to do well in history, which they enjoy, while the designer's objective is an organised science lab report. Teaching the report frame serves the designer's objective; showing that the same frame organises a history source analysis connects it to what the learner values. Record both, so success on the lab report is not read as the learner's goal met.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation against matching should weaken if a study using the crossover design, with learners classified by field dependence or reflectivity and randomly assigned to matched or mismatched tasks, found that matched learners learned more on a common measure. The "build the missing approach" proposal should be dropped or narrowed if supported practice in the less-used approach produced no better task performance than ordinary practice, or if gains appeared only while the frame or check was present. If style measures turned out to be mostly ability or language measures for a given population, the observation step should rely on task attempts alone. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the habit is too fixed" after the fact.

Matching, building an approach, compensating with a job aid, and a change in the measured style are separate claims. The present evidence establishes none of the session counts, timings or fading points above.

## Description
Teachers commonly speak of students' **learning styles** — preferred ways of engaging with material — but the term can misleadingly imply more cross-situational consistency than actually exists: a student who prefers diagrams to outlines can typically still learn well from either, given the chance (Loo, 2004; Stahl, 2002). **Cognitive styles** are a more specific and better-evidenced construct: relatively stable (though not perfectly so) individual differences in *how* a person typically perceives, remembers, and solves problems, rather than a mere preference (Zhang & Sternberg, 2006).

Two well-studied cognitive styles are **field dependence/independence** and **impulsivity/reflectivity**. **Field-dependent** individuals perceive patterns holistically, as a whole; **field-independent** individuals are more inclined to analyze a pattern's parts separately (Witkin, Moore, Goodenough, & Cox, 1977; Zhang & Sternberg, 2005). The distinction correlates with school preferences: field-dependent students tend to work better in groups and prefer open-ended fields like literature and history, while field-independent students tend to work better alone and prefer highly analytic fields like math and science — though these are only tendencies, with plenty of individual exceptions. An **impulsive** cognitive style reacts quickly but makes comparatively more errors; a **reflective** style reacts more slowly but makes fewer errors (Evans, 2004). Reflectivity suits many academic demands better — mathematical problem-solving and certain reading tasks specifically benefit from it — but some classroom-relevant skills actually develop better with some impulsivity: being a good cooperative-learning partner may depend on responding spontaneously to others' suggestions, and effective teamwork in sports may depend on not stopping to reflect on every move.

There are two distinct ways to use knowledge of a student's cognitive style (Pritchard, 2005). The more obvious approach is to **build on existing style strengths**: a field-independent, reflective student can be encouraged toward relatively analytic, independent tasks; a field-dependent, impulsive student toward more social or spontaneous ones. The less obvious, and often more valuable, approach is to **deliberately build balance where a student lacks it**: a student low in field independence may need explicit help organizing and analyzing academic tasks (e.g., structuring a lab report); an already highly reflective student may need encouragement to try ideas more spontaneously, as in a creative-writing exercise.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Enough observation of a student across multiple contexts to distinguish a genuine, relatively stable cognitive style from a momentary preference or a single task's demands
- Teachers must hold a dynamic rather than static view of intelligence and diagnose students' style preferences.
#### Constraints
- Field dependence/independence and impulsivity/reflectivity are tendencies, not fixed categories — a meaningful fraction of students will contradict the general trend for their style, so styles should never be used to "lock" a student into one mode of learning or to substitute for the student's own expressed preferences and choices
- The reflective/field-independent profile is not simply "better" — it fits some academic tasks well and others (spontaneous cooperative work, quick teamwork) poorly
- Some remediation of weaknesses is probably possible but not always; compensation mechanisms may be needed instead.

### Target Learners
- Students whose task performance seems to depend more on *how* they characteristically process information than on effort or general ability
- Students who could benefit from deliberately practicing the style they currently lack, not only tasks that flatter their existing style
- All students across diverse backgrounds and preparation levels

### Target Learning Objectives
- Task and activity design that matches a student's current cognitive-style strengths where efficient
- Deliberately built cognitive flexibility, for students whose style creates a real limitation in specific academic domains (e.g., an impulsive style undermining multi-step math problem-solving)
- Optimizing application of intelligence in and out of school

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Multiple Intelligences Theory](../theories/multiple-intelligences-theory.md) [~M] — critics (Morgan, 1996, cited on that page) argue MI theory's proposed "intelligences" may in part simply relabel existing cognitive-style research rather than identify genuinely separate abilities, suggesting real conceptual overlap between the two frameworks
