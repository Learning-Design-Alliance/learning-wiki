# Character Education

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 22 claims (14 for, 7 mixed, 1 against) · 10 studies (4 theoretical, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 10 report an effect size · 21 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For school-age learners (`child`, `adolescent`, `pre-k-12`), and in a narrower form for university students (`postsecondary`), character is expected to develop when a school or programme makes ethical understanding, caring response and concrete moral action part of everyday school life over a `term-plus` horizon: the same relationships and conflict-resolution routines across staff, regular democratic class meetings, service or collaborative projects tied to reflection, and moral problems worked through in specific situations inside ordinary subjects. That is expected to change observed behaviour (`behavioural` outcomes: conduct, violence, absence) and civic commitment more than a separate civic or moral subject on its own, or than teaching a list of rules by recitation, example, reward or punishment. The target is `attitude-motivation` and a `complex-skill` (judging and acting in a real situation), not the ability to state the right answer to a hypothetical dilemma.

The relationship is conditional on breadth (one enthusiastic classroom inside a punitive or competitive school is expected to reach less far), on whether learners act as well as discuss, on whether reflection connects the action to a moral question, and on which outcome is counted: the programme evaluations below find behaviour effects more often than effects on knowledge, attitudes and values or on achievement. **No claim in the wiki compares this integrated, schoolwide approach with a separate character or civic course for the same learners.** What the wiki has is (a) What Works Clearinghouse reviews of three branded programmes, each against usual practice or a narrower condition, and (b) philosophical arguments (Dewey, read by Liu 2014; the Naval et al. 2022 review of university civic learning) for why the integrated form should be preferred. So the design below is a default, labelled step by step.

This page folds in two thin pages (2026-10-07): one recommending character education grounded in virtue development to build civic competence at university, and one recommending, after Dewey, that character be cultivated indirectly through all of school life rather than in separate moral education courses. Their recommendations appear as steps 3, 5 and 7 and as rows in the situation table.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier pages' guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal unless a claim is linked beside it; quantities are untested proposals.

1. **Choose the scope first: a school, a grade or year group, or a programme, not one class.** Agree a short set of shared commitments (cooperation, care, fairness) with all adults who meet the learners, including support staff, and how each will respond to a conflict. Schoolwide programmes with several components have WWC-reviewed evidence on behaviour against usual practice ([Positive Action, behaviour](../claims/positive-action-positive-effects-behavior.md) [+M]; [Caring School Community, behaviour](../claims/csc-potentially-positive-behavior-effects.md) [+M]); whether breadth itself is the active part is untested.
2. **Frame discipline as conflict resolution.** When a rule is broken, the response is a short conversation about who was affected and what would repair it, not a punishment alone; distinguish moral matters (harm, fairness) from conventions (lining up, raising hands) when you explain a rule ([Turiel's Social Domain Theory](../theories/turiels-social-domain-theory.md)). Untested proposal, from the earlier page.
3. **Work moral problems in specific situations, not fixed virtue lists.** Once a week (a proposal: 20–30 minutes), take a real problem from the class, school or a text, ask what each option would do to whom, and decide; teach a virtue in relation to the situation where it applies, and let learners see two virtues pull against each other. From the folded Dewey page: [moral knowledge is a product of moral inquiry](../claims/moral-knowledge-product-of-moral-inquiry.md) [+W] and [virtues are taught in relation to specific situations](../claims/dewey-virtues-not-fixed-meaning.md) [~W] (both philosophical arguments, no data). Kohlberg-style dilemma discussion is the tested neighbour: a meta-analysis of 55 studies is reported second-hand to show a moderate to significant effect ([dilemma discussion](../claims/dilemma-discussion-meta-analysis-moderate-effect.md) [+W]); Liu argues for real problems over hypothetical dilemmas.
4. **Hold regular class meetings where learners help set rules and settle disagreements.** A proposal for dose: weekly, 15–30 minutes, with a standing agenda (appreciations, one problem, one decision, a check on last week's decision). Early in the year the teacher chairs and models; by mid-year learners chair in rotation with the teacher as a participant. Untested proposal; the earlier page names democratic practice as "a significant, recurring mechanism", and no claim isolates it.
5. **Let learners act, not only discuss: tie a service or collaborative project to the curriculum and to reflection.** One project a term (a proposal), with a defined need, a role for every learner, and a written or spoken reflection that links what happened to a moral question from step 3. A WWC review of one quasi-experiment found an ethics curriculum plus service learning ahead of senior English classes on knowledge, attitudes and values ([Building Decision Skills](../claims/bds-service-learning-potentially-positive-kav.md) [+M]), and ahead of service learning alone only on ethical awareness and ethical perspective ([curriculum adds beyond service alone](../claims/bds-curriculum-adds-beyond-service-learning-alone.md) [~M]). Service learning in integrated projects is reported second-hand with self-reported gains ([service learning](../claims/service-learning-improves-civic-and-personal-outcomes.md) [+W]); in Chicago high schools, service learning and in-class civic learning were the experiences most strongly associated with civic commitment two years later, controlling for prior commitment ([Sporte & Kahne](../claims/service-learning-and-in-class-civic-learning-strongest-predictors-civic-commitment.md) [+M], associational).
6. **Weave the moral question into ordinary subjects.** In literature, history and science, teach the subject itself and, where the material raises one, name the question about the common good it raises; do not attach a moral lesson to every text. From the folded Dewey page and the university page: a dedicated civic subject [gives necessary knowledge but is argued to be insufficient alone](../claims/civic-subject-alone-insufficient.md) [+W] (authorial argument); [didactic character education is criticised for de-emphasising reflection and judgement](../claims/character-education-didacticism-critique-dewey-bridge.md) [+W].
7. **Make the adults the model.** At university (the folded civic-competence page), staff and professors who hold a social view of their own profession act as role models; build leadership, teamwork and prosocial behaviour into curricular, extracurricular and research work. In school, adults show the conflict-resolution routine in their own disagreements. Untested proposal.
8. **Plan the arc across a year and hand over.** Early sessions (first 6–8 weeks, a proposal): the teacher chairs meetings, names the moral question in each problem, and structures reflection with two or three prompts. Middle: learners chair and propose problems; prompts drop to one. Late: learners run a project and its reflection with the teacher on request. Move a group to the next stage when most meetings reach a decision the group then keeps without adult enforcement. The point of the arc is that reflection becomes a habit, which is argued to need supportive social arrangements ([reflective methods internalised as habit](../claims/reflective-methods-internalized-as-habit.md) [+W], theoretical). Thresholds untested.
9. **Count behaviour and civic commitment separately from knowledge, attitudes and achievement.** Record conduct incidents, absence and participation, and survey commitment and attitudes, at the start and at the end of each year. Programme evaluations found behaviour effects without discernible effects on knowledge, attitudes and values or on achievement ([Caring School Community: knowledge, attitudes and values](../claims/csc-no-discernible-kav-effects.md) [~M]; [academic achievement](../claims/csc-no-discernible-academic-effects.md) [~M]), so one outcome does not stand for the others.

When a condition is not met: if the school will not adopt a shared approach, run steps 3, 4 and 6 in the classroom you control, expect a smaller reach, and say so; if there is no time for a project, use a classroom responsibility (a role learners hold for the whole term) as the action in step 5.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **How far the approach reaches.** One classroom, a whole school, or a programme with its own curriculum. If unstated, ask: which adults will meet these learners each week, and will they respond to a conflict in the same way?
- **Which outcome counts.** Conduct and violence, civic commitment, knowledge and attitudes about ethics, and achievement moved differently in the programmes reviewed. If unstated, ask: what would you see learners doing differently at the end of the year, and how will you record it?
- **The learners' age and setting.** Primary, secondary and university evidence comes from different programmes and arguments. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, and in what kind of institution?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: conduct incidents and how they ended, attendance, whether a class meeting reached a decision and whether the group kept it, what learners said or wrote when asked what an option would do to whom, what they did in a service project, and survey answers about commitment. "Has good character", "cares about others" and "reasons morally" are inferred states. A learner who gives the right answer in a meeting has shown what they will say in that room, not what they will do in the corridor.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Conduct incidents fall, survey attitudes do not change | Behaviour changed through routines and adult consistency, not through belief; the survey does not measure what changed; ceiling on attitudes | Ask learners to explain a recent repair conversation; compare classes with stronger and weaker routines. |
| Learners give model answers in meetings, behaviour unchanged | Learners know what the adults want to hear; the problems are hypothetical, not theirs; no action follows a decision | Switch to a real class problem with a decision the group then has to keep; watch whether it is kept. |
| A service project is enjoyed, reflection is thin | Reflection prompts are generic; the moral question was never named; the project felt like an outing | Ask one specific question (who was helped, who was left out, what would you do differently) and compare answers. |
| One group improves, another does not (for example boys and girls) | Different starting rates; the programme content fits one group's concerns; measurement differs | Check baselines by group and ask each what the programme felt like to them. |
| Effects fade the next year | Routines stopped when staff changed; habit not yet internal; new setting without the same arrangements | Record which routines continued and compare learners who moved to settings with and without them. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests the integrated, schoolwide form against a separate course, or tests class meetings or conflict-resolution discipline on their own. The claims below test branded programmes against usual practice, or argue for the integrated form.

- [Positive Action has positive effects on elementary school students' behavior](../claims/positive-action-positive-effects-behavior.md) [+M]: a WWC review aggregating one randomised trial and one quasi-experiment in elementary schools (56 schools, over 2,660 students); domain average effect size 0.51, improvement index +19 percentile points. Its [academic achievement domain](../claims/positive-action-positive-effects-academic-achievement.md) [+M] averaged 0.37 (+14), with grade retention and absence counted in it; but in the Hawaii trial alone, [state test differences were not significant](../claims/positive-action-no-significant-effect-state-test-scores.md) [~M] (0.17 maths, 0.19 reading), and [serious violence fell for boys but not girls](../claims/positive-action-violence-reduced-boys-not-girls.md) [~M]. Supports schoolwide programmes for behaviour; one programme, one country, two studies.
- [The Child Development Project/Caring School Community shows potentially positive effects on student behavior](../claims/csc-potentially-positive-behavior-effects.md) [+M]: WWC review of two K–6 studies (a randomised trial with reservations and a quasi-experiment), average +8 percentile points, range –2 to +27; no standardised effect size printed. The same studies show [no discernible effects on knowledge, attitudes and values](../claims/csc-no-discernible-kav-effects.md) [~M] and [none on academic achievement](../claims/csc-no-discernible-academic-effects.md) [~M]. **This is the main limit on the model:** a caring, schoolwide programme of the kind the earlier page describes moved behaviour a little and did not discernibly move the attitudes and values the page names as its target.
- [Building Decision Skills combined with service learning has potentially positive effects on knowledge, attitudes and values](../claims/bds-service-learning-potentially-positive-kav.md) [+M]: one quasi-experiment, 283 twelfth graders in an elective ethics-plus-service course against senior English classes; domain average 0.36, not statistically significant, rated potentially positive. Against service learning alone, the curriculum added only [ethical awareness and ethical perspective](../claims/bds-curriculum-adds-beyond-service-learning-alone.md) [~M], and there was [no significant effect on self-esteem, general social responsibility or anticipated community participation](../claims/bds-null-self-esteem-general-responsibility.md) [-M]. It supports pairing explicit ethical reasoning with action, and limits what service is expected to change.
- [SEL programs improve social emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses of universal programmes (213 K–12 school programmes; 63 early-childhood studies), with skill gains larger than gains in attitudes, behaviour or achievement; its table counts 8 distinct studies behind the claim and its narrower claims. Carried here from the neighbouring SEL family; it does not test moral or civic outcomes.
- [Service learning experiences and in-class civic learning opportunities ... larger impact on commitment to civic participation](../claims/service-learning-and-in-class-civic-learning-strongest-predictors-civic-commitment.md) [+M]: a longitudinal survey of 3,805 students in 47 Chicago high schools, controlling for prior commitment; associational, so "impact" in its title is stronger than the design allows. [Experiencing civic community at home, in the neighborhood and at school](../claims/experiencing-civic-community-fosters-commitment.md) [+M] comes from the same study.
- [Service-learning within integrated projects is associated with improvements in self-efficacy, self-esteem, social responsibility and community participation](../claims/service-learning-improves-civic-and-personal-outcomes.md) [+W]: the Naval et al. (2022) review reporting other studies second-hand, self-reported, no sample details; its table counts 3 distinct studies across it and its narrower claims. The Building Decision Skills null on self-esteem and general responsibility qualifies it.
- The philosophical arguments: [a civic subject alone is insufficient](../claims/civic-subject-alone-insufficient.md) [+W] (Naval et al.); [moral knowledge is a product of moral inquiry](../claims/moral-knowledge-product-of-moral-inquiry.md) [+W], [didacticism critique and Dewey as a bridge](../claims/character-education-didacticism-critique-dewey-bridge.md) [+W] and [virtues have no fixed meaning outside situations](../claims/dewey-virtues-not-fixed-meaning.md) [~W] (Liu 2014, reading Dewey); [reflective methods must become habit, supported by social arrangements](../claims/reflective-methods-internalized-as-habit.md) [+W] (Willower 1992). They give reasons for the integrated, situational form; none reports data.
- [Kohlberg's dilemma discussion had a moderate to significant effect](../claims/dilemma-discussion-meta-analysis-moderate-effect.md) [+W]: a meta-analysis of 55 studies reported second-hand in Liu 2014, no effect size printed; it bears on moral reasoning, not behaviour.

Learners, programmes and outcomes differ across these claims (US elementary schools, twelfth graders, Chicago high schools, universities), so do not rank them by effect label. All programme evidence is from WWC reviews of one or two studies each, against usual practice.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually observed conduct and civic participation over a year, sometimes ethical reasoning or attitudes. Elicit separately what learners want: to be treated fairly, to have a say in rules, to stop a conflict that affects them, to do something that matters to people outside school. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values having a say will judge the programme by whether meeting decisions are kept; one who values fairness may read a schoolwide values list as one more set of adult rules.

For example, a secondary school may aim at fewer exclusions, while a group of learners wants the canteen queue to be fair. Taking the queue as a class-meeting problem, deciding a rule and keeping it serves both; record whether exclusions changed and whether the learners thought the decision was theirs.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if schoolwide programmes with class meetings, service and conflict-resolution discipline show no more change in behaviour or civic commitment than a separate character course with the same learners, or if the behaviour effects found by WWC reviews disappear in independent replications. If curriculum alone matches curriculum plus service, step 5's action component is a preference, not a requirement. If attitude and value measures keep showing no discernible effect while behaviour changes, the model should say it changes conduct and say less about character. Do not protect the model by calling every null "not schoolwide enough" after the fact.

Behaviour, civic commitment, ethical reasoning, attitudes, achievement and what learners do after they leave are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose for class meetings, no project length and no stage-change criterion.

## Description
Character education goes beyond teaching a list of rules ("always tell the truth," "obey the teacher"). Effective programs integrate three components: cognitive understanding of ethical concepts, emotional/caring responsiveness to others, and concrete moral action — recognizing that mature moral behavior requires both conscious reasoning and embodied, intuitive response in the moment a choice actually arises. Programs that work well tend to operate at the schoolwide level, not just within individual classrooms: all staff — teachers, administrators, and support staff alike — orient around positive relationships, with cooperation and mutual care as the explicit theme rather than competition. Discipline in these settings is framed around conflict resolution rather than obedience or punishment, and democratic practices (class meetings where students help set rules and resolve disagreements) are a significant, recurring mechanism rather than an occasional activity.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sustained, schoolwide commitment rather than a standalone unit or lesson — the approach depends on consistency across settings and staff, not just within one classroom
- Structures for democratic participation (regular class meetings, student input into rules) and for service or collaborative projects that give students real practice acting on ethical commitments, not just discussing them
- An excellent group of educators for implementation; professors who possess and promote a social vision of professional work as role models
- Regular courses that connect their content to the common good so moral significance can be reflected across the curriculum
#### Constraints
