# Multiple Methods of Assessment

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (9 for, 6 mixed, 1 against) · 26 studies (6 causal, 6 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 theoretical, 2 qualitative, 2 design, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 26 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **how several kinds of evidence about one learning goal are planned, read together and turned into a decision**. When a teacher or designer must judge whether a learner has reached a goal, a judgement drawn from two or more methods that call for the same capability in different ways (a written answer and a short oral explanation; a product and the learner's account of the decisions behind it; a test and an observed performance; the teacher's, the learner's own and a peer's reading of the same work against one set of criteria) is expected to be more accurate (`assessment-accuracy`) than a judgement drawn from one method, **when the learner's result on that one method depends on something other than the goal**: reading and writing load, a symbol system the learner does not use, test anxiety, an unfamiliar context, or a capability that only shows with help. Where learners also get a bounded choice between formats held to one standard, the expected further gain is in motivation (`motivation-affect`), not in achievement. Adding peer and self-assessment as methods can also raise later performance (`general-achievement`), because learners apply the criteria, not because the evidence becomes more accurate.

The relationship is conditional on four things: **every method must sample the same goal** (a poster and a written explanation of photosynthesis may test drawing and writing more than photosynthesis); **the criteria must be common across methods** and the people applying them calibrated, or the variation added is noise; **the format must not itself be the goal** (if the goal is writing an argued essay, an oral alternative removes the construct rather than a barrier); and **a second method costs time** for the learner and the marker, so it pays only where the first is likely to mislead for this learner or where the decision has stakes.

**No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as a whole.** None compares decisions made from several methods with decisions made from one, against an independent measure of what learners can do, and none compares a choice of format with an assigned format on achievement. The nearest claims argue or illustrate that one format can hide what a learner knows (a review's argument about verbal tests, a design study of family-grounded tasks with young children, a theoretical argument for assisted assessment), show that peer assessment raises performance in a meta-analysis, show that autonomy support raises motivation, and limit the relationship (untrained self-assessment is inaccurate; raters differ in severity; matching format to a "learning style" does not help). The design below is therefore a labelled default.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Universal Design for Learning](universal-design-for-learning.md) owns the planning audit that offers several ways to show an outcome, held to one standard, so that a barrier does not stop a learner; this page owns what is done with the evidence once several methods exist: whether they agree, what to do when they do not, and when a second method is worth its cost. [Validity, Reliability and Bias in Classroom Assessment](validity-reliability-and-bias-in-classroom-assessment.md) owns the measurement properties of any one score; this page uses them and adds the combination of scores. [Assessment for Learning](assessment-for-learning.md) owns how evidence is used during instruction, with shared criteria and a revision step; [Competency-Based Assessment](competency-based-assessment.md) owns the threshold judgement and assessor agreement for a "demonstrated / not yet" decision. [Autonomy](autonomy.md) owns choice within structure. [Summative Assessment](summative-assessment.md) and [Standardized Test Fairness and Bias](standardized-test-fairness-and-bias.md) hold end-of-sequence judgement and large-scale cases. The [Authentic Assessment](../patterns/authentic-assessment.md) pattern is one design that uses a performance plus feedback and revision as the unit's assessment; the [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) pattern is the reusable loop for reading a response and acting on it.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (clear targets, comparable criteria, intentional evidence design, use of results, and its four examples), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name one goal per judgement, then decide which formats are part of it.** Write the capability as the learner will show it (verb, content, conditions). Mark which response modes belong to the goal (writing for a written argument; speaking for an oral presentation) and which do not (writing for a science concept). Only modes that do not belong to the goal may vary. Untested; from the earlier page ("clear learning targets"; "some skills still require specific formats").
2. **Choose two methods per goal, rarely more than three, chosen to fail differently.** Pair a method that depends on reading and writing with one that does not (a short oral explanation, a demonstration, a labelled drawing, an observed task), or a product with the learner's account of the decisions behind it. A single verbal test can underestimate what some learners know ([Typical verbal achievement tests may underestimate students' subject-matter knowledge because of a mismatch between the test's symbolic form and students' symbolic encoding](../claims/verbal-achievement-tests-may-underestimate-via-symbolic-mismatch.md) [+W], an argument in a review, not a tested result). The number of methods is an untested proposal; the earlier page warned against "assessment sprawl".
3. **Write one set of criteria for all methods, with an example of each level in each format.** Learners see the criteria and examples before they start. Markers score one example in each format together before marking, and double-mark a sample of about one in five (an untested proportion). Self-assessing against a rubric improved work in two non-randomised studies ([Self-assessing against a rubric improved the quality of student work in two non-randomised studies, one of which also found more stress and performance-avoidance](../claims/rubrics-improve-student-work.md) [+M]); raters still differ in severity ([Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis](../claims/raters-differed-severity-mfrm.md) [~M], open-ended items in one test).
4. **Read the methods together, with a stated rule.** If the methods agree, act on the result. If they disagree, do not average them: ask which demand differs between them (language, speed, context, help available) and give a short third check that removes that demand (2–5 minutes, one item). Record which method each decision rests on. The rule is an untested proposal; what to look for is in the observation table below.
5. **Add self- and peer assessment as methods only after learners have practised judging against the criteria.** Show a model of someone assessing work against the criteria, have learners judge two or three examples and compare their judgement with the teacher's, then use their judgements. Untrained self-assessment is inaccurate and tends to overestimate ([Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]); peer assessment raised performance by a small average amount in a meta-analysis ([Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]). Treat learners' judgements as evidence about their calibration as well as about the work; do not use untrained peer grades in a final mark.
6. **Where learners choose a format, bound the choice.** Offer two or three formats, each listed with what it must contain and the common criteria; check before release that each option demands the same capability at the same level, and that none is visibly easier or better supported. Bounded choice can raise motivation ([Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], a narrative review and a meta-analysis on rewards; not a test of assessment choice). Do not assign formats by a learner's "style" ([Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-M], tested for instruction, carried to assessment format). From the earlier page ("uneven rigor", "learner agency in assessment").
7. **Feed every method's result into the next instructional step.** After each round, name the one goal most learners have not shown in any method and plan the next lesson around it; do not collect evidence no one will use. Untested; from the earlier page ("use of results").
8. **Plan across the unit, not one task.** A default arc, all quantities untested proposals:
   - *Early sessions (first third of the unit)*: two low-stakes methods per session, each 3–5 minutes: a written exit item and a spoken or drawn one ("explain to a partner", a sketch with labels). The teacher chooses the methods; criteria and examples are on display.
   - *Middle sessions*: learners practise self-assessment on one piece of work a week against the criteria and compare it with the teacher's reading; one peer review with the criteria; one task where learners choose between two or three formats.
   - *Late sessions*: the summative judgement rests on at least two methods for each goal: one common format where the format belongs to the goal, plus one chosen format or an observed performance; a learner whose methods disagree near the cut gets a third, short check before the decision.
   - *Moving on*: a learner moves to the next goal once two different methods both show the goal at the criterion; one method at criterion and one below means a targeted third check, not a repeat of the same test.

When a condition is not met: if no marker time is available for a second method, use the second method only for learners whose first result is near a decision point or out of line with their other work; if the format is part of the goal, keep it for everyone and vary only support, time or context.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Which response modes belong to the goal.** If the goal includes writing, speaking or performing in a particular way, that mode stays fixed and only other demands vary; if it does not, the format is free to change. If unstated, ask: if a learner showed this capability by speaking, drawing or doing instead of writing, would you accept it as the same capability?
- **What the result will decide, and how much rides on it.** A check that steers tomorrow's lesson needs one quick method and a second only where it misleads; a grade, placement or certification near a cut needs at least two methods and calibrated markers. If unstated, ask: what decision will this result change, for whom, and can it be reversed?
- **Who can collect and read a second kind of evidence, and how much time they have.** An oral check costs teacher time per learner; peer and self-assessment cost learner time and training; a portfolio costs marking time. If unstated, ask: who will look at the second piece of evidence, for how many learners, and how many minutes each?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a particular method under stated conditions: the task, the mode (written, spoken, drawn, performed), the time allowed, the help available, who scored it and against which criteria, and the score. "Understands the concept", "can do it but cannot write it" and "was helped" are inferred states. Two methods agreeing does not prove the inference; they may share a demand (both in English, both timed).

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Weak written answer, strong spoken explanation of the same goal | Writing or reading load hides knowledge; the spoken exchange added cues or help; the spoken explanation is fluent but would not survive a precise question | Give a written item with minimal reading load (labelled diagram, short answer) and a spoken item with no prompts; ask one precise follow-up question in each. |
| Strong on the test, weak on the performance task | Recognition without use; the performance adds coordination, time pressure or an unfamiliar context; the performance rubric is scored more harshly | Give a short performance on a familiar case; have a second marker score the performance; ask the learner to talk through one decision. |
| Self-assessment much higher than the teacher's reading | Untrained or miscalibrated self-judgement; the learner reads the criteria differently; the teacher's reading missed something | Ask the learner to point to the evidence for each criterion in their work; compare their judgement of an example with yours. |
| Peer and teacher readings disagree | Peer lacks domain knowledge; criteria are ambiguous; teacher's severity differs from the group's | Have both mark a common example; check where on the rubric the readings split. |
| Learners who chose one format score systematically lower | That option is harder or less supported; weaker learners chose it; markers judge that format more harshly | Compare the same learners' results in a common format; have the options double-marked across formats. |
| Correct only with a hint | The capability is developing; the hint carried the decision; the task was unclear | Give a matched item unaided with clearer instructions; record the level of hint needed across two occasions. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Each extra check is itself practice and changes what it measures, so record it, and keep explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares judgements made from several methods with judgements made from one, or compares a choice of assessment format with an assigned one on learning. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

- [Typical verbal achievement tests may underestimate students' subject-matter knowledge because of a mismatch between the test's symbolic form and students' symbolic encoding](../claims/verbal-achievement-tests-may-underestimate-via-symbolic-mismatch.md) [+W]: a 1986 literature review (Shavelson, Webb & Lehman) contends that tests in a predominantly verbal code may place irrelevant demands on test takers and underestimate some students' subject-matter knowledge. Coded `q1`, theoretical; the review offers it as a contention to be examined, not a tested result. Its closest indirect evidence, that people acquire different knowledge from a map than from navigation, is recorded in [People acquire different knowledge from different media, the closest indirect evidence for symbolic encoding specificity](../claims/different-media-yield-different-knowledge-encoding.md) [+W] (`q2`, reported second-hand in the same review). This is the reason to add a second method in another mode; it does not show that doing so changes decisions.
- [Funds-of-knowledge-grounded tasks reveal computational thinking that decontextualized assessment misses](../claims/funds-of-knowledge-tasks-reveal-computational-thinking.md) [+W]: a design study with 13 K–2 children in a low-income district; tasks built from family practices (bus riding, a healthcare visit) and run as play-based clinical interviews showed sequencing, data reasoning and, for some children, abstraction. The claim page says there was no comparison with a standard assessment for the same children, so "misses" is an inference, and the approach was resource-intensive. Coded `q2`, design `r2`. It supports a context-grounded, interview-based method as a second source for young children; it does not show it is more accurate.
- [Static individual assessment misses developing functions; ZPD-based assessment reveals potential competencies](../claims/static-assessment-misses-emerging-functions.md) [+W]: a theoretical argument (Seng 1997, after Vygotsky) that unaided testing shows only matured functions, and assisted performance shows developing ones. Coded `q1`. It is the reason to record what a learner does with help as a separate observation; it does not say how to combine it with an unaided score.
- [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 54 control-group studies (141 effect sizes) across school and university: peer assessment improved performance against no assessment (g = 0.31) and against teacher assessment (g = 0.28), and did not differ significantly from self-assessment (g = 0.23); moderators tested did not change the effect. Coded `q4 i1`, rigour not established from the text read. It supports adding peer assessment as a method for its effect on performance; it says nothing about whether peer judgements are accurate enough to count in a grade.
- [Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]: a narrative review of self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci 2000), a meta-analysis of 128 experiments on rewards (d = −0.4 for expected tangible rewards on intrinsic motivation), and a conceptual framework that names choice of presentation media as procedural autonomy support. The outcome is motivation, not achievement, and no entry tests a choice of assessment format. The claim's own Discussion says autonomy works best with structure, and premature autonomy can make novices anxious.
- **Limiting:** [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]: in a randomised experiment, 80 Dutch pre-university students (about 15) who studied modelling examples of self-assessment judged their genetics problem-solving more accurately than those who did not (ηp² = .10); a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies, read as an abstract, found student and teacher marks agreed inconsistently, more closely in advanced courses and better-designed studies. Self-assessment adds a method only once learners have been taught to use the criteria.
- **Limiting:** [Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis](../claims/raters-differed-severity-mfrm.md) [~M]: three raters scoring open-ended items differed significantly in severity (one study, `q3`, associational). [In one community-college lab class, six scorers rating six teams with an engineering rubric differed by a mean of 1.2 of 9 points, which the authors read as a sign the rubric may not be reliable](../claims/engineering-rubric-low-scoring-reliability.md) [~W] is one class, with no reliability coefficient. Methods scored by judgement add rater variation; without calibration a second method can add noise as well as evidence.
- **Limiting:** [Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-M]: a systematic review and a randomised experiment with fifth graders found no learner-style × modality interaction for instruction. Carried here to assessment format: it is evidence against offering or assigning formats by "style". It is not evidence against offering an alternative mode to get round a barrier the goal does not include, which is a different claim.
- [Math Anxiety Degrades Performance](../claims/math-anxiety-degrades-performance.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 223 studies found a correlation of r = −.28 between mathematics anxiety and achievement, across nearly every moderator. Correlational; it does not test whether a less pressured method gives a truer picture, only that one pressured method may confound anxiety with achievement.
- [Self-assessing against a rubric improved the quality of student work in two non-randomised studies, one of which also found more stress and performance-avoidance](../claims/rubrics-improve-student-work.md) [+M]: middle-school writers (N = 162) and pre-service teachers (N = 218); the second study also found more accurate self-assessment with a rubric, and more stress. It supports common criteria that learners use, and warns of a cost.
- [Preschool children demonstrate behavioral knowledge of classification, one-to-one correspondence, and seriation during everyday free play with everyday materials](../claims/free-play-reveals-behavioral-math-knowledge.md) [+W]: one observed play episode with two children, in which a teacher recognised different stages of seriation. It illustrates observation in play as a method for young children; it is not a comparison.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (school and university peer assessment, pre-university self-assessment, children in play, reviews of testing), and only four print an effect size, each on a different outcome, so they cannot be ranked against each other.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is a decision about a learner (what to teach next, a grade, a certificate) that matches what the learner can do with the goal, in the conditions that matter. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the assessment: to show what they know in a way that feels fair, to get the grade with least risk, to have a product they can use or show, to avoid speaking in front of others. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values a safe grade may pick the format they think is easiest, which is evidence about the options' equality as well as about the learner; one who dreads speaking may do worse orally for reasons that are not the goal.

For example, an adult apprentice electrician may want a portfolio of real installations to show future employers, while the course objective is passing a written regulations exam. A photographed installation with a short spoken account of the regulations it follows serves the apprentice's goal and gives a second method for the regulations goal; the written exam stays where the qualification requires it. Record both, so that a strong portfolio is not read as exam readiness, or a weak exam as weak practice.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing decisions from several methods with decisions from one, against an independent measure of capability, show no gain in accuracy, or show that the added rater variation outweighs the added evidence. If learners given a choice of format, held to one rubric, reach the goal no more often and are no more motivated than learners assigned one format, the choice step is a preference only. If calibrated peer judgements prove as accurate as teachers' for a kind of task, peer review can carry more of the second reading. If a single well-designed method with low language load predicts later performance as well as two methods, the second method is a cost to cut. Do not protect the model by calling every disagreement between methods "evidence of hidden knowledge"; sometimes the lower result is the accurate one.

Agreement between methods, accuracy of a decision, later achievement and motivation are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no number of methods, no rule for combining them and no evidence that a choice of format improves achievement.

## Description
Using multiple methods of assessment means gathering evidence of learning through more than one type of task, representation, or performance. Instead of relying on a single assessment mode to stand in for all understanding, instructors use a mix of written, oral, visual, practical, performance-based, or reflective evidence to build a more credible picture of learner progress. ### Context
#### Requirements
- **Clear learning targets**: Multiple assessment methods only help when all formats still point to the same underlying outcome.
- **Comparable criteria**: Learners need transparent standards so different assessment modes are not judged arbitrarily.

- **Use of results**: Multiple methods should improve feedback, interpretation, and instructional adjustment, not just generate more grading.
#### Constraints
- **False equivalence**: Different formats may not provide equally strong evidence for every skill.
- **Assessment sprawl**: Too many methods can create confusion or excessive grading burden.
- **Uneven rigor**: If one option is clearly easier or better supported, flexibility can become inequitable.
- **Surface variety**: Changing format without changing evidence quality does not improve assessment validity.

- **Instructors seeking a fuller evidence picture**: Strong fit when formative and summative decisions need more than one indicator.

- **Fairer demonstration of learning**: Allowing more than one legitimate route to show mastery.
- **Instructional adjustment**: Using different evidence types to identify where support is needed.
- **Learner agency in assessment**: Supporting bounded choice in how understanding is demonstrated.
