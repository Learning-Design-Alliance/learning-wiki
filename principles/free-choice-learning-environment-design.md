# Free-Choice Learning Environment Design

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 unmarked) · 8 studies (3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 8 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Falk and Dierking's research, cited by Ashton, Nelson, and Millward (2018), estimates that only about 5% of a person's lifetime learning occurs in classroom settings — the rest happens across a lifetime in self-selected, informal contexts: museums, hobbies, and everyday exploration. **Free-choice learning environments** are deliberately designed for this reality: they give visitors genuine control over what and how deeply they engage, rather than imposing a fixed sequence or curriculum. This differs in kind from bounded choice inside a fixed curriculum (offering a menu of topics or formats within a course, as in [Learner Choice](autonomy.md)) — free-choice design assumes no externally imposed sequence or objective at all, and success means visitors can curate a coherent experience entirely of their own.

Because attendance and engagement are entirely voluntary, with no external accountability like grades or tests to fall back on, the design must be intrinsically compelling on its own terms — physically, emotionally, and cognitively engaging. The chapter illustrates this with the Exploratorium's Active Prolonged Engagement exhibits, which let visitors test their own theories and lead their own experiments rather than following a prescribed activity sequence, deliberately building in multiple entry points and levels of complexity, multiple valid perspectives instead of a single authoritative reading, and content that connects to whatever prior knowledge and personal interest a given visitor brings.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Multiple entry points and complexity levels within the same exhibit or resource, since visitors arrive with no shared prerequisite sequence
- Content designed to connect to a visitor's own prior knowledge and interests, since there is no captive audience to build a shared foundation with in advance
- Genuinely intrinsic engagement — the design has no recourse to grades, tests, or other external accountability to sustain participation
- A recurring, protected block of school time (the source case used 90 minutes weekly, year-long) with no or minimal grading tied to completion
- A large enough library of challenges (~30 in the source case) that students have genuine choice of topic, pace, and collaboration mode
- Shared materials and tools (e.g., a 3D printer, an online asset repository) accessible without requiring teacher gatekeeping
- Facilitators explicitly trained to observe and drop in occasionally rather than direct, and to route help-seeking students to peer experts before intervening themselves
#### Constraints
- Success cannot be measured against a fixed curriculum's completion or mastery criteria, since there isn't one — evaluation has to look at engagement quality and self-directed depth rather than standardized coverage
- Presenting multiple valid perspectives rather than one authoritative answer requires more design sophistication than a single fixed explanation, and can read as less "complete" by traditional curricular standards
- This is documented as an "extreme case" (the two focal students spent over 50% of their scheduled time on one self-extended project) — it illustrates what becomes *possible* under this infrastructure, not a typical or average outcome
- Requires school-level buy-in to protect ungraded, non-curriculum-aligned time, which is a significant institutional commitment
- Facilitator restraint (not directing) requires deliberate role redefinition; without it, the infrastructure can collapse back into conventional teacher-directed instruction

### Target Learners
- Voluntary, self-directed visitors and learners in museums, science centers, hobbyist communities, and other settings with no compulsory attendance or externally imposed objectives
- Upper-elementary/middle-school students (studied at grades 5-6) with sustained access to the infrastructure across a full school year

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained curiosity, exploration, and self-directed inquiry, rather than mastery of a predetermined body of content
- Personal, self-curated understanding built by connecting new content to a visitor's own existing interests and knowledge

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Informal Learning](../theories/informal-learning.md) [+S] — free-choice learning environments are a concrete institutional embodiment of the same "pulled," self-directed dynamic informal learning theory describes generally
- Interest as relational, practice-based engagement (Azevedo; Dewey) — interest develops through repeated engagement with an object in a social/material context, not as an innate trait
- Learning through problem-solving and breakdown (Dewey; Holzkamp) — disruptions to habitual ways of doing things trigger inspection, hypothesis, testing, and reflection
- Transformative agency (Sewell; Rainio) — student-initiated deviations from a given task become "productive" specifically when the surrounding community recognizes and legitimizes them

# Alternative In-School STEAM Learning Infrastructure

## Description
Interest-driven learning is usually associated with out-of-school settings (makerspaces, clubs, museums) precisely because school structures — grading, fixed pacing, curriculum coverage requirements — tend to crowd it out. This pattern describes an infrastructure that brings interest-driven learning inside the school day: a recurring, ungraded time block; a large library of self-contained "challenges" that can be leveled up, combined, or extended; adult "facilitators" (not "teachers") who observe and rarely intervene; and an expectation that students who progress further become peer experts others are directed to. The result is not that every student pursues a deep interest, but that the infrastructure makes it *possible* for a student's interest to be sparked, sustained, and extended well beyond the original activity — including into forms of learning the designed challenges never anticipated.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Establish a recurring, ungraded weekly time block dedicated to open challenge work, separate from standard curriculum periods.
2. Build or adopt a library of leveled, self-contained challenges spanning multiple STEAM domains, each with embedded optional support resources (tutorials, images) students are not required to use.
3. Train facilitators to circle the room, observe, and respond to direct questions, rather than direct activity or check for "on-task" compliance.
4. Establish a norm that students who progress furthest on a challenge become peer experts, and route help-seeking students to them before a facilitator intervenes.
5. Provide shared materials and tools accessible without teacher gatekeeping (e.g., an online asset repository, a 3D printer), so students can extend a challenge without waiting for facilitator-provided resources.
6. Allow and expect that some students will combine, alter, or extend challenges into self-directed projects beyond the original design; treat this as a sign of infrastructure health, not off-task behavior.
