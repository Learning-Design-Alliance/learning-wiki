# Free-Choice Learning Environment Design

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 17 claims (6 for, 10 mixed, 1 against) · 19 studies (5 review, 4 causal, 4 theoretical, 3 qualitative, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 19 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **open-environment** case: settings where the learner decides whether to come, what to do and how long to stay, with no imposed curriculum, sequence or grade (a museum floor, a science centre, a hobby community, an ungraded weekly studio block inside a school). For a learner in such a setting, an environment that offers several entry points at different levels of complexity, starts from what the learner already knows and cares about, attaches no grades or expected tangible rewards to taking part, and responds to what the learner does is expected to produce longer and more frequent **voluntary** engagement (`persistence`, `motivation-affect`), and, for some learners, self-extended projects that go beyond what the activity was designed to teach. Learning of a particular target (`conceptual-understanding`, `near-transfer`) is a separate outcome: it is expected only where the activity also gives feedback on the target idea, not from free exploration alone.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's starting interest and task knowledge (a learner with neither may sample and leave), on whether anything in the environment responds to an attempt (a person, a peer, the exhibit itself), on how many decisions the learner is left to make, and on which outcome counts. **No claim in the wiki tests a free-choice environment as a whole against a structured or compulsory one.** The nearest evidence is one randomised comparison inside a museum exhibit (responsive feedback against the same materials without it), two meta-analyses on rewards and later free-choice engagement, one school-fixed-effects study of field trips, and qualitative cases.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Autonomy](autonomy.md) holds bounded choice inside a fixed course and the case against controlling incentives; this page applies that model to environments with no fixed course at all and does not repeat it. [Engagement](engagement.md) and [Motivation](motivation.md) hold what raises participation in required work. [Self-Directed Learning](self-directed-learning.md) holds when learner-directed study helps and for whom, and the [Self-Directed Learning pattern](../patterns/self-directed-learning.md) its sequence; this page is about the environment an institution builds, not the learner's planning. [Game-Based Learning](game-based-learning.md) covers a game whose winning move is the target reasoning, which is one way an exhibit can respond to the visitor.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance and that of the in-school STEAM infrastructure page folded into it, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Give every exhibit or challenge a first action that needs no reading and no prior knowledge, finishable in two to five minutes, and at least two deeper levels behind it.** The first loop is what decides whether a passer-by stays. Untested proposal; the earlier page asked for "multiple entry points and complexity levels". In the one comparison in the wiki, average visits to an unfacilitated hands-on exhibit lasted about 1.5 minutes and to the responsive version about 6.25 minutes ([AI-mediated feedback in hands-on exhibits](../claims/ai-mediated-feedback-in-hands-on-exhibits-improves-learning-and-engagement.md) [~M]), which is the range a first loop has to work in.
2. **Open from the learner's own interests and knowledge.** Pose the starting question in everyday terms (what will make this tower fall, what do you want your game to do), and let the learner choose the topic, pace and whether to work alone or with others. Treat an interest as a starting point to go deeper from, not a fixed topic to cater to ([IDC's interest cycle](../claims/interest-cycle-triggering-immersing-extending.md) [+W], a theoretical commentary). Otherwise untested here.
3. **Attach no grade and no expected tangible reward to taking part; give information about what happened instead.** Badges, points or prizes for merely doing an activity the learner already finds interesting are the case where later free-choice engagement fell in a 128-experiment meta-analysis, while a competing 96-experiment meta-analysis found the effect minimal and confined to expected tangible rewards for doing a task; both found verbal praise did not undermine it ([expected tangible rewards and free-choice engagement](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]). A literature-based argument from maker education reaches the same caution about grades ([grading in maker education](../claims/grading-harms-maker-education-outcomes.md) [+W]).
4. **Make the activity respond to each attempt, then fade the response.** Where the outcome of a learner's action can be sensed or shown (a tower falls or stands, a circuit lights, code runs), ask for a prediction, show the result, and offer a short choice of explanations on contrasting cases that each isolate one idea; after a few cases, switch to open building where the activity only reports success or failure. Evidence: with grade-2 children in a museum, this cycle beat the same physical materials with static signage on explaining the principle and on a later unguided building task, with no loss of enjoyment ([AI-mediated feedback in hands-on exhibits](../claims/ai-mediated-feedback-in-hands-on-exhibits-improves-learning-and-engagement.md) [~M]). One study, one topic (structural stability); carrying it to other content is untested.
5. **Design for people explaining to each other.** Make the exhibit or challenge one that a group works at together, with physical materials and a shared display, so visitors talk about why something worked. A report on one museum game-exhibit says learning typically happened when visitors explained to each other, and that physical blocks plus a screen representation were needed for deeper practices ([Oztoc exhibit](../claims/oztoc-physical-blocks-plus-screen-representation-deeper-practices.md) [+W]). For group tasks, prefer a well-defined goal and materials everyone can handle ([well-defined tasks and accessible materials in a museum workshop](../claims/well-defined-tasks-and-accessible-materials-support-shared-creative-influence.md) [~M], one group of four adolescents).
6. **Inside a school, protect a recurring ungraded block and stock it.** The source case ran 90 minutes a week for a school year, with about 30 leveled challenges across STEAM domains, each with optional tutorials, and shared tools (an online asset repository, a 3D printer) learners could use without asking. These are the source case's values, not tested doses. Two students in one such studio extended a single challenge into a year-long project, spending over half their scheduled time on it and solving 23 technical problems the challenge never taught ([choice-rich infrastructure](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [~M]); the authors chose them as an extreme case, and most students did not go this far.
7. **Facilitators observe and drop in; they do not direct.** Train adults to circle the room, answer direct questions, and send a learner who asks for help to a peer who has gone further on that challenge before stepping in themselves. Do not check for "on-task" compliance. Untested proposal, from the folded STEAM infrastructure page; without the role being redefined, the block tends to slide back into teacher-directed instruction (the earlier page).
8. **Treat extending, combining or changing a challenge as a sign the environment is working.** Allow learners to leave the designed task, and make the tools for going further reachable. Same single case as step 6 ([choice-rich infrastructure](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [~M]).
9. **End each visit or session with a one-minute look back: what did you find out, what would you try next.** Learners left to their own problem-solving often skip checking what a failed attempt taught them, and often abandon projects ([youth skip the assess phase](../claims/youth-skip-assess-phase-free-choice-heuristics.md) [~W]; [minimal persistence in everyday problem-solving](../claims/minimal-persistence-everyday-problem-solving-youth.md) [-W], one qualitative study of urban high-school youth). The prompt is an untested proposal; keep it spoken or optional, not a form to complete.
10. **Plan an arc across sessions where learners come back.** Proposal, untested: in the first two sessions, a five-minute tour of the challenge library and tools and a level-1 start for everyone, with the facilitator checking in on each learner once; in middle sessions, learners choose their own challenges and the facilitator checks in only when asked or when someone has been stuck for about ten minutes; from mid-year, learners who have finished a challenge's top level act as the peer expert for it and may start their own extension. A learner moves up a level when they have finished the current level's product without a facilitator's help. In a museum visited once, the arc is inside one visit: guided cases first, then open building (step 4).
11. **Judge the environment by what learners chose to do, not by coverage.** Record dwell time, return visits, which levels were reached, which projects went beyond the designed task, and what learners could explain afterwards; there is no curriculum to measure completion against (the earlier page). Untested as a method here.

When a condition is not met: if nothing in the environment can respond to an attempt and no adult is free, use exhibits whose own result shows success or failure and add a printed contrast ("try a wide base and a narrow one"); if learners arrive with no interest in the topic, a free-choice setting may not reach them at all, and the design question becomes the opening hook rather than the depth behind it.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Is attendance voluntary, and how often do learners come back?** A one-off museum visit, a weekly school block that learners must attend but may spend as they choose, and a hobby community joined for years need different first loops and different arcs. If unstated, ask: will this learner be here once, weekly, or whenever they like, and can they leave at any moment?
- **What, if anything, responds to an attempt?** A responsive exhibit, a facilitator, peers who know more, or nothing at all. The one controlled comparison in the wiki is between a responsive exhibit and the same materials without a response. If unstated, ask: when a learner tries something, who or what shows them whether it worked and why?
- **Which outcome the institution needs.** Dwell time and return visits, a self-extended project, an explained idea, and a standardised test score are different outcomes, and the evidence for each is different. If unstated, ask: what would show, a month later, that the environment did its job?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is behaviour under stated conditions: whether a learner stops at an exhibit or opens a challenge, how long they stay, what they try, what they say to others, whether they come back, which level they reach, and whether they change the task. "Is interested", "is curious" and "is learning" are inferred states. Dwell time is behaviour, not understanding, and a long stay can be confusion as well as engagement.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves an exhibit within a minute | The first action was unclear; the topic does not connect to anything they care about; the group they came with moved on | Watch where the hand goes first and whether they read; ask one visitor what they thought it was for; compare dwell for visitors alone and in groups. |
| Stays a long time but cannot say what happened or why | Enjoying the action without noticing the idea; the exhibit gives no feedback on the idea; the explanation needs words they do not have | Ask for a prediction before the next attempt; offer a choice of explanations rather than asking for one; compare with a visitor who used the responsive version. |
| Repeats the same challenge level week after week | Comfort and enjoyment; the next level looks too hard; no one has shown them what is behind it | Show the next level with a peer who has done it; ask what they would like to make; note whether they move up when a starter is offered. |
| Abandons a self-started project halfway | A knowledge gap they could not get past; someone else solved it for them; competing activities | Ask what stopped them; check whether a peer expert or tutorial for that problem exists; see whether they return when the gap is filled. |
| Changes the challenge into something else entirely | A self-extended project (the environment working); avoidance of a hard step; copying a friend | Ask what they are trying to make and what they need next; check whether the new work uses the skills of the original challenge plus new ones. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking a visitor a question changes the visit, so record each probe.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares a free-choice environment with a structured or compulsory one on engagement and learning together. The claims below test parts of the relationship.

- [AI-mediated feedback in hands-on exhibits improves learning and engagement](../claims/ai-mediated-feedback-in-hands-on-exhibits-improves-learning-and-engagement.md) [~M]: 32 children (median grade 2) were randomly assigned in pairs, with matched timing, to an exhibit that sensed their tower-building and ran a predict–observe–explain cycle over contrasting cases, or to the same materials with static signage. The responsive group explained stability principles better (d = 1.45) and improved more on a later unguided building task (d = 0.96), with no difference in enjoyment; on the museum floor over two weeks, the responsive exhibit held visits about four times longer (6.25 against 1.51 minutes), a non-randomised comparison. One study (coded causal, `r2`). It is the main **qualification** of the old page: in this comparison, leaving visitors to explore the materials without any response produced less learning and shorter visits, so "no imposed sequence" should not mean "no feedback". Its own Discussion limits it to content whose outcome a machine can sense.
- [Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]: two competing meta-analyses of mostly laboratory experiments (128 and 96 studies), the original experiment and two reviews; its synthesis table gathers 13 distinct studies across it and eight narrower claims. Deci, Koestner and Ryan found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced free-choice behaviour after the reward stopped (d = −0.28 to −0.40), more for children than college students, while positive verbal feedback raised it; Cameron and Pierce found no overall effect and a minimal one for expected tangible rewards for doing a task. Outcomes are free-choice persistence and interest, not learning. It supports keeping rewards off taking part; the size is disputed.
- [Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]: a narrative review of self-determination theory, the same 128-experiment reward meta-analysis, and a conceptual framework from teacher vignettes; its synthesis table gathers 8 distinct studies. Its own Discussion says autonomy works best paired with structure and that premature autonomy can make novices anxious. It is the general motivational basis for free choice; it does not test free-choice environments or learning outcomes.
- [In one extreme case, two sixth-graders with a choice-rich, ungraded STEAM infrastructure extended a game-design challenge into a year-long self-directed project well beyond its designed scope](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [~M]: an ethnographic case of two sixth graders in one FUSE studio over a school year (about 22.5 hours across 34 sessions on one extended project, 23 technical problems resolved, two games shared publicly). Chosen by its authors as an extreme case; no comparison group and no measure of how common this is. It shows what the in-school version makes possible for some learners.
- [Exposure to field trips to informal science institutions produces small positive effects on middle school students' standardized science test scores](../claims/field-trips-small-positive-science-score-effects.md) [+M]: six years of New York City student data with school fixed effects; small positive effects on the eighth-grade science exam, no effect size printed. It is about school visits to such institutions, not about how they are designed.
- [Youth motivation to persist in everyday problem-solving is minimal, costing them collaboration and new knowledge](../claims/minimal-persistence-everyday-problem-solving-youth.md) [-W] and [Youth engaged free-choice learning heuristics in everyday problem-solving but typically skipped the assess phase](../claims/youth-skip-assess-phase-free-choice-heuristics.md) [~W]: one qualitative study of urban high-school youth solving everyday problems at home. Most did not persist, and few went back over a failed attempt to learn from it. These **limit** the model: left wholly alone, free-choice problem-solving often stops short of the learning it could give.
- [Not all students in connectivist courses could autonomously direct their own learning, and some felt disconnected and demotivated](../claims/connectivist-courses-student-agency-problems.md) [~W] and [Moderate learner agency in game-based learning yields better outcomes than no agency or high agency](../claims/moderate-agency-better-learning-outcomes.md) [~W]: second-hand reports (open online courses; one game-based study). Both limit how wide open the choice should be for learners new to a domain.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (grade-2 children at one exhibit, laboratory reward experiments, two sixth graders in a studio, city-wide school data, high-school youth at home), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures delayed learning from a free-choice environment.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The institution's objective may be visits and dwell time, interest in a field, understanding of a particular idea, or (for schools) achievement. The learner's goal is often something else: to have fun with family, to make a thing that works, to show friends, to get better at a hobby. In a free-choice setting the learner's goal decides whether they stay, so elicit it early ("what would you like to make or find out?") and record it beside the designer's objective. Agreement and divergence are both observations: a child building the tallest tower may value height while the exhibit's objective is base width, and a contrasting case that serves both (a tall tower that stands because its base is wide) is better than overriding either.

For example, the two sixth graders in the FUSE case valued making a game other people would play; the designed challenge's objective was level design. Their goal carried them to skills the challenge never taught, and public downloads were the signal they cared about. Recording only challenge completion would have missed what they learned.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of free-choice environments with structured ones, on comparable learners, show no difference in return visits or self-extended work, or show that responsive feedback lengthens visits without improving what learners can explain at a delay. If rewards for taking part turn out not to reduce later voluntary engagement in museums or studios (rather than laboratories), step 3 becomes a preference. If wide-open choice works as well for novices as narrowed choice, the low-confidence row should go. If responsive exhibits of the kind in the one controlled study fail on content other than structural stability, step 4 is a topic-specific design. Do not protect the model by calling every short visit a design failure or every long one engagement.

Dwell time, voluntary return, self-extended projects, explained understanding at the visit, delayed understanding and test scores are separate claims. The present evidence sets no dose for a studio block, no number of challenges and no threshold for moving up a level.

## Description
Falk and Dierking's research, cited by Ashton, Nelson, and Millward (2018), estimates that only about 5% of a person's lifetime learning occurs in classroom settings — the rest happens across a lifetime in self-selected, informal contexts: museums, hobbies, and everyday exploration. **Free-choice learning environments** are deliberately designed for this reality: they give visitors genuine control over what and how deeply they engage, rather than imposing a fixed sequence or curriculum. This differs in kind from bounded choice inside a fixed curriculum (offering a menu of topics or formats within a course, as in [Learner Choice](autonomy.md)) — free-choice design assumes no externally imposed sequence or objective at all, and success means visitors can curate a coherent experience entirely of their own.

Because attendance and engagement are entirely voluntary, with no external accountability like grades or tests to fall back on, the design must be intrinsically compelling on its own terms — physically, emotionally, and cognitively engaging. The chapter illustrates this with the Exploratorium's Active Prolonged Engagement exhibits, which let visitors test their own theories and lead their own experiments rather than following a prescribed activity sequence, deliberately building in multiple entry points and levels of complexity, multiple valid perspectives instead of a single authoritative reading, and content that connects to whatever prior knowledge and personal interest a given visitor brings.

### Context
#### Requirements

- A recurring, protected block of school time (the source case used 90 minutes weekly, year-long) with no or minimal grading tied to completion

# Alternative In-School STEAM Learning Infrastructure

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)

## Description
Interest-driven learning is usually associated with out-of-school settings (makerspaces, clubs, museums) precisely because school structures — grading, fixed pacing, curriculum coverage requirements — tend to crowd it out. This pattern describes an infrastructure that brings interest-driven learning inside the school day: a recurring, ungraded time block; a large library of self-contained "challenges" that can be leveled up, combined, or extended; adult "facilitators" (not "teachers") who observe and rarely intervene; and an expectation that students who progress further become peer experts others are directed to. The result is not that every student pursues a deep interest, but that the infrastructure makes it *possible* for a student's interest to be sparked, sustained, and extended well beyond the original activity — including into forms of learning the designed challenges never anticipated.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Establish a recurring, ungraded weekly time block dedicated to open challenge work, separate from standard curriculum periods.
2. Build or adopt a library of leveled, self-contained challenges spanning multiple STEAM domains, each with embedded optional support resources (tutorials, images) students are not required to use.
3. Train facilitators to circle the room, observe, and respond to direct questions, rather than direct activity or check for "on-task" compliance.
4. Establish a norm that students who progress furthest on a challenge become peer experts, and route help-seeking students to them before a facilitator intervenes.
5. Provide shared materials and tools accessible without teacher gatekeeping (e.g., an online asset repository, a 3D printer), so students can extend a challenge without waiting for facilitator-provided resources.
6. Allow and expect that some students will combine, alter, or extend challenges into self-directed projects beyond the original design; treat this as a sign of infrastructure health, not off-task behavior.
