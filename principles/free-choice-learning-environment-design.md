# Free-Choice Learning Environment Design

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Falk and Dierking's research, cited by Ashton, Nelson, and Millward (2018), estimates that only about 5% of a person's lifetime learning occurs in classroom settings — the rest happens across a lifetime in self-selected, informal contexts: museums, hobbies, and everyday exploration. **Free-choice learning environments** are deliberately designed for this reality: they give visitors genuine control over what and how deeply they engage, rather than imposing a fixed sequence or curriculum. This differs in kind from bounded choice inside a fixed curriculum (offering a menu of topics or formats within a course, as in [Learner Choice](learner-choice.md)) — free-choice design assumes no externally imposed sequence or objective at all, and success means visitors can curate a coherent experience entirely of their own.

Because attendance and engagement are entirely voluntary, with no external accountability like grades or tests to fall back on, the design must be intrinsically compelling on its own terms — physically, emotionally, and cognitively engaging. The chapter illustrates this with the Exploratorium's Active Prolonged Engagement exhibits, which let visitors test their own theories and lead their own experiments rather than following a prescribed activity sequence, deliberately building in multiple entry points and levels of complexity, multiple valid perspectives instead of a single authoritative reading, and content that connects to whatever prior knowledge and personal interest a given visitor brings.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Multiple entry points and complexity levels within the same exhibit or resource, since visitors arrive with no shared prerequisite sequence
- Content designed to connect to a visitor's own prior knowledge and interests, since there is no captive audience to build a shared foundation with in advance
- Genuinely intrinsic engagement — the design has no recourse to grades, tests, or other external accountability to sustain participation
#### Constraints
- Success cannot be measured against a fixed curriculum's completion or mastery criteria, since there isn't one — evaluation has to look at engagement quality and self-directed depth rather than standardized coverage
- Presenting multiple valid perspectives rather than one authoritative answer requires more design sophistication than a single fixed explanation, and can read as less "complete" by traditional curricular standards

### Target Learners
- Voluntary, self-directed visitors and learners in museums, science centers, hobbyist communities, and other settings with no compulsory attendance or externally imposed objectives

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained curiosity, exploration, and self-directed inquiry, rather than mastery of a predetermined body of content
- Personal, self-curated understanding built by connecting new content to a visitor's own existing interests and knowledge

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Informal Learning](informal-learning.md) [+S] — free-choice learning environments are a concrete institutional embodiment of the same "pulled," self-directed dynamic informal learning theory describes generally
