# Four Boundaries of Learning Community (Access, Relationships, Vision, Function)

## Description
West and Williams (2018) argue that "learning community" is used so inconsistently in research and practice that it undermines both research synthesis and design guidance. Rather than proposing one universal definition, they resolve the ambiguity by identifying four possible boundaries that define a community, any subset of which may be present in a given case:

- **Access** — physical or virtual co-presence: a common meeting place where members can hold social and intellectual interactions. Modern educational technology has blurred what counts as "presence," since access no longer requires physical co-location.
- **Relationships** — the emotional ties that bind members: a sense of belonging, interdependence (feeling needed by and mattering to others), trust, and shared faith that the community will meet members' needs. These relational characteristics apply to, and may matter even more in, virtual communities that lack face-to-face interaction.
- **Vision** — a shared sense of purpose or mission, distinct from believing the group is capable of achieving it. Members can share a vision (what the group is about) while disagreeing about whether the community can actually deliver on it.
- **Function** — members organized around common work, which dissolves once the task ends unless other boundaries also bind the group. A group of coworkers sharing an open office (access, no shared function or vision) and a distributed asynchronous project team (function and vision, little relational or access-based cohesion) are both real but differently-bounded "communities."

Two of these boundaries (access, function) can be observed externally; two (relationships, vision) are internal to members and can only be assessed by asking them directly about their feelings and thoughts. Real communities are typically defined by more than one boundary at once, and the boundaries can conflict — which is exactly why naming which boundary a design is actually trying to build clarifies what benefits that design can realistically produce.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clarity, before designing a "learning community" intervention, about which specific boundary(ies) the design is meant to build — access, relationships, vision, or function — since the interventions that build each differ
#### Constraints
- Cohort scheduling (shared class times) builds access and function but does not automatically build relationships or shared vision — a common design mistake is assuming co-enrollment alone produces "community"
- A functional community (working on the same project) dissolves once the task ends unless relational or vision-based ties have also formed, so a project-based community design that stops at function will not persist

### Target Learners
- Cohort-based programs (freshman learning communities, professional cohorts) where designers want the benefits associated with strong learning communities: higher retention, higher GPA, lower risk of withdrawal, increased cognitive skill, and better adjustment (Lichtenstein, 2005; Zhao & Kuh, 2004)
- Online and distributed learners, where access and relationships require deliberate design rather than arising from physical proximity

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not a content objective — a diagnostic framework for designing the social/organizational structure that supports other objectives (collaboration, sustained engagement, persistence)

## Examples
- A co-working space (e.g., Open Gov Hub) — access without shared function or vision
- A freshman cohort scheduled into the same classes — access and function, but not automatically relationships or vision unless separately designed for
- A distributed, asynchronous online project team — function and vision without much access-based or relational cohesion
