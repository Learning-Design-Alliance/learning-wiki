# Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 9 claims (5 for, 3 mixed, 1 against) · 18 studies (7 causal, 5 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 18 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner who fails or avoids a content task, where the failure may come from the wording rather than the concept (unfamiliar terms, dense syntax, ambiguous directions), two changes are expected to help: removing language difficulty that the objective does not require, and explicitly teaching the small set of disciplinary terms the objective does require. The expected change is in comprehension of the taught material, on a measure that uses the taught words, at an immediate horizon. Whether it carries over to untaught texts, authentic disciplinary language, standardized comprehension or `delayed-retention` is a separate claim, and the evidence here is weaker for it.

The relationship is conditional on three things that a page cannot settle in advance: which language the objective itself includes (a nursing learner must eventually read the drug label as printed; a mathematics learner need not parse an ornate word problem), the learner's language proficiency and prior knowledge of the domain, and whether the learner's starting response is in fact a language problem. The same wording may be clear for one group and opaque for another. No pattern page instantiates this principle yet; [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) holds the conditional model for withdrawing supports such as glossaries and sentence frames, and this page does not repeat it.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The page's earlier guidance, kept here as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and most of it is untested as written.

1. **Keep the content demand; decide which language the objective includes.** List the terms and structures the learner must eventually read or write as they are (the target language), and separate them from wording the objective does not require. Only the second kind is removed. For a mathematics word problem the mathematics stays; nested clauses and unfamiliar everyday words around it are candidates for removal or support.
2. **Diagnose before simplifying.** Give the same items in two versions, as written and with the non-target wording simplified, and compare each learner's scores. A large gap points to language; no gap points to the concept. (The observation table below lists rival explanations.) This is a proposal; no claim here tests it.
3. **Teach the essential terms explicitly**, a small set at a time, with plain explanations, examples and non-examples, and recycle them across reading, speaking and writing. Evidence: teaching word meanings improves comprehension of texts that use the taught words, much less of untaught texts (see below).
4. **Teach the structures, not only the words.** Model how to unpack the sentence forms learners will meet (for example, turning "the number of apples, which is twice the number of pears, is decreased by" into short statements), and have learners do it themselves. Untested here; it follows from step 1 and the old page's guidance.
5. **Use supports that bridge, then withdraw them.** Glossaries, sentence frames and simplified versions open the task. Move back to the authentic wording on a planned schedule, checking each learner on unsupported items before removing a support. Withdrawing supports is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).
6. **Judge the design on the authentic wording**, at the horizon the objective sets. A gain on simplified items alone is not the outcome.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a response to a particular text or prompt under stated conditions: a wrong answer, a non-answer, a request for help, a slow or silent reading, a correct answer when the item is read aloud or rephrased. "The language was the barrier" is an inferred state, and so is "the learner lacks the concept". Prior knowledge of the topic, reading fluency, proficiency in the language of instruction and willingness to speak all produce similar surface responses. Record the text, the response mode and every rephrasing or gloss given.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Fails a written item, answers correctly when it is rephrased orally | Wording or syntax barrier; decoding or reading-fluency limit; the rephrasing leaked the answer | Rephrase with the same terms but simpler syntax, then the same syntax with terms glossed, and keep the content demand constant; note whether the rephrasing named the answer. |
| Defines a taught term, cannot use it in a sentence or a new text | Declarative word knowledge without usable command of it; the new text's syntax is the barrier; the concept itself is not yet held | Ask for use of the term in a short explanation, then present it in an authentic passage with and without a gloss of the surrounding wording. |
| Comprehends the simplified text, fails the authentic one | Simplification removed language the objective includes; untaught vocabulary in the authentic text; topic familiarity differs between the two | Compare which words or structures in the authentic text are new, and test a passage that differs from the simplified one only in those. |
| Does not participate in discussion or writing | Lacks the language to express the idea; does not hold the idea; anxiety or low valuing of the task | Offer a sentence frame and, separately, a non-verbal or first-language response mode; see which, if either, changes the response. |
| Improves after glossaries, frames and re-teaching | Gain from the language supports; from added time on the topic; from repeated probes | Keep all exposures recorded. Only a comparison that holds time and topic constant can attribute the gain to the supports. |

These are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence between explanations; it does not identify a cause, and probing itself teaches.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as stated: none compares a text simplified in vocabulary and syntax, with content held constant, against the original, on comprehension and transfer. The claims below test neighbouring parts of it.

- [Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 37 pre-K–12 vocabulary interventions and a systematic review of 36. Teaching word meanings improved comprehension of passages containing the taught words (d = 0.50 on researcher-built measures) but barely moved standardized comprehension (d = 0.10); students with reading difficulties gained most on custom measures. The review found very limited evidence of gains in generalized comprehension and none for teaching one or two word-solving strategies. It supports teaching the essential terms; it does not predict general reading gains, and it does not test simplifying text. One of two entries has been checked by the judge, on abstracts. Its Discussion still says the page has no sourced entries, which is stale.
- [Textual material treated with embedded aids produced 10–15% higher reading comprehension scores than conventional text](../claims/embedded-aids-improve-reading-comprehension.md) [+W]: a quasi-experimental comparison (`controlled-nonrandom`) of aided against conventional science text with about 200 tenth graders in three schools; aided groups scored 10–15% higher on reading comprehension, significant at .01. The aids are not described on the claim page beyond "embedded aids"; a related claim describes them as a running commentary on key concepts, terminology and questions. Horizon and the comprehension measure's alignment are not reported. Not yet checked against its source.
- [Interactionally modified input aids comprehension but shows no advantage for retention among beginner learners](../claims/interactionally-modified-input-aids-comprehension-not-retention.md) [~M]: a review's report of a three-group study of L2 Japanese beginners (baseline, premodified, interactionally modified input). Interactionally modified input gave a clear immediate comprehension advantage and no advantage for retention of the structures. It qualifies the relationship: making language comprehensible in the moment is not the same as its later retention. Second-hand, no effect size, not yet checked.
- [Interactionally adjusted and premodified input yield statistically comparable comprehension overall, with negotiation benefiting lower-ability learners](../claims/negotiation-benefit-limited-lower-ability-learners.md) [~M]: the same review's report of a comparison matched for amount of input and time; comprehension was 88% against 81%, not significant overall, with a post-hoc advantage for interaction among learners rated lower in comprehension ability. A nonsignificant difference is not equivalence; it suggests that who needs the language adjustment, and responsive rather than fixed adjustment, may matter. Post-hoc, second-hand, not yet checked.
- [Passage cohesion showed no effect on delayed recall; means showed greater recall from less cohesive passages](../claims/cohesion-no-effect-delayed-recall.md) [-W]: in one study that varied passage familiarity and cohesion (repetition of concept labels across sentences) independently, cohesion had no effect on delayed recall, while familiarity did. One manipulation that reads as "more accessible wording" did not help delayed recall; prior knowledge of the topic did. Not yet checked.

Taken together: explicit teaching of target words has the strongest evidence and helps text that uses those words; language adjustment helps immediate comprehension in L2 settings with no shown retention advantage; and prior knowledge may matter more than surface wording. Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them by effect label.

How far each may be carried: the two input claims are spoken-interaction studies with beginning L2 learners, so they bear on making language comprehensible in the moment, not on rewriting written items; the cohesion claim is one study of passage recall, and it is not evidence against removing nested or ambiguous wording from a task. None of them argues for leaving avoidable wording barriers in place. The default design above rests on them only where it says so.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective names which language belongs to the capability (terms and structures the learner must eventually read, write or say) and which is incidental and may be removed. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to pass a test, to take part in discussion without embarrassment, to read workplace documents unaided. Agreement and divergence are both observations.

For example, an adult returning to study in a health-care programme may value reading a medication leaflet on their own, while the designer's immediate objective is understanding dosage concepts. A simplified explanation serves the designer's objective; only practice with the leaflet's actual wording, glossed and then unglossed, tests the learner's. Recording both keeps a simplified-text score from being read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The model should weaken if well-controlled comparisons of simplified against original wording, with content held constant, show no comprehension advantage for learners whose responses suggested a language barrier, or show that simplification lowers later performance on authentic texts. If prior topic knowledge consistently explains comprehension better than wording, as one cohesion study suggests, the first move should be building knowledge, not rewriting text. If explicit term teaching keeps helping only the taught words, keep that limit rather than claiming general reading gains. Do not protect the model by calling every failure on authentic text a fading problem.

Immediate comprehension of a simplified text, comprehension of the original, delayed retention and unaided use of the language in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how much to simplify, which syntactic features matter, or when to withdraw supports.

## Description
Use language that preserves disciplinary rigor while reducing unnecessary linguistic difficulty. Accessible vocabulary and syntax do not mean "watering down" content; they mean introducing essential terms intentionally, explaining them clearly, modeling sentence structures that learners will encounter in authentic texts, and removing avoidable wording barriers that would otherwise block participation and comprehension.

## Implications
Accessible vocabulary and syntax matter because learners can fail a task for language reasons even when the underlying idea is within reach. Limiting and organizing unfamiliar language can reduce unnecessary processing demands [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S], while asking learners to explain and reuse key terms in their own words makes vocabulary more durable than definition exposure alone [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The key design tension is that supports should open the door to authentic disciplinary language, not replace it permanently, so explanations, glossaries, and sentence frames should gradually give way to more independent use [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identify the small set of essential terms learners must understand and use, rather than introducing large numbers of unfamiliar words at once
- Provide plain-language explanations, examples, and non-examples before expecting independent use
- Model sentence structures learners will need in discussion, reading, and writing, with supports such as sentence frames, glossaries, or worked examples
- Recycle key vocabulary across speaking, reading, and writing so terms become usable tools rather than one-time definitions

#### Constraints
- Oversimplification can hide the actual language of the discipline; supports should bridge into authentic terminology, not replace it permanently
- Too many new terms or overly dense syntax can overwhelm working memory and suppress content understanding [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Accessibility depends on learner background knowledge, language proficiency, and context; the same wording may be clear for one group and opaque for another
- If instructors simplify language without explicitly teaching target terms, learners may understand the lesson but still fail when faced with authentic texts or assessments

### Target Learners
- Learners new to a discipline's vocabulary, genres, or sentence structures
- Multilingual learners and learners re-entering formal education who need explicit bridges into academic language
- Learners with lower reading fluency or working-memory limits who benefit from reduced linguistic load while acquiring new content
- Learners who avoid participation because they do not yet control the language needed to explain their thinking

### Target Learning Objectives
- Build access to disciplinary texts, instructions, and discussions
- Improve comprehension of new concepts without separating language from content
- Increase participation in discussion and writing by giving learners usable language structures
- Support transfer from simplified explanation to authentic academic or workplace language

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Social constructivist accounts (Vygotsky, Bruner) support language scaffolds as tools that let learners participate in meaning-making before they can do so fluently on their own
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) supports reducing unnecessary linguistic complexity so working memory can focus on the target concept rather than decoding the wording
- Information-processing perspectives support repeated, structured exposure to key terms and sentence forms so they can be encoded, retrieved, and reused
- Adult-learning perspectives qualify the need for transparent, relevant language that respects learners while making expectations legible

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Learners still need contact with authentic domain language; permanent simplification can underprepare them for real texts, conversations, and assessments
- Vocabulary support is strongest when paired with active use and explanation, not definition lists alone [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Language supports should fade as learner control grows, or they risk becoming a crutch [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]
