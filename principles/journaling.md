# Journaling

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 6 report an effect size

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **recurring written reflection kept over weeks**: a journal, learning log or diary in which a learner writes short entries at set points across a course or placement, and in which the entries are read again (by the learner, a teacher, a peer or a mentor) and used. For a learner working towards `conceptual-understanding`, a `complex-skill` or professional practice over a `term-plus` course, short entries written to **task-specific prompts** (explain what was learned and why, name what is still unclear, plan the next move) and **followed up** (the next lesson, feedback or attempt starts from them) are expected to produce better understanding on course assessments, and more analytic reflection, than either no journal or a journal kept to an open invitation ("write about what you learned"). The expected gains are on `course-assessment` and `general-achievement` measures at the end of a course, and on the analytic quality of the entries themselves; those are different outcomes, and better entries do not establish better learning. Whether gains hold at `delayed-retention` is not settled by anything here.

The relationship is conditional on four things. **The prompt**: specific, metacognitive prompts (what do I understand, how do I know, what will I do next) are the feature most associated with larger effects; open prompts produce mostly description. **Use**: a diary kept and never used showed no detected gain in one randomised trial. **Accuracy**: what a learner writes about their own understanding is often wrong, especially for a `novice`, so a journal entry is a hypothesis about the learner's state, not a measurement of it. **Cost**: journal time comes out of practice and feedback time, and long assignments did worse than short ones in the writing-to-learn synthesis.

**No claim in the wiki compares keeping a journal with not keeping one, everything else held the same, on a learning outcome.** The nearest evidence: a meta-analysis of school writing-to-learn interventions, in which journals are one format among several (small average gain; larger with metacognitive prompts and longer programmes, smaller with longer assignments); a quasi-experiment comparing specific with generic prompts in 52 reflections per student over a semester; a randomised trial in which a learning diary alone produced no detected gain but a diary with self-regulation training did on self-regulation measures; and one course in which guided journal prompts and private journals produced more analytic entries. Expressive (emotional) writing is a separate literature with small effects on health and test performance, not on learning.

How this sits inside the converted neighbours: [Reflection](reflection.md) owns the general relationship, examining a completed performance against a criterion to change the next one, and its default design (a concrete object, a visible criterion, three specific prompts, close the loop) applies to each journal entry. This page adds what a **recurring written record** changes: frequency and length of entries, who reads them, privacy, how the record is reviewed across weeks, and the extra forms a journal takes (dialogue journals with a teacher, fieldwork logs, concept journals). [Self-Monitoring](self-monitoring.md) owns checks made while a task is still under way; a journal is usually written after it, so use that page for in-task checklists and this one for the record kept between tasks. [Process-Based Writing](process-based-writing.md) owns writing taught as a staged, revisable product for a reader; a journal is unrevised, low-stakes writing to think, and is not judged as composition. [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) holds the whole plan–monitor–act cycle and regulation training, which the diary trial suggests a journal needs alongside it.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The page's earlier guidance (a clear purpose, prompts or routines, stated privacy, follow-through, a recurring focus, time and continuity), including the recurring-reflection design of the former journaling pattern, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step says what its evidence is.

1. **Say what the journal is for and who will read it, before the first entry.** Name one purpose (reflection on learning, planning, response to readings, fieldwork observation, dialogue with the teacher) and say plainly who reads entries (only the learner, the teacher, a mentor, peers) and whether anything is graded. Untested proposal, from the earlier page. Who reads matters to what is written: in one graduate service-learning course, private journal paragraphs were more often analytic or integrative than public blog paragraphs ([public vs private reflection](../claims/public-vs-private-reflection-mixed.md) [~W]).
2. **Write short entries often, over weeks, rather than long ones rarely.** A default dose: 5–10 minutes per entry, at the end of each class or practical session, or two or three times a week in a self-paced course, kept up for the whole unit or term. The minutes and frequency are untested proposals. The direction rests on the writing-to-learn meta-analysis, where effects were larger with longer treatment duration and smaller when the writing assignments themselves were longer ([Writing To Learn Improves Understanding](../claims/writing-to-learn-improves-understanding.md) [+M]; school-based programmes, elementary to college), and on a semester course in which each student wrote 52 short reflections ([structured reflection](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M]).
3. **Give two or three specific prompts per entry, tied to that day's content.** For example: "Explain [today's idea] in your own words and give one example"; "What is still unclear, and how do you know?"; "What will you do differently next time, or what will you ask?". Specific prompts beat generic ones on exams, project scores and problem sets with the number of reflections held equal, and metacognitive-reflection prompts were the only writing-content moderator associated with larger effects across 48 school studies ([structured reflection](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M]). Prompts asking for explanation follow [self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] (tested while studying examples and texts, mostly on immediate tests, not in journals). Guided prompts produced more analytic journal paragraphs than free reflection in one course ([guided vs free reflection](../claims/guided-reflection-yields-more-higher-order-paragraphs-than-free.md) [+W]).
4. **Ask for a check, not only a feeling.** When an entry says "I understand X", ask the learner to write one line that would show it (solve one item, define the term, predict a result), and compare with the answer key or the next scored task. Learners' self-assessments are often inaccurate without training and clear criteria ([Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]; secondary and university students). The check line is an untested proposal.
5. **Use the entries, every week.** Open the next class or module with two or three anonymised points from the entries (the commonest confusion, a good question), answer them, and have each learner reread their last "next step" and say whether they did it. A diary kept on its own showed no detected gain on any outcome in a randomised trial with prospective university students; with web-based self-regulation training added, the diary group gained on self-regulation knowledge and self-efficacy, though not on mathematics scores ([diary alone](../claims/learning-diary-alone-no-significant-srl-gains-online-math-prep-course.md) [-M]; [training with diary](../claims/web-based-srl-training-with-diary-raises-srl-knowledge-and-self-efficacy-not-math.md) [~M]). The weekly routine itself is an untested proposal.
6. **Respond briefly and selectively, not to every line.** A teacher or mentor writes one or two sentences on a sample of entries each week (for example, a third of the class in rotation, so each learner hears back every three weeks), aimed at the stated confusion or plan; no marks for spelling or style. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's caution against feedback overload and performative writing.
7. **Review the journal across entries at set points.** Every three to four weeks, and at the end, the learner rereads their entries and writes one review entry: what has changed in my understanding or practice, what keeps recurring, what I will work on next. Untested proposal; from the former pattern page's "track learning, sensemaking or growth over time".
8. **Fade the prompts as entries become analytic.** Early weeks: the teacher's two or three specific prompts, with sentence starters for those who need them. Middle weeks: the learner chooses one of several prompts and adds a "next step" line. Late weeks: the learner sets their own question for the entry and the teacher prompts only the review entries. Move a learner to the next stage when about three consecutive entries name a specific confusion or decision and a concrete next step, rather than only recount events. The stages and the threshold are untested proposals; that novices need more structure than advanced learners is argued on the [structured reflection](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) claim page's Discussion, not tested there.
9. **Never require personal disclosure.** Offer a content-focused prompt beside any personal one, and let learners mark an entry "not for reading". Untested; from the earlier page's caution on forced disclosure. Expressive writing about emotions is a different activity, with very small average effects on health and none shown on learning ([Expressive Writing Improves Health Outcomes](../claims/expressive-writing-improves-health-outcomes.md) [~M]).

When a condition is not met: if no one can read entries regularly, keep the journal private, make each prompt end in a check the learner can score (an answer key, a worked example) and use step 5 with self-reread only; if there is no next lesson or attempt for the entry to feed, replace the journal with a single end-of-task reflection (see [Reflection](reflection.md)); if writing itself is the barrier, allow spoken, recorded or drawn entries and judge the content, not the prose.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Will anyone use the entries, and when?** A journal that feeds the next class, a feedback reply or the next attempt has evidence behind it; one that is filed and never read had none in the one trial that tested it. If unstated, ask: who reads an entry, how soon, and what changes because of it?
- **What is the journal for?** Understanding course content, planning and self-regulation, professional reflection on practice, language practice with a teacher, and emotional processing are different aims with different evidence and different prompts. If unstated, ask: what should a learner be able to do or understand at the end that they could not before, and how will that be checked?
- **Who reads it, and is it graded?** Private, ungraded entries were more analytic than public ones in one course; graded or shared entries invite performance. If unstated, ask: who sees each entry, and does anything about it count towards a mark?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is an entry under stated conditions (the prompt, who would read it, whether it was graded, how long the learner had, whether it was written, typed or spoken), what happened to it (read, answered, summarised to the class, ignored), and what the learner did next (whether the named next step was tried, scores on the next check). "Reflective", "metacognitively aware" and "understands" are inferred states. A fluent, analytic entry is a writing performance; a short or descriptive one does not show that the learner did not think.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Entries recount events with no analysis | Prompt too open; nothing to judge against; low task knowledge; writing in a second language is the barrier | Swap to a specific prompt for two weeks and compare; ask the same prompt orally; give one strong and one weak sample entry and ask which is stronger and why. |
| Entries say "I understand it", next scored task shows otherwise | Miscalibration; fluency mistaken for understanding; the learner writes what a reader wants to hear | Add a one-item check to the entry and compare with the key; collect one private, ungraded entry and compare its content. |
| Entries name a next step, which is never tried | No time or occasion to try it; the plan is too vague to act on; nobody asks about it | Show the last next step at the start of the next session and record whether it is tried; ask what would make it doable. |
| Entries become identical or formulaic across weeks | Compliance without use; prompt fatigue; nothing new happened; entries are never read | Change the prompt to cite a specific artefact or task; check whether anyone has read or used recent entries; ask the learner what they want from the journal. |
| Learner writes very little, or misses entries | Writing burden; low value placed on it; reluctance to be read; time pressure | Offer a voice or drawn option and a private setting; ask what they would use a journal for; check whether entries clash with other deadlines. |
| Entries are long, personal and emotional where content was asked for | Content prompt touched a personal matter; the learner is using the journal to process something; the prompt invited disclosure | Offer a separate private space and a content-only prompt; follow the setting's pastoral procedure where an entry signals harm; do not grade the entry. |

These rows are proposals and have not been tested with learners as diagnostics. Reading an entry and asking about it is itself an intervention, so record each reply and prompt the learner received, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Writing To Learn Improves Understanding](../claims/writing-to-learn-improves-understanding.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 48 school-based writing-to-learn programmes (elementary to college) against conventional instruction found a small positive effect on conventional achievement measures, enhanced by metacognitive prompts and longer treatment duration and reduced in grades 6–8 and when the writing assignments were longer (read from the abstract only; no number is asserted there); a quasi-experiment with 64 eighth graders in two intact classes found writing-to-learn activities in a social-studies unit raised post-test achievement (d = 0.61). Journals are one format within writing-to-learn; neither study isolates them.
- [Reflective Practice Improves Outcomes When Structured](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M]: the same 48-study meta-analysis, as recorded on this page, gives the overall effect as d = 0.22 and metacognitive-reflection prompts as the only writing-content moderator associated with larger effects (b = 0.48); a quasi-experiment with 208 first-year engineering students who each wrote 52 reflections over a semester found specific prompts beat generic ones on exams, project score and two problem sets, with the number of reflections equal and no effect size reported. Not randomised; no delayed horizon. This is the closest evidence for prompt design in a recurring journal.
- [Guided vs free reflection](../claims/guided-reflection-yields-more-higher-order-paragraphs-than-free.md) [+W] and [public vs private reflection](../claims/public-vs-private-reflection-mixed.md) [~W]: in four sections of one graduate international service-learning course, guided journal prompts produced more analytic and integrative paragraphs than free reflection (more than half of free reflections descriptive only), and private journal paragraphs were more often analytic or integrative (64%) than public blog paragraphs (41%), though nearly a third of public paragraphs were integrative. The outcome is the quality of the entries, not learning.
- [A daily learning diary alone did not produce statistically significant pre-post gains](../claims/learning-diary-alone-no-significant-srl-gains-online-math-prep-course.md) [-M]: in a randomised four-group trial in an online mathematics preparation course for prospective university students, the diary-only group showed no significant gain on any measure; equivalence was not tested, so this is no detected gain, not a shown null. **This is the limit on the model**: keeping a diary is not enough by itself.
- [Web-based SRL training combined with a learning diary](../claims/web-based-srl-training-with-diary-raises-srl-knowledge-and-self-efficacy-not-math.md) [~M]: in the same trial, training plus diary raised self-regulation knowledge, the self-regulation overall score, planning, metacognition and self-efficacy, but not mathematics scores. It qualifies the model: the diary was useful as part of taught self-regulation, and the gains were on self-regulation measures, not content learning.
- [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]: modelling examples of self-assessment made 80 pre-university students' self-ratings on genetics problems more accurate (ηp² = .10); a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies found self–teacher agreement inconsistent, closer in advanced courses. It limits what a journal entry can be read as showing about a learner's state.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (schoolchildren, first-year engineers, graduate students in service learning, prospective university students online), and their outcomes range from course exams to self-report questionnaires to the coded quality of entries; do not rank them by effect label. None measures delayed retention, and none compares a journal with the same time spent on practice or feedback.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — cited before the rewrite; used in step 3 for explanation prompts. Tested while studying worked examples and texts (four studies, a meta-analysis at g = .55), mostly on immediate tests, not in journals.
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M] — cited before the rewrite; a review and a theoretical synthesis that self-monitoring against a goal is central to self-regulation. - [Students who use the ePSRL Management System post more scaffold reflections and self-reflections per month than non-users](../claims/epsrl-increases-metacognitive-reflection-posts.md) [+W] — a self-regulation management system increased entries in a learning reflective diary (d = 0.428); the outcome is the number of entries, not learning.

They bear on affective journals, not on learning content.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective for a journal is usually better understanding or practice at the end of a course, shown on an aligned assessment or in observed practice. Ask separately what the learner wants from it: a place to sort out what they did not follow, a record of their progress for a portfolio, practice writing in a new language, a private space to process a hard placement, or simply to meet a requirement. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the journal as a private space may write little if told it will be read; one who values the grade may write what the reader wants to hear.

For example, a nursing student on a first clinical placement may want somewhere to work through a distressing shift, while the programme's objective is reflection on clinical decisions against stated competencies. Keep the two apart: a private section for the first, which is never graded, and a decision log for the second, which the mentor reads before the next shift. Record which entries serve which aim, so that a full private journal is not read as evidence of clinical reasoning, and a thin decision log is not read as indifference.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners keeping a prompted, followed-up journal over a term show no gain over a defensible comparison (no journal, or the same time spent on practice with feedback) on an aligned course outcome. If specific prompts improve the analytic quality of entries but never the next assessment, the model describes better reflective writing, not better learning, and should say so. If diaries help only when combined with taught self-regulation, as the one trial suggests, the active ingredient is the training and the journal is its record, and the default should lead with the training. If short frequent entries and long rare ones do equally well once total time is equated, drop the dose guidance. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "prompts not specific enough" or "entries not read" after the fact.

Better entries, more entries, self-regulation questionnaire scores, course exams, changed practice and delayed retention are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no optimal entry length, frequency, prompt set, reading schedule or audience, and nothing for journals in primary schools, for language learning beyond an argument, or for adults outside higher education.

## Examples
- **Reflection journals**: Learners respond regularly to prompts about what they learned, where they struggled, and what they will try next.
- **Dialogue journals**: Instructor and learner exchange short written responses over time.
- **Fieldwork journals**: Learners document observations, questions, and pattern recognition during practice-based experiences.
- **Concept journals**: Learners explain an idea in their own words and connect it to examples or prior knowledge.

## Description
Journaling is the practice of having learners write regularly to externalize thinking, notice experience, and make learning more visible to themselves or to an instructor. By writing about what they understand, what they are struggling with, and how new learning connects to prior experience, learners can process meaning more deliberately and track change over time.
