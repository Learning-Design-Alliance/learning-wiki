Educators should act as mediators and facilitators of learning rather than content disseminators¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 44 claims (29 for, 13 mixed, 2 against) · 40 studies (9 quant-synthesis, 9 review, 9 theoretical, 8 qualitative, 3 causal, 2 associational), q1–q4 · 4 of 40 report an effect size · 39 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the teacher's role during a lesson: what share of the work of making sense of the content the teacher does by presenting it, and what share the learners do while the teacher mediates. The relationship: for learners whose goal is understanding they can use (a concept, principle, a communicative or writing skill, a value or attitude) rather than recall of delivered content, a teacher who (a) states the goal and why it matters, (b) organizes a task or materials the learners act on, (c) questions, challenges and helps contingently while they work instead of supplying answers, and (d) has learners express what they understood in their own words and carry it to a new situation, is expected to produce more engagement and more usable understanding (conceptual-understanding, near-transfer) than presenting the same content for learners to receive.

Three conditions bound it. Prior knowledge: for a novice on the target, "facilitating" that leaves the essential content to be discovered does worse than explicit instruction, so the mediator still explains, models and gives feedback; what changes is that the explanation serves the learners' work instead of replacing it. What the teacher actually does while learners work: guidance (prompts, feedback, worked examples, teacher-led steps) is what the stronger evidence credits, not the label "facilitator". Outcome and horizon: the recorded comparisons are end-of-course exams, concept tests and reported engagement; delayed retention is not established here.

No claim in this wiki compares a mediating teacher with a content-disseminating teacher directly. Most claims the earlier page cited are theoretical arguments or a single source's assertions (q1–q2, one study each, no effect size): MLE theory, Piaget, Mead, social constructionism in composition, constructivist reform in China, and a second-language acquisition argument that grammar knowledge cannot be directly taught. The nearest tested comparisons are neighbouring ones: active-learning courses against lecture in undergraduate STEM, teacher-led against student-led inquiry in school science, contingent against fixed support in one-to-one tutoring, and unassisted against explicit or guided discovery. One historical case runs the other way (a constructivist mathematics reform in Taiwan was stopped after its students underperformed). So the default below is a labelled proposal built from the earlier guidance.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Active Learning owns what learners are asked to do with the content and its course-level evidence; Direct Instruction owns explicit explanation, modelling and the comparison with discovery, which this page's mediator still uses for novices; Scaffolding and Fading owns how help is raised, lowered and withdrawn; Inquiry-Based Learning owns investigation around a question. This page adds the teacher's side of those designs: which moves the teacher keeps, which pass to the learners, and how that division shifts across a unit. It does not repeat their models.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page and the six pages folded into it, kept as a concrete default. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it. Minutes assume a 50-minute session and are untested proposals; scale them to the time available.

  1. Open with the goal and why it matters (about 5 minutes). Arouse curiosity with a material, object or question, then state the learning goal and what it is for, and check that learners can say it back. Untested proposal; from MLE's intentionality and meaning (the five-stage MLE lesson opens this way).
  2. For learners new to the target, explain or model it briefly first (up to 10 minutes). Do not leave the essential idea or procedure to be discovered: show it, with one worked example, then hand the task over. Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [~S]; Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]. Skip or shorten for learners who can already begin the task.
  3. Give learners something to act on (about 20 minutes). A structured task, problem, text, materials or a communicative situation with a clear product, introduced simply and, for young children, one variable at a time. The teacher stays responsible for structure, order and progress; "facilitator" does not mean letting learners do whatever they choose. Untested proposal; from the Piaget handbook and the constructivist-classroom page folded in here. The argument that a method of thought must be gained through the learner's own abstractions is theoretical (Mead holds that a method of thought cannot be transferred but must be gained through the learner's own abstractions [+W]).
  4. Mediate while they work: question, challenge, help contingently. Circulate; ask "why" and "how" questions, offer a counter-suggestion when an answer is unexamined, and when a learner is stuck give the least help that lets them continue (a question, then a hint, then a demonstration of the step), less after a success. Hold back the answer: wait for the learner's next move before supplying it. Contingent help: Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+S] (one-to-one tutoring, mostly; its table counts 20 distinct studies); questioning and holding back the answer as the teacher's role are untested proposals from the earlier page (MLE critical questions, Piagetian counter-suggestion, studio faculty who "had to work against always jumping in to provide the right answer").
  5. Have learners express and check their understanding (about 10 minutes). Learners summarize in their own words, pose a critical question, or explain to a partner; in pairs or groups they take turns as explainer, evaluator and judge of each other's work against stated criteria, while the teacher acts as coach, co-worker and referee. Untested proposal; from the communicative-competence packet (teacher as coach, students moving "in and out of roles such as evaluator, judge, or instructor"), the composition paper (If writing and thinking are forms of conversation, collaborative learning is an extension of social constructionism into the composition classroom [+W]) and the constructivist-classroom page.
  6. Close with a transfer task (about 5 minutes in class, or as follow-up). Ask learners to use the idea in a situation beyond the lesson (a poster for a real issue, a new problem, their own text) and say how it connects. Untested proposal; MLE's transcendence stage. Reported engagement after one such English lesson: Students reported greater engagement, motivation and language use after MLE-mediated English lessons [+W] (7th graders, interviews, no comparison group).
  7. Ask the learners how it is going, and adapt. At the end of each week or project stage, collect two or three lines of learner feedback (what helped, where they were stuck) and change the next session's task or support accordingly. Untested proposal; from the studio-course page (a flexible course structure, instructors "proactively seeking learner feedback throughout the course").
  8. Shift the division of labour across sessions. Early sessions of a unit: the teacher explains and models each new idea (steps 1–2 take up to a third of the time) and asks most of the questions. Middle sessions: explanation shrinks to a short framing, learners run the peer-check roles in step 5 and pose some of the questions. Late sessions: learners plan or choose part of the task and the teacher acts as consultant, reviewing work at agreed check-ins. Move a group to the next stage when most learners can explain the idea in their own words and complete a changed task unaided in two consecutive sessions; move an individual back to more explanation when they cannot start a task. Untested proposal; the endpoint (teacher as "designer of teaching environment, the guider for students' learning, and the academic consultant") is from the basic-education reform page folded in here.

When a condition is not met: if learners are novices and the class is large, keep step 2 and make step 4 a whole-class scan of answers rather than circulation; if learners are already competent, drop step 2 and move straight to the late-session division of labour.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How much the learners already know about this target. A novice needs the teacher to explain and model before handing over the work; a learner who can already start needs the teacher as questioner and consultant. If unstated, ask: can these learners begin a representative task now, unaided?
  • What the learners must be able to do, and when it is checked. Explaining an idea, using a language in conversation, holding a value, and recalling content for an exam call for different shares of teacher presentation. If unstated, ask: what will learners do, with what aids, at what point, to show they have it?
  • How much of each learner's work the teacher can see and respond to. Mediation by questioning is contingent help, and most of that evidence is one-to-one. If unstated, ask: during the task, how many learners can the teacher reach, how often, and who else (peers, software, a checklist) can respond to a learner's work?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: who talks and for how long, what learners produce during the task, the help asked for and given (at what level), what learners say when asked to explain in their own words, and how they do on a changed task without help. "Has constructed the meaning", "is dependent on the teacher" and "is engaged" are inferred states. Learners producing more talk or saying they enjoy the lessons is engagement, not yet understanding.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Learners are busy and talkative during the task but cannot explain the idea afterwards The task could be done without the idea; the key content was never made available; explanation in words is the barrier, not the idea Ask for the idea on a changed case and accept a demonstration or drawing; check whether the task's product required the idea.
Learners wait for the teacher to give the answer They are novices who cannot start; earlier lessons rewarded waiting; the goal was unclear Give a matched task with one worked example; ask a learner to say the goal; withhold the answer for one more move and see whether they continue.
Learners do well in class tasks but poorly on the course exam Tasks were not aligned to what the exam asks; the exam tests recall the tasks did not practise; gains fade by the exam Compare task items with exam items; give one exam-like item unaided at the end of a session.
A few learners dominate peer checking, others stay silent Low confidence; language level; roles not assigned Assign rotating roles; give silent learners a written or private channel; compare their written explanations with their spoken ones.
Second-language learners' accuracy on a taught form rises in exercises but not in free speech The exercise affected performance only; the form is not yet acquired; time pressure in free speech Check the form in a later unprompted task; record exposure in between. Second-language theorists argue that taught-form gains often stay at performance: form-focused input manipulation is said to affect performance, not competence [~W]; learners were sensitive to untaught syntax but not to agreement they had been taught and practised [~W].

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. A question that probes understanding also teaches, so record each probe.

Evidence and qualifications¶

Neighbouring comparisons that bear on the teacher's role (tested, but not of the role itself):

  • Active learning improves exam performance [+S]: undergraduate STEM courses; across 158 studies, exam and concept-inventory scores were 0.47 SD higher under active learning than traditional lecturing, largest in classes of 50 or fewer; in 15 studies only high-intensity active learning narrowed gaps for underrepresented and low-income students. Its table counts 5 distinct studies. It shows that changing what learners do in class changes exam results; it does not isolate the teacher's mediating moves. Qualified by Most active learning college science conditions contain a substantial lecture component [~M]: 79.5% of 88 active-learning conditions in a systematic review kept lecture, so "mediator" in practice usually includes some presenting.
  • Teacher Guided Inquiry Outperforms Student Led [+S]: in a meta-analysis of 37 studies of inquiry-based school science, studies with teacher-led activities had mean effects about .40 larger than studies with student-led ones (a between-study contrast); a synthesis of 138 studies found a positive trend for inquiry over more passive instruction. It supports the teacher as active guide inside learner work, not a teacher who steps back.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+S]: mostly one-to-one tutoring (fourth and fifth graders on long division, eighth graders on the circulatory system); a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding. Its table counts 20 distinct studies. It supports the questioning-and-help part of mediation; generalizing it to whole classes is not shown.

Limiting claims:

  • Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [~S] and Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]: across 580 comparisons explicit instruction outperformed unassisted discovery (d = 0.38), while discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations outperformed other instruction; with 112 children, many more learned to design unconfounded experiments by direct instruction than by discovery. The tables count 13 and 5 distinct studies. A "facilitator" who leaves novices to find the content is the failing condition; a mediator who guides is not.
  • Taiwan's constructivist mathematics reform was stopped in 2003 after students taught under it underperformed [-M]: a historical account in a review; no statistics. It does not say what teachers did, so it limits wholesale reform more than it tests the role.
  • Explicit L2 grammar instruction leads to larger learning effects than implicit instruction [~S]: meta-analyses of L2 instruction (49, 34 and 41 studies) found explicit types more effective than implicit, with no effect sizes printed in the abstracts read. It qualifies the second-language claims in the next paragraph, which argue explicit teaching cannot reach linguistic competence.

Second-language acquisition: the argument that grammar cannot be taught directly (from the VanPatten and Young-Scholten pages folded in here; one essay or review each, no effect sizes). The argument runs that the language faculty works only on input, so explicit rules, drill and correction do not change what learners actually know (Mental representation is not amenable to direct explicit instruction because UG and parsers operate only on input data [~W]; Applying skill theory to grammar conflates constructs [~W]; Instruction shows little or no effect on route of L2 acquisition [~W], reported second-hand), and that form-focused gains are performance only (Successes of form-focused manipulation of input are illusory with respect to linguistic competence [~W]; A 1999 conference paper reports, citing White (1991) and Schwartz and Gubala-Ryzak (1992), that explicit negative evidence on English adverb placement produced only superficial change in French learners [~W], reported second-hand; learners acquired untaught syntactic properties but not the agreement they had been explicitly taught and practised [~W], the authors' own processing study reported in an essay). What it recommends instead is designed input and use: Structured input alone is sufficient to cause changes in learner knowledge [+W] and skill develops through engaging in the target activity itself [+W]. For this page that supports step 3's "something to act on" in a second language, but it is set against the explicit-instruction meta-analyses above, which measure performance on tests; the two disagree about what the outcome should be (test performance or unprompted use), so the L2 row keeps both and the observation table checks the form in unprompted use.

The theoretical and single-source claims the earlier page rested on (each one study, no effect size): MLE holds that unmediated experience cannot yield meaningful learning (Feuerstein argues learning through direct, unmediated experience cannot yield meaningful learning or full modifiability [+W]); Qiong Jia argues that learning is the learner's own construction from old and new knowledge (Learning is an initiative construction of meanings completed by the interaction of learners' old and new knowledge [+W]); and 7th graders interviewed after one MLE-mediated English lesson reported more enjoyment and purpose (Students reported greater engagement, motivation and language use after MLE-mediated English lessons [+W]). These state the rationale; none compares conditions.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (STEM undergraduates, school science, one-to-one tutoring, Taiwanese mathematics, adult L2), so do not rank them by effect.

The evidence by question¶

  • If inquiry or discovery is chosen, how much should the teacher or system guide it? Teacher Guided Inquiry Outperforms Student Led [+S]
  • Must support be contingent on the learner's last response, rather than fixed? Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+S]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually that learners can explain and use the idea unaided on a new task, by a stated point. Ask separately what the learners want from the lessons: to pass the exam, to be told clearly what is right, to use the language with real people, to have a say in what they work on. These can diverge sharply from the mediating design. Learners who value being told may read the teacher's questions as withholding, and their frustration is an observation, not resistance to learning; learners who value autonomy may disengage under heavy explanation. Record both the objective and what learners say they value, and when they diverge, say so to the learners and show how the task serves their goal.

For example, adult language learners may want to pass a grammar-heavy placement test, while the designer's objective is conversational use. A mediated communicative task serves the designer's objective; one explicit rule review followed by test-format items serves the learners'. Do both, and report them separately.

What would revise this model?¶

The model should weaken if comparisons of teachers using the mediating moves (questioning, holding back answers, learner explanation, transfer tasks) against teachers presenting the same content, with comparable learners and aligned unaided outcomes, show no advantage; or if the gain on reported engagement does not appear on understanding or a delayed test. If the active ingredient turns out to be what learners do, whoever prompts it (software, peers, materials), then this page's model reduces to Active Learning and the teacher's role is a matter of logistics. If explicit instruction followed by practice matches the mediating design on transfer and use, the model should say so for that domain (second-language grammar is the likeliest). Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not real facilitation" after the fact.

Reported engagement, end-of-unit understanding, exam performance, delayed retention and use outside class are separate claims. The present evidence in this wiki sets no ratio of teacher talk to learner work and no rule for when to step in.

Examples¶

  • Five-stage MLE lesson pattern: curiosity arousal, explicit goals, meaning mediation, critical questioning, and transfer task
  • Teacher training packets operationalize communicative competence theory through workshop activities, pre/posttests, and guided discussion rather than expert knowledge alone
  • Lecture-free, problem-centered, collaborative course pattern built on model construction and evaluation
  • Introduce Piagetian activities simply, one variable at a time, with ample time and repetition
  • Use questioning and counter-suggestions (contrepreuve) to produce disequilibration and support cognitive growth
  • Emphasize cooperation and communication to train students' cooperative consciousness
  • Create an interactive learning environment in real or semi-real contexts using modern teaching media
  • Use Flexible Discussion Groups for Science Content Analysis
