# Flipped Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 11 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 11 report an effect size

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one scheduling decision: **where the learner's first exposure to new material happens.** For learners who can take in a topic's first explanation on their own (a short video, a reading, a worked example) and whose difficulty lies in applying it, moving that first exposure before the live session and spending the freed session on application with feedback (problems, discussion, explaining, coached practice) is expected to give better course outcomes (`course-assessment`, `general-achievement`, `conceptual-understanding`) than spending the same live time on the same exposition. The expected gain comes from what the live time is then used for, not from the relocation: a flip that moves the lecture to video and fills class with more lecture, or with review of the video, is not expected to help.

The relationship is conditional on four things. **Preparation actually happens**: the design assumes learners arrive having met the material, and many learners do not do assigned pre-class reading. **The pre-class material can be understood without a teacher**: a `novice` facing material with many interacting parts may need the explanation live, with someone to ask. **The live time is used for application with feedback**, at a dose that changes what learners do. **Learners can reach the material** (device, connection, time and a place to study outside class). The horizon recorded in the evidence is the end of a course; durable retention is not established here.

**No claim in this wiki compares a flipped course with the same course taught unflipped, with the same content, time and assessment.** The nearest evidence is of three other kinds: two flipped variants compared with each other (one calculus study), blended courses compared with face-to-face ones (a meta-analysis whose blended arms usually also had extra time and resources), and active learning compared with lecture (meta-analyses in undergraduate STEM). Each is read below for what it does and does not show. The steps that follow are a default design to act on and revise, not a tested recipe.

How the neighbouring converted pages sit beside this one: [Active Learning](active-learning.md) owns the relationship between what learners do in class and what they learn; this page uses it for the live session and does not repeat it. The [Blended Learning pattern](../patterns/blended-learning.md) owns the wider decision of dividing a course between online and in-person work; flipping is one configuration inside it (exposition online, application in person). The [Team-Based Learning pattern](../patterns/team-based-learning.md) is a complete flipped cycle with a readiness test and team application; use it when a stable team structure is wanted. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) owns how the coached practice in the live session is run.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. Quantities are labelled proposals where no claim gives them.

1. **Decide what moves out and what stays in.** Move out only the first explanation of material learners can follow alone: definitions, a demonstration of a procedure, a worked example, a short reading. Keep in the live session anything that needs a teacher's observation, a partner, equipment or discussion, and any explanation of material learners repeatedly fail to follow alone. Untested; from the earlier page ("moving lecture out of class is not enough by itself").
2. **Keep the pre-class part short and active.** Proposal: 10–15 minutes of video or 3–6 pages of reading per live session, in segments of a few minutes, with two or three questions embedded in or after it that ask the learner to predict, explain a step or answer an item before seeing the solution. Basis: a review reports that learners prefer short videos, with no length given ([Guo et al. found engagement depends on video production style](../claims/guo-video-style-engagement-findings.md) [+W], engagement on one platform, not learning); answering a question before studying the material improved retention of it in laboratory studies ([Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M], carried from lab passages to pre-class work); prompting learners to explain improved immediate understanding ([Prompting learners to self-explain…](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], mostly lab and immediate tests). The minutes are an untested proposal.
3. **Make the preparation visibly matter, and check it at the start.** Open each live session with a short individual check on the pre-class material (proposal: 3–5 items, 5 minutes, low stakes, answers shown at once), and start the first task from it. Never re-teach the whole video because some did not watch it; that teaches learners the preparation is optional. Basis: many students report rarely or never reading assigned material ([Students report rarely reading their physics textbooks…](../claims/students-rarely-read-textbook.md) [~M], one university physics course, self-report); in-class quizzing improved exam performance in school and college classes ([Classroom quizzing…improves student performance on classroom exams](../claims/classroom-quizzing-improves-exam-performance-across-grades-and-content.md) [+M], reported second-hand in a review chapter). Learners track their own preparation better against a visible checklist and the check's results ([Self-monitoring improves self-regulation…](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M], theoretical syntheses, no flipped study).
4. **Clarify only what the check shows.** Proposal: at most 5–10 minutes of targeted explanation on the one or two items most learners missed, then move on. Untested.
5. **Spend most of the live session on application with feedback.** Proposal: at least two thirds of the session (for a 50-minute class, about 30–35 minutes) on problems, cases, discussion or practice in pairs or small groups, with the teacher circulating and responding to what each group produces: a cue first, then a hint, then a demonstration of the step. Basis: active learning against lecture in undergraduate STEM ([Active learning improves exam performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S], the only claim here with the class-time dose varied, and only high-intensity active learning narrowed gaps); contingent help ([Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M], one-to-one tutoring, untested at class scale); and one flipped calculus study in which adding peer teaching to the class time gave higher scores than a conventional flipped class ([Peer-teaching flipped classroom students show higher calculus conceptual understanding…](../claims/ptfc-beats-cfc-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]). What is done in the session matters, not only that the exposition moved.
6. **Close with an individual exit item.** Proposal: one 3–5 minute unaided item on the session's target, collected from everyone, which sets the next pre-class assignment (more worked material if many fail). Untested.
7. **Count the home time and keep it within budget.** Record the minutes the pre-class work takes (ask learners) and keep total home time near what the course asked before the flip; do not add the video on top of the old homework. Basis: in a review of college science comparisons, active-learning conditions often required more home time than their comparisons ([Time-on-task is frequently unequal across compared conditions…](../claims/time-on-task-unequal-in-active-learning-comparisons.md) [~M]), which is both a cost to learners and a confound in any comparison.
8. **Plan an arc across sessions, not one lesson.** Proposal for a term: **weeks 1–2**, short teacher-made videos with a note-taking guide and embedded questions, a 5-minute recap at the start of class for those still learning the routine, and the check results discussed openly; **middle weeks**, no recap, the check only, and pre-class work with fewer prompts (one explain-why question instead of a guide); **late weeks**, pre-class sources learners must select or summarise themselves (a chapter section, a paper), and live time on whole tasks of the kind the course assessment asks for. A learner moves from guided to unguided preparation when they pass the opening check on two consecutive sessions; a learner who fails two in a row gets a short in-class or office-hours route to the material rather than more video. The arc and its thresholds are untested proposals; how to withdraw support is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).

When a condition is not met: if most learners cannot or will not prepare (no access, shift work, no time), give the first 10–15 minutes of the session to the pre-class material in class ("in-class flip") and keep the application; if the material cannot be followed alone, teach it live and flip a later, more familiar topic instead.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether learners will and can do the pre-class work.** Access, time outside class, and whether earlier assigned reading was done decide whether the session can assume preparation. If unstated, ask: what share of these learners did last term's assigned preparation, and what device, connection and time do they have between sessions?
- **Whether the first explanation can be understood alone.** A learner with prior knowledge of the topic can learn from a video; a `novice` facing complex material may stall with nobody to ask. If unstated, ask: when learners met similar material without a teacher before, could they answer a simple question on it afterwards?
- **Who gives feedback in the live session, and to how many.** The gain is expected from coached application; one teacher with 200 learners, a volunteer tutor with ten minutes, and a small seminar call for different session designs. If unstated, ask: during the session, who will look at what each learner or group produces, and how soon?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: whether each learner opened and finished the pre-class material (platform data, a short self-report), their answers on the embedded and opening-check items, what they produce during application (work, talk, questions asked), and their unaided exit item. "Prepared", "engaged", "self-regulating" and "understands" are inferences. A completed video view does not show that the learner followed it; a correct check item does not show they can apply it.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Many learners arrive without having done the pre-class work | No time or access; previous sessions did not use the preparation; the material is too long or too hard; they expect the teacher to re-teach it | Check completion by learner against access and schedules; ask, privately, why; see whether completion rises after a session visibly built on the preparation. |
| Learners watched the video but fail the opening check | The video did not teach enough for learners to follow alone; the check asks for more than the video showed; learners watched without attending | Ask one learner to explain the first step aloud; compare check scores of those who answered the embedded questions with those who skipped them. |
| Pass the opening check, but stall on the application task | The gap is in applying, not knowing (the flip is doing its job and practice is needed); the task jumps too far beyond the material; group work hides who can do it | Give a closer intermediate item with a hint; record who produced each part of the group's work. |
| Exit items are fine, but course exam scores do not move | The exit items are closer to the session than the exam; the exam measures something the sessions do not practise; too little application time | Compare exit items with exam items for alignment; record the minutes of application in each session. |
| Learners say they prefer lectures, or rate the flipped course lower | More work at home; discomfort with being exposed in class; a real goal the course does not serve (a clear account from an expert); a feeling of fluency from lectures that is not matched by performance | Compare self-report with the exit items and exam; ask what they want the live time for; record home time. |
| Some learners fall steadily behind on preparation | Competing work or family time; no place to study; low confidence; a missing prerequisite | Look at when they open the material and what they skip; ask how they plan their week; offer the in-class route and see whether performance recovers. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A check is itself a learning event and changes what it measures; record what remains unresolved.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares a flipped course with the same course unflipped. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

- [Active learning improves exam performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]: what the freed time is for. A meta-analysis of undergraduate STEM courses found exam and concept-inventory scores 0.47 SD higher under active learning than under traditional lecturing (158 studies), and lecture students 1.5 times as likely to fail; a second meta-analysis found the gap for underrepresented and low-income students narrowed from −0.62 to −0.42 SD, and only under high-intensity active learning. It supports using live time actively; it does not say where the first exposure should happen, and its courses are not described as flipped.
- [Peer-teaching flipped classroom students show higher calculus conceptual understanding than conventional flipped classroom students after controlling for pretest](../claims/ptfc-beats-cfc-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]: the only claim here that studies flipped classes. Two intact freshman calculus classes (36 and 34 students), a nonequivalent-groups pretest–posttest design; the class that added peer teaching scored higher on conceptual understanding after controlling for pretest (78.89 against 73.44; F(1,67) = 6.51, p = .013), with no effect size printed. It shows that two flipped designs can differ, so what happens in the live time matters; it does not compare flipped with unflipped teaching, and the claim page does not describe what the peer teaching involved. One study, `q2`.
- [Blended Learning Improves Outcomes](../claims/blended-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]: a limit on attribution. A meta-analysis found 23 blended-versus-face-to-face contrasts favouring the blend (g+ = 0.35), mostly in higher education and professional training, but its authors caution that the blended arms usually had extra learning time and resources. Flipped designs are one of the blends the claim page names; the average cannot be credited to moving exposition online.
- [Time-on-task is frequently unequal across compared conditions, with active learning conditions typically requiring more home time](../claims/time-on-task-unequal-in-active-learning-comparisons.md) [~M]: a limit and a cost. A review of 59 college science comparisons found 44.1% gave conditions different home time, and in each the active-learning condition was designed for more, partly through recorded lectures and longer homework. A flipped course that wins may be winning on extra hours, and the hours are a cost to learners. Descriptive.
- [Students report rarely reading their physics textbooks, with half claiming never to have used it in an entire semester](../claims/students-rarely-read-textbook.md) [~M]: a limit on preparation. In one introductory physics study only 16% of completers said they read the textbook once a week or more and 50% said they never used it; the entry notes a three-year survey at the same university in which about 70% reported rarely or never reading before class. Self-report, one university; it is why the default design checks preparation and builds on it.
- [Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M]: carried to the pre-class questions. In laboratory studies, answering questions on a passage before reading it, then reading it, gave better retention than spending the same time studying; every pretest was followed by the full passage. Not tested in a flipped course; the marker is set below the claim's cap because it is carried.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: carried to the explain-why prompts in the pre-class work and the session. A meta-analysis found self-explanation prompts improved learning by g = .55 across task types; a mathematics meta-analysis found classroom and delayed evidence much more limited. Not tested in flipped courses.
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]: carried to the teacher's coaching in the session. Fourth and fifth graders tutored one-to-one on long division under fully contingent support outperformed fixed or no support (8 per condition); a review notes most evidence is one-to-one and contingency is scarce in whole-class teaching. The circulating teacher in a large flipped class is not one-to-one tutoring.
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]: carried to preparation. Two theoretical syntheses describe self-monitoring against a goal as a central process in self-regulated learning; no study of preparation for flipped classes is recorded.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (undergraduate STEM courses, one calculus class pair, laboratory passages, children with a tutor), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures delayed retention after a flipped course.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually performance on the course's assessment, or on a representative task, at the end of the course, with live time spent where the teacher adds most. Ask separately what learners want from the live time and from the course: an expert's clear explanation, a chance to practise with help, fewer hours at home, flexibility to study around work, or simply the credential. Agreement and divergence are observations. A learner who values the teacher's explanation may experience a flip as being told to teach themselves; one who works shifts may value replayable video and dread fixed preparation deadlines.

For example, a part-time adult in an evening accounting course may want to pass the certificate exam with as little home time as possible, while the instructor's objective is that learners can prepare a set of accounts from messy source documents. Pre-class videos that add an hour a week serve the instructor and cost the learner; a 10-minute video with two questions, an in-class check and most of the evening spent on a real set of documents may serve both. Record both aims, and do not read low preparation as low motivation before asking about time.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of flipped and unflipped versions of the same course, with the same total time, materials and assessments, show no advantage on aligned outcomes; if gains in flipped courses disappear once home time is equated; or if courses that use the freed time for application do no better than those that use it for review of the video. If learners who do not prepare do as well as those who do, the preparation step is not doing the work credited to it. If novices consistently fail to learn the first explanation alone, the relationship should be restricted to learners with some prior knowledge of the topic. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "a bad flip" after the fact.

An end-of-course exam advantage, delayed retention, self-efficacy, preparation rates and learner satisfaction are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no best video length, no share of live time, no preparation rate needed and no effect of flipping itself.

## Description
Flipped learning is the principle of shifting initial content exposure outside class so that live time can be used for application, coaching, and discussion.

## Implications

Flipped learning is most valuable when synchronous time is scarce and worth preserving for the kinds of work learners cannot do as well alone: coached practice, discussion, feedback, and collaborative sensemaking. The principle is not simply “watch videos at home.” Its value depends on redesigning in-person or live time around adaptive coaching and responsive support [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] rather than moving a lecture file to another location. Flipped designs are also stronger when learners monitor and complete pre-class work reliably [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] and when both pre-class prompts and in-class discussion ask them to explain ideas rather than merely consume content [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The central risk is that preparation moves out of class while the pedagogy stays the same.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **Accessible pre-class materials** — learners need reliable, usable access to the initial exposure content
- **Clear connection between preparation and class activity** — what learners do before class must clearly matter to what happens during class
- **Active live-session design** — class time must be repurposed for problem solving, discussion, coaching, or feedback
#### Constraints
- **Preparation compliance is variable** — the model weakens when learners arrive unprepared and the class design assumes prior exposure
- **Access inequities can weaken the model** — devices, bandwidth, schedule constraints, and home conditions matter
- **Moving lecture out of class is not enough by itself** — the pedagogical gain comes from redesigning class time, not from relocation alone

### Target Learners
- Learners who benefit from replayable initial exposure and guided live practice
- Learners in courses where conceptual explanation can be handled asynchronously but application benefits from teacher presence
- Learners in blended or technology-supported environments where pre-class preparation is realistic

### Target Learning Objectives
- Use synchronous time for higher-value guided application
- Increase learner access to replayable initial explanations
- Improve feedback, discussion, and supported problem solving during live sessions

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Digital Learning](digital-learning.md) — digital delivery often makes replayable pre-class exposure feasible
- [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) — the main instructional benefit comes from shifting live time toward coached performance
- [Active Learning](active-learning.md) — flipped learning is strongest when class time is redesigned around activity rather than passive review

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — flipped models depend heavily on learner preparation and time management before class

### Claims
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — flipped learning depends in part on learners tracking and completing pre-class work
- [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M] — the payoff increases when class time is used for adaptive coaching and responsive support
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — pre-class prompts and in-class explanation can make flipped designs stronger than content exposure alone
