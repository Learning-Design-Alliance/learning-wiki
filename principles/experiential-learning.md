# Experiential Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 12 claims (9 for, 3 mixed) · 22 studies (7 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 22 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

Experiential learning means learning from something the learner does or undergoes (a placement, a simulation, a lab, a field or community project, a hands-on task) rather than only from being told. This page owns the general relationship: **an experience becomes learning when it is followed by structured reflection, connection to a concept or criterion, and a further attempt with feedback; experience alone, given to learners who lack the knowledge to interpret it, is the condition under which the relationship fails.**

**No claim in the wiki tests experiential learning as a whole**, a full experience–reflection–conceptualization–experimentation cycle against an alternative of matched time. The only claim naming it directly is a second-hand report, inside one program-design article, that experiential learning increased engagement, practical skill and ethical reasoning in human services education ([Reviewed studies report experiential learning benefits](../claims/experiential-learning-engagement-skills-ethical-reasoning.md) [+W]; no design, sample or effect size reported). What the wiki does test are components of the relationship, each in its own population:

- **Structured reflection after doing** beats unstructured reflection: in school writing-to-learn and in a first-year engineering course (below).
- **Repeated, feedback-rich practice in a simulated experience** beats traditional clinical education for procedural clinical skills in medical education (below).
- **Doing with materials** beats symbol-only instruction in mathematics from kindergarten to college, and manipulation, physical or virtual, beats no manipulation for undergraduate physics concepts (below).
- **Unguided discovery** learns less than explicit instruction, across domains and in an experiment with `child` learners who were `novice`s on the target procedure, while discovery with feedback, worked examples or scaffolding does better than other instruction (below). One of the reviews behind that claim names experiential teaching among the minimally guided forms it argues against.

So the expected change is in `skill-performance`, `conceptual-understanding` and `near-transfer`, conditional on: the learner's task-specific knowledge of what the experience is meant to teach; whether the experience is guided and coached; whether reflection is structured by specific prompts rather than invited; whether the experience is connected to a named concept, rule or criterion; whether learners try again in a changed situation with feedback; the outcome chosen; and the horizon. No claim here reports a delayed outcome of an experiential cycle, an effect on attitudes or dispositions beyond self-report, or an effect on far transfer.

**Siblings and pattern.** [Inquiry-based learning](inquiry-based-learning.md) is one experiential form in which the experience is an investigation of a question; its conditional model (guided inquiry in school science, unguided discovery as the failure condition) sits inside this one and should be read for question-driven designs. [Problem-based learning](problem-based-learning.md) sits inside inquiry, organized around an authentic problem. Placements, simulations, service and practice-based designs are experiential without being inquiry, and are what this page adds. The [experiential learning cycle](../patterns/experiential-learning-cycle.md) is the reusable design built on this relationship; it has not yet been converted to this format, and its sequence is a proposal.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, with the pattern's sequence, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it, and no claim tests the sequence as a whole.

1. **Name the capability before choosing the experience**: what the learner should be able to do afterwards (a clinical procedure, a judgement, an explanation, a way of working with people), in what situation, and how it will be checked. Untested; from the earlier page's learning objectives.
2. **Check what the learner can already do on this task, and teach what is missing first.** Ask for the key idea or first move in a short probe. Where it is absent, demonstrate or explain it, with a worked example, before the experience, and narrow the experience so only one or two things can vary. Evidence: unassisted discovery learned less than explicit instruction, and discovery with feedback, worked examples or scaffolding did better ([Guided Inquiry Outperforms Pure Discovery](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [~S], below). Carried from discovery tasks to experiential tasks by analogy; not tested on placements or service.
3. **Give a meaningful experience tied to the goal**: a task, role or situation the learner acts in, with consequences that can surprise them, and a stated noticing focus ("watch what happens to the patient's oxygen saturation when you..."). Untested; from the earlier page's requirements and the pattern's preparation step.
4. **Coach during the experience in response to what the learner does**, raising support after a failure and lowering it after a success. From the earlier page; the [contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] claim it cited rests mostly on one-to-one tutoring of school pupils and has not been read against this model (Further evidence, below).
5. **Debrief straight after with specific prompts**, not "reflect on the experience": what did you expect, what happened, where did they differ, what would you do differently and why. Evidence: specific prompts beat generic ones with the number of reflections held constant (the claim page reports 52 reflections apiece over a semester in the engineering study), and metacognitive-reflection prompts were the strongest content moderator in a 48-study writing-to-learn synthesis ([Reflective Practice Improves Outcomes When Structured](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M], below). Neither study debriefs an experiential task; how soon after the experience is not tested.
6. **Connect the experience to the concept**: the learner names the rule, principle or criterion the experience illustrates and produces an artifact (a stated rule, a written account, a revised plan). Untested; from the earlier page's requirement that facilitation connect experience to concepts, and the pattern's conceptualization stage.
7. **Try again in a changed situation, with feedback, and repeat**: rehearse to a stated standard, raising difficulty across attempts. Evidence: simulation with deliberate practice (repetition to mastery standards, immediate feedback, increasing difficulty) beat traditional clinical education on procedural skills ([Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M], below). Tested on procedural and psychomotor clinical skills; untested for interpersonal or judgement goals.
8. **Judge the design on separate outcomes**: performance on a new, unaided task; explanation of the concept; and, where it matters, a delayed check, not on satisfaction, journals completed or time spent doing. Untested; from the earlier page's constraint that experience can be mistaken for progress.

Constraints from the earlier page, still untested: experience alone can let learners repeat ineffective habits; high-complexity experiences can overwhelm novices; experiential tasks can become performative or logistically heavy if not tied to the goal; and reflection must go beyond narrating what happened toward interpretation and a next step. If a step cannot be provided (no facilitator, no second attempt), keep the steps that remain in the same order and shorten the experience rather than dropping reflection or the second attempt (untested proposal).

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

1. **The learner's starting knowledge of what the experience is meant to teach.** A `novice` cannot tell which parts of an experience matter, so an unguided experience is mostly noise; a learner with prior knowledge can interpret more of it unaided. If unstated, ask: have they done or seen a task like this before, and can they say what to watch for?
2. **The kind of goal.** A `procedure` or psychomotor skill gains from repeated attempts to a standard; a `concept` gains from manipulation linked to its symbolic form; a judgement or an `attitude-motivation` goal has, in this wiki, only self-reported evidence. If unstated, ask: what will the learner do differently afterwards, and how would we see it?
3. **Who can structure reflection and feedback, and when.** The tested components all depend on specific prompts and feedback; a placement with no one to debrief is experience without the rest of the relationship. If unstated, ask: who will be with the learner afterwards, for how long, and can learners try again?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are `novice`s on the target task | Teach or demonstrate the key idea first, with a worked example; narrow the experience to one or two variables; supply the reflection prompts and the vocabulary for naming the concept; check an unaided step before removing support | [Guided Inquiry Outperforms Pure Discovery](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [~S]; tested on discovery tasks, school and mixed domains, not on placements |
| Learners are `advanced` or experienced practitioners | Shorten the preparation, make the experience less structured, and have learners write their own debrief questions; keep the second attempt with feedback | untested proposal; the guided-inquiry claim's narrative review argues that the advantage of guidance recedes with prior knowledge, which no recorded study tests here |
| Learners are `child`ren, or school-age | Use short, concrete hands-on tasks and connect each to its symbol or written form in the same session; tell them what to try, not only to explore | [Hands-on learning improves achievement](../claims/hands-on-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] (mathematics, kindergarten to college); [Guided Inquiry Outperforms Pure Discovery](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [~S] (third and fourth graders) |
| Adult professionals, `workplace-clinical`, a `procedure` or psychomotor skill | Replace one-off exposure with simulated practice repeated to a stated mastery standard, immediate feedback after each attempt, rising difficulty, and a debrief; add booster sessions if the skill is not used soon | [Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]; medical education, procedural skills only; boosters untested |
| The goal is a science or mathematics `concept` | Have learners manipulate the system (physical or virtual), predict before acting, and map the result onto the formula or diagram afterwards; if a physical lab is unavailable, a virtual one is a fair substitute | [Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning](../claims/physical-experience-enhances-science-learning.md) [~M] (undergraduate physics); [Hands-on learning improves achievement](../claims/hands-on-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M] |
| The goal is an attitude or disposition (civic responsibility, empathy, respect for diversity) | Use service or community experience with structured reflection, and measure a behaviour or a decision in a new case, not only self-report; treat any benefit as unconfirmed | [Service-learning is associated with improvements in self-efficacy and social responsibility](../claims/service-learning-improves-civic-and-personal-outcomes.md) [+W]; second-hand and self-reported |
| `online-self-paced`, no facilitator present | Replace the live debrief with specific written prompts after each task and a model answer to compare against; let learners retry a changed task with automatic feedback | [Reflective Practice Improves Outcomes When Structured](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M] for specific written prompts (engineering undergraduates, in a classroom course); untested online |
| One session (`single-session`) | Run one complete loop (brief experience, prompted debrief, named rule, one retry) rather than a long experience with no debrief; cut loops, not stages | untested proposal, from the experiential-learning-cycle pattern |
| Large group, one facilitator | Run debriefs in pairs or small groups against a shared written protocol; spend facilitator time on the conceptualization step, where a wrong rule is hardest for peers to catch | untested proposal, from the pattern; the reflection synthesis reports peer interaction as a moderator but the claim page does not say in which direction |
| Assessment is high-stakes, or credit attaches to reflection | Assess performance on a new unaided task; do not grade journals for completion; score explanation and performance separately | untested; from the earlier page's constraint that reflection must go beyond narration |
| Learners reflect in a second language, or are uncomfortable reflecting in public | Offer written or recorded reflection, in the language they choose, before any group debrief; give sentence starters for the prompts | untested proposal, from the pattern's personalization |

Six rows rest on a claim (novices, children, clinical procedures, concepts, attitudes, self-paced online), each only as far as its population; the rows for advanced learners, one session, large groups, assessment and language are untested proposals.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what a learner does in and after an experience under stated conditions: the actions they take, what they say they noticed, the rule or reason they state in the debrief, the help and feedback they received, and what they do on the next, changed attempt. "Learned from the experience", "reflective" and "competent" are inferred states. Enthusiasm, time on task and a fluent journal are observations of participation; none shows that the intended capability changed.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Debrief narrates what happened, names no rule or next step | The prompt invited narration; the learner lacks the concept needed to interpret the event; reluctance to reflect in front of others | Repeat with a specific prompt ("what did you expect, what happened, why the difference?") in writing, privately; if still no rule, probe the concept directly. |
| States the right rule in the debrief, repeats the error on the next attempt | Rule held verbally but not yet proceduralized; next situation differed in more than intended; time pressure or load on the second attempt | Give a component probe of just that step, then a matched retry with the same load; record the feedback given between attempts. |
| Performs well in the simulation, poorly on placement | Simulation fidelity differs on the features that matter; anxiety or social demands of the real setting; skill not used since training | Compare the two tasks feature by feature; observe a second real attempt with a known stressor removed; note the interval since practice. |
| A `novice` is busy throughout but draws the wrong lesson | Unguided search without a schema; experience did not contain the target contrast; misreading surface features as causes | Ask what they watched for; check whether the experience as run could show the target relation; demonstrate it and retry. |
| Reports large personal growth, little change in observed behaviour | Self-report sensitive to the experience's emotional weight; change real but not in what was observed; observation too soon | Observe a decision in a new case at a stated later point; compare self-report with a peer's or supervisor's record. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause on its own, and each probe is itself an experience that may change the learner.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Reflective Practice Improves Outcomes When Structured](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 48 school-based writing-to-learn interventions found writing overall produced a small gain (d = 0.22) and prompts for metacognitive reflection were the only content-type moderator that reached significance (b = 0.48); a quasi-experiment in two sections of a first-year engineering course (N = 208, `adult`) found specific reflection prompts beat generic ones on exams, project scores and two problem sets with the number of reflections held constant, no effect size reported. Not settled: the abstracts could not confirm the entries. Neither study reflects on an experiential task; it supports the structuring of reflection, not experience itself.
- [Reflective practice shows mixed evidence of effectiveness in professional education](../claims/reflective-practice-evidence-mixed-in-professional-education.md) [~M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 23 controlled studies of reflection interventions in higher education (2,010 participants) found a medium positive pooled effect (g = 0.56) that depended on intervention duration, whether reflection included peer interaction, and the reflective activity used. The claim page records that this one synthesis does not show mixed evidence, so its title is unconfirmed. Marked `~` because the average hides variation by implementation; it does not say which configuration to choose. Not settled: abstract only.
- [Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice Improves Clinical Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 14 studies (mostly randomized trials and pre/post comparisons) of medical education found simulation with deliberate practice (repetition, mastery standards, immediate feedback) beat traditional clinical education on clinical skill acquisition (d = 0.71). Procedural and psychomotor skills only; the claim page notes patient-level outcomes and team or decision-making skills are less established. Not settled: abstract only. It supports a structured, feedback-rich experience, not experience as such.
- [Hands-on learning improves achievement](../claims/hands-on-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 55 studies (N = 7,237, kindergarten to college) found mathematics instruction with concrete manipulatives beat abstract-symbol-only instruction, with moderate-to-large effects on retention and small effects on problem solving, transfer and justification, moderated by instructional and methodological features. Mathematics only; not yet checked against its source. It does not separate manipulation from the prediction, discussion and reflection that usually accompany it.
- [Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning](../claims/physical-experience-enhances-science-learning.md) [~M]: a brief physical experience of angular-momentum forces improved college physics quiz scores (lab experiments and a randomized classroom experiment, no effect size in the abstract); in a second study, 234 undergraduates learning heat and temperature through physical, virtual or combined manipulation did equally well, and all beat traditional instruction without experimentation. Marked `~` because it suggests manipulation, not physicality, carries the gain. One of two entries passes the judge.
- [Guided Inquiry Outperforms Pure Discovery](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 164 studies found explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery across 580 comparisons (d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), while enhanced discovery (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) beat other instruction across 360 comparisons (d = 0.30); an experiment with 112 third- and fourth-grade `novice` children found many more mastered the control-of-variables procedure under direct instruction, and they did as well on a later transfer task as the few who discovered it; a narrative review argues minimally guided teaching, experiential teaching among its forms, is less effective for learners without enough prior knowledge. Partly checked: 1 of 3 entries passes; one judge failure was reviewed and dismissed. This is the boundary of the principle: it predicts poor learning from unguided experience for novices, not that guided experiential designs fail. The abstract does not break results down by prior knowledge.

How far each may be carried: the reflection evidence is school writing and one engineering course; the simulation evidence is procedural medical skills; the hands-on evidence is mathematics manipulatives and undergraduate physics; the discovery evidence is mostly school learners and mixed domains, read from abstracts. None is a placement, internship, service project or fieldwork study. Retain learner age and starting knowledge, the comparator (traditional clinical education, generic prompts, symbol-only instruction, explicit instruction), the amount of guidance and feedback, time on task, outcome type and horizon when transporting these results. A pooled effect over unlike implementations is not a forecast for one course or learner, and a nonsignificant difference does not establish equivalence.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

Ask what the learner wants from the experience, and why, separately from the designer's objective. Experiential designs are often chosen because they feel relevant, and relevance to the designer is not the same as value to the learner. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations; enjoyment of a placement or a simulation does not establish that the learner values the target capability, and a learner who values the experience may value something other than what is assessed. Choose a representative instrument, criterion, permitted help and horizon for each outcome that matters: skill performance, explanation, transfer to a new case and changed practice are different claims.

For example, a social-work student on a first placement may want to be trusted by the team and to feel able to sit with a distressed client, while the designer's objective is that the student can run a structured risk assessment and justify its conclusion. A confident debrief establishes neither. Record both aims, use the student's wish to be trusted to frame the debrief questions ("what would make the team rely on your assessment?"), and check the designer's objective separately on a new case, unaided.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if a full experiential cycle (experience, structured reflection, conceptualization, a changed retry with feedback) fails to beat a comparison of matched time on aligned performance or conceptual measures for comparable learners. If structured reflection adds nothing once feedback on a second attempt is given, the reflection step is not carrying the effect and should be shortened. If unguided experience is found to work for learners with stated prior knowledge, record that boundary rather than generalizing it. If attitude or disposition gains appear only in self-report and not in observed behaviour, say so rather than claiming them. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as a shallow debrief or an inauthentic experience after the fact.

An engaging experience, an immediate skill gain, an explanation, near transfer, delayed retention and changed practice in the learner's own setting are separate claims. The evidence recorded here establishes no optimal number of cycles, no fixed order of Kolb's four stages, no readiness threshold for unguided experience, and no effect of placements, internships or service as such.

## Description
Experiential learning is learning by doing, which may include self-directed learning activities. Helping learners make sense of experiences is foundational to adult learning. There are four key elements of experiential learning: concrete experience, reflection and observation, development of new ideas, and experimenting with new ideas. Active participation in experiential learning benefits both learners and the community. Adult learners gain concrete experiences and reflect on them by comparing the new experiences with Background Knowledge. It fosters the upgrading of life skills, such as a respect for diversity, exposure to multiple perspectives, and offers opportunities for critical reflection and Cognitive Flexibility. Experiential learning can also foster personal autonomy, self-fulfillment, interpersonal effectiveness and Social Awareness and Relationship Skills.

## Implications
Experiential learning works because it puts learners in situations where concepts have consequences. Integrated tasks, realistic roles, and repeated cycles of action and reflection tend to improve transfer more than isolated practice alone [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+S], but only when experience is interpreted rather than merely lived through. That is the central design point: reflection and explanation are what turn experience into learning [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and coaching is often necessary so learners can revise decisions in real time rather than simply repeat them [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Without those supports, learners may simply reinforce ineffective habits or mistake activity for progress, especially if novices are immersed without guidance on what to notice or how to act [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A meaningful task, role, or situation that learners can actively engage in rather than only hear about
- Structured reflection so experience becomes interpretable and transferable
- Opportunities to test revised understanding in a new action cycle
- Facilitation that connects experience back to concepts, models, or criteria

#### Constraints
- Experience alone is not enough; without reflection and feedback, learners may simply repeat ineffective habits
- High-complexity experiences can overwhelm novices unless tasks are scaffolded
- Experiential tasks can become performative or logistically heavy if not clearly tied to the learning goal
- Reflection must go beyond narration (“what happened”) toward interpretation and next steps

### Target Learners
- Learners developing applied, professional, or interpersonal competence
- Adult learners who benefit from relevance, realism, and visible use-value
- Learners who need to integrate concepts, judgment, and action rather than memorize isolated facts
- Learners who may disengage from abstract instruction unless they can test ideas in practice

### Target Learning Objectives
- Strengthen transfer from theory to practice
- Develop reflective judgment by comparing action, outcome, and alternative choices
- Build autonomy and confidence through repeated cycles of doing, reflecting, and retrying
- Deepen understanding by linking concepts to consequences in realistic contexts

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Experiential Learning Theory](../theories/experiential-learning-theory.md) [+S] — Kolb's experiential cycle directly supports the move from concrete experience to reflection, abstraction, and experimentation
- Deweyan and constructivist perspectives support learning through doing and meaning-making from consequences
- Sociocultural perspectives qualify experiential learning by emphasizing the role of guided participation, context, and tools
- [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+S] supports integrated performance tasks as especially useful for transfer

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Unguided experiential learning can impose too much cognitive load on novices
- Reflection quality matters; learners need prompts, models, and sometimes expert feedback to interpret experience well
- Some foundational knowledge may need explicit instruction before an authentic experience becomes productive
