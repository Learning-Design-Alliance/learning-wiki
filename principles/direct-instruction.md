# Direct Instruction

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 9 claims (4 for, 5 mixed) · 18 studies (7 causal, 6 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 3 of 18 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner whose response shows no working method for a structured target (a procedure, concept or principle with a defined correct performance), explicit explanation and modelling followed by guided practice with checks and feedback is expected to produce better near-term performance on that target than leaving the learner to discover it with little support. The relationship is qualified by four things the evidence names: how much the learner already knows about this task (guidance that helps a `novice` can become redundant for an `advanced` learner), the outcome sought (procedural performance and conceptual understanding or transfer do not behave alike), the learner's age, and the horizon at which the outcome is checked. "Direct" means explicit and guided, not lecture without learner activity. The [Direct Instruction pattern](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) specifies a reusable design policy; its local diagnostic branches remain proposals.

## Observation, state and explanation

Record a response **under stated conditions**, not a learner type. What can be observed is an attempt at a representative task, the reasoning given for it, the help that was available and used, and the time taken. "Novice", "has the schema" and "guidance is now redundant" are inferred states. Experience in the wider subject does not settle them for this task, and a correct answer during guided practice does not show what the learner can do alone. Decide which capability matters (an unaided procedure, an explanation, a changed problem, use at a delay) before interpreting a response as readiness to fade support.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot begin a structured task before instruction | No applicable method; method known but not recognised in this representation; task wording or access blocks the attempt | Ask for a first step on the same relation in a familiar and a changed representation, with an accessible response mode, recording any hint. |
| Follows the modelled steps during guided practice, fails an unaided item | Steps copied without the relation behind them; reliance on the visible model or prompt; the unaided item changed more than intended | Give a matched item with the model removed but the prompt kept, then one with both removed; ask why a step is licensed. |
| Succeeds unaided and is slower or less engaged when the full explanation is repeated | Guidance has become redundant for this task; boredom or other disengagement unrelated to the task; the repeat added nothing new | Offer a shorter or no-explanation version of a comparable item and compare accuracy and reasoning, not engagement alone. |
| Correct procedure, cannot explain or apply it to a changed problem | Procedure learned without the concept; the explanation task demands language the learner lacks; transfer was never taught | Compare a non-verbal application probe with a verbal explanation of the same relation, and a near with a further transfer item. |
| Strong at the end of the lesson, weaker a week later | Little consolidation or practice; the immediate check was too close to the modelled examples; interference from later material | Re-test with a new item at the delay, and record what practice and exposure happened in between. |

These rows are proposals for local observation, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence among explanations; one contrast does not identify a cause, and the probe is itself a learning exposure. Record what remains unresolved rather than choosing the explanation whose name fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Unguided discovery against explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis pooled 580 comparisons and found explicit instruction ahead of unassisted discovery (d = 0.38), while discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations came out ahead of other instruction (d = 0.30); one experiment with 112 third- and fourth-grade children found many more mastered the control-of-variables procedure under direct instruction, and those who did matched the few discoverers on a later transfer task. The meta-analysis was read from its abstract, which does not break results down by prior knowledge and gives no horizon; the judge's flag on it was a wrong registry abstract, dismissed on review. It does not predict that explicit teaching beats *guided* discovery, and the third entry is an argument, not new data.
- [Problem solving before instruction](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~S]: across 53 studies, attempting problems before instruction beat instruction first on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36) and made no difference on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03); the advantage reversed for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. Not settled against its sources (one of two entries passes). It qualifies "instruction first" for conceptual and transfer goals with older learners; the horizon of the outcomes is not reported on the claim page, and it does not test a direct-instruction lesson without any instruction.
- [Guidance and expertise](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S]: a review of cognitive-load paradigms and three experiments with electrical trainees found formats that help less experienced learners lose their advantage, and sometimes reverse, once learners hold domain schemas. Not settled against its sources; no effect sizes are recorded. It does not say when to fade or how to measure the point.
- [Teacher-led within inquiry](../claims/teacher-guided-inquiry-outperforms-student-led.md) [+M]: in a meta-analysis of 37 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of inquiry-based science teaching, teacher-led activities had effect sizes about .40 larger than student-led ones. One of two entries passes the judge. It compares degrees of guidance inside inquiry, not explicit instruction against inquiry, and states no learner expertise or horizon.
- [Engelmann's Direct Instruction curricula](../claims/direct-instruction-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 328 studies found positive, significant effects on academic outcomes in reading, mathematics, language and spelling, maintained over time and larger with more exposure. Read from its abstract, not yet checked against its source, with no pooled effect size and no comparator stated. It concerns one family of scripted curricula and does not show that explicit teaching in general beats discovery.

Retain learners, comparator, outcome, horizon and assignment when transporting these results. Syntheses pooled over unlike comparators do not forecast an individual learner's response, and none of these entries tests the full lesson sequence (explain, model, guided practice, checks, independent practice) against the same sequence with one phase removed.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

Ask what the learner wants to be able to do and why, separately from the designer's objective. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations to record; compliance during a well-run lesson is not evidence that the learner shares its aim. Choose a representative task, the aids permitted, a local criterion and a horizon, because fluent procedure, explanation, transfer and delayed retention need different evidence and the evidence above does not treat them alike.

For example, an adult learner may value understanding why a spreadsheet formula gives a wrong total so they can fix their own budgets, while the designer's objective is that they reproduce the formula steps correctly by the end of the session. An end-of-session procedural check can show the second and say little about the first. The pattern shows how to keep both aims, decide whether a conceptual or transfer probe belongs in the objective, and choose between instruction first and a short attempt first accordingly.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners, starting responses and aligned outcomes fail to show an advantage for explicit, guided instruction over minimally guided discovery under defensible comparisons, or if the advantage seen at the end of instruction routinely disappears at a delay. It should be narrowed further if problem-first designs keep beating instruction first on conceptual and transfer outcomes for the learners and tasks a design targets. If redundancy for more knowledgeable learners appears much earlier or later than responses suggest, revise how readiness to fade is observed. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as a missing phase, a poor implementation or insufficient prior knowledge.

Immediate procedural performance, conceptual understanding, transfer, delayed retention and use the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish a fading point, an optimal lesson script or a numerical forecast for an individual learner.

## Implications

Direct instruction is strongest when learners need a clear model of what success looks like, what steps matter, and how to avoid unproductive confusion early in learning. The principle is often misunderstood as “lecture,” but its instructional core is explicitness: the teacher names the goal, demonstrates the process, checks understanding, and supports early performance before fading responsibility. That matters because novice learners benefit when instruction reduces unguided search and makes correct structure visible [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M], and it works best when teacher support adapts to learner performance rather than following a rigid script alone [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]. The main design challenge is calibration. Too little guidance leaves novices searching blindly; too much guidance, held for too long, becomes redundant as expertise grows [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A clearly specified target performance or concept** — direct instruction works best when the instructor can say what learners should do or understand and what correct performance looks like
- **Modeling plus guided practice** — explanation alone is weaker than explicit demonstration followed by coached attempts
- **Frequent checks for understanding** — the teacher needs evidence that learners are actually tracking the explanation before moving on
#### Constraints
- **Can become passive if it stops at telling** — the principle depends on active checking, practice, and feedback, not presentation alone
- **Less suitable for open-ended exploration as a first move** — where the learning goal is broad generation or divergent inquiry, heavy front-loaded direction may narrow thinking too early
- **Guidance should fade with competence** — the same structure that supports novices can create redundancy for advanced learners

### Target Learners
- Novices who need explicit models, worked steps, and reduced ambiguity
- Learners with weak prior knowledge, fragile schemas, or high risk of cognitive overload during unguided discovery
- Learners who benefit from a clear lesson structure and visible success criteria

## Description
Explicit instruction is the principle of teaching with clear explanations, modeling, guided practice, and deliberate checks for understanding rather than leaving key steps or criteria implicit. This page serves as the canonical short-form target for links that refer to explicit instruction broadly rather than to a domain-specific variant.

## Implications
Explicit instruction is especially useful when learners do not yet know what to attend to, what successful performance looks like, or how to begin a task productively. Clear explanation, modeling, guided practice, and checks for understanding can reduce unnecessary ambiguity and help novices build an initial workable routine faster than unguided discovery alone [Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes](../claims/worked-examples-example-problem-sequences.md) [~M]. The tradeoff is that explicitness should support learning, not replace thinking: instruction is strongest when support is responsive to actual learner need [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M], and once learners have enough footing it needs to shift toward application, judgment, and increasing independence rather than permanent step-by-step direction.

#### Requirements
- **Clearly defined target knowledge or skill**
- **Modeling or explanation of the critical moves**
- **Guided practice with feedback**
#### Constraints
- **Can become overly transmissive if application and feedback are weak**
- **Needs adaptation for learner expertise and task complexity**

### Target Learning Objectives
- Accelerate early understanding and reduce unnecessary ambiguity for novices.
