# Wise Feedback Across Racial and Ethnic Difference

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 12 claims (5 for, 6 mixed, 1 against) · 19 studies (8 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 7 of 19 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **framing of critical feedback when the learner has reason to doubt the giver's intent**: a teacher of one racial or ethnic background giving critical comments to a student of another, and more widely a learner from a group subject to negative ability stereotypes in the domain. For such a learner a critique is ambiguous. It can mean "your work falls short of a high standard" or "I expect little of people like you", and the second reading is not unreasonable given how critical comments have been used. The relationship the page proposes: when specific, task-level critique comes with **an explicit statement of the high standard it is held to and an explicit assurance that the giver believes the learner can reach it**, the learner is expected to trust the critique more and act on it (revise, re-attempt, keep going: `persistence`, `motivation-affect`), and the revised work to improve (`skill-performance` on a draft-and-revise product), compared with the same critique given without that framing, or with praise alone in place of critique. The earlier page called this "wise" feedback, from Cohen, Steele and Ross (1999): positive comments, honest criticism, and the assurance, all three together. The same three parts apply when talking to a student's parents or guardians.

The relationship is conditional on the critique itself being specific and usable (the framing amplifies real feedback and cannot replace it), on the assurance being stated rather than left to inference, on the learner having a chance to act on the critique, on the giver's other conduct not contradicting the message, and on the learner actually doubting the giver's intent: where trust is already established, the framing is expected to add little. It is proposed for `adolescent` and `adult` learners and for `child` learners in spoken form, in `classroom`, `online-instructor-led` and `workplace-clinical` settings, over `days-weeks` to `term-plus`, with feedback on drafts, problem solutions and performances.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship.** The studies the earlier page and the [wise feedback strategy](../strategies/wise-feedback.md) rest on (Cohen, Steele & Ross 1999; Yeager et al. 2014, cited on the strategy page) have no claim page here, so their findings are described only as the earlier page described them and carry no evidence marker. What the claims test are neighbouring parts: what the critique must carry to help at all (two meta-analyses: feedback helps on average, by how much information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions make performance worse, more so as feedback turns attention to the self); that praise for ability rather than effort sets children up badly for a later failure (one set of experiments, one review); that teachers' expectations predict students' later performance (one large correlational cohort); that relationship quality goes with engagement (two meta-analyses of correlational studies); and that other brief, identity-targeted "wise interventions" help some stereotyped groups in some settings and fail to replicate or depend on context in others (belonging, values affirmation, growth mindset, empathic discipline).

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete as a default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is beside it.

1. **Write the critique first, to the criteria, as if no framing were coming.** Say what in the work falls short of which criterion, and what to do next; limit it to the two or three changes that matter most for the next draft. Evidence: feedback helps on average and helps most when it carries task information (goal, gap, next step) ([Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S]); the three-question structure itself is a review's model, not a tested format ([Feedback that answers three questions (Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next?) improves learning](../claims/feedback-answers-three-questions-improves-learning.md) [+W]). The earlier page's rule that the criticism should be "just as honest and specific as it would be otherwise" is kept: do not soften it.
2. **Name one thing the work does well, at the level of the work or the strategy, not the person.** "Your second paragraph uses the source to make the argument, not just to report it", not "you're a natural writer". Evidence: praise for intelligence after success left fifth graders worse off after a later failure than praise for effort ([Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [-M], carried from children's problem solving); the positive comment as part of wise feedback is from the earlier page, untested here.
3. **State the standard, out loud or in writing.** One sentence saying that these comments hold the work to a high standard, and naming it: the course's rubric level, the professional standard, the audience's expectation. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's account of Cohen, Steele and Ross (1999).
4. **State the assurance explicitly and ground it in evidence.** One sentence saying you are giving these comments because you believe this learner can reach that standard, tied to something in their work or record ("…and the way you handled the counter-argument on page 2 is why I think so"). The earlier page is firm that it must be stated, not implied. Untested here; keep it about reaching the standard, not about fixed talent (step 2's claim).
5. **Give a revision or re-attempt, with a date.** Without one the assurance has nothing to attach to and the critique ends the episode. Ask for a revision within about a week (an untested proposal), and check the revised work on the criteria named in step 1. How the loop runs is held by [Feedback Loops](feedback-loops.md).
6. **Check how it landed, privately.** After returning the work, ask the learner one question: what they took the main comment to mean, and what they will change. A reading of "you think I can't do this" is the observation the framing exists to prevent; record it and talk it through. Untested proposal.
7. **Keep it consistent across the term, and hand the standard over.** A default arc for a term (untested proposal): from the first piece of graded work (in the first two or three weeks) every critical comment carries steps 2–4, in one or two sentences, by the teacher, one-to-one or in writing; by mid-term, ask the learner to say which criterion they are working towards before you comment, and keep the assurance in every piece; by the end, the learner assesses the draft against the standard first and the teacher's comment confirms or corrects, still with the assurance. Move a learner to the self-assessment stage when they have revised on at least two consecutive pieces without prompting. A single framed note among otherwise dismissive or impersonal comments is expected to read as insincere (the strategy page's caution; no claim tests it).
8. **Use the same three parts with parents and guardians.** Open with a specific positive about the student's work, give the concern with the evidence for it, state the standard and your belief that the student can reach it, and end with one thing the family can support at home. From the earlier page; untested. Communicating high expectations is one of the forms of parental involvement most associated with achievement in correlational meta-analyses ([Parent Involvement Improves Achievement](../claims/parent-involvement-improves-achievement.md) [+W], carried; it does not test teacher–parent conversations).

When a condition is not met: if there is no chance to revise, give the critique as advice for the next comparable piece and say which piece; if the learner plainly already trusts the giver, keep the critique and the standard and shorten the assurance to a clause, rather than dropping the critique.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Whether the learner has reason to doubt the giver's intent.** The framing is aimed at a learner for whom critique is ambiguous: across a racial or ethnic difference, in a domain where their group is stereotyped, early in a course before trust exists, or after a history of low expectations. Where the learner already trusts the giver, it adds little. If unstated, ask: who gives the feedback, who receives it, how long have they worked together, and has the learner had reason to read criticism as a low expectation?
- **Whether the critique is real and usable.** The framing is a frame around specific critique the learner can act on. If the feedback is a grade, a mark or general praise, fix that first. If unstated, ask: what will the comment say, against which criteria, and what can the learner do next with it?
- **Whether the learner gets a next attempt and the giver's other conduct agrees.** An assurance with no revision, or amid grading, discipline or comments that say otherwise, has nothing to stand on. If unstated, ask: is there a revision or re-attempt, and what else does this learner hear from this giver and this institution?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: the comment as given (the critique, the positive comment, the standard and assurance sentences, and the revision offered), whether the learner revised, what changed in the revision and on which criteria, what the learner says the comment meant, and later attendance, submission and help-seeking. "Trusts the feedback", "read it as bias" and "feels the teacher believes in them" are inferred states. A revision shows the comment was used; it does not show why.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Does not revise, or revises nothing the critique named | Read the critique as a low expectation; the critique was not clear enough to act on; no time; the revision did not count for anything | Ask what the main comment meant and what they would change; offer protected time and a reason the revision counts; see whether a clearer, single next step is taken up. |
| Revises only surface features | The critique named surface problems first; the learner does not know how to make the deeper change; the learner is meeting the letter of the request | Give one worked example of the deeper change on another piece, and ask for that change only. |
| Says the teacher "has it in for" them or "doesn't think I can do it" | Earlier experience with this or other teachers; the assurance was missing, generic or contradicted elsewhere; the critique was harsher in tone than intended | Read the written comment again as the learner would; compare the comments given to other learners on similar work; talk it through one-to-one. |
| Discounts the positive comment and the assurance ("you say that to everyone") | The comment was generic or templated; the learner has heard praise used to soften low expectations before | Replace the generic line with one tied to a specific feature of the work, and check whether the response changes. |
| Accepts the critique and improves, with or without the framing | The learner already trusted the giver; the critique alone was enough | Note it: the framing is not needed here, and the difference between learners is itself information. |
| A parent or guardian responds defensively | They heard the concern before the belief; earlier contact with the school was mostly bad news; language or interpreting barriers | Reopen with the specific positive and the standard; ask what they see at home; use their language. |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. Asking a learner how they read a comment is itself a message about the relationship, so ask privately and record it, and keep open the explanations that remain rather than settling on bias or on misunderstanding because one fits.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares critique with and without a stated standard and assurance, across a racial or ethnic difference or otherwise. The studies behind the idea (Cohen, Steele & Ross 1999; Yeager et al. 2014) have no claim pages, and their findings appear here only as the earlier page and the strategy page state them. The claims below bear on parts of the model.

- [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S] for the critique's content, [~S] as a limit: two meta-analyses (Wisniewski et al. 2020, 435 educational studies, d = 0.48 overall, with high-information feedback d = 0.99 against reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24; Kluger & DeNisi 1996, feedback interventions in laboratory and field settings, d = .41, more than a third reducing performance, worse as feedback draws attention to the self). It supports making the critique specific and task-focused, and it is the strongest reason for caution: a framing that makes the exchange about the person rather than the task could cost performance. Wise feedback's standard and assurance are about the task's standard and the learner's capacity to reach it, not praise of the self, but no study here tests where they fall.
- [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [~S]: the same two meta-analyses read for the level of feedback; effectiveness declines as feedback moves attention towards the self. The same limit as above.
- [Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [-M]: experiments with fifth graders (Study 1, N = 128) on a nonverbal problem set, and a narrative review that finds the harm moderated by age and gender and finds sincere process praise can help. It shapes the positive comment and the assurance (about the work and about reaching a standard, not about being smart); it does not test praise inside critical feedback on academic work.
- [Teacher expectation bias predicted Dutch secondary students' performance up to five years later, after controls for prior achievement, IQ and motivation, in one large correlational cohort study](../claims/teacher-expectation-effects-on-achievement.md) [+W]: about 11,000 students, associational, no standardized effect size. It supports the premise that expectations matter for later performance; it does not show that stating high expectations changes anything.
- [Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of correlational and longitudinal studies (Cornelius-White 2007, mean r = .31; Roorda et al. 2011, 99 studies). It supports treating trust as a condition of the relationship; neither tests whether improving relationships, or framing feedback, raises engagement.
- **Limiting: brief, identity-targeted "wise interventions" are fragile and context-dependent.** [A brief values-affirmation writing exercise raised African American seventh-graders' grades in two small field experiments, but a large same-district replication found no effect](../claims/self-affirmation-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]; [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M] (two randomized experiments at college entry, abstracts only; the large one found the effect only where students' groups were afforded opportunities to belong); [Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial](../claims/growth-mindset-improves-achievement.md) [~M]; [Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~M] (halved suspensions in one field experiment; a small, marginal main effect in the preregistered replication, with the racial disparity narrowed from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points). None tests feedback framing. Together they say a short message targeting how a learner interprets their situation can matter for some groups in some contexts and can also fail to replicate with the same procedure, so a designer should expect small and variable effects, not a reliable gain, and should check locally.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (children on puzzles, ninth graders' GPA, college persistence, suspensions, correlations across many studies), so do not rank them. None measures revision uptake after wise-framed critique, the outcome the earlier page's sources are described as measuring.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually that critical feedback is used: the learner revises on the named criteria and the work improves, and over a term the learner keeps submitting, revising and asking for help. Elicit separately what the learner wants: a better grade on this piece, to be treated fairly, to be seen as capable by this teacher, to get through the course without being singled out, or to reach a standard they care about (a profession, a performance, their family's hopes). Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values being treated fairly may watch how others' work is commented on before trusting anything said to them; one who values not being singled out may reject any feedback given in front of others, however well framed.

For example, a first-year nursing student from a group under-represented in the programme may want above all to show a clinical placement that they belong there, while the module leader's objective is a care-plan assignment that meets the rubric's top band. Framed critique on the care plan serves the leader's objective; saying in the assurance that the standard is the one the placement will hold them to, and that their reasoning on one patient shows they can meet it, links the two. Record both, so a revised care plan is not read as the confidence the student came for, or the reverse.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies that vary only the standard-and-assurance framing, around the same critique, find no difference in revision or quality, or find it only on the first piece and not across a term. It should narrow if the framing helps only where trust is low (across a difference, early in a course) and adds nothing elsewhere, which is the model's own prediction; and it should be rethought if the assurance turns out to work like person-level praise and lower performance, as the feedback meta-analyses would predict for feedback that draws attention to the self. A replication failure of the kind recorded for values affirmation would mean treating the framing as a low-cost option to test locally, not a requirement. Revision uptake, quality of the revised work, later achievement, persistence in the course, and the learner's trust are separate outcomes; evidence for one is not evidence for the others.

## Description
Feedback that both encourages and includes genuine criticism is already a delicate balance to strike, and it becomes measurably harder across a racial or ethnic difference between teacher and student: students of color have historically grounded reasons to distrust critical comments from a white teacher, since such comments can be (and too often have been) a vehicle for lower expectations rather than genuine developmental feedback. Cohen, Steele, and Ross (1999) found that effective — "wise" — feedback across this kind of difference needs three components together, not just the usual two: **positive comments**, **criticism**, and — the addition that makes it "wise" — **an explicit assurance that the teacher believes the student is capable of reaching a higher standard**. Without that third component, criticism risks being read (not unreasonably, given the historical pattern) as confirmation of a lower expectation rather than as an investment in the student's actual growth.

This same three-part structure extends directly to conversations with parents and guardians: the same trust dynamic that makes wise feedback necessary with a student of a different racial or ethnic background applies when discussing that student's performance with their parents, and the same three components — positive framing, honest criticism, and an explicit statement of belief in the student's capability — apply there too.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine, specific criticism — wise feedback is not a way to avoid giving critical feedback, it is a way to give it so it lands as intended
- An explicit, stated assurance of belief in the student's capability, not merely an implied or assumed one — the whole mechanism depends on this being made visible, not left for the student to infer
#### Constraints
- Feedback that only ever offers uncritical praise across this kind of difference avoids the trust problem by avoiding the substance of feedback altogether, which serves the student's actual learning far worse than doing the fuller, harder work of wise feedback properly
- This principle does not imply that critical feedback should be softened or diluted — the criticism itself should be just as honest and specific as it would be otherwise; only the framing around it changes

### Target Learners
- Students whose racial or ethnic background differs from their teacher's, particularly students of color receiving feedback from a white teacher

### Target Learning Objectives
- Critical feedback that is actually received and acted on as intended, rather than filtered through a reasonable, historically-grounded suspicion that it reflects lowered expectations rather than genuine developmental investment
