# Goal Setting & Monitoring

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (4 for, 3 mixed) · 12 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 causal, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 3 of 12 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner about to work on a task, replacing no goal or a vague intention ("do your best") with a goal that is specific, challenging and accepted, and adding a way to check progress against it, is expected to raise effort, persistence and task performance in the near term. The relationship is conditional on four things. **Task complexity and expertise**: the pooled advantage of specific, difficult goals is smaller on complex tasks, and for a learner who is a `novice` on the task a goal naming the process to carry out (the steps of a strategy) is expected to help skill acquisition more than a goal naming only the result; an outcome goal may become useful once the process is under control. **Commitment and self-efficacy**: a goal the learner does not accept, or does not believe reachable, is not expected to work. **A plan and feedback**: a goal with an if-then plan for the expected obstacle, and progress information the learner can read against it, is expected to do more than the goal alone. **The outcome measured**: most of the evidence below measures work performance, health behaviour, course completion or on-task behaviour, not learning on a `delayed-retention` or `near-transfer` test, and no claim here tests goal setting plus monitoring against a learning outcome in an education setting.

This page owns the general relationship between a goal, monitoring against it, and performance. The [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) principle sits beside and around it: it models the whole plan–monitor–act cycle and is chiefly about whether learners can monitor accurately and act on what they notice. Read that page for monitoring accuracy, metacognitive prompts and regulation training; read this one for what the goal itself should be (specific, challenging, process or outcome, proximal or distant, with or without an if-then plan) and how progress against it is made visible. The [SRL pattern](../patterns/self-regulated-learning.md) carries a reusable design policy for both.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it; none has been tested as a sequence with learners.

1. **Name one goal per task, specific and checkable.** State what will be done, to what criterion, by when (a [SMARTER goal](../elements/smarter-goals.md)): "revise two paragraphs so each has a topic sentence and one piece of evidence, by Friday", not "improve my writing". Keep to one or two active goals at once; the earlier page warned that competing goals fragment attention (untested; from the earlier page). Evidence for specificity: [specific, difficult goals](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+W] (one narrative review, not yet checked against its source).
2. **Make it challenging but reachable for this learner.** Set it just above the learner's last observed performance on the same task, not at the final standard. Evidence: [goal setting improves performance](../claims/goal-setting-improves-performance.md) [+M], with smaller effects on complex tasks. How far above is not stated by any claim here; one step beyond the last success is a proposal.
3. **For a learner new to the task, set process goals first.** The goal names the steps of the strategy ("apply the three revision checks to each paragraph"), not the score. Shift to an outcome goal ("score 3 of 4 on the rubric") once the learner carries out the process without prompting on two consecutive tasks (the switching criterion is a proposal). Evidence: [process goals for novices](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M], tested with high-school students learning writing revision.
4. **Break a distant goal into proximal sub-goals**, each reachable within a session or a week, so progress is visible early. Evidence: [proximal goals](../claims/proximal-goals-feedback-peer-models-build-learning-efficacy.md) [+W], reported second-hand in a review.
5. **Add an if-then plan for the likeliest obstacle**: "If I have not started by 8 pm, then I will do the first sub-goal for ten minutes." Evidence: [implementation intentions](../claims/implementation-intentions-improve-goal-attainment.md) [~M], with a field qualification (below).
6. **Make progress visible against the goal at every session.** A [progress tracker](../elements/progress-tracking.md) or a short [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) records the result against the criterion, not effort or time alone; the learner then chooses a next action (continue, change strategy, seek help). The earlier page held that monitoring without feedback is ineffective (untested here as a learning claim; the [self-regulated learning](self-regulated-learning.md) page covers whether learners' monitoring is accurate).
7. **Judge the design on the learning outcome the objective names**, at its horizon. Completion of goal sheets, streaks and on-task rates are process observations, not the outcome.

When a stated condition is not met: if the learner will not commit to the goal, ask what they want from the task before setting another (see the objective section below); if the learner doubts they can reach it, lower it to a reachable sub-goal and show the first success before raising it; if there is no feedback channel, do not add goals alone, add the check first.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **The learner's experience with this task.** A `novice` needs a process goal and small sub-goals; an experienced learner can work to an outcome goal and may set their own. If unstated, ask: have they carried out a task like this before, unaided, to the standard required?
- **What the goal is for: a behaviour, a completion, or learning.** Most goal evidence here is on task performance, health behaviour and course completion; a goal to "finish the module" can be met without learning. If unstated, ask: what will the learner be able to do afterwards, measured how and when?
- **Whether the learner accepts the goal and believes it reachable.** An assigned goal the learner rejects or doubts is expected to fail however well it is written. If unstated, ask the learner what they are aiming for and how confident they are of reaching it this week.

## Observation, state and explanation

A goal sheet, a progress chart, a streak count, a self-rating against a target and a completed check-in are observations. Commitment, self-efficacy and "monitoring" are inferred states. A learner who writes a goal may not hold it; a learner who logs progress may log what is expected. Record what was observed, under which supports (template, teacher conference, prompt), and keep the inference separate.

The mechanism the theory offers (a goal directs attention and effort, and feedback against it shows the gap to close) is drawn from narrative reviews and theory; the claims linked here do not isolate it.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Sets an easy or vague goal | Low self-efficacy for the task; unclear criterion; protecting a grade; does not value the task | Ask how confident they are of a harder goal; show a worked example of a specific goal; check whether goals are graded; ask what they want from the task. |
| Sets a specific goal, makes no progress | Missing task knowledge or strategy; obstacle with no plan; goal too far above current performance | Give a short supported probe of the first step; ask what stopped them and whether an if-then plan exists; compare with a nearer sub-goal. |
| Progress log is kept, performance flat | Logging is compliance, not monitoring; the logged measure is not the target capability; no action follows the log | Compare log entries with task traces (attempts, revisions); check that the logged measure matches the outcome; ask what they did differently after the last check. |
| Reaches an outcome goal, cannot repeat the task | Goal met through help or a narrow route; outcome goal set before the process was learned | Repeat a matched task without support; ask the learner to describe the steps; switch to a process goal and reobserve. |
| Abandons goals after early setbacks | Goal too distant; low self-efficacy after failure; competing goals | Break into proximal sub-goals and observe whether persistence returns; ask how many goals they are carrying; record the setback that preceded abandonment. |

These rows are proposals and have not been tested with learners. A single contrast shifts confidence among explanations; it does not identify a cause, and a goal-setting conversation or probe is itself an intervention, so record every exposure.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Goal setting improves performance](../claims/goal-setting-improves-performance.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 125 goal-setting studies on work and laboratory tasks found specific difficult and hard goals raised performance, with the effect strongest on easy tasks (d = .76) and weakest on complex ones such as business simulations and research productivity (d = .42); a meta-analysis of randomized trials (141 papers, 16,523 participants) found goal setting alone had a small effect on behaviour change (d = .34), larger when goals were difficult, public or set for a group, on mostly health and everyday behaviours. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). It does not test learning outcomes, and the claim page calls itself underspecified.
- [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M]: one narrative review of 35 years of goal-setting research, coded `q2`, with no effect size and n not applicable; it states the relationship holds provided the person is committed and has the ability. Not yet checked against its source. It is theory and summary, not a study a reader can inspect here; it does not predict effects for learners in education settings.
- [Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]: in an experiment with high-school students (`adolescent`) learning writing revision, students assigned process goals outperformed those assigned outcome goals, and the results suggested shifting to outcome goals once the skill is mastered; a theoretical chapter synthesizes similar studies (including dart-throwing). Sample size and effect size are not reported on the claim page. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). It does not set a criterion for when to switch.
- [Proximal goals raise self-efficacy for learning more than distant goals](../claims/proximal-goals-feedback-peer-models-build-learning-efficacy.md) [+W]: a review reports, second-hand, a fractions study in which close-at-hand goals led to higher self-efficacy, motivation and skilful performance than temporally distant goals. No sample, design or effect size is on the claim page; not yet checked against its source. Treat it as a pointer to a primary study the wiki has not read.
- [Forming implementation intentions improves goal attainment](../claims/implementation-intentions-improve-goal-attainment.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 94 tests (8,461 participants, mostly university students) found adding an if-then plan to a goal raised attainment over the goal alone (d = .65; d = .72 in the academic domain); two randomized field experiments in online courses (n = 17,963) found a mental-contrasting-plus-if-then writing activity made learners in individualist cultures 32% and 15% more likely to complete, and left learners in collectivist cultures unaffected. Not settled (abstract); one judge failure reviewed and dismissed. It qualifies the default: a plan adds to a goal, but not for every learner, and completion is not learning.
- [Self-monitoring improves on-task behaviour](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-on-task-behavior.md) [~M]: two meta-analyses of single-case studies with K–12 students with or at risk for disabilities found that recording one's own on-task behaviour at prompted intervals (an app, or a vibrating cue) raised on-task behaviour (all 14 designs in one; median Phi = 0.74 across 27 comparisons in the other). Not yet checked against its sources. It limits the model: monitoring changes the monitored behaviour, and the claim page says that is not shown to change achievement.

Keep the task, learners, comparator (no goal, "do your best", outcome goal), outcome type and horizon when transporting these findings. Work-task, health-behaviour and course-completion effects are not learning effects, a review's summary is not a test, and a non-significant difference is not evidence of no effect.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The goal a learner sets and the designer's objective are different things, and this principle is unusual in that the learner's goal is the intervention. Ask what the learner wants to accomplish and why before proposing a goal; agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. An assigned goal the learner does not accept is not expected to work, and a goal written to satisfy a teacher is not evidence of commitment.

For example, in an adult online language course the designer's objective may be holding a five-minute conversation at A2 by the end of the term, while the learner wants to order food on a trip in six weeks. A shared process goal ("practise the ordering dialogue three times this week, recording each attempt") can serve both; a streak count serves neither as evidence. Record the learner's own goal with the record, and judge the course on the conversation outcome, at its horizon.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners given specific, accepted goals with progress feedback show no gain over a "do your best" comparison on an aligned learning outcome under a defensible comparison. The process-before-outcome sequence should be revised if novices given outcome goals learn the process as well, or if the switch point cannot be observed reliably. If goal setting changes completion or on-task behaviour but never the learning outcome, the page should say goals act on engagement, not learning. If if-then plans help only some learners (as the field experiments suggest), the default should become conditional on the obstacle each learner names. Do not protect the model by calling every failure low commitment.

Effects on effort, persistence, completion, on-task behaviour, self-efficacy, immediate task performance and delayed learning are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal goal difficulty for a learner, a criterion for switching from process to outcome goals, a monitoring frequency, or durable effects once goal support is removed.

## Description
Goal setting and monitoring is the instructional principle of helping learners define specific, challenging targets and systematically tracking their progress toward those targets. This process is central to self-regulated learning (SRL), as it directs attention, mobilizes effort, and provides a framework for metacognitive evaluation [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S].

## Implications
Goal setting and monitoring improve learning because they give effort a target and feedback a reference point. Specific, challenging goals usually focus attention and sustain performance better than vague intentions [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S], while regular monitoring helps learners notice whether their current strategy is working.
