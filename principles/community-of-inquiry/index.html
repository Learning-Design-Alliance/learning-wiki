Conditional relationship¶

The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework describes a group of learners, usually adult and postsecondary, often online-instructor-led, working over days-weeks or a term-plus on a shared problem, case or text through discussion. It holds that the inquiry moves from a triggering question through exploration and integration to a resolution when three things are present together: social presence (learners can communicate openly and trust the group enough to disagree), cognitive presence (the task and prompts demand sustained reasoning, not only exchange) and teaching presence (someone designs, organises and facilitates the discourse). The expected change is in the depth of the group's inquiry, and through it each learner's understanding of principle and complex-skill goals, at the end of a unit or course.

As stated, that is a framework's proposal, not a tested relationship. No claim in the wiki compares learning outcomes under designs that differ in any of the three presences. What the wiki holds is survey evidence from two small online graduate programmes, in which students' ratings of the presences are high, uneven across facilitation and resolution, and associated with self-reported metacognition, and a few neighbouring studies of online discussion. So this page is conditional in two senses: on the setting (discussion-led, with a shared object of inquiry and a facilitator), and on evidence still to come. It does not forecast that adding forums, social activities or instructor posts will raise achievement.

Collaborative learning holds the conditional model of when joint work of any structure changes what each learner can do, and peer discussion the narrower model of discussing a committed answer to a conceptual question; this page sits beside them, owning the facilitated, sustained, often online discourse community and what to observe when it stalls. Social presence treats one of the three presences on its own, and the theory page CoI framework: three presences and a metacognition lens records the framework as theory. No pattern page instantiates this principle; online course design and discussion-based learning apply it and have not been rewritten against this model.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The page's earlier guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and most of it is untested as written.

  1. Give the group one shared object of inquiry: a problem, case, text or question that has more than one defensible answer and needs the group's reasoning to resolve. Untested; from the earlier page.
  2. Organise before facilitating. State the topics, goals, instructions and due dates clearly. In one MBA programme these organisation items were the teaching-presence items students rated highest (uneven presence profile [~W], descriptive); that is a rating, not evidence that organisation improves learning.
  3. Set norms that make disagreement and revision safe, and say so explicitly at the start. Untested; from the earlier page. Social presence was the presence most strongly associated with self-reported metacognition in one course (see below), which is an association, not a cause.
  4. Facilitate towards resolution, not only participation. Plan prompts that push from exploring sources to integrating and testing ideas, and a synthesis step that asks the group to state and check a resolution. In the MBA survey, facilitation and resolution-phase items were the ones rated below 4.0; whether planned facilitation would raise them, or learning, is untested.
  5. Use a synchronous or instructor channel where correction matters. In one study of second-language discussion boards, the board produced no corrective feedback at all, against text chat and video (see below). An asynchronous forum alone may not surface errors.
  6. Judge the design on each learner's own understanding of the target, on an individual task at the horizon the objective sets, not on post counts or presence ratings. Interaction counts correlated with posttest scores in one online group study, which says nothing about whether more posting causes learning.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: whether discussion is synchronous or asynchronous, how much facilitator time is available, and what individual task will show each learner's understanding at the end.

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is the discourse and what each learner can do afterwards: who posts, when, and how often; whether posts build on, question or merely agree with others; whether a thread reaches a stated resolution; what the facilitator did; and each learner's individual performance on the target. "Low social presence", "low cognitive presence" and "weak teaching presence" are inferred states, usually inferred from a learner's own survey ratings, and the same surface pattern can arise from several of them. Record the prompt, the channel (synchronous or not), every facilitator move, and any private or off-forum help.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Many posts, mostly agreement or sharing; threads never reach a resolution The prompt has one obvious answer (weak cognitive demand); norms discourage disagreement; no one prompted integration or synthesis Rewrite the prompt to admit competing answers and, separately, add a facilitator synthesis request; see which, if either, produces posts that test or integrate ideas.
A learner reads but rarely posts Low trust or fear of public error; lacks the concepts or language to contribute; low valuing of the task, or time constraints Ask privately for the learner's view of the question; offer a lower-risk mode (small group, draft-first, audio); compare the private answer with the group's level.
Rich discussion, but individual tests show little gain Others carried the reasoning; the discussion addressed a different capability from the test; the gain will appear only later or not at all Give an individual probe on the discussed reasoning, then on a new case; check whether the test and the discussion target the same capability.
Students rate the facilitator low, discussion still productive Ratings track organisation or tone rather than facilitation that helped; peers supplied the facilitation (co-regulation); the course's structure did the work Record who made the moves that redirected the thread (instructor or peer); compare ratings with that record.
Errors in posts go uncorrected Asynchronous posting suppresses correction; peers lack the knowledge to spot errors; politeness norms Compare the same group's handling of a seeded error in a synchronous session and on the board.

These are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence between explanations; it does not identify a cause, and asking about it can change participation.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as stated: none assigns groups to designs that differ in social, cognitive or teaching presence and measures learning. The claims below are survey associations, descriptive ratings and neighbouring studies of discussion.

  • Social presence shows the strongest association with metacognition in an online case-based course, while teaching presence shows no significant relationship [~M]: a survey of 47 graduate students in one online case-based instructional design course. Social presence had the strongest association with self-reported self-regulation and co-regulation, then cognitive presence; teaching presence had no statistically significant relationship with either. Associational (r1), one course, self-report on both sides, no learning outcome, not yet checked against its source. It qualifies the framework's emphasis on teaching presence in that course; it does not show that designing for social presence would change metacognition.
  • Co-regulation shows stronger relationships with the three CoI presences than self-regulation in an online case-based course [+W]: the same survey; co-regulation correlated .653 with social presence against .397 for self-regulation, with a similar, slightly weaker pattern for cognitive and teaching presence. Checked by the judge against the full text. It is consistent with presence being a group-level matter, and predicts nothing about outcomes.
  • CoI survey ratings reveal uneven presence profiles: course organization and exploration rated highest while most facilitation items and three cognitive-presence items (curiosity, reflection, testing and applying knowledge) rated below 4.0 [~W]: descriptive CoI Survey ratings (n = 32 completions) in an accelerated online MBA programme. Teaching presence was felt mainly through organisation (for example clear instructions, M = 4.31) while facilitation items fell below 4.0; cognitive presence was rated highest for exploration (M = 4.31) and lower for curiosity, reflection and testing knowledge (M = 3.88–3.94). It suggests where inquiry may stall (after exploration), from perceptions, not observed discourse or learning. Not yet checked.
  • Structuring positive interdependence (role, reward, or both) did not affect posttest achievement for undergraduate business majors in asynchronous online small group work [~M]: an experiment with random assignment of triads to role, reward, role-plus-reward or no interdependence in asynchronous online group work; posttest achievement did not differ significantly by condition (about 71% overall), no effect size printed. A nonsignificant difference is not equivalence. It qualifies the assumption that structuring online group work, one kind of teaching presence, by itself raises achievement. The participants were 280 undergraduate business majors. Not yet checked.
  • Higher numbers of interactions are associated with higher posttest scores in asynchronous online small group work [+W]: the same study; learners with more interactions had higher posttest scores, no coefficient printed. Correlational: stronger learners may simply post more. Not yet checked.
  • Discussion board interactions generated no negative feedback at all, suggesting synchrony is required to foster corrective feedback [-W]: in one study of second-language learners' negotiated interaction, discussion boards produced no explicit correction, recasts or self-correction, against 61 occurrences in text chat and 58 in videoconferencing. It limits asynchronous forums as a channel for corrective discourse, in a language-learning setting; the authors' explanation (posts can be checked before posting) is their suggestion. Not yet checked.

Taken together: the presences are perceived, are uneven, and go with self-reported regulation, mostly social presence; structuring online groups did not raise achievement in one experiment; and asynchronous boards may not surface errors. Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them.

How far each may be carried: the survey claims are two programmes of graduate and MBA students rating their own courses, so they describe perceptions there, not effects anywhere. The interdependence and interaction claims come from one online business course. The discussion-board claim is about corrective feedback in second-language interaction, not about reasoning in content discussions. None is evidence against facilitated discussion, and none is evidence for it as a cause of learning.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective names the capability the inquiry should build (for example, analysing a case with the discipline's criteria), the individual task that will show it and the horizon. Elicit separately what each learner wants from the community: to be heard by peers, to test ideas with professionals, to finish with the least time online, to get the instructor's view. Agreement and divergence are both observations, and a learner's presence ratings are not evidence that they share the designer's objective.

For example, a working MBA student may value peers' judgement of how a strategy would play out in their own firm, while the designer's objective is applying a financial model correctly to the case. A lively thread of workplace stories can satisfy the first and miss the second; a facilitator's synthesis that asks each learner to apply the model to their own firm can serve both, and an individual case analysis checks the designer's objective rather than the thread's activity.

What would revise this model?¶

The model should gain support if comparisons that vary facilitation, prompts or social-presence activities, with time and content held constant, show better individual understanding or transfer than discussion without them. It should weaken if such comparisons show no learning difference while presence ratings rise, which would make the presences descriptions of a satisfying course rather than conditions for learning. If teaching presence keeps showing no relationship with learners' regulation while peer co-regulation does, shift the emphasis from instructor facilitation to designing peer roles. Do not protect the model by calling every stalled discussion a lack of one presence after the fact.

Perceived presence, the quality of the discourse, each learner's understanding at the end of the course and its later use are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish that the three presences cause learning, how much facilitation is enough, or whether the framework holds outside online postsecondary courses.

Claims¶

  • Students' resolution of both direct and soft disagreements promoted collective knowledge advancement while maintaining a positive socio-emotional climate [~W]
  • A small-scale pilot study explored the impact of a variety of roles on discussion-forum interaction across participative, social, cognitive, and teaching dimensions [~W]
  • Social presence influences how social interaction unfolds in online group learning, which in turn affects learning outcomes [+W]
  • Cognitive presence comprises four phases of critical thinking — triggering event, exploration, integration, and resolution — which are not always sequential [+W]
  • In the coded dataset, exploration was the most frequent phase (727 units, 48%), followed by integration (444, 29%), resolution (235, 15%), and non-cognitive presence (115, 8%) [~W]
  • A visible instructor increased social presence in an exploratory field study (d = 0.85) [~W]
  • Teaching presence was positively related to cognitive presence but had no direct relationship with learning engagement; cognitive presence fully mediated the teaching presence–engagement relationship [+W]

Illustrative¶

Online Course Design (Community of Inquiry) — Applies this principle at the scale of a full online course, structured around Moore's three types of interaction.

Discussion Group — A structured discussion environment where facilitation, prompts, and synthesis shape the quality of inquiry.

Online seminar forums with active facilitation — Learners respond to a shared problem or text, build on peers’ ideas, and receive guidance that keeps the discourse substantive rather than merely social.

Problem-centered cohort discussions — Groups investigate a shared case or challenge over time, with instructor prompts that sustain both participation and conceptual depth.
