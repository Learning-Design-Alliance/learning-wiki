# Elaborative Reasoning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 42 claims (21 for, 17 mixed, 4 against) · 35 studies (20 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 3 associational, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 13 of 35 report an effect size · 36 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **elaborating content the learner is meant to remember and understand**: asking the learner to say why a stated fact or idea is true, how it connects to what they already know, or what it implies, instead of reading or rereading it. For a learner meeting statements in a text or lesson (facts, causal statements, descriptions of how something works; mostly `verbal-association` and `concept` targets) who holds **relevant and accurate prior knowledge of the topic**, answering a "why is this true?" prompt for each statement, or generating one's own connection or example, is expected to improve recall and recognition of that content (`immediate-performance`, `delayed-retention`) more than reading it for understanding. The expected cost is time: elaborating takes markedly longer than reading, and feels harder.

The relationship is conditional on four things, and the evidence here bears on each:

- **Prior knowledge of this content.** The gain is larger in a domain the learner knows well than in one they know little about, and passages learners know more about are recalled better a week or a month later. The observation that matters is the learner's starting response on this material (what they can say about it now), not a label.
- **The kind of target.** The positive experiments are about facts and prose (science facts, a biology text, passages). In one experiment where undergraduates who could not yet do the task learned to calculate a posterior probability from a written lesson, answering why-questions produced *lower* accuracy than simply reading the lesson twice, although it took far longer. For a `procedure` or `principle` the learner does not yet understand, an explanation or worked example comes first.
- **What the learner can generate.** For facts, even inadequate or missing answers to why-questions helped relative to reading. For the probability lesson, most answers were missing, wrong or inadequate, and better answers went with better posttest scores. Whether answer quality matters therefore seems to depend on the target and the learner's knowledge; it is not settled here.
- **Who generates the elaboration.** In one small classroom experiment in introductory accounting, students who generated their own elaborations scored higher than students given the instructor's elaborations, but not higher than students who did both.

**Which claims test the relationship.** Two randomised experiments with grade 6 and 7 students (one study, two experiments) test elaborative interrogation of science facts against reading for understanding, with recognition gains reported up to six months; a classroom study in an undergraduate biology course, reported in a review, tests interspersed why-prompts; one experiment tests it against reading twice for a probability lesson and finds the reverse. So the relationship is tested for facts and prose with learners who know something about them, and qualified for novices learning a calculation principle. **No claim in this wiki tests elaboration across a whole course or term, or compares the prompt types (why, how does this relate, give an example) against each other.**

## Default design

The relationship is tested for facts and prose with learners who know something about them; the session design, doses and the arc across sessions below are proposals built from the earlier page and the folded pages, each step labelled with its evidence status.

1. **Check what learners already know about this material.** Before choosing elaboration, ask learners to write or say two or three things they know that relate to the topic (one minute per topic), or count the related sentences they produce to a short prompt, the measure one report used for familiarity. Where most learners produce little or nothing, teach or activate the background first (see [Activation](activation.md)) or pick material closer to what they know. Basis: [Elaborative interrogation benefits increase as learners' prior knowledge increases](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-prior-knowledge-moderator.md) [~M]; [Passage familiarity (prior related knowledge) correlates .52 with recall, and only familiarity, not imageability, affected one-week delayed recall](../claims/passage-familiarity-correlates-with-recall.md) [+M]; [Subjects with a more developed schema for a body of knowledge show higher recall for materials related to that knowledge](../claims/developed-schema-higher-recall.md) [+W] (second-hand in an essay, mostly narratives). Where the choice is between familiar material and rewriting unfamiliar material to be more cohesive, prefer familiar material: in the same report, passage cohesion (repetition of concept labels across sentences) did not affect delayed recall while familiarity did ([Passage cohesion showed no effect on delayed recall; means showed greater recall from less cohesive passages](../claims/cohesion-no-effect-delayed-recall.md) [~M]). The one-minute probe is an untested proposal.
2. **Attach a why-prompt to specific statements, not to whole passages.** Mark the statements worth remembering and put "Why is this true?" or "Why does this make sense, given what you know?" beside each, roughly one prompt per paragraph or per 150 words of text. Basis: the classroom biology study interspersed 21 prompts, about one per 150 words, and the prompted group outperformed controls on a true/false test ([Elaborative interrogation improved true/false test performance in an introductory biology course context](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-biology-course-context.md) [+M]); attaching prompts to discrete statements is otherwise an untested proposal from the broader claim's discussion.
3. **Have each learner answer, briefly, from what they know.** One or two sentences per prompt, written or spoken, drawing on the lesson, earlier reading and everyday experience. Basis: [Elaborative interrogation instructions produce better recall and recognition of science facts than reading-for-understanding instructions](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-science-fact-learning.md) [+M] (grade 6 and 7, science facts); [Elaborative Interrogation Improves Learning](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M].
4. **Model one good answer first and look at answer quality.** Before learners start, show one answer that names a cause or a link to something known, beside one that only restates the fact. While they work, read a sample of answers; where many are missing, circular or wrong, give the explanatory link and add a worked answer. Basis: the earlier folded page's recommendation, from a study where most answers were poor and answer quality went with posttest scores ([Answers to elaborative interrogation questions were mostly poor in quality, and answer quality correlated positively with posttest scores](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-answer-quality-poor-correlates-with-posttest.md) [~M]); modelling and monitoring as a remedy is an untested proposal. For fact learning, do not hold learners back for imperfect answers: [The quality of elaborative-interrogation answers had little effect on retention; even failed or inadequate why-answers facilitated learning relative to reading](../claims/response-quality-little-effect-on-elaborative-interrogation-retention.md) [~M].
5. **Let learners generate first, then show the expert's elaboration.** Ask for the learner's own connection or example before showing the instructor's, so that both are used. Basis: [In one introductory accounting experiment, self-generated elaboration scored above instructor-assisted elaboration on procedural knowledge at the p = .05 boundary, and not significantly above a combined strategy](../claims/self-generated-elaboration-improves-procedural-knowledge-accounting.md) [+M] (one class, three groups of about 26; the combined strategy did as well as self-generated). The same class's other outcomes point the same way, weakly: on total posttest score self-generated beat instructor-assisted ([Cognitive elaboration strategy significantly affects overall posttest performance, with self-generated elaboration highest](../claims/elaboration-strategy-affects-posttest-performance.md) [+M]); on case analysis the difference fell short of the 5% level and the omnibus test was not significant ([In one accounting classroom experiment, self-generated elaboration scored marginally above instructor-assisted elaboration on intellectual skills (p = .06, omnibus test not significant) and no higher than a combined strategy](../claims/self-generated-elaboration-improves-intellectual-skills.md) [~M]); and attitudes to the method did not differ ([Elaboration strategy does not significantly affect students' attitudes toward the learning method](../claims/elaboration-strategy-no-attitude-effect.md) [~M]). The order "generate, then compare" is an untested proposal.
6. **Aim prompts at statements learners are likely to believe wrongly.** Where a fact contradicts a common belief, a why-prompt is the place to put it. Basis: [Elaborative interrogation produced greater pretest-to-posttest gains on items about common misbeliefs than reading control in Experiment 2](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-misbelief-items-pretest-posttest.md) [+M]; [Elaborative-interrogation students are less confident in their incorrect recognition selections than reading controls, who express great confidence in erroneous answers](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-reduces-confidence-in-errors.md) [+M].
7. **For a calculation, rule or procedure learners cannot yet do, explain or show a worked example first.** Put why-prompts on the steps of a worked example once the learner has seen one, rather than on a lesson the learner is meeting cold; if time is short, a second reading is a safer use of it than unsupported why-questions. Basis: [Elaborative interrogation produced lower posttest accuracy than reading the lesson twice in a written lesson on posterior probability](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-lower-posttest-than-read-twice-posterior-probability.md) [-M]; for prompts on worked examples, [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].
8. **In whole-class discussion, unfold a correct-sounding answer before accepting it.** When a student offers a formula or shortcut ("3 × 8"), ask them (or another student) to show the structure it stands for ("3 floors of 8 cubes"), link that structure across objects, words and symbols, then reconnect it to the original answer. Do not jump to the procedure or funnel the class to the answer. Basis: [Explicitly unfolding higher-order contributions into lower-order concept elements produces deeper conceptual learning opportunities than jumping directly to procedure](../claims/unfolding-knowledge-elements-produces-deeper-conceptual-learning-opportunities.md) [+M] (eight teachers, grades 5 to 7, volume; the outcome is the depth of the learning opportunity in the talk, not measured learning).
9. **Budget the time and say it will feel harder.** Plan for elaboration to take roughly two to three times as long as reading the same material, and tell learners that it feels harder because it is doing more work. Basis: [Elaborative interrogation increased perceived difficulty and time spent with the lesson](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-increases-difficulty-and-time-on-lesson.md) [~M] (much more time than reading twice, and rated harder); the "two to three times" figure is a proposal read from that claim and from the prompt-based variant in [Elaborative Interrogation Improves Learning](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [~M], where prompted learners spent about three times as long with no retention or transfer gain.

**Dose and progression across sessions (all untested proposals).** Within one session: about 5 minutes to probe what learners know and model one answer, then 15 to 25 minutes of reading with why-prompts (around 10 to 20 prompts), then 5 minutes comparing a few answers with a reference answer. Across a unit of several sessions:

- *Early sessions:* the teacher or text supplies every prompt, one modelled answer is shown before each set, and the teacher reads a sample of answers and supplies the explanatory link where answers fail.
- *Middle sessions:* prompts are still supplied, but learners check their own answers against a reference answer or a partner's, and the teacher only samples.
- *Late sessions:* learners mark the statements they find surprising and write their own why-questions for them, then answer each other's; the teacher checks that the questions target causes or links rather than definitions.

Move a learner from supplied to self-generated prompts once most of their answers in a set name a reason or a link to known material, for two sets in a row. Move back to explanation or a worked example when most answers in a set are missing or wrong. If retention matters, retest the key statements at a delay (a week or more) rather than inferring it from answers during study; the six-month result in [Elaborative-interrogation learning gains are durable, with performance maintained up to six months after study](../claims/durable-elaborative-interrogation-gains-six-months.md) [+M] is from one study's recognition tests.

When a condition is not met: if learners know little about the topic, teach it first and use elaboration later in the unit; if the content is arbitrary (labels, foreign vocabulary, symbols) and there is nothing meaningful to connect it to, use a different technique (mnemonics or retrieval practice) rather than why-prompts.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What learners already know about this material.** Elaboration builds on knowledge the learner has; with little of it, answers are poor and the gain shrinks. If unstated, ask: can a typical learner say anything accurate about why these statements might be true before the lesson?
- **What kind of target it is.** Facts and prose content suit why-prompts; a calculation, rule or procedure the learner cannot yet do needs an explanation or worked example first; arbitrary associations have nothing to explain. If unstated, ask: what must learners be able to do afterwards: recall and recognise statements, explain them, or carry out a procedure?
- **How much time there is, and who will look at answers.** Elaboration costs time, and its quality depends on someone or something checking answers when learners are novices. If unstated, ask: how many minutes are there for this material, and who will see learners' answers, and when?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is an answer to a prompt under stated conditions: the statement, the prompt, the time taken, what the learner wrote or said, whether it names a cause or link to known material, and later recall or recognition of that statement. "Has integrated it with prior knowledge" and "lacks the background" are inferred states.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Answers restate the fact ("because it is") | No relevant knowledge to draw on; does not understand what a why-answer is; low effort or time pressure | Ask what they know about the topic in general; show one modelled answer and give one more prompt; compare answers given with and without a time limit. |
| Answers are confident and wrong | A misconception being recruited; an analogy carried too far | Ask for the reason behind the reason; give a statement where the misconception and the correct idea predict different outcomes. |
| Good answers during study, poor recall a week later | Answers came from the text, not from the learner's own knowledge; little review; interference | Retest with a new prompt at the delay; check whether answers repeated the text's own words. |
| Fast answers, no gain over reading | Prompts on statements learners already knew; prompts answered superficially | Probe which statements were already known at the start; vary which statements carry prompts. |
| Learner complains it is slow and hard | Elaboration is effortful and feels harder than reading; or the material is beyond them | Compare their answer quality with their time: poor answers and long time suggest missing knowledge; good answers and long time suggest normal cost. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself elaboration and changes what it measures; record each.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Elaborative interrogation instructions produce better recall and recognition of science facts than reading-for-understanding instructions](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-science-fact-learning.md) [+M]: two randomised experiments in one report (60 and 80 grade 6 and 7 students) studying 32 science facts; the why-prompt group recalled and recognised more than readers told to read for understanding. One study; the printed effect statistics on the claim page look garbled from the scan, so do not quote them.
- [Elaborative-interrogation learning gains are durable, with performance maintained up to six months after study](../claims/durable-elaborative-interrogation-gains-six-months.md) [+M]: the same study's 75-day and 180-day recognition tests; the authors report the gains maintained up to six months.
- [Elaborative Interrogation Improves Learning](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M]: three studies: a narrative review rating the technique moderate utility and noting it was not adequately evaluated in educational contexts; an experiment with 114 grade 6 and 7 students in which generating why-elaborations beat underlining for main ideas; and an experiment with 97 adults in which a variant (generating removed segments of a text in answer to prompts) gave no retention or transfer advantage over reading the full text and took about three times as long. Read from abstracts except the last.
- [Elaborative interrogation improved true/false test performance in an introductory biology course context](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-biology-course-context.md) [+M]: a classroom-embedded experiment reported in the review: 21 prompts in a course text on digestion, 76% against 69% on 105 true/false items, after controlling for prior knowledge and verbal ability. Second-hand; no effect size printed.
- [Elaborative interrogation benefits increase as learners' prior knowledge increases](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-prior-knowledge-moderator.md) [~M]: reported in the review: a 24% increase in the students' high-knowledge domain against 12% in their low-knowledge domain. Elaboration still helped in the low-knowledge domain in that study.
- [Elaborative interrogation produced lower posttest accuracy than reading the lesson twice in a written lesson on posterior probability](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-lower-posttest-than-read-twice-posterior-probability.md) [-M]: **the limiting claim.** Undergraduates (198 analysed, those who already passed the pretest removed) in a randomised factorial experiment: why-questions led to lower posttest accuracy than reading twice. The same study found the why-question condition spent much longer and rated the lesson harder ([Elaborative interrogation increased perceived difficulty and time spent with the lesson](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-increases-difficulty-and-time-on-lesson.md) [~M]), and that most answers were poor, with answer quality correlating with posttest scores ([Answers to elaborative interrogation questions were mostly poor in quality, and answer quality correlated positively with posttest scores](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-answer-quality-poor-correlates-with-posttest.md) [~M]; correlational).
- [The quality of elaborative-interrogation answers had little effect on retention; even failed or inadequate why-answers facilitated learning relative to reading](../claims/response-quality-little-effect-on-elaborative-interrogation-retention.md) [~M]: in the grade 6 and 7 science-fact study, items answered adequately, inadequately or not at all were retained about equally. It points the other way from the probability study, which suggests answer quality matters more when the target is a principle learners do not yet know; that reading is this page's, not a tested moderator.
- [In one introductory accounting experiment, self-generated elaboration scored above instructor-assisted elaboration on procedural knowledge at the p = .05 boundary, and not significantly above a combined strategy](../claims/self-generated-elaboration-improves-procedural-knowledge-accounting.md) [+M]: one introductory accounting class, three groups of about 26, a short materials-based treatment; self-generated beat instructor-assisted at the 5% level but not the combined strategy. The authors note the small single-class sample. In the same class, self-generated also beat instructor-assisted on total posttest ([Cognitive elaboration strategy significantly affects overall posttest performance, with self-generated elaboration highest](../claims/elaboration-strategy-affects-posttest-performance.md) [+M]; the omnibus strategy effect was significant at the 10% level, and at 5% with GPA in the model), but only at p = 0.06 on case analysis with a non-significant omnibus test ([In one accounting classroom experiment, self-generated elaboration scored marginally above instructor-assisted elaboration on intellectual skills (p = .06, omnibus test not significant) and no higher than a combined strategy](../claims/self-generated-elaboration-improves-intellectual-skills.md) [~M]); attitudes did not differ ([Elaboration strategy does not significantly affect students' attitudes toward the learning method](../claims/elaboration-strategy-no-attitude-effect.md) [~M]); and pretest, GPA and strategy jointly affected case analysis but not procedural knowledge or attitudes ([GPA, pretest score, and self-generated elaboration jointly predict intellectual skills but not procedural knowledge or attitudes](../claims/gpa-pretest-elaboration-interaction-intellectual-skills.md) [~M]), so which learners gain most from generating is not settled by this class. No effect sizes are printed.
- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: four studies including two meta-analyses (69 effect sizes, g = .55; and one in mathematics finding delayed and classroom evidence much more limited). ## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually recall, recognition or explanation of specific content at a stated delay, with the aids stated. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the material: to pass Friday's test, to understand something that puzzled them, to be able to use it at work. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants only to pass a recognition test may see why-prompts as slow and skip them; one who wants to understand may value them and still answer poorly if the background is missing.

For example, a nursing assistant reading about infection control may want to know why a step matters so they can explain it to a patient's family, while the course objective is passing a multiple-choice module test. Why-prompts serve both if the answers draw on the learner's own ward experience; record whether they can explain the step to someone, not only the test score.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if further comparisons of why-prompts against reading or rereading, with comparable learners and aligned delayed tests, show no advantage when study time is matched, or if the six-month result does not replicate. If answer quality turns out to matter for facts as well as for principles, the design should require checked answers, not just answering. If the probability-lesson result recurs for other `principle` and `procedure` targets with novices, the page should restrict why-prompts to content learners can already partly explain. If self-generated and supplied elaborations come out equal in larger studies, step 5 should go. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough prior knowledge" after the fact; record the prior-knowledge probe before the lesson.

Immediate recall, delayed recall, explanation and transfer are separate claims; the present evidence establishes no number of prompts, no answer-quality threshold and no rule for when to move from supplied to self-generated prompts.

## Description
Elaborative reasoning is the practice of prompting learners to expand on new material — explaining why it is true, connecting it to what they already know, generating their own examples, and drawing inferences beyond what was explicitly taught. Rather than receiving or rehearsing content verbatim, learners construct additional meaning around it. The recommendation is to design tasks and prompts that require this generative work, because elaborated encodings are richer, better organized, and more retrievable than surface-level ones.

## Implications

Elaboration works because it changes how information is encoded and organized. Asking "why is this true?" (elaborative interrogation) and constructing links between new ideas and prior knowledge produces deeper processing than reading or rereading [Levels-of-processing theory holds that deeper semantic processing yields more durable memory.](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) [+S]. Dunlosky et al.'s (2013) review rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as moderately useful techniques, with benefits concentrated in domains where learners have enough prior knowledge to generate meaningful connections [+M]. Elaboration also supports transfer: generating analogies and cross-domain connections helps learners apply concepts to novel situations [Analogical reasoning improves transfer to novel problems.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The technique is not free, however — elaboration consumes working-memory resources, so prompts must be calibrated to load [Cognitive overload degrades learning when task demands exceed working-memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M], and low-knowledge learners may generate inaccurate elaborations that are then learned as fact [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient prior knowledge to generate meaningful connections ([Activation](activation.md) or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) — learners cannot elaborate on what they have no schema for)
- Prompts that demand generation ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) or [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)) — "why," "how does this relate to," "give your own example"
- Feedback or verification opportunities — self-generated elaborations can be wrong and need checking
- Time and cognitive budget — elaboration is slower than passive review and must be planned for
- Students must provide answers indicative of the connections among ideas the questions are intended to stimulate
- Elaboration must be precise and meaningful to the individual, presented from a perspective or context meaningful to the reader
- Students must take a central role and generate their own way of integrating new course material with pre-existing knowledge structures.
- A topic with an identifiable network of prerequisite concept elements beneath the target procedure (e.g., structured counting in rows/layers beneath the formula for volume), so "unfolding" has a concrete destination
- Willingness to slow down and treat a correct-sounding student answer as a prompt for justification rather than a stopping point
- A way to assess learners' prior related knowledge, such as the linkage measure of the average number of related sentences generated to passage stimuli

#### Constraints
- Backfires when learners lack relevant prior knowledge: they generate plausible but incorrect elaborations that interfere with accurate encoding [-M]
- Less effective for arbitrary, non-meaningful content (e.g., vocabulary in a foreign language, arbitrary symbols) where no semantic connection exists [~M]
- High element interactivity plus elaboration demands can overload working memory and degrade learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning when task demands exceed working-memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Benefits shrink under time pressure, where learners default to keyword-style elaborations rather than substantive ones [~M]
- The authors state it is premature to indicate implications because some findings are inconsistent with previous work; the recommendation holds only if supported by future work with similar findings
- The essay ties encoding depth to frequency of activation: the recurrence of activation deepens the level at which the schema is encoded and allows faster, more efficient access
- The evidence comes from a single ten-minute materials-based treatment in one introductory accounting class; the authors state generalizations are limited because the sample size is too small and drawn from one class.
- Skipping straight to symbolic manipulation ("3×4 is 12") when a student is mid-way through articulating a structural idea (e.g., "4 rows") cuts off that construction and tends to produce students who can state a formula but not justify it
- Narrow, funneling questions can produce a correct final answer while bypassing the conceptual grounding entirely — the answer alone does not indicate the learning opportunity was deep
- Familiarity was more important than imageability and than passage cohesion as defined in these studies

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate to rich prior knowledge in the domain, who can generate accurate connections
- Learners preparing for transfer or application rather than verbatim recall
- Less suitable for complete novices, who benefit more from [worked examples](worked-examples.md)-style structure before generating their own connections
- undergraduate students reading lessons independently
- students reading expository and narrative texts
- first accounting course (introductory) students
- Students in whole-class mathematics discussions transitioning from concrete/structural understanding (e.g., counting in rows and layers) to a general symbolic procedure (e.g., a volume formula)
- middle school students
- poor comprehenders

- Conceptual understanding and integration of new material with prior knowledge
- Long-term retention of meaningful content
- Transfer to novel problems and contexts
- Inference-making and explanatory reasoning, not rote recall
- probabilistic reasoning, calculating posterior probability
- encoding and retrieval of new information into existing knowledge structures
- procedural knowledge of accounting concepts
- intellectual skills such as case analysis and problem solving
- long-term recall of information learned from prose
- reading comprehension

#### Supporting
- [Information Processing Theory](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) — deeper semantic processing at encoding produces more durable, better-connected memory traces (levels of processing)
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — germane load devoted to schema construction is productive; elaboration is a primary mechanism for it, provided extraneous load is controlled
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — self-explanation and elaborative interrogation are core strategies learners use to monitor and deepen comprehension
- Hiebert & Carpenter's network theory of conceptual understanding — degree of understanding is a function of the number and strength of connections among knowledge elements
- Aebli's four epistemic processes (constructing, linking, compacting, unfolding) — this pattern specifically operationalizes the underused "unfolding" process (reversing compaction back into constituent elements)

- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — broadly supportive, but cautions that learner-generated constructions may be idiosyncratic or wrong; elaboration requires verification against expert understanding

# Grounding and Unfolding Facilitation Moves

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
During whole-class discussion, students often volunteer higher-order contributions — a formula, a shortcut, a partially-justified calculation — before the class has collectively built the underlying concept those contributions depend on. A teacher can respond in at least three ways: jump to the procedure alongside the student (skipping the underlying concept), funnel the class toward the answer through narrowing questions (arriving at a correct-sounding formula without verifying understanding), or explicitly unfold the contribution — asking the student (or class) to articulate the concrete structure (e.g., rows, layers) that justifies it, then leverage back upward to reconnect the unfolded structure to the original higher-order idea. Only the third move reliably produces learning opportunities that let students later justify, not just state, the procedure.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A topic with an identifiable network of prerequisite concept elements beneath the target procedure (e.g., structured counting in rows/layers beneath the formula for volume), so "unfolding" has a concrete destination
- Willingness to slow down and treat a correct-sounding student answer as a prompt for justification rather than a stopping point
#### Constraints
- Skipping straight to symbolic manipulation ("3×4 is 12") when a student is mid-way through articulating a structural idea (e.g., "4 rows") cuts off that construction and tends to produce students who can state a formula but not justify it
- Narrow, funneling questions can produce a correct final answer while bypassing the conceptual grounding entirely — the answer alone does not indicate the learning opportunity was deep
#### Grain Size
Lesson (a single whole-class discussion segment, typically several minutes)

### Target Goals
- Building justified (not just procedural) understanding of a formula or shortcut, by explicitly connecting it to the concrete structure it generalizes
- [Explicitly unfolding higher-order contributions into lower-order concept elements produces deeper conceptual learning opportunities than jumping directly to procedure](../claims/unfolding-knowledge-elements-produces-deeper-conceptual-learning-opportunities.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Students in whole-class mathematics discussions transitioning from concrete/structural understanding (e.g., counting in rows and layers) to a general symbolic procedure (e.g., a volume formula)

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Hiebert & Carpenter's network theory of conceptual understanding — degree of understanding is a function of the number and strength of connections among knowledge elements
- Aebli's four epistemic processes (constructing, linking, compacting, unfolding) — this pattern specifically operationalizes the underused "unfolding" process (reversing compaction back into constituent elements)
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- (none yet linked)

### Claims
#### Supporting
#### Contradicting
- (none yet linked)

## Design

### Sequence
1. A student volunteers a higher-order contribution (an informal or formal calculation) before the class has collectively established the concept elements it depends on.
2. The teacher does not accept the contribution as final and does not replace it with their own procedure; instead, the teacher asks the student (or another student) to unfold it — explicating the concrete structure it corresponds to (e.g., "what do you mean by 'slices'? show us.").
3. The class collectively links this structure across representations (material/iconic, verbal, symbolic) — for example, connecting a physical model of stacked layers, the phrase "3 layers of 8," and the symbolic expression "3 × 8."
4. The teacher leverages back upward: once the structure is explicit, the class reconnects it to the original higher-order contribution, and (optionally) repeats the cycle for a further step (e.g., adding a third dimension).
