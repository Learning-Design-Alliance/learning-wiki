# Elaborative Reasoning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Elaborative reasoning is the practice of prompting learners to expand on new material — explaining why it is true, connecting it to what they already know, generating their own examples, and drawing inferences beyond what was explicitly taught. Rather than receiving or rehearsing content verbatim, learners construct additional meaning around it. The recommendation is to design tasks and prompts that require this generative work, because elaborated encodings are richer, better organized, and more retrievable than surface-level ones.

## Implications

Elaboration works because it changes how information is encoded and organized. Asking "why is this true?" (elaborative interrogation) and constructing links between new ideas and prior knowledge produces deeper processing than reading or rereading [Levels-of-processing theory holds that deeper semantic processing yields more durable memory.](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) [+S]. Dunlosky et al.'s (2013) review rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as moderately useful techniques, with benefits concentrated in domains where learners have enough prior knowledge to generate meaningful connections [+M]. Elaboration also supports transfer: generating analogies and cross-domain connections helps learners apply concepts to novel situations [Analogical reasoning improves transfer to novel problems.](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+M]. The technique is not free, however — elaboration consumes working-memory resources, so prompts must be calibrated to load [Cognitive overload degrades learning when task demands exceed working-memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M], and low-knowledge learners may generate inaccurate elaborations that are then learned as fact [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient prior knowledge to generate meaningful connections ([Activation](activation.md) or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) — learners cannot elaborate on what they have no schema for)
- Prompts that demand generation ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) or [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)) — "why," "how does this relate to," "give your own example"
- Feedback or verification opportunities — self-generated elaborations can be wrong and need checking
- Time and cognitive budget — elaboration is slower than passive review and must be planned for

#### Constraints
- Backfires when learners lack relevant prior knowledge: they generate plausible but incorrect elaborations that interfere with accurate encoding [-M]
- Less effective for arbitrary, non-meaningful content (e.g., vocabulary in a foreign language, arbitrary symbols) where no semantic connection exists [~M]
- High element interactivity plus elaboration demands can overload working memory and degrade learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning when task demands exceed working-memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Benefits shrink under time pressure, where learners default to keyword-style elaborations rather than substantive ones [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate to rich prior knowledge in the domain, who can generate accurate connections
- Learners preparing for transfer or application rather than verbatim recall
- Less suitable for complete novices, who benefit more from [worked examples](worked-examples.md)-style structure before generating their own connections

### Target Learning Objectives
- Conceptual understanding and integration of new material with prior knowledge
- Long-term retention of meaningful content
- Transfer to novel problems and contexts
- Inference-making and explanatory reasoning, not rote recall

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Information Processing Theory](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) — deeper semantic processing at encoding produces more durable, better-connected memory traces (levels of processing)
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — germane load devoted to schema construction is productive; elaboration is a primary mechanism for it, provided extraneous load is controlled
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — self-explanation and elaborative interrogation are core strategies learners use to monitor and deepen comprehension

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — broadly supportive, but cautions that learner-generated constructions may be idiosyncratic or wrong; elaboration requires verification against expert understanding

### Claims
- [Analogical reasoning improves transfer to novel problems.](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+M] — generating analogical connections supports application beyond the original context
- [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M] — comparing cases prompts elaboration of deep structure rather than surface features
- [Concept mapping improves learning.](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [+M] — building explicit relational links is a structured form of elaborative reasoning

## Examples

### Validated
- [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M] — Schwartz & Bransford's (1998) "time for telling" studies showed that learners who first compared and elaborated on contrasting cases of bird-adaptation data learned far more from a subsequent lecture than lecture-only controls.

### Illustrative

**Elaborative interrogation prompts** — After each paragraph of a text, learners answer "Why does this make sense?" or "Why is this true?" rather than summarizing. A widely studied classroom variant of [articulation](../elements/articulation.md); effective mainly when learners have background knowledge to draw on.

**[Concept mapping](../elements/advance-organizers.md)** — Learners construct node-and-link diagrams connecting new concepts to each other and to prior knowledge, labeling each link with a relational phrase. Tools such as [CmapTools](https://cmap.ihmc.us) support this; the mapping task forces explicit elaboration of relationships rather than listing facts.

**"Why?" chains in science discussion** — A teacher repeatedly asks "Why do you think that happens?" during [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), pushing students past restating a phenomenon to proposing mechanisms. Common in [CGI-style math and science discourse routines](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md).

**Self-generated examples** — After studying a definition or principle, learners must produce their own example that fits it and explain why it fits. Stronger retention than studying provided examples, because generation forces elaborative encoding.

**[Elaboration through analogy writing](../elements/analogies.md)** — Learners write their own analogy for a new concept (e.g., "an electrical circuit is like…") and then evaluate where the analogy breaks down, combining generative elaboration with critical comparison.
