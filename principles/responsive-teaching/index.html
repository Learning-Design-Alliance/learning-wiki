Responsive Teaching¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 26 claims (14 for, 10 mixed, 2 against) · 41 studies (10 review, 9 quant-synthesis, 9 qualitative, 8 causal, 2 associational, 2 theoretical, 1 design), q1–q4 · 5 of 41 report an effect size · 17 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the teacher's in-the-moment departure from the plan: what a teacher does with the ideas learners actually voice during a lesson, and the knowledge that lets the teacher read those ideas and choose among them. For learners working toward a concept or principle (or toward choosing and explaining a strategy) whose partial or mistaken ideas are likely to diverge from the planned path, a teacher who (1) makes each learner's thinking visible at planned points in the episode, (2) reads it against a prepared model of the ideas learners typically hold (a learning trajectory, or the reasons a curriculum sequences its activities as it does), and (3) chooses the next move from what was read (press for reasoning, contrast cases, confront the idea, give task- or process-level feedback, hold back, re-sequence) is expected to produce better conceptual-understanding and near-transfer than the same plan delivered unchanged. The expected change is at the horizon of the next aligned task or unit (immediate-performance, general-achievement); nothing here bears on delayed-retention.

No claim in the wiki tests responsive teaching against delivering the same plan unchanged. The nearest evidence tests parts of the relationship: support contingent on a learner's last response against fixed or no support (one-to-one tutoring of children), classroom formative assessment against instruction without it (K-12 meta-analyses with small average effects), the level that feedback addresses, contrasting correct with erroneous examples, confronting misconceptions, and one case study in which novice instructors who learned why a curriculum was designed as it was made six kinds of responsive move. That case study measured the instructors' moves, not their students' learning. The earlier page cited Carpenter et al. (1989), the Cognitively Guided Instruction experiment, for larger achievement gains; no claim page records that study, so it is not used as evidence here.

The relationship is conditional on four things: whether learners' thinking is actually visible (an idea not voiced cannot be responded to); whether the teacher has a model of likely ideas to read it against (without one, responses become generic praise or re-explanation, as the earlier page warned); whether the teacher has the time and authority to depart from the plan; and whether the class is diverging at all (where learners are already on track, departures add little and cost coverage).

How the converted siblings sit beside this page. The formative assessment principle and pattern own the general loop of eliciting a response, interpreting it against rival explanations, acting and re-observing; this page is that loop run by a teacher inside one episode, and adds the teacher's idea map and the triage of which idea to pursue. Assessment for Learning owns who uses the evidence, against what criteria and under what stakes. Scaffolding and Fading owns how support is raised, lowered and withdrawn for one learner. Check-ins owns self-reports about a learner's state. Adaptive Learning owns selection of the next task by a system from a learner's answers. Cognitive Activation owns the tasks whose outputs make reasoning visible. This page does not repeat their models.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it.

  1. Before the unit, build an idea map. For each planned task, write down the two or three ideas learners most often bring (including the misconception the materials were designed to confront) and one prepared move for each. If a research-based curriculum is in use, read why each activity is placed where it is: which idea it is meant to surface, which later section resolves it. Dose: about 15 minutes of preparation per lesson (an untested proposal). Basis for the curricular part: Developing curricular design knowledge enables small-scale responsive adaptations within a structured curriculum [+M] (14 undergraduate Learning Assistants in introductory physics; moves documented, student learning not measured).
  2. Plan two or three elicitation points per lesson where every learner's thinking becomes visible, not only that of volunteers: a prediction before a demonstration, a one-line explanation, a worked attempt on a mini-whiteboard, a poll with a "why" box. Place the first within the first 10 minutes. Basis: classroom formative assessment, which elicits and uses evidence of learning, raised achievement by a small average amount in K-12 meta-analyses (Assessment for learning improves achievement [+M]); the number and timing of points are untested proposals.
  3. Sort the responses against the map in under two minutes: on track; an expected idea (which one); unexpected. Count roughly how many hold each. Untested proposal.
  4. Triage before moving. Pursue an idea when it bears on today's goal and several learners hold it. Note and set aside an idea that is tangential, and tell the learner when it will come back. Hold back when a later section of the sequence is designed to confront that very idea. Basis for holding back: one of the six moves in the Learning Assistant case study (curricular knowledge [+M]); the triage rule itself is untested. Waiting before stepping in can leave room for learners to solve the problem themselves (Effective intervention timing in AI-based instruction does not always mean immediate response [~W], a review citing one study).
  5. Choose the move from a short menu matched to the idea.
  6. Press for reasoning with a question or hint rather than an explanation, and raise the support only if the learner still cannot go on: Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] (one-to-one tutoring; contingent prompting matched explaining on immediate learning and did better on transfer in one small study).
  7. Contrast a correct and an incorrect solution, using the class's own error (anonymised) as the incorrect one, and ask learners to find and fix it: Erroneous examples improve conceptual understanding by forcing comparison with correct models. [+M] (fourth and fifth graders, decimal magnitude, brief tutoring).
  8. Confront the idea: a discrepant demonstration whose outcome the idea predicts wrongly, or an explicit statement of the idea followed by its refutation: Instruction that confronts science misconceptions produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition [+M] (two syntheses, science and reading education, read from abstracts).
  9. Give feedback on the task and the process, not on the person: say what in the work meets the goal, what does not, and which strategy to change: Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S] (two meta-analyses; the levels were not coded directly).
  10. Re-sequence or reteach one part, when most of the class shares a gap the plan assumed was closed. Untested proposal.
  11. Re-observe before going on. After the move, give one new item on which the idea and the target predict different answers; go back to the plan when most responses are on track. Untested here; the re-observation step is modelled in the formative assessment pattern.
  12. Cap each departure. In a 50-minute lesson, give an unplanned departure no more than about 10 minutes before returning to the plan or carrying the idea into the next lesson, and record what was cut. The earlier page warned that pursuing every idea fragments instruction; the cap is an untested proposal.
  13. Fade the teacher's role across sessions. Weeks 1–3: the teacher runs the idea map and the menu, and names the ideas aloud. Weeks 4–8: learners compare and name ideas in pairs before the teacher responds; the teacher adds new ideas met in class to the map after each lesson (5 minutes). Later: learners check their own work against a worked contrast and ask for help only when stuck. Move a learner off teacher-led elicitation when two consecutive elicitation items are on track. Basis: Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+M] (example-to-problem fading, near transfer); the weekly arc and its thresholds are untested proposals.
  14. For a teacher or tutor who is still learning this (a new teacher, a Learning Assistant, a volunteer): build curricular knowledge over a term, not in one workshop. Weekly preparation of about an hour that asks why the coming activities are sequenced as they are, plus a short written reflection on one interaction where that knowledge changed a response. The case study documented the knowledge developing over about 30 weeks (curricular knowledge [+M]); the weekly dose is the study's programme, carried here as a proposal.

When a condition is not met: if learners' thinking cannot be seen during the lesson (a lecture to hundreds, self-paced online work), use items whose answers can be checked automatically plus one free-text explanation reviewed between sessions, and respond in the next session; if the teacher has no idea map and no time to build one, keep the plan and use only steps 2, 5 (task and process feedback) and 6.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Can every learner's thinking be seen during the lesson, and by whom? Most evidence for contingent response is one-to-one; with one teacher and thirty learners, or no teacher, elicitation must reach everyone at once and the response must target the commonest idea. If unstated, ask: during the lesson, how will the teacher see each learner's reasoning, and how soon?
  • Does the teacher have a model of likely ideas for this content? Without one, responses become generic. If unstated, ask: what ideas do learners usually bring to this task, and does the curriculum say which misconception each activity targets?
  • How much freedom to depart from the plan, and how much time? A scripted pacing guide, a fixed short session or a shared exam date limits the moves to small ones inside the sequence. If unstated, ask: what must be covered by when, and what may be moved?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response under stated conditions: the prompt, who answered (all, or volunteers), the words, drawing or worked steps, and what the learner did after the teacher's move. "Holds misconception X", "is on track" and "understood the contrast" are inferred states. A teacher's sort of responses is itself an inference and can be wrong in the same ways.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
A learner's answer matches an expected misconception The learner holds the idea; the learner guessed or copied a neighbour; the prompt was ambiguous and invited that answer Ask for the reason behind the answer, privately; give an item where the idea and the target predict different results.
Most of the class is right, a few are not The few share an idea the map lacks; they lack a prerequisite; they did not understand the prompt Ask one of them to say how they got it; check the prerequisite with a single item.
An idea appears that is not on the map A genuinely new idea; a familiar idea in unfamiliar words (often in a second language); off-task humour Restate it and ask the learner whether that is what they meant; add it to the map only after it recurs.
After the move, responses are right but explanations are not Learners picked up the answer, not the reasoning; the re-observation item was too close to the contrast Give a changed item that the copied answer would not solve; ask for a non-verbal explanation.
Learners stop offering ideas after a few lessons Errors felt exposed; the teacher's responses read as correction of the person; elicitation never changed anything Switch to anonymous responses for a week and compare the range of ideas offered.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Every probe is also teaching and changes the state it measures, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain rather than choosing the one that fits the map.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim compares responsive teaching with the same plan delivered unchanged, or varies how much the teacher departs from it. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders tutored one-to-one in long division under fully contingent support solved more problems than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition, a quasi-experiment); in a small study of eighth graders, interactive tutoring (questions and hints rather than explaining) matched on immediate learning and did better on transfer; a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching. Its table counts 20 distinct studies behind it and the narrower claims that bear on it, 3 with an effect size coded. The claim's own discussion says most evidence is one-to-one and that contingency is scarce in whole-class teaching. It supports the responding half of this page in tutoring, not at class scale.
  • Assessment for learning improves achievement [+M]: two K-12 meta-analyses of formative assessment with control conditions report small average effects (d = .29 over 33 studies; about .20 over 13 studies, after a published correction), and the founding narrative review reports substantial gains without a pooled estimate. Its table counts 10 distinct studies. It supports eliciting and using evidence during instruction; it does not isolate the teacher's in-the-moment departure from the other parts of formative assessment, and the feature estimates are meta-regression, not direct comparisons.
  • Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]: in an education meta-analysis of 435 studies, high-information feedback had a very large effect (d = 0.99) and reinforcement or punishment a small one (d = 0.24); a cross-setting meta-analysis found feedback effects fell as feedback drew attention toward the self, and more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance. Neither coded the four levels directly. It governs the content of a feedback move, not when to depart from a plan.
  • Erroneous examples improve conceptual understanding by forcing comparison with correct models. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders (N = 74) comparing correct and incorrect decimal examples in a brief tutoring session learned more correct procedures and key concepts than those comparing correct examples only; a meta-analysis of prompted self-explanation is read as supporting erroneous examples (no effect size printed). It supports using a learner's error as a contrast case; the caution about learners with no prior knowledge is a precaution on the claim page, not a finding.
  • Instruction that confronts science misconceptions (conceptual-conflict interventions, refutation text) produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition [+M]: a meta-analysis of science and reading-education interventions found that those that worked shared conceptual conflict, and a review of two decades of refutation-text studies found refutation text more likely to produce conceptual change than expository text; both read from abstracts, no pooled effect recorded. Its table counts 4 distinct studies. It supports the confrontation move.
  • Developing curricular design knowledge enables small-scale responsive adaptations within a structured curriculum [+M]: 14 undergraduate Learning Assistants in one university's introductory physics sequence, over 30 weeks of teaching, weekly preparation and a pedagogy course, documented six moves they attributed to knowing why the tutorial curriculum was designed as it was (explaining a stuck section by its strategy, referring to other sections, prioritising exercises, choosing when not to intervene, anticipating targeted misconceptions, deviating when a learner's idea was not the anticipated one). A qualitative case study with no comparison group and no measure of student learning; the study calls the moves small-scale and curriculum-embedded.

Limits. Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M] bounds the relationship from above: support that helps learners who lack the schema can be redundant for those who hold it, so a class already on track gains little from detours. Emerging evidence indicates generative AI tutors currently struggle to accurately infer student knowledge [-W] (a review citing two studies) limits handing the reading of learners' thinking to a generative system, and ITS effectiveness is not moderated by whether the ITS provides feedback or models student misconceptions [~M] (one meta-analysis; intelligent tutors had moderate effects whether or not they modelled misconceptions) qualifies the assumption that diagnosing a specific misconception is what makes adaptive response work.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (children tutored one-to-one, K-12 classrooms, undergraduate instructors, general feedback research), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a teacher's in-the-moment responsiveness and its students' learning together.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a concept or principle the learner can explain and use on a new case at the end of the unit. Elicit separately what learners want from voicing their ideas: to get the right answer quickly, to be heard, to avoid being wrong in public, or to understand something that puzzles them. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values not being wrong in public will offer the expected answer, not their own idea, and a teacher responding to that answer is responding to a performance; a learner who wants the answer quickly may read a pressing question as withholding.

For example, in an introductory physics tutorial the instructor's objective is that students can predict wave interference, while a student wants to finish the worksheet before the end of the section. Holding back on the student's early misconception (because the next section confronts it) serves the objective but slows the student down. Saying why the instructor is holding back ("the next part will test this prediction; keep it") lets both goals be seen, and the student's later explanation, not the finished worksheet, is the record of whether the objective was met.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of teachers trained to elicit and build on learners' ideas against teachers delivering the same materials as planned, with comparable classes and aligned outcomes, show no advantage in conceptual understanding or transfer, or if the gain at the end of a unit disappears at a delay. If teacher responsiveness works only where the teacher holds a domain-specific idea map, the model should say "responsive teaching with a learning-trajectory model", not responsive teaching in general. If contingent response cannot be shown at class scale, the whole-class version reduces to formative assessment with a fixed menu of moves. If time spent on departures lowers coverage enough to lower achievement on the shared exam, the departure cap should be a requirement, not a proposal. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not responsive enough" after the fact.

Visible thinking, the teacher's accurate reading of it, a well-chosen move, the learner's changed response, and later independent performance are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of elicitation points, no departure cap and no idea-map format.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Immersive learning tools should surface misconceptions and track learner difficulties so instructors can adapt teaching and assessment [~W]: the authors' recommendation from a thematic analysis of a mixed-reality engineering toolkit study (q1). It is a design argument for instrumenting tools so instructors can respond, not evidence that responding helps; it bears on the online and large-class rows only as a suggestion.
  • Developing and delivering an integrated CBL/CEL/COIL planetary health course yielded three key lessons on flexibility, structural reform, and equitable partnerships [+W]
  • The increased number and specificity of policy requirements challenged implementation flexibility, with some viewing the policy as one-size-fits-all [-W]
  • Rather than the initial linear theory of action, a cycle of learning emerged generated by flexibility, relationships, and responsiveness [+W]
  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S]
  • A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills [~M]
  • Part-task practice reduces cognitive load for absolute novices during initial skill acquisition. [~M]
  • STEM PLC participation increases teachers' deliberation about students' mathematics or science thinking and shifts instruction toward attention to student reasoning and diverse problem-solving modes [~W]
  • Discussion promotes more active thinking than lecture, though lecture is no worse for transmitting information [+M]
  • Teacher responsiveness is uniquely related to substantive quality of first-grade written composition after accounting for child predictors and other instructional quality variables [+W] — attached 2026-10-17 from Kim et al. (2013), which proposed "Promote teacher responsiveness to students' inquiries and responses as a lever for improving beginning writing quality"; tests this page's relationship.

Examples¶

  • Three Moment Architecture for secondary mathematics lessons with English Learners
  • A Learning Assistant, knowing that a tutorial section was designed to elicit and later confront a specific misconception, chose not to intervene when a student expressed that misconception early — because the curriculum's own later sequence would surface and resolve it.
  • A Learning Assistant invented an on-the-fly elicit-confront-resolve sequence (using overlapping transparent pages to test a student's incorrect prediction about wave interference) when a student's actual misconception didn't match what the printed curriculum anticipated.
  • Combining challenge-based learning, community-engaged learning, COIL, and cross-university collaboration in one course design
  • Early numeracy lessons where multiple addition or subtraction strategies are surfaced and compared.
  • Math talk classrooms that use learner solutions as the basis for whole-group discussion.
  • Problem sequences that move from familiar contexts toward more abstract structures.

Illustrative¶

Cognitively Guided Instruction — A professional development program that trains elementary teachers to elicit children's informal solution strategies for arithmetic word problems, classify them against research-based strategy trajectories, and design instruction that builds from each child's current strategy. Carpenter et al. (1989) showed large achievement gains for students of CGI teachers.

Formative Assessment — Classroom cycles of eliciting evidence, interpreting it against learning goals, and adjusting instruction — the institutionalized version of responsive teaching at the lesson scale.

Class Discussion with strategic follow-up — A teacher poses a problem, polls or samples multiple student answers, then sequences and connects responses so the class confronts the key conceptual contrast — responding to the ideas actually in the room rather than the anticipated ones.

Coaching in the moment — During Practice, an instructor observes a learner's attempt, diagnoses the specific flaw in their approach, and offers a targeted hint rather than a full correction — the one-on-one form of responsiveness.

Anchored Instruction — Problem-rich anchors (e.g., The Adventures of Jasper Woodbury) generate diverse student approaches that teachers can surface and build on, making responsive moves natural rather than improvised.
