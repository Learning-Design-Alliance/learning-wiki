# High-Stakes Testing and Accountability Effects

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Standardized testing is "high-stakes" when performance carries real consequences — for students (a graduation test required for a diploma, a licensure exam required for a career), or for schools (under the No Child Left Behind Act, or NCLB, failing to reach targeted proficiency levels can trigger reduced funding or restructuring). Accountability, standardized testing, academic content standards, and teacher quality are the four components of the broader Standards-Based Reform movement in U.S. education, dating to the 1980s.

**Content standards must be neither too broad nor too narrow.** A standard as broad as "students should be able to construct meaning through experiences with literature, cultural events and philosophical discussion" (with no grade level indicated) gives teachers nothing to prepare students for; a standard as narrow as "students can define, compare, and contrast synonyms and antonyms" produces a restricted curriculum. A 2005-06 American Federation of Teachers study found standards in several states weak, particularly in reading, with one-fifth of reading standards redundant across grade levels — the same standard repeated word-for-word at least half the time across different grades. Compounding the difficulty, even a handful of broad standards typically expands into dozens of specific goals, benchmarks, and objectives underneath — one state's first-grade math standard, judged high quality, contained 5 broad standards, 10 goals, and 29 total objectives — leaving more content than any single school year can realistically cover.

**Tests must actually align with the standards they're meant to assess**, or the whole premise of accountability collapses: a mismatch between what's tested and what the standards specify means test results simply cannot speak to students' standards-based proficiency, undermining the "theory of action" NCLB depends on. The 2006 American Federation of Teachers study found only 11 states had all their tests aligned with their own state standards.

**When there are too many standards to test them all, tests must sample, which creates genuine unpredictability.** Reliably assessing any one content standard requires more than one or two items, so testing all standards every year is not feasible — and when a significant fraction of standards go untested in a given year, teachers are put in the position of guessing which ones will actually appear, with real instructional cost when the guess is wrong (studying content that isn't tested, and not studying content that is). One prominent testing expert, Popham (2004), called this "a muddleheaded way to run a testing program" (p. 79).

**Absolute proficiency and student growth are not the same thing, and rewarding only the former creates a real distortion.** NCLB's Adequate Yearly Progress (AYP) framework is based on the absolute level of performance reached at one point in time, not on how much a student improved to get there — which means a school whose students improve substantially but still fall short of the proficiency line gets no credit for that improvement, while a school whose already-proficient students stagnate can still meet AYP. Because AYP timetables require every school to reach the same proficiency target by a fixed date, schools starting from a lower baseline must show much larger year-over-year gains than schools starting closer to the target already. Growth (or value-added) models correct for this by tracking how much an individual student's score changed year to year rather than only where it landed — and the evidence for why this matters is substantial: in one study, low-achieving fourth-grade students in Dallas, Texas were followed for three years, and 90 percent of those with effective teachers passed the seventh-grade math test, versus only 42 percent of those with ineffective teachers (cited in Bracey, 2004) — see [Effective Teachers Compound Their Advantage Over Consecutive Years](../claims/teacher-effectiveness-compounds-over-consecutive-years.md). The same study found low-achieving students were *more* likely than high-achieving students to be assigned an ineffective teacher three years running, compounding the disadvantage rather than correcting for it.

**States define "proficient" very differently from each other, which undermines any comparison across state lines.** Because NCLB lets each state set its own content standards, assessments, and proficiency thresholds, apparent proficiency rates can diverge sharply from a common external yardstick: in 2003, Colorado and Missouri reported 67 percent and 21 percent of fourth-graders proficient in reading on their own state tests respectively, but the same students scored 34 percent and 28 percent proficient on the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) — a well-regarded, nationally-standardized test (Linn, 2005). The gap shows there is no common meaning behind "proficient achievement" as states currently define it.

**The predictable downstream effect of high-stakes testing is curriculum narrowing and teaching to the test.** Surveys of elementary teachers found more instructional time devoted to math and reading, and less to social studies and science, in 2004 than in 1990 (Jerald, 2006); principals in high-minority-enrollment schools in several states reported reduced arts instruction. This has a real cost beyond the narrowing itself: reading comprehension in a subject like science or social studies depends on prior vocabulary and background knowledge in that subject (Recht & Leslie, 1988), so students can develop strong general reading skills yet still struggle with subjects they were given little instructional time to build background knowledge in. Teachers surveyed nationally (over 4,000 respondents) largely reported adapting instruction to the tests — elementary teachers reported the heaviest influence (56 percent said the tests shaped their daily or near-daily teaching, versus 46 percent of middle school and 28 percent of high school teachers) — while also expressing real skepticism about the tests themselves: 40 percent reported teachers had found ways to raise scores without improving actual learning, and over 70 percent said the scores didn't accurately reflect what minority students know and can do (Pedulla, Abrams, Madaus, Russell, Ramos, & Miao, 2003).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content standards specific enough to guide instruction but few enough to actually be taught and assessed within a school year
- Genuine, verifiable alignment between published standards and the content actually tested — not merely asserted alignment
- A growth or value-added component in accountability calculations, not absolute-proficiency measurement alone, if the system is meant to fairly credit schools serving initially lower-performing students
#### Constraints
- Standards too broad give teachers nothing concrete to teach toward; standards too narrow restrict the curriculum to trivial specifics — neither failure mode is safer than the other
- When only a sample of many standards can be tested each year, some amount of teacher guessing about what will appear is structurally unavoidable, not merely a matter of insufficient effort
- Comparing "proficiency rates" across states (or even across a state's own tests from different years) without a common external reference like NAEP risks comparing numbers that don't mean the same thing
- Heavy accountability pressure predictably narrows instructional time away from untested subjects, with a genuine downstream cost to comprehension in exactly those subjects once they are addressed

### Target Learners
- All students in accountability-tested grades and subjects; students in initially lower-performing schools bear a disproportionate share of the pressure created by absolute (non-growth) proficiency targets
- Students in untested subjects (art, and often social studies/science at some grade bands) are indirectly affected by instructional-time reallocation even though they are not themselves being tested

### Target Learning Objectives
- Accurate, comparable information about whether students are meeting content standards, without the curriculum-narrowing and teaching-to-the-test side effects that undermine the deeper goal of actual learning

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Assessment for Learning](assessment-for-learning.md) [-M] — high-stakes accountability testing is explicitly assessment *of* learning for external certification, and the pressure it creates can actively distort assessment *for* learning's low-stakes, revision-friendly design goals

## Examples
- Tennessee's practice of reporting both AYP status and a growth category (below-average, average, above-average) on school report cards, so a school with high proficiency but low growth, or low proficiency but high growth, is visible either way rather than collapsed into a single pass/fail label
