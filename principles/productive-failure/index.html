Productive Failure¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 49 claims (19 for, 23 mixed, 7 against) · 29 studies (17 causal, 6 review, 4 theoretical, 2 quant-synthesis), q1–q4 · 12 of 29 report an effect size · 46 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the decision of whether a new concept is taught first or attempted first. When the goal is conceptual understanding of a new concept and transfer to problems not yet seen, and learners already have enough prior knowledge to generate several partial or wrong solutions of their own, letting them first attempt problems the concept solves (problem solving followed by instruction, PS-I), and then teaching the concept by comparing their attempts with the canonical solution, is expected to improve conceptual knowledge and transfer over teaching first and practising after (I-PS). Pooled across studies, it is not expected to improve procedural skill; one trial of a brief, repeated variant for a multi-step procedure is the exception recorded in the wiki (see the procedure rows below).

The relationship is conditional on five things, each from the evidence below:

  • The outcome. Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons the advantage was g = 0.36 on conceptual knowledge and transfer, and g = −0.03, no difference, on procedural knowledge (Sinha & Kapur 2021).
  • How the attempt and the teaching are designed. The advantage grew with fidelity to the productive-failure design: problems that admit several solution methods, learners actually generating several, group work, and, most of all, instruction that builds on the learners' own solutions (g = 0.56 where it did, 0.20 where it did not). A theory-building review (Loibl, Roll & Rummel 2017) reached the same point from the other side: problem solving before instruction helped only where the instruction built on student solutions or used contrasting cases. Group work is the weakest of these features: one randomised trial with tenth-graders found no difference in conceptual knowledge between attempting alone and in groups (Brand et al. 2023).
  • Who the learners are. The effect was negative for second to fifth graders and rose with grade level. Young learners may not have enough prior knowledge to generate several solutions.
  • What is being learned. For domain-general skills, such as the control-of-variables strategy, the instruction-first order did better.
  • How long the intervention runs. In the meta-regression, the fidelity benefit tended to shrink for interventions spanning only a few hours (a marginal interaction, p = .055); longer interventions also had higher fidelity.

How this sits beside its neighbours: Guided Discovery Learning and the evidence against minimal guidance concern learning without the instruction that follows; productive failure always ends with full instruction, and the question is only its order. Worked Examples and Cognitive Load Management argue for teaching first, especially for novices learning procedures; the evidence here agrees for procedural outcomes and for young learners, and differs for conceptual outcomes in learners old enough to generate solutions. Pretesting is a smaller cousin: an attempt before study, without the generation of methods.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

The meta-analysis establishes the direction, the outcomes it holds for and which design features go with larger effects; it does not set a dose. This default is a labelled proposal: the two phases and the features follow the evidence, the timings are untested choices.

  1. Check the condition. Is the target a concept with an underlying structure (variance, ratio, a physical law, a statistical idea), and is the outcome conceptual understanding or transfer rather than fluent execution of a procedure? Are learners past the early primary years, with the prior knowledge to attempt the problem in several ways? If not, teach first.
  2. Choose one rich problem the concept is the answer to: one that admits several reasonable methods, most of them partial or wrong (for variance: which of three players is the most consistent scorer?). Give it a story or a reason to care. Pilot it with a few learners first where you can: the sign of the right problem is that they produce several ideas containing the errors the instruction will address, and do not reach the canonical method; a problem almost nobody makes any headway on is too hard (studies with very low solution rates in the attempt phase tended not to find the benefit). Where the time allows a second problem, set it in a different context from the first: in two studies of the same design, one short problem before instruction did worse for transfer than explaining a worked solution, and two problems did better.
  3. Problem-solving phase, 30–60 minutes, in small groups or individually. Groups are optional: one trial found no conceptual difference between attempting alone and in groups. Where groups are used, mix prior attainment so each group has at least one member able to drive several methods. Ask for as many ways of solving it as they can find. Do not teach, give hints toward the canonical method, or grade correctness; content-general prompts that help without telling the content ("what else could you try?", "what does your method leave out?", or a think-ask-explain role script for pairs) are compatible with the attempt. Say plainly that not solving it is expected and that trying several ways is the task.
  4. Collect the attempts. Note the three or four most common or most instructive methods, including wrong ones.
  5. Instruction phase, building on the attempts. Present the learners' methods, ask what each captures and misses, compare them with each other and then with the canonical method, and show which features of the problem the canonical method handles that theirs did not. Where the learners' own attempts cannot be collected (a self-paced course, no time to sort them), set prepared typical student solutions, or contrasting cases, side by side instead: the review evidence counts either as doing the job, and an attempt followed by instruction that does neither is the version that did not help. Contrasting cases have to be used, not just present: ask learners to compare them and say what differs; in one trial, cases placed in the problem material without guidance on their use did not help. Then teach the concept in full.
  6. Then practise the procedure, as you would after instruction-first teaching: the attempt phase does not replace it. (For a multi-step procedure whose fluency is the goal, the default stays teach first; brief attempts before each step are a recorded alternative, on one trial, in the procedure rows below.)
  7. Across sessions, run the attempt and the instruction on different days or at least with a break between them where the course allows; do not compress the whole design into one short lesson. Over a unit, use a full attempt for each foundational concept rather than every topic; where a content-general script is used, expect the first session to go no better than teaching first (in one four-week study the scripted group pulled ahead only from week two, as students internalised the script).
  8. Judge it on a conceptual or transfer test, not on the attempt phase, which will look like failure (learners taught first produce fewer but better attempts, and in one trial almost all of them solved the practice sheets while the attempt-first learners mostly did not), and not on a procedural test alone, where no difference is expected.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Four facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: is the outcome conceptual and transfer, or procedural?; how old are the learners and what do they already know?; is the target a domain concept or a domain-general skill?; and how much time is there?

Observation, state and explanation¶

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Learners produce one method, or none, during the attempt The problem admits too few methods; learners lack the prior knowledge to generate; they are waiting to be told Ask what they would try if the first method were not allowed; check the prior knowledge the problem assumes. Do not treat the count of methods as the measure of success: one study found it predicted learning, two did not (see the evidence list).
Almost nobody gets any part of the way, even to a partial method The problem is too hard for this group; the prior knowledge it assumes is missing Look at how much of the canonical solution the best attempts contain; simplify the problem or teach the prerequisite first, since attempt phases with very low solution rates went with no benefit.
A group settles on one method within minutes, or one member does all the generating The group is all high-attaining (someone already knows a method) or all low-attaining (nobody can start); the problem admits too few methods Look at the group's composition and at the methods other groups found; regroup to mix prior attainment, or ask each member to write a method before the group discusses.
Learners disengage or say the task is unfair The failure is not framed as expected; the attempt is too long; the attempt is graded Ask how they understood the task; shorten the attempt and restate that it is not graded.
Conceptual test no better than with teaching first The instruction did not build on the attempts; the learners were too young or too new to the domain; the test measured procedure Check whether the instruction compared their methods with the canonical one; check what the test asked.
Procedural test worse than with teaching first Practice time was lost to the attempt phase Give the same amount of practice after instruction in both designs before comparing.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Productive failure improves conceptual learning [+M]: Sinha & Kapur's (2021) meta-analysis of 53 studies and 166 comparisons, g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] on conceptual knowledge and transfer, larger (g up to 0.58) with high fidelity to the productive-failure design; and Kapur's (2014) two randomised studies, where the number of solutions students generated predicted what they learned.
  • PS-I shows no pooled benefit for procedural knowledge [~M]: g = −0.03, not significant. The design is not a way to teach procedures.
  • After accounting for publication bias, the estimated true effect favoring PS-I is strong [+M]: a bias-corrected estimate of g = 0.87. A correction of this kind is model-dependent; the observed 0.36 is the safer figure to plan with.
  • PF design fidelity predicts larger PS-I effects [+M]: of the seven features coded, group work and instruction building on student solutions showed significant subgroup differences.
  • PS-I fosters learning only if contrasting cases or instruction built on student solutions are implemented [+W]: Loibl, Roll & Rummel's (2017) review of PS-I implementations; no pooled effect printed. It widens the fidelity finding: contrasting cases can stand in for the learners' own solutions.
  • Collaborative and individual problem solving in traditional PF do not differ in conceptual knowledge after instruction [~M]: Brand et al. (2023), 137 tenth-graders randomised to attempt the variance problem alone or in groups of three; no significant difference (c = 0.57, 95% CI [−0.39, 1.53]). This qualifies the meta-analytic subgroup finding for group work: a non-significant difference is not equivalence, but groups are not a precondition.
  • Mixed-ability groups generated a broader range of solution methods [+W]: two undergraduate variance-invention studies reported in Kapur & Rummel's (2012) editorial; the outcome is the range of methods and uptake of the formula, not a learning test.
  • Role-script support during generation led university relearning students to outperform direct instruction [+W]: a four-week study with 76 university students, reported in the same editorial, no effect size; the advantage appeared from week two as the script was internalised.
  • Micro productive failure improved sixth-graders' isomorphic algebraic procedural problem solving [+M] and showed no effect on conceptual explanation [~M]: Ziegler, Trninic & Kapur (2021), 81 sixth-graders, brief attempts before each step of an algebraic procedure; η²p = 0.06 for the procedural advantage, stable from one day to one week; on conceptual explanation no main effect, and a small interaction in which teaching first was ahead (non-significantly) at one day and behind at one week. The reverse of the pooled pattern, for a different variant: one trial.
  • In one meta-regression, the PS-I fidelity benefit tended to shrink for interventions spanning only a few hours [~M]: β = −0.0116, p = .055, marginal; an earlier wording on this page called it a reversal, which overstated it.
  • PS-I effects are negative for 2nd–5th graders and increase with age [-M].
  • For domain-general skills, one meta-analysis finds a negligible effect (g = −0.17) in favour of I-PS over PS-I [-M].
  • Solving novel problems before instruction improves conceptual understanding and transfer but not procedural knowledge [+M]: Kapur's (2016) review of the classroom experiments with ninth-graders learning standard deviation.
  • Guidance during initial problem solving does not improve learning from subsequent instruction [~M]: Kapur (2011) and Loibl & Rummel (2013), as reviewed by Kapur (2016).
  • Invention tasks prepare future learning [+M]: Schwartz & Martin (2004); ninth-graders who invented a procedure before a worked example transferred better, but only when a learning resource was available at test.
  • Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [-M]: the boundary with discovery learning that ends without instruction.
  • PS-I outperforms I-PS on conceptual knowledge only when instruction builds on student solutions [+W], merely delaying instruction does not improve conceptual understanding [~W] and contrasting cases without explicit guidance on their use do not help [~W]: Loibl & Rummel (2014), as summarised by Loibl et al. (2025), and Loibl et al. (2020), 181 students in a 2×2 trial. Together they locate the active ingredient in what the instruction does with the attempts, not in the order alone. The 2020 trial's PS-I versus I-PS difference was not significant (p = 0.22), which is not a finding of no effect.
  • Across studies, high solution rates in the initial problem-solving phase co-occur with beneficial PS-I effects and low rates with no beneficial effects, a cross-study pattern not yet tested in a shared sample [~W]: a cross-study reading in a framework article (rates of 14–28% with no benefit, 80–90% with one); not a meta-regression. It qualifies "failure": the attempt should fail to reach the canonical method, not fail to get anywhere.
  • Whether better or more attempts predict learning is mixed in the wiki: the number of canonical components in students' solutions correlated with conceptual knowledge [~W] (r = .42, n = 37; the range of components did not, r = .21), while neither the quantity nor the quality of attempts correlated with conceptual knowledge [~W] (r = .10 and .12, n = 55) in another trial, and solution quantity did not mediate the effect of collaboration [~W] in a third; Kapur (2014) found the number of solutions predictive. Treat the count of methods as a sign the problem worked, not as a target.
  • Instruction before problem solving leads students to generate fewer but higher-quality solution attempts [~W] (η² = 0.16 for number, 0.34 for quality) and the manipulation worked as intended: I-PS students largely succeeded on the problem-solving sheets while PS-I students largely failed [~W] (η²p = 0.76): the attempt phase looks worse by design, which is why it is not the place to judge the design.
  • Across two studies that differed in the duration of the initial problem-solving phase, the PS-I versus WS-I transfer advantage reversed [~W]: Glogger-Frey et al. (2015, 2017), as summarised by Loibl et al. (2025); one problem before instruction lost to a worked solution on transfer, two problems in different contexts won. The comparison is with studying a worked solution, not with teaching first.
  • Vicarious failure with high solution diversity is as effective as productive failure [~W], and outperforms low-diversity vicarious failure and direct instruction [+M]: Braas et al. (2025), 152 secondary students randomised to four conditions; VF-high over teaching first d = 0.65, VF-high against attempting oneself d = 0.29 in its favour, not significant. With observing the whole failing process beat generating one's own attempts [+M] (Hartmann et al. 2021, 173 students, the advantage growing with prior knowledge: prior knowledge moderated the VF-process advantage [~M]), this suggests that meeting diverse failed methods before instruction matters more than producing them oneself. Two trials, by overlapping authors.
  • PF students did not outperform DI students when learning social science research methods [-W] and of eight PF-similar studies in non-STEM or domain-general settings, only one found a positive effect [-W]: Nachtigall, Serova & Rummel (2020), two quasi-experiments with 212 and 152 tenth-graders in an out-of-school lab, after pilots in which the problem met the sweet spot of complexity [+W]. The meta-analytic advantage is a STEM finding.
  • PS-I with a virus transmission simulation showed better transfer of multivariable causality [+W] (Saba et al. 2025) and PS-I over a year in a university linear algebra course was followed by higher final exam performance than baseline cohorts [+W] (Baumgartner et al. 2025): both reported secondhand in an editorial (Loibl, Nachtigall & Leuders 2025), no effect sizes; the second compares cohorts, not randomised groups.

Taken together: for conceptual understanding and transfer in learners past the early primary years, attempting first and being taught second, with the teaching built on the attempts, beats teaching first by about a third of a standard deviation; for procedures, for young children and for domain-general skills it does not, pooled, though one trial of brief repeated attempts found a procedural gain in sixth-graders. Group work helps less than the fidelity coding suggested; building the instruction on solutions (the learners' own or typical ones) matters most, and delaying the instruction without it did not help. Watching a modelled learner fail in several different ways did as well as failing oneself in two classroom trials. Outside mathematics and science, the wiki records no advantage. No claim in the wiki tests a dose directly (how long the attempt), beyond two studies in which two problems beat one, or the full design in a self-paced online course.

The evidence by question¶

  • Should learners attempt a problem before the idea is taught, and what must the teaching afterwards do? Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [+M]
  • Which features of the attempt and of the instruction after it, and how much time, go with a larger advantage for attempting first? PF design fidelity predicts larger PS-I effects, with four fidelity criteria showing significant subgroup differences [+M]
  • Should the attempt be made in groups or alone? Collaborative and individual problem solving in traditional PF do not differ in conceptual knowledge after instruction [~M]
  • Can attempting first help a procedure rather than a concept? Micro productive failure improved sixth-graders' isomorphic algebraic procedural problem solving [+M]
  • Must learners fail themselves, or can they watch someone else fail? Students observing high-diversity vicarious failure outperform VF-low and DI students on conceptual knowledge [+M]
  • Does the advantage hold outside mathematics and science? PF students did not outperform DI students when learning social science research methods [-W]
  • Is it the order, or what the instruction does with the attempts? Merely delaying instruction does not improve conceptual understanding [~W]

  • Pretesting enhances learning [+M]: an attempt before study, without the generation of methods; a cousin of this design, named in the conditional relationship. Its studies are laboratory retention tests with undergraduates, not tests of conceptual transfer, so it does not change the model.
  • Giftedness interacts significantly with instruction type on the learning and recall outcomes in the 2×2×2 study [~M] — attached 2026-10-18 from Lim et al. The claim records that the effect of the order differed between gifted and other students (η²p = .077) but not which way, so it cannot yet set a row; reading the article for the direction is the next step.

  • PF students outperformed direct-instruction students on conceptual understanding and transfer without compromising procedural fluency [+W]: Kapur's ninth-grade variance study as summarised in Kapur & Rummel's (2012) editorial; the same finding the meta-analysis already carries, so it does not change the model.
  • PS-I research demonstrates beneficial effects on conceptual knowledge and knowledge transfer relative to I-PS [+W]: a framework article's narrative summary of the literature the meta-analysis pools; no new comparison.
  • The three preparatory conditions did not differ on procedural knowledge acquisition [~W]: Hartmann et al. (2021), attempting against two kinds of watching; no procedural difference among preparation variants, consistent with the procedure row but not a comparison with teaching first.
  • The proposed cognitive mechanisms explain both positive and negative effects of PS-I [~W]: Loibl, Roll & Rummel's (2017) theoretical move; the review's design findings are already in the fidelity row.
  • The intervention significantly benefited 6th-grade students but showed no significant effects for 7th or 8th graders [~W] (not about this page): an evaluation of ASSISTments homework support with teacher professional development, not an attempt-before-instruction design.
  • Struggling with a challenging task before receiving instruction prepares learners to benefit from explicit connections [+W] — attached 2026-10-20 from Fries et al. (2020), which proposed "Engage students in productive struggle before presenting explicit connections and solutions"; tests this page's relationship.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is understanding deep enough to use a concept on problems not yet seen. Ask separately what the learner wants: a learner who must pass a procedural test next week is not served by an attempt phase that costs practice time, and a learner who reads failure as a verdict on ability needs the framing in step 3 before anything else.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if new comparisons with comparable learners, holding total time and practice equal, find no conceptual or transfer advantage for attempting first; or if the advantage disappears when the instruction-first condition also builds on typical student errors, which would locate the effect in the instruction rather than the order. It should be extended, not reversed, by studies of self-paced online versions. The non-STEM row should change if randomised trials outside mathematics and science find the advantage the two quasi-experiments recorded here did not; the vicarious-failure option should be dropped if trials by other research groups find watching diverse failed attempts worse than making them. The procedure rows should change if further trials of brief repeated attempts replicate, or fail to replicate, the one procedural gain recorded here, and the group rows if a powered trial shows attempting alone equivalent to, or worse than, attempting in groups.

Claims¶

  • DI students relied on notes rather than exploring the agent-based models, while PF classes showed lively collaborative exploration and questioning [+W]
  • Instructional design and preferences interacted in predicting knowledge: DI-PS assignment and DI-PS preference were negatively associated with conceptual knowledge gain [-W]
  • PS-DI learners reported higher intrinsic in-scenario cognitive load during problem solving (d = .53) [~W]

  • Invention — the invention activity as a building block.
  • When implementing PS-I, include contrasting cases or build the instruction phase on student solutions
  • Use collaboration scripting to take advantage of the hidden learning in productive failure
