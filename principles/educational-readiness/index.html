Educational Readiness¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 20 claims (14 for, 6 mixed) · 19 studies (8 associational, 3 review, 2 causal, 2 qualitative, 2 theoretical, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 design), q1–q4 · 1 of 19 report an effect size · 19 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

Readiness has two sides, and this page treats them as one decision. The learner side is what a learner can already do that the first tasks of a new stage assume: for a child entering kindergarten, managing their own physical needs, some social skills, using a pencil for simple shapes, a working spoken vocabulary; for an older student, the prerequisites of a course (algebra before physics). The teacher or school side is whether the instruction has been adjusted to the learners who actually arrive: play and movement for five-year-olds, a different approach for a student with a visual impairment, a bridge for a missing prerequisite.

The relationship: for learners starting a stage, course or unit whose tasks depend on earlier capabilities, observing each learner's task-specific starting response on those capabilities, and then adjusting where instruction starts, how much support it gives and how the setting is arranged, is expected to let more learners reach the stage's goals (immediate-performance on the early tasks, general-achievement across the first year or course) than either of two common alternatives: holding the learner back until judged ready (delaying school entry, repeating a year, refusing entry to a course), or teaching everyone from one assumed starting point and treating the learners who cannot follow as "not ready". The expected change is in how many learners keep up with the stage's goals, not in a fixed trait of the learner.

The relationship is conditional on four things: that the check is about the capabilities the first tasks actually use, not a general label (age, a diagnosis, family background); that the adjustment responds to what the check showed; that the learner is in instruction often enough to benefit (attendance); and that the outcome and horizon are stated, because early-grade differences and later-grade differences have behaved differently in the evidence.

No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as a whole. None compares diagnosing and adjusting at entry with holding learners back or with one uniform start. What the wiki holds bears on its parts: correlational and regression-discontinuity evidence that an extra year of age or a repeated kindergarten year buys an early advantage that fades or is absent by the early grades (US data, children aged five to eight); correlational evidence that more preschool attendance goes with higher readiness scores, most of all for children who start with lower skills; a meta-analysis showing that what learners know at the start strongly predicts what they know at the end but says little about how much they gain; and cognitive-load evidence that support which helps novices can hinder learners who are already past the step. Most readiness claims here are about early childhood in the United States; carrying them to secondary, post-secondary or adult prerequisites is an untested transfer.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Mastery Learning owns what happens once a unit is under way (a check, correction, and a second check before advancing); this page owns the decision at the entry to a stage. Activation owns making relevant knowledge visible just before a lesson. Flexible Grouping owns who works with whom once the entry check has shown differences. Universal Design for Learning owns removing demands that are not part of the goal, which is much of the teacher-side readiness described here. Scaffolding and Scaffolding and Fading own how support is given and withdrawn once a gap is found. The learner variable behind the whole page is prior knowledge.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (readiness indicators, the two-sided readiness table, "adjust to the student rather than blame the student"), made concrete. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is beside it.

  1. List the prerequisites of the first two or three weeks, on both sides. For each early task, write what the learner must already be able to do, and beside it what the teacher or setting must provide (the earlier page's two-column table: a working spoken vocabulary and complete sentences on the child's side; answering children's questions, pointing out letters and sounds, paper and marking tools on the teacher's side). Keep the list short (five to eight items) and specific to the tasks. Untested proposal; the table form is from the earlier page (Copple & Bredekamp, 2006).
  2. Observe each learner on those prerequisites before or in the first week. For young children, watch them in ordinary activities and short one-to-one tasks (about 10 minutes per child, spread across the first week); for older students, five to eight items on the prerequisites, unaided, with no grade attached. Record the task, the conditions and what the learner did, not a "ready / not ready" label. Entry scores are informative about later risk: Kindergarten test scores show patterns that can predict academic success or identify students at risk of falling further behind by third grade [+W]. A starting score predicts where a learner ends but not how much they gain (Prior Knowledge Determines New Learning [~M]), so use the check to place instruction, not to forecast who will benefit. Dose is an untested proposal.
  3. For each gap, decide one of three moves, and do not default to waiting.
  4. Teach the prerequisite now, inside the stage: a short bridging segment (10 to 15 minutes, two or three times a week, for the first three to four weeks) for the learners who showed the gap. Untested proposal.
  5. Adjust the setting or the route: more play and physical activity for young children, accommodations for a sensory or physical need, materials at hand. Untested here; the earlier page's central example, and the subject of Universal Design for Learning.
  6. Move ahead faster for learners already past the prerequisite: skip the bridging and give extension tasks, since guidance that helps those who lack the step can hinder those who have it (Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M], carried from cognitive-load studies to entry decisions).
  7. Holding a child out of school a year, or repeating a year, has little recorded support as a route to readiness: Redshirted students' early-grade academic advantage over on-time peers fades as students move through school [+W]; Older entrants show higher kindergarten growth rates but lower growth rates in 1st and 2nd grades [+M]; At the end of first grade, children who repeated kindergarten had lower reading and math knowledge and skills than children who started on time [+M] (associational; children who repeat were selected for difficulty, so it does not show repetition caused the gap).
  8. Prepare families before the start, not only the child. Before entry, share what the first weeks will ask of the child and give families a few things to do at home (for example a short summer transition programme, or two or three meetings). Families without preschool experience reported this as especially useful: Understanding kindergarten expectations and acquiring tools to support children at home were especially beneficial for families without prior preschool or structured school experience [+W] (parent interviews, no comparison). See the three-week summer transition programme and support for children entering kindergarten and first grade.
  9. Watch attendance from week one, and act first for learners who started with gaps. In one Chicago preschool study, missing more school went with lower readiness scores in mathematics, letter recognition and social-emotional development after controlling for entering skills (Four-year-olds who miss more preschool score lower on kindergarten readiness measures… [~M]), and the link was strongest for children who entered with lower skills (Good attendance is more strongly related to academic gains for students who enter preschool with lower incoming skills than for those entering with higher skills [+M]). Both are associational. Contact the family after a learner's third absence in a month (an untested threshold).
  10. Recheck at about three and six weeks, and let learners leave the bridging segment. Repeat a parallel form of the entry check. A learner moves out of bridging after meeting the prerequisite on two consecutive checks; if many still fail after six weeks, the bridging itself is not working, so change it rather than relabelling the learner. Untested thresholds. Expect early differences to narrow: Pre-K benefits at the start of kindergarten diminish by a little more than half during the kindergarten year [~M] (2,581 low-income children; the gap narrows because non-attenders catch up), so an entry gap is not a fixed placement.
  11. Shift support across the term. Weeks 1 to 2: teacher-led routines made visible, more play and movement for young children, bridging for those who need it. Weeks 3 to 6: bridging fades as rechecks are met, learners take on more of the routine themselves. After about six weeks: the separate readiness check folds into ordinary formative assessment (Mastery Learning holds that loop). Untested proposal.

When a condition is not met: if no one can observe learners individually before the start (a large intake, a self-paced course), use a short self-check on the prerequisites with worked answers and a pointer to a bridging resource for each item missed; if the stage has no real prerequisites (the first tasks assume nothing the learners lack), skip the check and start.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the first tasks actually assume. A prerequisite list written from the tasks gives a check that can be acted on; a general readiness label (age, a screening score, a diagnosis) does not say what to teach. If unstated, ask: what must a learner be able to do in the first two weeks, and what will the teacher or setting provide?
  • Who the learners are and what they bring. Young children entering school, students with disabilities, learners in a second language and adults returning after years away need different checks and different adjustments; criteria built on one population misclassify others. If unstated, ask: what are the learners' ages, languages, prior schooling and any needs already known, and who knows them (families, previous teachers)?
  • What the decision options really are. Whether holding back, a separate bridging course, extra time inside the stage, or a different route are actually available changes the design. If unstated, ask: can the timetable carry a short bridging segment, and who decides entry?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a learner's response to a stated task under stated conditions (a child drawing a circle when asked, writing a name, following a two-step instruction; a student solving a prerequisite item unaided), plus attendance and what families report. "Ready", "not ready", "immature" and "lacks prerequisites" are inferences, and the earlier page's point is that they are inferences about the match between a learner and a setting, not about the learner alone.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares diagnosing and adjusting at entry with holding learners back, or with one uniform start. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship. None prints an effect size for its finding.

  • Older entrants show higher kindergarten growth rates but lower growth rates in 1st and 2nd grades [+M]: a multi-state regression discontinuity analysis of assessment data (Johnson & Kuhfeld 2020, causal, q3) finds that being a year older gives higher initial achievement and faster kindergarten growth, but slower growth in first and second grade. It is the strongest design here; the abstract only was read. The early academic advantage of entering school older may fade in later grades [+M] is the brief announcing the same study, so the two are one study, not two.
  • Redshirted students' early-grade academic advantage over on-time peers fades as students move through school [+W]: an NWEA fact sheet summarising "multiple studies"; no studies are named on the claim page.
  • Redshirting kindergartners likely carries costs that outweigh its academic benefits [+W]: the title of an NWEA brief analysing MAP Growth data; the claim page records the conclusion only, not the costs counted.
  • At the end of first grade, children whose kindergarten entry was delayed were behind their classmates who began kindergarten on time in math [+M] and … slightly higher reading knowledge and skills [~M]: one ECLS-K report (Malone et al. 2006, associational, q3). The two results point in opposite directions by subject, which is a reason not to read the redshirting evidence as "delay always hurts".
  • At the end of first grade, children who repeated kindergarten had lower reading and math knowledge and skills than children who started on time [+M]: the same report. Children who repeat were held back because they were struggling, so the comparison cannot separate the effect of repeating from the reasons for it.
  • Four-year-olds who miss more preschool score lower on kindergarten readiness measures of math, letter recognition, and social-emotional development, even after controlling for incoming skills [~M] and Good attendance is more strongly related to academic gains for students who enter preschool with lower incoming skills [+M]: one Chicago study of 1,265 four-year-olds (Ehrlich et al. 2014, associational, q3). For pre-literacy, once beginning-of-year letter recognition was controlled, attendance showed no significant relationship; the marker is ~ for that split.
  • Pre-K benefits at the start of kindergarten diminish by a little more than half during the kindergarten year [~M]: 2,581 low-income children in one county (Ruzek et al. 2020, associational, q3). It qualifies any reading of readiness at entry as fixed: the gap narrowed within the year.
  • Prior Knowledge Determines New Learning [~M]: a meta-analysis of 8,776 effect sizes (Simonsmeier et al. 2022, q4) finds prior knowledge correlates strongly with later knowledge (r+ = .534) but hardly at all with normalised gains (r+ = -.059), with wide variation. Its table counts five distinct studies behind the claim and its narrower ones. It is the limit on this page: a starting check predicts where a learner will stand, not how much they will learn from instruction, so it is a reason to adjust instruction to the starting point, not to sort learners by expected benefit.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: cognitive-load reviews and experiments; its table counts 14 distinct studies behind it and its narrower claims. It supports letting learners who already hold a prerequisite skip the bridging; it is about instructional formats, not entry decisions.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (US preschool and early-grade cohorts, electrical trainees, mixed meta-analytic samples), and most are associational, so do not rank them by effect label.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does holding a child back a year until judged ready (delayed entry or repeating kindergarten) give a lasting academic advantage over on-time entry? Redshirted students' early-grade academic advantage over on-time peers fades as students move through school [+W]
  • Does more time in preschool instruction (attending pre-K, attending more days) raise readiness at kindergarten entry, and does the difference last? Children who attend pre-kindergarten are more ready for kindergarten at the end of their pre-k year than children who do not attend [+W]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims the earlier page cited that the model above does not use as evidence for its own relationship. They describe the population this page most often serves, or the effect of preschool programmes, more than the decision at entry.

  • Many children enter kindergarten without the foundational mathematics skills needed to succeed [+W] — background statement in one article, citing Garcia & Weiss (2015); describes the size of the need, tests nothing. Possibly a duplicate of the next claim (its own page flags it as a merge candidate).
  • Many U.S. students enter elementary school with poor math skills [+W] — opening assertion of a curriculum-evaluation design report, no data; same bearing as the claim above.
  • Children who attend pre-kindergarten are more ready for kindergarten at the end of their pre-k year than children who do not attend [+W] — a regional lab summary of a consensus statement, no effect sizes; bears on readiness as built before entry, not on the entry decision. Hub of the second question above.
  • A year of West Virginia Pre-K produced substantial gains in print awareness at kindergarten entry, and the program meets all 10 NIEER quality standards [+W] — a state case study reporting researchers' finding second-hand; bears on programmes that raise readiness, not on the entry decision.
  • Difficulty adjusting to kindergarten and first grade can put young children at academic risk [+W] — a fact sheet's assertion with no data; used in the situation table only as a reason to plan the transition.
  • Number naming and number comparison are better predictors of kindergarten arithmetic than number line estimation [+W] — attached 2026-10-17 from Praet et al. (2013), which proposed "Assess kindergarten language and number naming to inform targeted instruction and interventions for children at risk of arithmetic difficulties".

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective at entry is usually that learners can take part in and benefit from the first weeks of the stage (immediate-performance on the early tasks) and keep up across the year or course. What the learner, or for a young child the family, values may differ: a five-year-old wants to play, make friends and feel safe; a family may want their child to be "ahead"; an adult returning to study may most want not to be embarrassed in front of others. Elicit these separately (ask the family before entry; ask an older learner what they hope to be able to do). Agreement and divergence are both observations. A family that wants to hold a child back so that the child will be ahead is valuing a relative position the evidence above says fades; that is a conversation about the child's specific concern and the adjustment planned for it, not a verdict on the family.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of entry-check-and-adjust designs against uniform-start designs, with comparable learners and outcomes measured across the first year, show no difference, or if a well-designed study shows lasting gains from delayed entry or repetition for a defined group of learners. If the attendance link disappears under designs that separate attendance from the reasons for absence, the attendance step becomes a matter of family support rather than of exposure to instruction. If entry checks of the kind proposed here misclassify many learners (especially those with disabilities or in a second language), the check should be replaced by observation in the first tasks themselves. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the adjustment was not enough" after the fact.

Early-grade levels, growth across a year, later-grade achievement and the learner's own sense of belonging are separate outcomes; the present evidence establishes no readiness threshold, no length for a bridging segment and no rule for when a learner should be held back.

Related Principles¶

  • Scaffolding — once a mismatch between student readiness and task demand is identified, scaffolding is one primary response
  • Transfer of Learning — prerequisite mastery (a form of readiness) is itself a factor affecting transfer
  • Mastery Learning — the check, correction and recheck loop once a stage is under way
  • Activation — making relevant prior knowledge visible just before a lesson
  • Flexible Grouping — temporary groups formed from the entry check, re-formed as rechecks change
  • Universal Design for Learning — removing demands that are not part of the goal, much of the teacher-side readiness

Examples¶

  • A reading-readiness table can list parallel expectations for the child (e.g., a productive vocabulary of 5,000-8,000 words, using complete sentences, matching some letters to sounds) and for the teacher (e.g., answering children's questions, highlighting letters and sounds in the classroom, providing paper and marking tools) side by side (Copple & Bredekamp, 2006).
  • Three-week structured summer kindergarten transition program — a transition programme that prepares children and families before entry
  • Provide support to children entering kindergarten and first grade — the strategy for easing school transitions
