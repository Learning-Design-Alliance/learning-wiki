Text Chats¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 23 claims (12 for, 10 mixed, 1 against) · 20 studies (7 qualitative, 6 causal, 2 review, 2 design, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 1 of 20 report an effect size · 21 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page covers live or near-live written exchange among learners, or between learners and a teacher, used for a learning purpose: a task conversation, a question channel during a presentation, a short peer response. For learners who need practice formulating ideas in writing for a present audience (including adult and adolescent second-language learners working on a meaning-focused task), or who are less ready to speak aloud in a group, a focused text chat, on a task with a defined outcome, with the content prepared beforehand and someone keeping the pace legible, is expected to produce written turns in which learners negotiate meaning, notice and correct their own errors, and ask questions they would not ask aloud. The expected gains are in participation and interaction during the task, and in short-form written communication; whether they carry to delayed-retention, skill-performance in speaking, or course achievement is a separate question the wiki does not settle.

The relationship is conditional on the task (closed tasks with one outcome drew more negotiation than open ones in one study), on preparation (familiarity with the task content raised negotiation in another), on the mode chosen (in the one study comparing modes, text chat turns were short, about 9 words, and carried a smaller share of negotiated turns than videoconferencing or a discussion board, but chat, unlike the board, produced corrective feedback), and on pace and moderation. No claim in the wiki compares learning with a text chat against the same task without one, or against speaking. The nearest evidence is one descriptive study comparing three computer-mediated modes with the same French biology students, one small quasi-experiment varying preparation before chat tasks, one correlation between chat activity and test scores in a course run with AI agents, and one teacher's redesign of a chat-based feedback task.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Peer Discussion owns the commit-then-discuss relationship for conceptual questions; a chat can be its medium, and its model applies unchanged. Social Presence owns being seen and heard as a person in an online course, including when to add live contact with a chat alongside. Community of Inquiry owns the movement of an online discussion towards resolution. Digital Learning holds that a tool helps only through the method it makes affordable. This page owns the choice and running of the written live channel itself.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it; the durations and group sizes are untested proposals unless a claim is linked.

  1. Name the job the chat does in this lesson. One of: a task conversation (learners must reach an outcome together), a question and example channel during a presentation, a short peer response to work just shown, or brainstorming before writing. Do not open a chat "for discussion" with no outcome. From the earlier page; untested.
  2. Prefer a closed task for task conversations. Give a task with one outcome the group must agree on (choose, rank, solve, decide), rather than an open exchange of opinions. In one study of 12 groups of four second-language biology students, negotiation routines were about 50% more numerous in closed than in open tasks, across chat, board and video (Closed meaning-focused tasks foster more negotiation of meaning and negative feedback than open tasks in CMC [+M]); a second study, whose mode the claim page does not state, found more confirmation checks and clarification requests in unfocused tasks (the row on novices below).
  3. Prepare the content before the chat. Give learners 5–10 minutes with the material the task draws on (a short subtitled video, a text, a worked case) before the chat opens. In one quasi-experiment with 10 non-native-speaker dyads doing four 50-minute tasks in a course chat, dyads given subtitled videos beforehand produced a higher share of negotiated turns than dyads without them (Non-native speaker dyads provided with subtitled videos before tasks produce a significantly higher percentage of negotiated turns in online TBLL than dyads without videos [+M]). The 5–10 minutes is an untested proposal.
  4. Keep groups small and the segment short. Two to four learners per chat (the studies above used dyads and groups of four), 10–20 minutes per task segment, one task per segment; with a whole class in one chat, use it only as a question channel (step 6). Group size from the studies' own groupings; durations untested.
  5. Ask for a specific written move, not "comments". Instead of "give feedback in the chat", ask learners to type what they heard, one question they still have, or one example; make posting anonymous or ungraded where fear of being seen wrong is the barrier. In one blended language class, open chat feedback on a speaking task drew four students; asking them to type notes on what they heard drew more specific and varied posts, and an anonymous two-column whiteboard later engaged both cohorts (In a single-teacher case study, redesigning a peer-feedback task produced more specific and varied student notes, which the authors read as a more engaged class [+M]; one teacher's classes, no comparison group).
  6. For a question channel during a presentation, assign a monitor and stop for it. One person (a co-teacher, teaching assistant or rotating learner) reads the chat, groups questions, and raises them at planned pauses every 10–15 minutes; the presenter answers there rather than reading the chat while speaking. Untested proposal, from the earlier page's backchannel example and its warning that fast chats need moderation; the reason to respond to what the chat shows is carried from one-to-one tutoring (Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M]; most of its evidence is one-to-one, not chat).
  7. Set and show norms before the first chat. Turn-taking (reply to the previous point before starting a new one), one idea per message, how to signal a question, respect and tone. In one case study of a synchronous classroom chat, the discussion followed rules of tool use, institutional rules and rhetorical routines of opening, topical discussion and closing, and participants took recurring roles such as technological leader and socio-emotional facilitator (SCMD activity systems exhibit three categories of rules and four types of participant roles [+M]; descriptive, one course); naming those roles is an untested proposal.
  8. Leave correction mostly to the learner, and prompt it. In chat, almost all corrective moves were learners correcting their own messages unprompted (112 of 119 in one study), read by its author as avoiding loss of face (Unprompted self-correction dominates negative feedback in NNS-NNS CMC interaction, interpreted as a face-saving preference [+M]). After the chat, give each learner the log and ask them to mark and fix two of their own messages against a stated criterion; the review step is an untested proposal, with the reason carried from self-monitoring research (Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M]; reviews of self-regulation, not chat studies).
  9. End with a product outside the chat. Close each chat segment with a short summary, decision or list one learner posts for the group, and use it in the next activity, so the exchange feeds composition or a decision. Untested; from the earlier page's caution against surface participation.

Dose and progression across sessions (untested proposals): over a unit of four to six sessions, start with closed tasks, prepared content and a teacher or assistant monitoring every group; in middle sessions, rotate the monitor and summariser roles among learners and move to tasks with two acceptable outcomes; in late sessions, use open tasks with less preparation and learner-led moderation. Move a group to the next step when its last chat reached an outcome every member contributed to, with the norms kept without reminder. Where the goal is spoken interaction, move the same tasks from chat to audio or video once learners complete them in chat with few clarification requests, because chat turns stay short (row below).

When a condition is not met: if no one can monitor a fast chat, switch to a slower channel (a shared document or a board with a 24-hour reply window) and lose live correction; if typing is the barrier, allow dictation or voice notes in the same channel.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the written exchange is for. Negotiating meaning in a second language, asking questions during a talk, giving peer response, or reasoning at length each need a different task and mode; chat turns are short, so extended reasoning belongs elsewhere. If unstated, ask: what should a learner have done or produced by the end of the chat, and would a spoken or asynchronous exchange do it better?
  • Who can watch the chat, and how many threads it carries. A dyad or a group of four can run itself with a closed task; thirty people in one live chat need a monitor. If unstated, ask: who reads the chat while it runs, how many learners post into one stream, and when will someone respond?
  • Learners' typing, language and access. Keyboard speed, proficiency in the language of the chat, device and bandwidth decide who can keep pace. If unstated, ask: can every learner type short replies in this language at conversation speed, on the device they will use?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is the log: who posted, how often, message length, replies to others, questions asked, clarification requests and confirmation checks, self-corrections, and whether the group reached the task's outcome. "Engaged", "understands" or "too shy to speak" are inferred states. A busy chat shows activity, not learning; a quiet learner may be reading closely.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares learning with a text chat against the same task without one, against speaking, or against an asynchronous board on a learning outcome. The claims below describe what happens in chats and test design features within them.

  • Completing meaning-focused tasks through CMC fosters negotiated interactions in all three modes (discussion board, text chat, videoconferencing) [+M]: 12 groups of four French non-native-speaker biology students completing five English-for-specific-purposes scenarios online (24 chat logs, 24 board files, 24 video files). Negotiation of meaning occurred in every mode. Coded causal · r1, but the evidence entry describes counts across modes with no comparison condition; it shows chat can carry negotiation, not that it improves learning.
  • Videoconferencing was conducive to more negotiation of meaning than text chat and discussion board, measured as proportion of negotiated turns [~M] and Content-related problems trigger most negotiation routines, and syntactic triggers are absent from text chat where turns are short [~M]: the same study. Triggers were 3.4% of turns in video, 1.9% on the board and 0.6% in chat; chat turns averaged 9 words against 55 and 130; no syntactic trigger appeared in chat. They limit chat for spoken-interaction or syntax goals.
  • Discussion board interactions generated no negative feedback at all, suggesting synchrony is required to foster corrective feedback [+M] and Unprompted self-correction dominates negative feedback in NNS-NNS CMC interaction, interpreted as a face-saving preference [+M]: the same study. Chat produced 61 corrective moves (2.7% of turns), video 58 (6.4%), the board none; of 119 in all, 112 were unprompted self-corrections, 6 recasts and 1 explicit correction. The "synchrony is required" reading is the author's suggestion from one comparison.
  • Closed meaning-focused tasks foster more negotiation of meaning and negative feedback than open tasks in CMC [+M]: the same study, six tasks, negotiation routines 102 in closed against 68 in open tasks. Qualified by Unfocused tasks elicit significantly more confirmation checks and clarification requests than focused tasks [~M] (36 learners, a chi-square on strategy counts; the claim page does not say the tasks were done in chat, and "focused/unfocused" is not the same contrast as "closed/open").
  • Non-native speaker dyads provided with subtitled videos before tasks produce a significantly higher percentage of negotiated turns in online TBLL than dyads without videos [+M]: 10 dyads in intact sections randomly assigned, four 50-minute chat tasks; negotiated-turn ratio .30 against .17, reported d = 2.82. The one claim here that varies a design feature of a chat with a comparison group; tiny sample, interaction measure, no learning outcome.
  • In a single-teacher case study, redesigning a peer-feedback task produced more specific and varied student notes, which the authors read as a more engaged class [+M]: one teacher's blended language classes, stimulated recall and recordings; open chat feedback drew four students, typed notes drew more varied posts, an anonymous whiteboard engaged both cohorts. Qualitative, no comparison.
  • In-class chat engagement significantly predicts higher test scores in MAIC courses [~M]: over 100,000 learning records in a course run with LLM-driven agents; message number and length predicted test and exam scores, controlling for earlier module scores. Correlational, and the chat partners were AI agents; more motivated learners may both chat more and score higher.
  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] and Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M]: general claims carried to chat by the earlier page. Self-monitoring rests on a review and a theoretical synthesis. Contingent scaffolding gathers 25 distinct studies in its table, mostly one-to-one tutoring and none of chat; its own Discussion says contingency is scarce in whole-class teaching. They support reviewing one's own messages and responding to what the chat shows, not chat as such.

Learners, tasks and measures differ (French university students in English, intermediate language learners, a large AI-agent course), and four of the chat-specific claims come from one study, so do not rank them. None measures delayed learning.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims bearing on text chat that are not part of the model above, with how far each bears on this page.

  • Student chat questions in the pilot were distributed across seven categories, dominated by study strategies (23%), conceptual quantum (20%), and mathematical formalism (17%) [+W] — cited by the earlier page; questions graduate students typed to an AI tutoring system in a quantum course, descriptive. It suggests a course question channel will draw questions about how to study as well as content, so plan who answers those; it concerns chat with an AI system, not among people.
  • SCMD discourse activity is best understood as an ecology of nested activity systems within and beyond the classroom [+M] — one case study's interpretation: course design, university and tool designers shape a classroom chat. Bears on the page only as context for why norms and tools need setting.
  • A collective unit of subjects emerges when two utterances form a dialogical pair in SCMD [+M] and The dialogical pair of responding and responded utterances, not a single utterance, is the minimum unit of analysis for SCMD research [+M] — the same case study; claims about how to analyse chat logs (read a post with the one it answers). They bear on reading a log, not on design.
  • The change in tools caused the most disruption in e-teachers' activity system, replacing body language and visual cues with text and voice mediation [~M] — online high-school teachers; reported using instant messaging for quick feedback. Bears on the miscommunication constraint: text removes the cues teachers relied on.

(2014) on interactive chat against independent writing, Lin (2015), a meta-synthesis of computer-mediated communication in second-language acquisition, and Heafner & Plaisance (2014) on chat in remote field experiences. No claim page in the wiki records their findings yet, so the model above takes nothing from them; Tare et al. and Lin are the nearest to a test of the relationship and are the first sources to extract.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually stated as participation, interaction practice or a product of the exchange (a decision, a summary, a set of questions answered). Elicit separately what learners want from the chat: to get a question answered without being seen asking it, to practise a language without speaking in front of others, to finish the task quickly, or to stay in touch with a group they rarely see. A learner who values not being seen wrong may post only anonymously or only correct themselves; one who values speed may post fragments that reach an outcome without the language practice the designer intended. Record both, and do not read a lively log as the capability either side wanted.

For example, an adult learning English for work may want to follow and answer messages in their team's chat at work, while the course objective is negotiating meaning in a task. One closed task run in the team's own tool, with a real decision, serves both; a classroom chat on an invented topic serves only the second.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of the same task with and without a chat (or chat against speaking) show no difference in learning, or in the participation of learners who speak least, or if negotiation in chat does not predict later language gains. If open tasks routinely produce as much negotiation as closed ones in chat, drop step 2; if prepared content does not replicate beyond small samples, drop step 3 to a suggestion. If anonymous posting raises participation but lowers the quality of posts, the low-confidence row needs a limit. If studies with children show chat working differently from university students, the age rows need their own evidence. Do not protect the model by calling every quiet chat "a moderation problem" after the fact.

Participation in the chat, the share of negotiated turns, self-correction, unaided writing or speaking afterwards, and course achievement are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no group size, segment length, or best mode for any of them.

Related Principles¶

  • Peer Discussion — text chat is one medium for structured peer exchange.
  • Authentic Audiences & Purposes — chats give learners immediate audiences for communication.
  • Multimodal Instruction — text chat adds a written interaction mode alongside oral or visual channels.
