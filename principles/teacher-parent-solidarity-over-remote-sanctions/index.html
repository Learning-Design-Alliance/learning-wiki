Strengthen ties between teachers and parents rather than pressing for remote sanctions behind teaching¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 27 claims (20 for, 7 mixed) · 22 studies (7 causal, 5 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 associational, 2 qualitative), q1–q4 · 8 of 22 report an effect size · 23 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns how a teacher gets families' cooperation with what happens in class. Margaret Mead (1942, as Monroe 1992 reports her) saw that every teacher action carries emotional and social weight in a community, and that a teacher's departures from what parents expect arouse anxiety in them. She set out two roads: increase the ties of solidarity with parents, sharing in their community life and continually interpreting the teaching to them; or press for more powerful and remote sanctions behind the teaching (read here as the principal, district rules, formal notices, grades and discipline used as leverage). The people a design here acts on are teachers and parents; the learners whose outcomes count are their children, mostly child and adolescent in pre-k-12.

The relationship, stated so it can be checked: for a teacher whose work depends on parents' cooperation (homework, attendance, behaviour, a teaching method parents find unfamiliar), relationship-first contact and regular two-way communication about learning, begun before any problem arises, are expected to produce more teacher–parent trust, more of the involvement that goes with learning, and fewer escalations than relying on rules notices, problem calls and appeals to authority outside the relationship. The expected changes are in trust as both sides report it (self-report), in children's school behaviour and attendance (behavioural), and, more weakly, in general-achievement. The horizon is a school year or more (term-plus).

It is conditional on four things: whether contact starts before a problem (a first call about a problem is itself a remote sanction from the parent's side); whether communication is about learning (what the child is doing and how to help) rather than rules and incidents; whether the family can take part in its own language, channel and time; and whether the teacher has time and guidance from the school to do it. It also depends on the family's starting trust in the school, which differs between schools, between communities and between primary and high school.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as Mead framed it. None compares teachers who build ties with parents against teachers who rely on remote sanctions. What the claims test are parts of it: randomised trials of teacher–parent partnership programmes against controls (relationships and behaviour), a matched-comparison study of teacher home visits before the school year against no visit (discipline, attendance, test scores), correlational meta-analyses of parent involvement and achievement, survey and interview studies of where teacher–parent communication is weak, and a meta-analysis of parent training showing that coaching parents through practice beats giving information. Mead's own argument is an anthropological analysis, not a tested comparison.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

A default a teacher (or a school team) can act on and revise, kept from Mead's two-road framing and filled in from the nearest evidence. Each step's evidence status is beside it.

  1. Make the first contact a relationship contact, before the school year or in its first two weeks. Offer every family one 20–30 minute meeting at home or where the family chooses, with no academic agenda and no problem to report: ask what they want for the child this year, what the child is good at and likes at home, and how and when they prefer to be contacted. Evidence: in DC Public Schools grades 1–5, students whose teacher made a structured relationship-building home visit before the school year were less likely to have a disciplinary incident that year (9.27% against 12.22% of matched non-visited students) (A home visit before the school year reduced the likelihood of a disciplinary incident (effect size = +0.10) [+M]). The meeting length and the "no problem" rule are untested proposals.
  2. Send a positive, specific message about learning to every family in the first month. One or two sentences about something the child did or is learning, before any message about a rule or a problem. Evidence for the need: in Chicago, interviewed high-school parents felt informed about rules but far fewer got enough information about what their child was doing in class or when the child did well (Parents want more academic interaction with high schools, but schools focus communication on rules and problems [+M]). The first-month message as a remedy is untested.
  3. Keep a short two-way message about learning going every one to two weeks. What the class is learning, one question the family can ask the child, one way to help, and an open invitation to reply. Use the channel families answer (a text or app message is more likely to be seen and answered than a voicemail) and the family's language (apps' translation features let Spanish-speaking parents and teachers write to each other directly) (Mobile apps increased timely, direct communication between parents and educators during remote schooling [~W]; Translation features in mobile apps enabled direct communication between educators and parents who speak languages other than English [+W]; both from focus groups, no outcome measured). The interval is an untested proposal.
  4. Interpret the teaching before parents meet it. When you change a method, a grading scheme or a homework routine that differs from what parents knew at school, explain what it is, why you do it and what the child will bring home, before it starts, and invite questions. Mead's "continually interpreting to them"; untested proposal (old page; Monroe 1992).
  5. Coach, do not only inform, when you ask parents to help at home. Once a term, run a 30–45 minute family session in which parents practise one home routine (a reading prompt, a homework check that asks about effort and process) with the teacher's feedback, rather than sending a handout. Carried from parent-training programmes for young children, where having parents practise skills in sessions went with larger effects than conveying information, and coached delivery beat written materials at post-test (Parent Coaching Outperforms Information Only [+M]; carried from child-behaviour programmes to school learning, untested there). Steer the routine away from compliance-pushing ("Do your homework! Now!") towards supporting effort and the process, which a review reports going with better effects (Parental focus on compliance harms homework learning, while supporting effort and process helps [~M]; a white paper's reading of other studies, no effect sizes).
  6. When a problem arises, go to the family before going over their heads. Call or meet, describe what you saw (not a judgement), ask what the family sees at home, and agree one thing each side will do and when you will speak again (one or two weeks). Escalate to the principal or a formal procedure only if two such contacts have not settled it, or at once where policy requires it (safeguarding, legally mandated reports). Untested proposal; this is Mead's choice between the two roads, made at the moment a remote sanction is easiest to reach for.
  7. For a child with persistent behaviour difficulties, use a structured teacher–parent partnership, not a sequence of notices. In two randomised trials, a Teachers and Parents as Partners programme left teachers reporting stronger relationships with parents, and in the rural trial (45 schools, K–3) parents reported stronger relationships with teachers and teachers reported fewer school-related behaviour problems (In a rural randomized trial, TAPP strengthens both teacher-reported and parent-reported family-school relationships [+M]; TAPP improves school-related behavioral problems per teacher ratings in the rural trial (effect size = +0.45) [+M]). The claim pages do not describe the programme's sessions, so adopt it from its own materials rather than from this page.
  8. Plan the shift across the year. Early (first six weeks): the teacher initiates every contact and mostly listens. Middle (through the second term): families set the agenda in drop-in hours or replies, and practise home routines in coached sessions. Late: families see and respond to their children's work (a showcase, a portfolio conference led by the child) and are asked for input on a class decision. Move a family from teacher-initiated to family-led contact once they have replied to or started contact twice; for a family that has not answered three messages, change the channel, language or time, or phone, before concluding they are uninterested. Thresholds and the arc are untested proposals.
  9. Ask the school for time and guidance, and treat this as part of the instructional work. Teachers in remote schooling said they were left on their own with a spreadsheet and no guidance on how to communicate with families (Teachers and caregivers called for additional guidance and support in their communication and collaboration [+M]). In Chicago, the high schools that improved their focus on learning and teacher collaboration were also those whose teachers reported the largest gains in communication with parents (High schools improving instruction and teacher collaboration also showed the largest improvements in parent communication [+M]; associational).

When a condition is not met: if a visit is impossible, hold the first meeting by phone or video at the family's chosen time (Zoom Family Hours describes one format); if the teacher has 150 or more students, start with the families of the students they know least and use a short form for the first message; if the family's trust in the school is already low, expect slower replies and keep contact positive for longer before asking anything of them.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether there is already a problem, and who raised it. A first contact about a problem reads as a sanction whoever makes it; a relationship that already exists can carry a hard conversation. If unstated, ask: has the teacher spoken to this family before, and about what?
  • The family's language, channel, hours and starting trust in the school. A plan built on evening meetings and English email reaches the families who were already reachable. If unstated, ask: in what language and through what channel does this family answer, at what time of day, and how have past contacts with the school gone for them?
  • The teacher's time and the school's backing. A secondary teacher with five classes, or a teacher whose school gives no time for family contact, cannot run a weekly message to every family. If unstated, ask: how many families, how many minutes a week, and does the school expect and support this work?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: whether and how fast a family replies, who starts contact and about what, attendance at sessions, what a parent says about the teaching, and the child's attendance, incidents and work. "Trusts the teacher", "is uninterested" and "is hostile to the school" are inferred states. Silence from a family is an observation, not a judgement.

A parent complains about a teaching method Anxiety at an unfamiliar method (Mead's "teacher deviations"); the child is struggling and the method is blamed; a real mismatch with the family's goals Explain the method and its reason with an example of the child's work; ask what the parent saw at home; check the child's work with the parent.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim compares ties with parents against remote sanctions. The claims below test parts of the relationship.

  • In a rural randomized trial, TAPP strengthens both teacher-reported and parent-reported family-school relationships [+M]: randomised trials of a teacher–parent partnership programme; the evidence entry describes the second trial (45 rural schools, 267 K–3 students, 152 teachers), where teachers (ES +0.46) and parents (ES +0.51) reported stronger relationships than controls. The claim's title and first subclaim speak of both trials (+0.47 for the first), but its evidence entry covers only the rural trial. One study recorded, q3, from an evidence-clearinghouse summary (r?). Outcome: relationship ratings (self-report) and teacher-rated behaviour.
  • ParentCorps improves family engagement measures (Inclusive Leadership, Trust in School Leader, Communication and Trust in Teacher) in a 61-school trial [+M]: a randomised evaluation in 61 New York City elementary schools, 2,242 parents on the city school survey; effect size +0.17 on each of three measures, including communication and trust in the teacher. Small, from the programme's own description. It shows trust in the teacher can be moved by a school programme; it does not say which part moved it.
  • A replication trial with 267 preschoolers found greater gains in social skills and stronger teacher-child and parent-teacher relationships than controls [+M]: cluster randomised, Nebraska preschools; parent–teacher relationships ES +0.36, social skills +0.24 against controls. One study, programme summary.
  • A home visit before the school year reduced the likelihood of a disciplinary incident (effect size = +0.10) [+M]: matched-comparison study of structured relationship-building teacher home visits, DC Public Schools grades 1–5 (9.27% against 12.22% with an incident). The page lists two studies, but both entries report the same Mathematica study (an Evidence for ESSA summary and the REL report). Not randomised: families who accept a visit may differ.
  • Teacher home visits slightly improved student attendance in the following school year (ES = +0.02) [~M] and Home visits improved standardized math scores (ES = +0.11) but showed no difference in ELA scores [~M]: the same study; attendance 95.28% against 94.93%, maths +0.11, no ELA difference. These limit the model: one relationship-building contact moves attendance and achievement little, so the default plans it as a start, not a remedy.
  • Parent involvement is associated with higher academic achievement in meta-analyses of mostly correlational studies [+M]: three meta-analyses (50 middle-school studies, r = .18 overall; 37 K–12 studies; 41 urban elementary studies, about 0.7–0.75 SD). Academic socialisation and communication about school go with achievement most; help with homework was negatively associated at middle school. Correlational: selection and reverse causation are live, and intervention studies show smaller effects. It supports making contact about learning, not about compliance.
  • Schools with high relational trust showed marked gains in student learning while schools with weak trust relations saw virtually no improvement in reading or mathematics scores [+M] and In schools characterized by high relational trust, educators were more likely to experiment with new practices and work together with parents to advance improvements [+M]: Bryk and Schneider's three-year study of 12 Chicago elementary schools, associational; no statistics printed on the claim pages. Trust goes with improvement; the direction is not shown.
  • Home visits are rarely offered and rated lowest of any engagement resource, with Black, Latino, and multilingual parents rating them less favorably [~M]: EdTrust surveys; only 10% of parents said their school offered home visits, and Black, Latino and multilingual parents with access were less likely to say they were done well. A qualification: the contact meant to build ties can be experienced as intrusion, so the default asks families first.
  • Current ideas about the home/school relation and parent involvement may inhibit justice, equity, and excellence in education [~W]: a narrative review that poses this as a question to investigate, not a finding. It warns that "solidarity" defined on the school's terms can become another way of governing families.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (preschool to high school, US cities and rural districts, relationship ratings, incidents, test scores), so do not rank them by effect label.

The evidence by question¶

  • Is closer parent involvement in a child's schooling associated with better student outcomes, and does the form of involvement matter? Parent involvement is associated with higher academic achievement in meta-analyses of mostly correlational studies [+M]
  • Do teachers' visits to and learning about students' homes change how educators read their students, and does it reach the students? Teachers who took part in funds of knowledge household research came to view their minority students as competent and raised their expectations of them. [+W]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before the rewrite, and others found while rewriting it, that are not part of the model above. Each says how far it bears on the page.

  • Meta-analyses find parental involvement associated with better student outcomes, effect sizes 0.2 to 0.75 standard deviations [+W] — cited by the old page; a policy review (Oakes et al. 2017) summarising meta-analyses of parent involvement, correlational; overlaps the three-meta-analysis claim used above and bears on the outcome, not on the teacher's choice of road.
  • Ninth-grade teachers report less communication with and academic support for parents than eighth-grade teachers [+W] — cited by the old page; Chicago teacher surveys, used in the high-school row; shows where ties weaken, not what restores them.
  • Mobile apps increased timely, direct communication between parents and educators during remote schooling [~W] — cited by the old page; focus groups during remote schooling, used in step 3 and the phone row; perception only.
  • Chicago high school teachers reported better communication and more positive relationships with parents in 1997 than in 1994 [+M] — teacher surveys; communication rose in 24 of 39 high schools in three years. Shows the relationship can change at school scale; no cause identified.
  • Teacher-parent interaction and trust are lower in bottom-quartile schools, and even top-rated schools show limited very strong trust [+M] — Chicago 2005 teacher survey; describes the starting state, which differs between schools.
  • Family engagement in schools is closely linked to improved student academic achievement, social skills, and fewer behavior issues [+M] — a principal playbook's research summary citing no specific study; general background only.
  • A field guide asserts that authentic family–school partnerships benefit student achievement and argues that without staff training and coaching the school–family divide persists [+W] — a field guide's assertion and recommendation, q1; consistent with step 9, not evidence for it. Its title appears to say the opposite of its subclaim (the subclaim says the divide persists without training).
  • Teachers who took part in funds of knowledge household research came to view their minority students as competent and raised their expectations of them. [+W] and Home visits in funds of knowledge research led teachers to see culture as a dynamic process rather than only dances, food and folklore. [+W] — an ERIC digest's qualitative report of teacher change through household visits, q1; bears on what visits do to the teacher, which Developing Your Cultural Awareness owns. Its studies table counts 3 distinct studies, but two of the rows are the same Mathematica home-visit study reported twice.
  • TAPP improves school-related behavioral problems per teacher ratings in the rural trial (effect size = +0.45) [+M] — used in step 7; teacher-rated, so not independent of the teacher's view of the relationship.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a working relationship between teacher and family that carries the child's learning: trust on both sides, contact about learning, cooperation on attendance and behaviour without escalation. Elicit separately what each party values. Parents interviewed in Chicago high schools most wanted teachers to tell them what their child needed to do well, and many wanted to know how to help with schoolwork (Parents want more academic interaction with high schools, but schools focus communication on rules and problems [+M]); a family may also value being left alone, privacy, or respect for its own ways of raising the child. The child may value not having home and school talk about them at all. Agreement and divergence are both observations.

For example, a teacher wants a ninth-grader's family to enforce homework; the family wants to know whether their son is on track for college. A rules notice serves the teacher's aim and misses the family's. A call that says what he needs to do well in this course, and asks what would help at home, serves both and gives the teacher something to report on next time.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of relationship-first teacher contact against usual practice, with comparable families and outcomes rated by parents as well as teachers, show no gain in trust or behaviour, or gains that vanish within a year. If school-wide accountability or firm rules enforcement produce the same cooperation with no loss of trust, Mead's choice is less of a trade-off than the page assumes. If home visits and similar contacts help only families who already trust the school, the default must lead with something else for the families it is meant for. If partnership programmes work through their structured content rather than through the relationship, the page should recommend the content. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough contact" after the fact.

Trust as rated by teachers, trust as rated by parents, children's behaviour, attendance and achievement are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose of contact, no best channel, and no comparison with sanctions.
