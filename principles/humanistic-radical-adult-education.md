# Adopt humanistic and radical approaches so adult learners develop as individuals and can question social injustice

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 14 claims (10 for, 4 mixed) · 10 studies (4 theoretical, 2 associational, 2 qualitative, 2 design), `q1`–`q3` · 1 of 10 report an effect size · 14 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns the **humanistic and radical stance** in teaching adults: an educator who treats each learner's view as worth hearing and answering even when it differs from the educator's own, who brings social events that touch learners' lives into the room as material to question, and who leaves learners free to imagine alternatives and decide whether and how to act. For `adult` learners (and, carried from the studies below, `adolescent` learners) whose usual instruction gives them an authoritative account to accept, a course run this way is expected to change three things: learners' sense of themselves as contributors whose thinking counts (`motivation-affect`, self-reported empowerment or peer validation); their accounts of social problems, moving from individual blame or personal concern towards systemic explanation (`conceptual-understanding` of a social `principle`); and their stated readiness to take part in civic or collective action (`other`: civic commitment). The knowledge type is mostly `attitude-motivation` and `principle`; the setting is a `classroom` or community group over `days-weeks` or `term-plus`. Ethical development and "becoming fully human", which the old page names as the goal, are not measured by anything recorded here.

The relationship is conditional on whether the educator can actually hear and answer views unlike their own (rather than steering discussion to a predetermined critique), on how safe it is in this group to voice a minority or personal view, on whether discussion is tied to a reading, case or task that gives learners something to reason from, on who in the group the social issue directly touches, and on whether action is the learner's choice rather than a course requirement.

**No claim in the wiki compares a humanistic or radical approach with the same course taught another way.** What is recorded is one author's first-person reflection on her own adult classes, two single-site design studies with school students and no comparison condition, one teacher's reflective report on a university assignment, and one longitudinal survey of youth groups in which *how often* members discussed a social issue did not predict change in their critical reflection or sociopolitical efficacy. That survey is the only evidence here with controls, and it limits the expectation rather than supporting it. The default design below is therefore a set of labelled proposals.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete as a default a designer can run and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. The default assumes an adult course of about ten to twelve weekly sessions of 90 minutes (an untested proposal; a shorter fit is in the table further down).

1. **Open by establishing that disagreement is welcome and how it will be handled (session 1, about 30 minutes).** Agree with the group how a view will be voiced and answered (each view is restated before it is challenged; challenges go to the idea, not the person). The educator says where they stand and that a different view will not cost a learner marks. Ask each learner what they want from the course and which social questions matter to them; keep the list. From the old page ("educator ability to learn from students and value differing opinions"; "self-reflexivity about the author's positionality"); untested proposal. A single author's reflection reports feeling empowered by discussions in which views were "spoken and addressed": [One adult educator's first-person reflection reports that facilitated class discussions left her feeling empowered as an individual contributor](../claims/facilitated-self-directed-learning-empowers-adults.md) [+W].
2. **Run each session as starting point, source, discussion, written stance (about 10, 30, 35 and 15 minutes).** Begin from a learner's own experience of the issue; give one reading, case or data set about a social event; hold a facilitated discussion in which every view raised is addressed before the group moves on; close with a short private written stance that answers "what would I need to know before accepting the view I disagreed with most?". The old page's "willingness to think about a situation before accepting another point of view"; the timings are untested proposals. Two single-site design studies built units on this kind of reading–talking–writing cycle and report students moving from personal to systemic accounts: [Positioning personal experience as an epistemic resource supports expansion from inward to outward critical orientations](../claims/positioning-personal-experience-as-epistemic-resource-supports-critical-orientation-expansion.md) [+M] (69 students in two 12th-grade English classes, about 50 sessions over three months) and [Critical speculative design pedagogy supports critical consciousness and justice-oriented sensemaking in science](../claims/critical-speculative-design-supports-critical-consciousness-in-science.md) [+M] (8th graders in physics; most detail from two focal students). Neither had a comparison condition, and both are carried here from school students to adults.
3. **Do not count on frequency of discussion alone.** Tie each discussion to a source and a written stance rather than adding more open discussion time. Basis: in a two-wave survey of youth in school gender and sexuality alliances, how often members discussed immigration did not predict change in their critical reflection or sociopolitical efficacy, individually or as a group: [Immigration discussion frequency shows no significant association with critical reflection or sociopolitical efficacy](../claims/immigration-discussion-null-critical-consciousness.md) [~M]; [Collective GSA-level immigration discussion frequency is not associated with group-level gains in any empowerment or critical consciousness outcome](../claims/gsa-collective-discussion-null-group-level.md) [~M]. A null association is not evidence of no effect, and equivalence was not tested.
4. **Hand over the choice of questions across the term.** Sessions 1–3: the educator chooses the social event and frames the question. Sessions 4–7: learners bring events from their own lives or news and the educator helps frame them. Sessions 8 onwards: learners pose the questions and lead parts of the discussion. A learner is ready to frame a question for the group when their written stance has named a cause beyond individual choice in two sessions running. The arc, the session numbers and the criterion are untested proposals; the direction (teacher-structured to learner-directed) is from the old page's "radical, self-directed learning".
5. **Ask learners to imagine an alternative, then examine its assumptions (one or two sessions, middle to late in the term).** Set a speculative piece (a short fiction, a manifesto, a eulogy for something lost) describing a more just situation, and require a reflexive note: more just for whom, and does the solution create new problems? Then hold a dialogue that examines what the imagined futures rely on; the old page reports that futures not relying on violence, discipline or "an imagined community of ecological do-gooders" are hard for students to imagine, and that ecofascism-adjacent ideals occasionally emerge. Untested proposal, from the folded page on ecologically just futurity and the [speculative theory-fiction assignment](../strategies/speculative-theory-fiction-assignment.md). The nearest recorded evidence is about teachers, not learners: in 50 teacher essays on AI in inclusive education, the commonest markers were the future becoming more imaginable and awareness that present actions shape it ([Teachers perceived AI-supported futures as increasingly imaginable and linked to present actions](../claims/approaching-markers-future-attainability.md) [+W]).
6. **Where the course concerns environment or ecology, widen who counts.** One session in which learners eulogise an extinct or threatened species, place or way of life and the group mourns it together, followed by discussion of which beings and places the course's own language treats as resources. One teacher's reflective report, no measures: [Collectively mourning extinct or near-extinct more-than-human entities facilitates recognition of the more-than-human as co-participants in shared reality](../claims/species-eulogy-fosters-more-than-human-recognition.md) [+W]. From the folded page on expanding critical communication pedagogy's concern; untested as a step.
7. **Let action be the learner's decision, and let some work outlive the course (final two or three sessions).** Offer, without requiring, a product addressed to people outside the class: a letter, a resource others can reuse, a proposal to a local body. Do not grade whether a learner took action or which side they took; grade the reasoning in the written stances. One conceptual article argues that a "renewable" assignment, in which students write quiz questions for an open textbook, reaches beyond the classroom and has an economic-justice effect by supporting free materials: [Renewable assignments such as student-created quiz questions transcend classroom boundaries and can ameliorate economic injustice](../claims/renewable-assignments-economic-justice.md) [+W]. In a longitudinal survey of Chicago high-school students, service learning and in-class civic learning opportunities were the factors most strongly related to civic commitment two years later: [Service learning and in-class civic learning opportunities are more strongly associated with high school students' later commitment to civic participation than the other school, family and community factors studied](../claims/service-learning-and-in-class-civic-learning-strongest-predictors-civic-commitment.md) [+M] (associational, adolescents).
8. **Keep a log of when the educator steered.** After each session, note where the educator pushed a view, closed a line of discussion or rewarded agreement. Review it at mid-term with the group. Untested proposal; it answers the old page's requirement of self-reflexivity and the caution, from a theoretical argument, that work done "in the name of empowerment" can disempower the people it claims to serve ([Narrative and storying genres in educational inquiry risk disempowering the very people they claim to empower](../claims/narrative-genres-may-disempower-while-claiming-empowerment.md) [~W]).

When a condition is not met: if the educator cannot or will not have their own view challenged, run steps 2 and 7 only, with sources chosen to present more than one position, and say so to learners; if the group contains people directly affected by the issue and others not, see the table rows on affected learners and on private material.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Who in the group the social issue touches, and how safe it is to say so.** A discussion that is identity work for some learners is an academic exercise for others, and what it does may differ between them. If unstated, ask: is anyone here directly affected by the issues we will discuss, and can they choose how much of that to share?
- **What the course is for, and whether it is the learners' choice to be there.** A voluntary community course can make social questioning its goal; a mandatory workplace or credentialing course has a set target and learners whose employer or assessor holds power over them. If unstated, ask: what must learners be able to do at the end, who decides that, and did they choose to come?
- **How much time and facilitation there is.** Hearing and answering each view takes facilitator time; the evidence here comes from units lasting months. If unstated, ask: how many sessions, how long each, and how many learners per facilitator?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what learners say and write under stated conditions: who speaks, whose view is restated and answered, what explanation a written stance gives for a social problem (individual, structural, or both), what sources it cites, and whether a learner chooses to act. "Empowered", "critically conscious" and "has become more fully human" are inferred states, and a learner who has learned what the educator wants to hear will produce the observable signs of all three.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Written stances shift to systemic explanations over the term | Real change in understanding; learning the educator's preferred vocabulary; the sources chosen made one account easy | Ask for a stance on a new issue the course did not cover, from a source the educator did not choose; ask what evidence would change their view. |
| A learner stops speaking in discussion after the first few sessions | Their view was answered harshly or not at all; the issue touches them and sharing feels unsafe; they prefer to think in writing | Read their private stances; offer a paired or written route; ask privately, with no pressure to explain. |
| A learner always agrees with the educator | Genuine agreement; reading the educator's position as the graded answer; low confidence | Present a sourced position the educator disagrees with and ask for the strongest case for it; check the steering log. |
| An imagined future relies on coercion or exclusion | The learner has not met alternatives; the task framing invited a heroic solution; a sincere political view | Ask "just for whom?" and "who enforces it?"; set one compared case beside it; record the response without grading the view. |
| No learner takes any action at the end | The issue does not matter to them; action was framed as risky; time or power to act is absent | Ask what would have to be true for them to act, and whether they acted elsewhere; do not read absence of action as absence of learning. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself a prompt to think and changes what it measures, so record it, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one that suits the course.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares a humanistic or radical approach with the same content taught another way, and none measures ethical development or "becoming fully human". The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

- [One adult educator's first-person reflection reports that facilitated class discussions left her feeling empowered as an individual contributor](../claims/facilitated-self-directed-learning-empowers-adults.md) [+W]: a first-person case reflection (Ellis 2012) from the author's adult education classes; no measures, only that discussions in which views were "spoken and addressed" left the author feeling empowered. The title generalises from one author's experience to adult learners. It is the only adult evidence on this page.
- [Positioning personal experience as an epistemic resource supports expansion from inward to outward critical orientations](../claims/positioning-personal-experience-as-epistemic-resource-supports-critical-orientation-expansion.md) [+M]: a social design-based study in two 12th-grade English classes (69 students, about 50 sessions over three months, a US charter school); essays, discussions, journals and pre/post surveys indicated students moving from personal concerns to systemic critiques of algorithmic bias. Single site, no comparison condition, teacher-researchers were the designers; `adolescent`, `classroom`, `term-plus`.
- [Critical speculative design pedagogy supports critical consciousness and justice-oriented sensemaking in science](../claims/critical-speculative-design-supports-critical-consciousness-in-science.md) [+M]: an ethnographic case study of 158 8th-grade students in two middle schools, 13 with enough data for pathway analysis and two focal students analysed in depth; the focal students moved from disengagement to systemic accounts of injustice and produced speculative artefacts. No comparison and no quantified outcome; the `i2` code reflects documented qualitative patterns, not an effect size.
- [Immigration discussion frequency shows no significant association with critical reflection or sociopolitical efficacy](../claims/immigration-discussion-null-critical-consciousness.md) [~M] and [Collective GSA-level immigration discussion frequency is not associated with group-level gains in any empowerment or critical consciousness outcome](../claims/gsa-collective-discussion-null-group-level.md) [~M]: one two-wave survey of youth in 38 school gender and sexuality alliances (580 youth, per the sibling claims); discussion frequency did not predict residualized change in critical reflection or sociopolitical efficacy, and group-level frequency predicted no group-level gain. **This limits the model**: talking about a social issue more often was not, here, followed by more critical reflection. It is associational, measures frequency rather than a designed approach, and did not test equivalence.
- [More frequent immigration discussion predicts increased hope for immigrant-origin GSA members but not non-immigrant-origin members](../claims/immigration-discussion-hope-immigrant-origin-only.md) [~M] and [Discussing immigration more frequently than fellow GSA members predicts increased perceived peer validation by year's end](../claims/immigration-discussion-in-gsas-predicts-peer-validation.md) [+M]: the same survey; discussion went with more hope only for youth the issue directly touched, and with more perceived peer validation for those who discussed it more than their group. It qualifies the model: what discussion does may depend on who the issue is about, and the benefit seen was in hope and validation rather than critical reflection.
- [Service learning and in-class civic learning opportunities are more strongly associated with high school students' later commitment to civic participation than the other school, family and community factors studied](../claims/service-learning-and-in-class-civic-learning-strongest-predictors-civic-commitment.md) [+M]: a longitudinal survey of 3,805 students in 47 Chicago high schools, controlling for prior commitment. Associational; adolescents; bears on the "take action" end of the model.
- [Narrative and storying genres in educational inquiry risk disempowering the very people they claim to empower](../claims/narrative-genres-may-disempower-while-claiming-empowerment.md) [~W]: a theoretical argument (Goodson 1994) about research genres, carried here to teaching as a caution; no data.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually stated in the course's terms: learners who can explain a social problem from sources, weigh views unlike their own, and decide on a response. Elicit separately what each learner wants: a qualification, a community, to be heard, to understand something that has happened to them, to change something specific. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who came for a qualification may experience social questioning as an imposition; one who came to change something may find source-reading slow. The humanistic stance itself requires taking the learner's goal seriously, so record both and let the learner's own questions (step 1) set later topics where the course allows.

For example, an adult in an evening course on community organising may want to win a specific dispute with a landlord, while the designer's objective is a systemic account of housing policy. A written stance on housing policy serves the designer's objective; helping the learner draft the letter they actually want to send, and then asking what the policy reading changed in it, tests the learner's. Record both, so that a well-argued essay is not read as the capability the learner came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of courses run this way against the same content taught with an authoritative account, with comparable adult learners and measures of critical reflection or civic action not tied to the course's own vocabulary, show no difference, or show gains only in self-report. If, as in the youth survey here, more discussion is not followed by more critical reflection, while structured source-reading and writing are, the model should centre the source and the written stance and treat discussion as the means of hearing views rather than the active ingredient. If learners the issue directly touches gain and others do not (or the reverse), the model should become two models. If learners' stances converge on the educator's position more than on any source, the change is compliance, and the steering log should become a required step. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "not radical enough" after the fact.

Feeling heard, voicing a view, explaining a problem systemically, imagining an alternative and taking action are separate claims, and a course may produce one without the others. The present evidence establishes no dose, no session length and no sequence for any of them.
