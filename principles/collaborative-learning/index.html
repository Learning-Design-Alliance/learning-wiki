Collaborative Learning¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 39 claims (16 for, 21 mixed, 2 against) · 45 studies (10 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 7 theoretical, 6 review, 6 associational, 5 qualitative, 3 design), q1–q4 · 7 of 45 report an effect size · 33 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For learners who can already take part in the target task, and for goals that gain from explaining, comparing and reconciling ideas (concepts, principles and complex skills more than verbal associations), working jointly on a task, so that each member has to engage with and respond to the others' reasoning, is expected to raise individually measured understanding compared with working alone on the same task. Co-presence is not this relationship. The expectation attaches to what the joint work makes learners do, and it is weakened by three things: an individual measure that the group product stands in for, equal or enthusiastic participation that does not change each member's own understanding, and a delayed check on which shared understanding has not held. The synthesis evidence comes from school and undergraduate classrooms, on general or course achievement, at the end of multi-week programmes. It does not forecast a single session, an adult workplace team, an online group or a particular learner.

Cooperative learning sits inside this relationship. It is its most structured form, in which positive interdependence and individual accountability are built in, and its conditional model concerns that structure against individualistic and competitive arrangements. This page owns the broader question of when joint work of any structure changes what each learner can do, and what to observe when it does not. The collaborative learning pattern describes a reusable design. It has not yet been rewritten against this model.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: is the target each member's own understanding, a shared product, or collaboration itself?, is the group work synchronous in a room or asynchronous online?, and is this one session or recurring work over several weeks?

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record what happens under stated conditions: the task and whether one person could complete it alone, group size and composition, any roles or scripts, the talk and actions the group produced, and each member's individual response before and after. A group product, a group's report of its own success and a member's satisfaction are observations about the group or about feelings. None of them is an observation of any member's understanding. "Co-constructs", "is engaged", "agrees" and "understands" are inferences. Equal turn-taking can be seen. Whether it changed anyone's thinking cannot.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
The group reaches a correct shared explanation; some members later revert to their earlier idea Shared understanding was carried by one member; the member followed the talk but did not reconstruct it personally; the later probe is worded differently from the group task Ask each member individually, straight after the group task and again later, for the explanation on matched and changed wording; record who proposed each step in the group.
Talk is balanced and on task, but individual gains are uneven Participation was equal but not about the same reasoning; members differed in prerequisite knowledge; one member's gain came from explaining, another's loss from deferring Compare each member's pre- and post-task individual response; probe prerequisite knowledge individually; note who explained and who accepted explanations.
Members report a satisfying, successful collaboration Aligned perceptions of the task; real coordinated regulation; general goodwill that inflates every self-report; learning that a knowledge check would also show Set an individual knowledge check beside the self-report; ask each member separately what problems the group met and how it dealt with them.
A member notices the group disagrees but the disagreement is never taken up Uncertainty how to raise it; lower status in the group; no time or task structure for resolving it; the disagreement does not matter to the task Ask the member privately what they would have said; give the group a prompt that requires the disagreement to be stated and settled, and see whether it is. Noticing alone was not associated with better regulation or knowledge (Awareness of heterogeneous problem perceptions does not by itself predict successful regulation [~M]).
A group of capable members fails the task Proposals were dismissed or ignored rather than discussed; one member's idea won on status rather than reasons; the task was beyond the group Record whether each proposal was discussed, by whom, and why it was accepted or dropped (High-performing groups often failed without collaborating [~M]); on an open task, see whether influence followed reasons or dominance (Well-defined tasks support shared creative influence [~M]).
Individual scores rise after collaborative work Peer explanation and comparison; the individual checks and feedback themselves; more time on task; the teacher's monitoring Only a comparison holding the task, checks, time and teaching constant can credit the joint work. Record all of them.

These rows are proposals and have not been tested with learners. A single probe shifts confidence among explanations; it does not identify a cause, and the probe itself is a learning exposure.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Structured collaboration against individualistic instruction [+M]: a meta-analysis of 148 studies of early adolescents associates cooperative goal structures with higher achievement than competitive or individualistic ones (direction only; the abstract gave no magnitude). A meta-analysis of 39 North American undergraduate STEM classroom studies finds small-group work raised achievement over instruction without group work (d = 0.51), with smaller effects where the investigator did not teach (0.41), in single-group pre/post designs (0.30) and a non-significant 0.21 at two-year colleges. Partly checked (1 of 2 entries pass, abstract). The claim page reads it as about structured collaboration, but the undergraduate synthesis compared group work with no group work, not structured with unstructured. It overlaps heavily with the evidence under cooperative learning and does not separate which feature of the joint work matters.
  • Collaborative against individual concept mapping [+W]: an article on the ICAP framework reports, second-hand, a study in which concept mapping with a peer enhanced learning more than concept mapping alone. This is the comparison this page states, with the task held constant. The study's learners, design, outcome and horizon are not reported on the claim page, and it has not been checked against its source.
  • Structuring interdependence in asynchronous online groups [~M]: in a randomised experiment, triads given role, reward, role-plus-reward or no structured interdependence did not differ significantly on an individual posttest (about 71% overall; no effect size printed). Not yet checked against its source. A non-significant difference does not establish equivalence, and the study has no individual-work comparison. Note that the claim's title says adult reentry students while its evidence entry says 280 undergraduate business majors. It predicts that adding a structure to online group work need not change achievement; it does not test collaboration against individual work.
  • Aligned problem perceptions and regulation in online groups [~M]: in a cross-sectional path-modelling study of 311 pre-service teachers in 112 online groups on a single case-analysis task, groups whose members saw their collaboration problems similarly reported higher satisfaction, perceived regulation success and subjective learning gain (β = .31–.57), but homogeneity was unrelated to objectively measured knowledge. There was no pretest, and all the associated outcomes are self-reports. Not yet checked against its source. It limits the model: how well a group feels it worked is not evidence of what its members learned.
  • Personal construction alongside co-construction [~M]: across seven case studies of student dyads in computer-based force-and-motion lessons (learners not described on the claim page), conceptual change visible in the pairs' talk often did not hold when students were probed later. The authors report that it was stable only when shared construction was accompanied by each student's personal sense-making. This is qualitative, with no comparison condition, and it has been checked by the judge (full text). It predicts that the group's talk overstates each member's delayed understanding; it does not say how to bring about personal construction.
  • Equal, mutual engagement and individual change [~M]: from the same study, in two dyads whose on-task engagement was high in equality and mutuality, one student changed substantially and the other did not. Two case dyads, with no effect size, and not yet checked against its source. It predicts that balanced participation is not a sufficient observation of each member's learning.
  • Collaborative problem solving and critical thinking [+M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 79 effects from 36 experimental and quasi-experimental studies (2000–2021) reports ES = 0.82 (95% CI 0.69–0.95) on students' critical thinking, with very high heterogeneity (I² = 86%). The claim page does not say what the comparison conditions were or how critical thinking was measured, and the synthesis is coded r2. Its group-size subgroups [~M] were ES 0.99 for 2–3 members (17 effects), 0.71 for 4–6 (14), 0.42 for 7–10 (7) and 0.48 above ten (10), with 31 effects not reporting group size. These are between-study subgroups, not a manipulated group size, so they suggest rather than establish that smaller groups gain more.
  • Which activities a collaboration script targets [~M]: in a meta-analysis of computer-supported collaboration scripts, scripts prompting coordination alone (k = 3, g = 0.46) or negotiation with information sharing (k = 4, g = 0.43) raised domain learning, information sharing alone was marginal (g = 0.34), and three-way combinations were smaller (g = 0.20). The cells rest on three or four studies each. It qualifies the model's structure: more scripted activity is not more learning.
  • Peer interaction added to extensive reading [+M]: a conference paper reports, second-hand, a pretest-posttest study of 415 US fourth graders randomly assigned to four conditions, in which reading with peer interaction gave larger reading-achievement gains than reading without it, no extensive reading, or reading with teacher conferences; no effect size is printed and it rests on an ERIC document. It is one of the few entries here that holds the task constant and varies only whether learners engage with each other. The same author cautions that peer activities do not help students who have not read [~W].
  • Awareness of disagreement in online groups [~M]: in the 136 members of groups whose problem perceptions differed (from the 311-member study above), recognising the difference was not associated with satisfaction, perceived regulation, subjective learning or measured knowledge; a case example shows a member who saw the disagreement but did not know how to raise it. Associational, r?. It predicts that the group needs a structure for settling disagreement, not only for surfacing it.
  • Process evidence from computer-mediated pairs [+M]: in a 2×2 experiment with 80 students in dyads on a jigsaw explanation task, showing each partner's gaze increased information pooling (ηp² = .55). The claim page reports the process outcome only, not learning. A log study of 13 adult cases (real-time collaborative programming [+M]) found two-way revision loops and joint interface work in shared editing that individual programming did not show; it reports no learning outcome either. Both show that a tool can change how members coordinate; neither shows that it changes what each member can do.
  • Who reasons when an AI is a partner [~M]: across 2,831 dialogue turns of students solving problems with AI agents, the agents produced significantly more reasoning, coordination and agreement moves and the students more invitations, connections and shared regulation (associational, r1, no learning outcome). It qualifies the AI row above: if the AI does the reasoning, the joint work this page credits may not be happening in the learners.
  • Naming perceptual insights during joint construction [+W]: a microgenetic case study of four graduate students building an icosahedron found that insights one member named in speech, gesture and material action became shared terms the group later used to reason formally. A single case, with no comparison, from a JLS article; a mechanism to look for, not evidence of an effect.

Keep each study's learners, task, comparator, setting, duration and outcome when transporting these observations. A pooled d is a programme-level average, not a learner's probability of benefit. Self-reported learning and objectively measured knowledge are different outcomes and should not be read for each other.

The evidence by question¶

Each question below links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it into one table, one row per distinct study, with each study's design and effect as recorded and no averaged verdict (eval/synthesis/collaborative-learning.json, scripts/synthesize_claim.py). The count is of distinct studies across the claim and the narrower claims that bear on it.

  • Does working jointly on a task raise achievement over working alone or instruction without group work? Collaborative Learning Improves Outcomes [+S]: 20 distinct studies.
  • Does what the group achieves together show what each member has learned? Co-construction of shared understanding needed to be accompanied by students' personal construction and sense making for conceptual change to be stable over time [~M]: 3 distinct studies.
  • What makes joint work fail: the task, group size and composition, or how members take part? Collaborative situations often fail to be productive in practice, so orchestrating collaboration is not simple [~W]: 12 distinct studies.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cites that do not change the model above. Each was read against it on 2026-10-19 (the claims linked since the rewrite, including those from the journal sweep, were read the same day and those that bear on the model moved into it); each note says why it stays here.

  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] — read 2026-10-19: its studies prompt individual learners, not groups; a candidate mechanism (explaining) for why joint work helps, but it does not compare joint with individual work.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M] — read 2026-10-19: about tutor-to-learner support, not peer collaboration; bears on how a teacher monitors groups, not on the model.
  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] — read 2026-10-19: individual self-regulation theory; no group comparison.
  • Excessive dependence on AI may reduce human interaction and dehumanize adult education [+W] — read 2026-10-19: a policy discussion with no data; consistent with the AI rows above, but changes nothing.
  • Dialogue underpins networked learning: thinking in networks means thinking through collaborative dialogue [+W] — read 2026-10-19: a theoretical position, no test.
  • Meanings Grow Out Of Social Intercourse [+W] — read 2026-10-19: Mead's philosophical position as reconstructed in a conference paper; background for the relationship, not evidence for it.
  • The article advances a five-part rationale for benefits of adding a peer element to extensive reading [~W] — read 2026-10-19: the author's argument for the extensive-reading row (peers as models, sources, tutors and audience), with no new data.
  • A 1994 adult-literacy practice guide asserts, citing no specific study, that primary and secondary students learn more through non-competitive collaborative group work than in individualized competitive classrooms [+W] — read 2026-10-19: an uncited assertion whose extension to adults the guide itself calls untested; the syntheses above carry the same point with evidence.
  • Transition network analysis shows the chatbot's prompts and explanations structured the interaction while participants generated their own interpretations [~W] — read 2026-10-19: one user at a time with a chatbot on institutional analytics, not learners working together (not about this page).
  • AI agent comment patterns include validation (22%) at rates comparable to human peer learning communities, with validation-before-extension sequences structurally resembling collaborative knowledge building [~W] — read 2026-10-19: AI agents commenting to each other, with no human learners (not about this page).
  • GenAI feedback limitations require students to exercise evaluative judgement [+W] — read 2026-10-19: about individual students judging AI feedback (not about this page).
  • Agentic AI invalidates the three assumptions (evaluation, reversibility, control) on which existing AI literacy frameworks rest [-W] — read 2026-10-19: AI literacy theory (not about this page).
  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [~S] — read 2026-10-19: individual working memory research (not about this page).

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what each learner wants from working with others and why, separately from the designer's objective. A learner may value hearing other views, being heard, working with friends, finishing the shared product or avoiding the exposure of working alone. The designer may want each member to be able to explain the idea independently a week later. These can diverge without anyone noticing, because a pleasant, well-coordinated group can satisfy the learner's goal and still leave the designer's unmet. Record agreement or divergence as an observation, and negotiate the task, the individual checks or the group's purpose when they conflict.

For example, a secondary-school physics student working in a pair at a simulation may value agreeing with her partner and producing a shared answer the teacher accepts. The designer wants each student to predict, a fortnight later and alone, how a moving object behaves when the force on it changes. The pair's agreed answer cannot show that the objective was met. A short individual prediction, agreed in advance, given straight after the task and again later, can. Keeping both aims in view also tells the designer whether the student's own goal is being met.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners doing a task jointly fail to outperform matched learners doing the same task alone, on individually measured, independent outcomes at the horizon that matters. If gains turn out to be carried by the individual checks, feedback, extra time or teacher monitoring rather than by learners engaging with each other's reasoning, the model should credit those components instead. If group self-reports and individual knowledge keep coming apart, as the online-group study above found, treat self-reports as observations of the group's process only. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as "not real collaboration" after the fact; record the task and structure in advance.

Group performance during the task, individual understanding straight after it, delayed retention, collaboration skill and satisfaction are separate claims. The present evidence in the wiki does not establish an optimal group size (the one synthesis that breaks effects down by size does so across studies, not by manipulating it), composition or script (the script meta-analysis rests on three or four studies per combination), whether coordination tools such as shared gaze change learning rather than process, effects for adult workplace teams or online groups in general, or benefit for every learner in a group.

Examples¶

  • Individual assessment during pair work: rotating roles, separate predictions and explanations, and independently-demonstrated ratings per outcome
  • Workshop arc: oral conversation, then imagistic, then somatic, ending in ritual performance
  • Co-Creating Classroom Norms
  • Seminar-style text discussion with facilitator prompts and synthesis.
  • Structured peer discussion after a pre-reading question.
  • Online forum discussions tied to a case, reading, or weekly question.
  • Small-group problem discussion before whole-class synthesis.
  • Professional learning groups discussing a shared scenario or decision point.
  • Use heterogeneous-pair CPS in which students first generate individual solutions then revise them after collaboration

Illustrative¶

Collaborative Learning — A shared-work pattern in which learners are jointly responsible for meaning-making rather than simply co-located.

Jigsaw Method — Learners build distributed expertise and then teach one another, making interdependence explicit.

Collaborative Inquiry — Groups investigate a question together and synthesize evidence, requiring reasoning to be externalized and coordinated.
