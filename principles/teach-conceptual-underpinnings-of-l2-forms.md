# Teach the conceptual underpinnings of L2 forms rather than only their structural aspects or formulaic units

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 18 claims (8 for, 8 mixed, 2 against) · 16 studies (6 theoretical, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 2 review, 2 associational, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q3` · 0 of 16 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page covers **explaining the meaning behind a second-language form** so that learners choose it in the right contexts. The relationship it proposes is conditional. For a second-language learner (usually `intermediate` or `advanced` in the L2, usually `adult`) who already produces a form, such as a preposition, a discourse connective or an aspect or article choice, but uses it in a pattern unlike that of proficient users (overusing it, avoiding it, or extending it to contexts where it does not fit) because the L2 divides up the meaning differently from the learner's first language, an explicit explanation of the meaning that motivates the form's uses (its core sense, and how each further use extends from that sense, shown in a diagram or schema), followed by practice in choosing the form across contexts, is expected to produce more appropriate choices (`concept` knowledge of where the form fits, and `skill-performance` in the learner's own comprehension and production) than presenting only the form's structure or teaching its uses as unconnected fixed phrases. The expected change is at an `immediate` horizon on researcher-designed measures; use in the learner's own unplanned speech and writing, and `delayed-retention`, are separate claims.

It is conditional on four things: that the form's uses do have a describable meaning motivation (for prepositions this is the cognitive-linguistic account of a spatial core sense; for an arbitrary agreement rule there is no such story to tell); that the learner's difficulty is one of distribution, not of not knowing the form; that the first language's nearest equivalent covers a different range of meaning; and that the learner can follow an explanation about meaning, in the L2 or the L1.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship.** The one reported study ([Advanced L2 learners taught the core senses of to, for and at with diagrams show pre- to post-test gains in knowing when to use them, in one uncontrolled study reported second-hand](../claims/cl-core-sense-preposition-instruction-improves-use.md) [+M]) is described second-hand in a theoretical article, as a pre-test to post-test gain for advanced learners taught the core senses of *to*, *for* and *at* with diagrams; the claim page reports no comparison group and no effect size, so it does not compare conceptual explanation with structural or formulaic teaching. The nearest comparative evidence is the larger literature on explicit against implicit grammar instruction ([Explicit L2 grammar instruction leads to larger learning effects than implicit instruction, as reported in the reviewed synthesis literature](../claims/explicit-l2-grammar-instruction-superior-to-implicit.md) [+S]), which supports making a form explicit but does not say which kind of explicit explanation, conceptual or structural, does better.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance (identify the conceptual underpinnings and core senses, teach them explicitly, aim at appropriate use of forms whose distribution differs subtly from the L1), made concrete. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it.

1. **Confirm that the problem is distribution, and for which form.** Collect a sample of 15–20 of each learner's uses of the candidate form from their own writing or speech, plus a short comprehension task, and tally overuse, misuse and absence against a reference (a proficient-writer corpus of the same genre, or a teacher's judgement, stated as such). Choose forms where learners' choices deviate from proficient use, and spend class time there. Basis: [Awareness of how learners' conceptual representations deviate from native-speaker preferences can help teachers decide where to focus classroom attention, promoting conceptual fluency](../claims/conceptual-deviation-awareness-guides-teacher-attention.md) [+W] (an attributed proposal, no data). Include the comprehension task and an elicitation that requires the form, because a form a learner avoids shows no errors ([Error analysis has stated limitations: overattention to errors, overstressing production data, failure to account for avoidance, and language-specific focus](../claims/error-analysis-limitations.md) [~W]). The sample size is an untested proposal.
2. **Set the L1 and L2 meanings side by side.** For the chosen form, write down which contexts the L1's nearest equivalent covers and which the L2 form covers, and mark where they part. Basis: [Each language entails a conceptually different world, so exact translation of conceptual structure between languages is impossible](../claims/language-entails-conceptually-different-world.md) [~W] (conceptual analysis of prepositions across four languages; theoretical) and [Thai students overuse the causal connective because, a pattern the article attributes to L1 influence](../claims/thai-overuse-because-l1-influence.md) [+W] (a corpus pattern, with the L1 cause the authors' attribution). Do not assume an L1 cause before checking it: in one judgement study, learners placed English manner adverbs correctly although Chinese places them differently ([no first-language transfer for manner adverbs](../claims/no-l1-transfer-manner-adverb-positions.md) [~W]). The contrast step is an untested proposal; the strategy [Address Mother Tongue Interference](../strategies/address_mother_tongue_interference.md) is a recipe for it.
3. **Present the core sense first, with a diagram.** Show the form's central meaning as a picture of a scene (for *at*, a point of location; for *to*, movement towards a goal), then derive each further use from it, two or three examples per use, with the link to the core sense said aloud. About 10–15 minutes for the core sense and the first two or three uses. Basis: [Advanced L2 learners taught the core senses of to, for and at with diagrams show pre- to post-test gains in knowing when to use them, in one uncontrolled study reported second-hand](../claims/cl-core-sense-preposition-instruction-improves-use.md) [+M] (advanced learners, prepositions, pre-to-post, no comparison group). Timings are untested proposals.
4. **Have learners explain the concept.** Each learner explains to a partner why the form fits each example, using the diagram, then explains it to themselves in writing for one new example (about 5–10 minutes). Untested here; this is the verbalization phase of [Concept-Based Language Instruction](../elements/concept-based-language-instruction-galperin-verbalization.md), which carries no claim in the wiki.
5. **Practise choosing, not only producing.** Give items where the form competes with its near neighbours (*to*/*for*/*at*; *because*/*since*/*so*/*as*), including contexts where the form does not fit, and ask for the choice and a one-line reason. Practise in the skill the target uses: if learners must write it, they practise writing it; if they must understand it, they practise understanding it, because grammar practice has been reported to improve only the skill practised ([Practice of grammar rules improves only the practiced skill, showing skill specificity of L2 automatization](../claims/skill-specificity-l2-grammar-practice.md) [~M]). Expect a low-salience form to need much more practice than one explanation suggests ([Low-frequency or low-salience L2 elements are hard or impossible to learn through mere exposure and require extensive practice to proceduralize form-meaning mappings](../claims/low-salience-elements-need-extensive-practice.md) [~W]). About 15–20 minutes per session; untested.
6. **Use the form in a meaning-focused task, and give feedback that names the meaning.** Learners write or speak in a task that needs the form (an argument, a set of directions, a report). Feedback on a misused form points back to the core sense ("this is a goal, not a location: which form shows a goal?") rather than only marking it wrong. Untested proposal; the principle [Integrate Explicit Correction with Meaningful Activities](flexible-error-treatment-strategies.md) is the neighbouring guidance.
7. **Re-sample later.** Two to four weeks on, take a fresh sample of uses from learners' own work and tally it as in step 1. An explicit-teaching gain on one form has been reported not to last over months when the syllabus taught structures separately and never brought them back ([Explicit teaching is more effective for EFL copular be learning but its effect is not durable](../claims/explicit-teaching-effective-but-not-durable.md) [~M]). The interval is an untested proposal.

**Dose and progression across sessions** (an untested default for one form with several uses, over three to five sessions of about 45 minutes):

- *Session 1:* the diagnostic task (10 minutes), the core sense and two or three uses (15), learners' explanations (10), a short choice set (10). The teacher draws the diagram and supplies the examples.
- *Sessions 2–3:* a 5-minute recap in which a learner, not the teacher, explains the diagram; one or two further uses per session (10); choice practice (20); one short meaning-focused task (10). Examples now come partly from texts the learners are reading, which they sort by use.
- *Sessions 4–5:* the diagram is no longer on display; examples come from learners' own drafts; practice moves into whole tasks; a learner explains a choice only when asked or when it is wrong.
- *Moving on:* a learner (or class) moves to the next form when a fresh sample from their own work is close to the reference distribution, within a tolerance the teacher sets and records, and the learner can say why the form fits in about four of five new contexts. Learners still far off stay with that form, with examples from their own writing. Both thresholds are untested.

When a condition is not met: if the form has no describable meaning motivation, teach the pattern as a rule or as fixed phrases and do not invent a conceptual story; if the learner does not yet produce the form at all, teach high-frequency uses first and come back to the core sense once there are several uses to connect; if the sample shows no deviation, do not teach the form.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner's use of the form actually looks like.** Overuse, avoidance and misuse each call for different work, and a learner who does not yet produce the form needs something else. If unstated, ask: in a sample of the learners' own writing or speech, how often do they use the form, where, and where do they avoid it?
- **The learners' first language, and whether its nearest equivalent covers a different range.** That decides whether a side-by-side contrast helps, and whether one contrast serves the whole group. If unstated, ask: what L1s are in the group, and what does each use where the L2 uses this form?
- **The use that counts.** Choosing the form in one's own unplanned writing or speech, passing a gap-fill, and understanding it in reading call for different practice. If unstated, ask: where, and under what time pressure, must the learner use the form, and how will that be checked?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a use of the form in a stated task: the context, whether the form was chosen, avoided or misused, what the learner would have said in the L1, whether the task was timed or edited, and the reason the learner gives when asked. "Has the concept", "is transferring from the L1" and "avoids the form" are inferred states.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Overuses one form (as Thai writers did with *because*) | The L1 equivalent covers more contexts; the learner knows few alternatives; the form was taught early as a safe all-purpose choice | Ask what the learner would use in the L1 in those contexts; give a choice task that offers the alternatives and see whether they are known. Learner writers overall overused more connectives than they underused, and drew on a limited range ([L2 writers tend to overuse more discourse connectives than they underuse](../claims/l2-overuse-tendency-limited-dc-range.md) [+W]). |
| Never errs on the form | It is known; it is avoided; the tasks so far never required it | An elicitation task in which the form is the natural choice, and a comprehension item ([Error analysis has stated limitations](../claims/error-analysis-limitations.md) [~W]). |
| Correct on gap-fill items, wrong in own writing | The choice is known but not yet used without time to think; the gap-fill cued the answer; the writing genre differs from the practice items | Compare a timed with an untimed version of the same item, and a cued with an uncued one ([Practice of grammar rules improves only the practiced skill](../claims/skill-specificity-l2-grammar-practice.md) [~M]). |
| Can explain the core sense but still chooses wrongly | The explanation is held but not used under load; the extension from the core sense to this use was not clear; this use is conventional and the core sense does not predict it | Ask for the reason at the moment of choice; test a new context the core sense does predict and one it does not. |
| Chooses correctly but cannot explain | Learned from exposure; the explanation needs language the learner lacks | Allow the explanation in the L1, or by pointing at the diagram; check a new context. |
| Is less accurate when editing carefully than when writing freely | Conscious checking overrides an intuition that was right; the rule being applied is wrong | Compare edited and unedited versions of the same text, form by form ([Monitoring lowers accuracy for unlearnable rules](../claims/monitoring-lowers-preposition-accuracy.md) [~W], one learner). |

These rows are proposals, untested as diagnostics. An elicitation task is also practice, so record each one, and keep open the explanations that remain open.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki compares teaching the conceptual motivation of an L2 form with teaching its structure or teaching it as fixed phrases. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship; all but one come from theoretical, review or single-learner sources, and none prints an effect size.

- [Advanced L2 learners taught the core senses of to, for and at with diagrams show pre- to post-test gains in knowing when to use them, in one uncontrolled study reported second-hand](../claims/cl-core-sense-preposition-instruction-improves-use.md) [+M]: a theoretical article (Sáez 2014, coded `q2`, theoretical `r2`) reporting Tyler, Mueller and Ho (2011), in which advanced learners taught seven senses of *at* from its core meaning, plus *to* and *for*, with diagrams of core spatial scenes showed significant pre- to post-test improvement in knowing when to use the three prepositions. One study, second-hand, no comparison group reported on the claim page, no effect size, `immediate` horizon. It supports the feasibility of the core-sense step with advanced learners; it does not show the conceptual explanation beats another kind.
- [Explicit L2 grammar instruction leads to larger learning effects than implicit instruction, as reported in the reviewed synthesis literature](../claims/explicit-l2-grammar-instruction-superior-to-implicit.md) [+S]: three meta-analyses (Norris and Ortega 2000, 49 studies; Goo et al. 2015, 34 studies, 11 shared with Norris and Ortega; Spada and Tomita 2010, 41 studies of English features) and one review narrating the first, all read from abstracts, found explicit instruction more effective than implicit; no effect size is printed. Its table counts 7 distinct studies across it and its narrower claims. It supports making the form explicit; it does not separate conceptual from structural explanation.
- [Awareness of how learners' conceptual representations deviate from native-speaker preferences can help teachers decide where to focus classroom attention, promoting conceptual fluency](../claims/conceptual-deviation-awareness-guides-teacher-attention.md) [+W]: an attributed proposal (citing Odlin 2006), `q1`, no data. It is the basis for choosing forms by diagnosis.
- [Metaphoric competence is proposed to be central to all aspects of language use, including grammatical and sociolinguistic competence](../claims/metaphoric-competence-central-to-language-use.md) [+W]: a researcher position (Littlemore and Low 2006, reported by Sáez 2014), `q1`, not a tested result. It is the rationale for treating extended uses as motivated by meaning.
- [Thai students overuse the causal connective because, a pattern the article attributes to L1 influence](../claims/thai-overuse-because-l1-influence.md) [+W]: a corpus comparison of argumentative essays (associational, `q2`) in which *because* was the most frequent connective in Thai students' essays, while Hong Kong and Singaporean students underused it relative to L1 writers. The L1 explanation is the authors' attribution. It illustrates the kind of distributional deviation this page addresses, and that it differs by L1 group.
- [Each language entails a conceptually different world, so exact translation of conceptual structure between languages is impossible](../claims/language-entails-conceptually-different-world.md) [~W]: conceptual analyses of prepositions and equivalent words in English, Italian, German and Russian (von Glasersfeld 1995, theoretical, `r?`). It supports the premise that L1 and L2 forms do not map one to one; it says nothing about teaching.

What limits or qualifies the model:

- [Explicit teaching is more effective for EFL copular be learning but its effect is not durable](../claims/explicit-teaching-effective-but-not-durable.md) [~M]: a review reporting a seven-month study of 89 Japanese seventh graders in three intact classes; the explicit group did best but the advantage was questionable on delayed tests, which the review attributes to a syllabus that taught structures separately. It is why the default design re-samples later and brings the form back in later tasks.
- [Mental representation is not amenable to direct explicit instruction because UG and parsers operate only on input data](../claims/mental-representation-not-directly-instructable.md) [-W]: a theoretical argument (VanPatten 2010) that explicit information does not reach the learner's underlying grammar. If it holds for a form, explanation can at most help learners notice the form in input and monitor output.
- [Monitoring lowers accuracy for unlearnable rules: preposition usage was significantly less accurate in the more monitored style](../claims/monitoring-lowers-preposition-accuracy.md) [~W]: one Korean ESL student's preposition accuracy was lower in edited writing than in less monitored styles (`q2`). One learner, but it touches the very forms (prepositions) whose teachability the earlier page said is questioned.
- [Practice of grammar rules improves only the practiced skill, showing skill specificity of L2 automatization](../claims/skill-specificity-l2-grammar-practice.md) [~M]: a review reporting DeKeyser (1997); practice in comprehension or production improved the skill practised. An explanation is not a substitute for practice in the target skill.
- [Low-frequency or low-salience L2 elements are hard or impossible to learn through mere exposure and require extensive practice to proceduralize form-meaning mappings](../claims/low-salience-elements-need-extensive-practice.md) [~W]: a theoretical argument (DeKeyser 2010) that such mappings take a very large amount of practice.
- [Error analysis has stated limitations: overattention to errors, overstressing production data, failure to account for avoidance, and language-specific focus](../claims/error-analysis-limitations.md) [~W]: a conceptual critique (`q1`). The earlier page cited it in support of moving beyond structural attention; here it qualifies the diagnosis step, which must look for avoidance and comprehension as well as errors.
- [Learners show no first-language transfer for manner adverbs, judging sentence-final placement acceptable and pre-verbal placement unacceptable despite Chinese word order](../claims/no-l1-transfer-manner-adverb-positions.md) [~W]: ten Chinese learners in a judgement study (associational `r1`); not every L2 difficulty, or its absence, follows the L1.
- [L1-induced errors persist in advanced learners and risk fossilization if learners rely on compensatory communication strategies](../claims/l1-interference-errors-fossilization-risk.md) [+W]: one learner's diary (qualitative, `q1`), attributing most of his errors to translation from Chinese.

Learners, forms and sources differ across these claims, so do not rank them by label. None measures appropriate use of a form in learners' own unplanned production at a delay.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

- [Explicit grammar teaching outperforms implicit teaching; one review found the advantage only for complex rules, but a meta-analysis found it for simple rules too](../claims/explicit-teaching-complex-rules-only-advantage.md) [+M] — a review and one meta-analysis on whether the explicit advantage depends on rule complexity; bears on the "complex distributional patterns" scope of this page, not on conceptual explanation as such.
- [A 1982 bilingual-education teacher-training packet asserts, citing Krashen, that repetitive drill and grammar-focused instruction are ineffective for second language acquisition](../claims/drill-and-grammar-focus-ineffective-for-l2-acquisition.md) [-W] — an authorial assertion, no data; the opposing position on grammar focus.
- [Monitoring significantly improves a second language learner's accuracy on learnable rules (subject-verb agreement, regular past tense) in the most monitored style](../claims/monitoring-improves-learnable-rule-accuracy.md) [~W] — the same single-learner study as the preposition finding above, for rule-like forms; bears on which forms conscious checking helps.
- [L2 writers tend to overuse more discourse connectives than they underuse, and both L1 and L2 students have a limited range of DCs at their disposal](../claims/l2-overuse-tendency-limited-dc-range.md) [+W] — the same corpus as the Thai finding; used above in the observation table.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually that learners choose the form where proficient users of the L2 would, in their own writing or speech, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to be understood, to sound natural, to stop being corrected, to pass an examination, to write a report a manager will accept. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values being understood may see no point in a form whose misuse never blocks understanding; one who values sounding natural may care about exactly the subtle differences this page targets.

For example, an engineer writing reports in English may want her reports to read as a native colleague's do, while the course objective for the week is prepositions of time and place in a textbook unit. Teaching the core senses of *at*, *on* and *in* serves the course objective; a re-sample of prepositions in her next real report tests hers. Record both, so that a textbook score is not read as the capability she came for.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing conceptual explanation with structural or phrase-based teaching of the same form, with comparable learners and measures of use in the learners' own production, show no advantage, or show that a gain at post-test is gone at a delay. If explanation helps learners only to judge or edit the form and not to use it in unplanned speech, the model should say so and move its weight to practice. If deviations attributed to the L1 turn out, on checking, to be shared by learners of many L1s, the contrast step should give way to L2-internal contrasts. If the core-sense account does not predict the uses learners get wrong, it is the wrong account for that form. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough practice" after the fact.

Understanding the core sense, choosing correctly on practice items, choosing correctly in one's own writing, and doing so weeks later are separate claims. The present evidence establishes none of the doses or thresholds given above.
