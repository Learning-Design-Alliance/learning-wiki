# Responsive Teaching

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 8 claims (5 for, 2 mixed, 1 against) · 14 studies (4 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 qualitative, 2 review), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 14 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

## Description
Responsive teaching means continuously eliciting learner thinking and adjusting instruction in real time — re-sequencing, re-explaining, extending, or redirecting — based on what students actually say and do rather than on the planned lesson script. It treats student ideas, including errors and partial understandings, as the primary evidence for instructional decisions.

## Implications

Responsive teaching operationalizes formative assessment as a moment-to-moment practice: the teacher gathers evidence of understanding and acts on it within the same instructional episode. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the evidence — instruction can only respond to thinking that has been made visible, which is why structured elicitation is a prerequisite rather than an add-on. Once thinking is surfaced, the teacher's move matters: contingent scaffolding that is calibrated to the learner's current understanding outperforms generic support [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+S], and feedback targeted at the task and process levels drives the largest gains [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. In mathematics, teachers who learned to interpret children's solution strategies and build instruction on them produced substantially larger achievement gains than controls [Carpenter et al., 1989](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312026004511) [+S]. Responsiveness is not permissiveness: it requires a model of likely learning trajectories so the teacher can decide which student idea to pursue and which to set aside.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Frequent, low-stakes evidence gathering ([Check-ins](check-ins.md), [Eliciting Student Thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md), [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)) — unexpressed thinking cannot be responded to
- A working model of learning trajectories in the domain ([Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)) — teachers need to know what typical progressions look like to interpret what they hear
- Authority and time to depart from the planned lesson — scripted pacing that must be "covered" crowds out productive detours
- A classroom culture where students will voice partial or incorrect ideas ([Communities of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) norms, low-stakes participation)
- A research-based curriculum with an identifiable underlying design logic (recurring structures such as elicit-confront-resolve sequences, or a hierarchy of "genesis," "tool," and reinforcement exercises)
- Deliberate professional preparation time devoted to analyzing *why* activities are sequenced as they are, not just what content they cover
- Integration of formal knowledge (curriculum designers' research on common student difficulties) with the teacher's own lived experience as a learner and instructor of the material

#### Constraints
- Degrades when teachers lack domain knowledge of common misconceptions — responses become generic praise or re-explanation rather than targeted moves
- Can fragment instruction if every student idea is pursued; the teacher must triage against learning goals
- Less feasible in very large classes or fully asynchronous settings without instrumentation ([Adaptive Learning](adaptive-learning.md) systems can substitute algorithmic responsiveness, but respond to answers, not reasoning)
- Over-scaffolding in response to struggle can undermine productive persistence; support should fade as competence grows [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- The instructional moves this knowledge enables (referring students to another section, choosing not to intervene, deviating from the sequence) are small-scale, curriculum-embedded adaptations — they do not by themselves constitute the most flexible, curriculum-independent forms of responsive teaching
- Developing this knowledge took an extended period (documented over roughly 30 weeks) and required sustained formal-lived concept integration, not a single training session

### Target Learners
- Novices whose partial understandings are most likely to diverge from the planned path
- Young learners and emergent bilinguals, whose reasoning may be ahead of their ability to express it in standard academic form
- Struggling learners who benefit from instruction that starts from their current strategy rather than the canonical one
- Effects diminish when learners are already aligned with the instructional trajectory — responsiveness adds little when the class is uniformly on track [~W]
- Novice or early-career instructors (the source study used undergraduate Learning Assistants) working within a structured, research-validated curriculum

### Target Learning Objectives
- Conceptual understanding, where student misconceptions must be surfaced and worked through
- Strategy development and flexible problem solving (choosing among methods, explaining reasoning)
- Disciplinary practices (argumentation, modeling) that depend on responding to authentic student ideas
- Less central for pure fluency or recall goals, where direct practice sequences suffice
- Enacting authentic partnership with a research-based curriculum: adapting it responsively to student thinking while remaining grounded in its designed logic, rather than either rigidly following it or abandoning it

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — learning builds on prior conceptions, so instruction must be shaped by the conceptions learners actually hold
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the teacher's contingent responses model expert diagnosis of student reasoning
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — responsive feedback loops mirror and model the monitoring-and-adjustment cycle learners must internalize
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — instruction anchored in learners' own contributions keeps learning situated in authentic disciplinary discourse
- Responsive teaching (Ball 1993; Hammer 1997; Hammer et al. 2012) — attending to and building on the beginnings of disciplinary thinking in student talk
- Sociocultural theory (Vygotsky) — integration of formal (research-based) and lived (experiential) concepts as the mechanism of knowledge development
- Curriculum use as participation with the text (Remillard 2005) — contrasted with curriculum use as merely following or subverting a script

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — highly scripted, teacher-led models assume the instructional sequence can be specified in advance; responsiveness trades some efficiency and coverage for adaptivity, and scripted approaches can outperform improvised ones when teacher diagnostic skill is weak [~M]

### Illustrative

**[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)** — A professional development program that trains elementary teachers to elicit children's informal solution strategies for arithmetic word problems, classify them against research-based strategy trajectories, and design instruction that builds from each child's current strategy. Carpenter et al. (1989) showed large achievement gains for students of CGI teachers.

**[Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md)** — Classroom cycles of eliciting evidence, interpreting it against learning goals, and adjusting instruction — the institutionalized version of responsive teaching at the lesson scale.

**[Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) with strategic follow-up** — A teacher poses a problem, polls or samples multiple student answers, then sequences and connects responses so the class confronts the key conceptual contrast — responding to the ideas actually in the room rather than the anticipated ones.

**[Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) in the moment** — During [Practice](../elements/practice.md), an instructor observes a learner's attempt, diagnoses the specific flaw in their approach, and offers a targeted hint rather than a full correction — the one-on-one form of responsiveness.

**[Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md)** — Problem-rich anchors (e.g., The Adventures of Jasper Woodbury) generate diverse student approaches that teachers can surface and build on, making responsive moves natural rather than improvised.

## Description
Responsive teaching is often framed as being in tension with structured, research-based curricula — as if following a designed sequence of activities necessarily limits a teacher's ability to attend and build on student thinking in the moment. This principle proposes an additional component of curricular knowledge, beyond knowing what materials exist: knowledge of *why* a curriculum's specific activities and question sequences are designed the way they are — what misconception each section anticipates, what conceptual tool each activity builds, and how sections relate to one another. Teachers who develop this knowledge can use a structured curriculum as a responsive partner rather than a script: deciding when to intervene or hold back, which sections to prioritize under time pressure, how to explain a stuck point using the curriculum's own designed logic, and when to deviate from the sequence because a student's actual thinking doesn't match what the curriculum anticipated.
