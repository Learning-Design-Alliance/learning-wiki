# Evidence-Based Teaching and Scientific Reasoning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Ben Goldacre (2013) argues that teaching should be an evidence-based profession, which would require a cultural shift: recognizing that we don't necessarily "know" what works best and need evidence that something works; giving teachers better access to research outcomes; helping teachers understand how research works so they can become critical consumers of it; and giving teachers access to networks of others interested in research. This isn't only research done by academics — research by teachers on their own practice is itself part of the same evidence base, and is increasingly embedded in schools as a contributor to school improvement.

Personal inquiry — drawing conclusions from one's own experience — is a weaker basis for this than it feels like from the inside. **Confirmation bias** is the tendency to look for evidence that we're right while ignoring evidence that we're not; because our assumptions guide our perceptions, "believing is seeing" at least as much as "seeing is believing." This can be an unconscious cognitive blind spot or a more deliberate defense of an existing view, and much of what passes for personal inquiry consists of validating what we already believe by discussing it with others who already agree.

Philosopher Karl Popper proposed the criterion that separates scientific from unscientific claims: falsifiability. Scientific inquiry involves genuine attempts to reject or refute a theory or assumption; a theory that cannot be falsified is not scientific (Thornton, 2005). The scientific method itself rests on three fundamental features (Stanovich, 2010): **systematic empiricism** — learning through carefully planned, recorded, and analyzed observation rather than casual impression; **empirical questions** — questions about how the world actually is, which can therefore be answered by systematic observation, as opposed to questions no observation could settle; and **public knowledge** — publication that lets other researchers detect and correct errors in method or conclusion, so that scientific knowledge is self-correcting over time in a way one researcher's private conviction is not.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Structures that give teachers real access to research findings and to networks of other research-engaged practitioners, not just an individual expectation to "read more"
- A habit of treating a striking personal classroom observation as a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion already reached
#### Constraints
- A belief or practice that cannot in principle be shown wrong by any classroom evidence isn't a testable claim about what works, however confidently it's held
- Discussing a teaching approach only with colleagues who already agree reinforces confirmation bias rather than correcting it — some form of outside challenge or published, peer-reviewed evidence is needed to catch what a like-minded group will not

### Target Learners
- Preservice and practicing teachers evaluating a new technique, a colleague's confident recommendation, or their own striking classroom experience

### Target Learning Objectives
- The ability to distinguish evidence that could in principle have come out otherwise (and so genuinely tests a claim) from evidence that would look the same regardless of whether the claim was true

## Examples
- A teacher who notices a technique "worked great" for one class and, before adopting it broadly, checks whether a controlled study backs the impression or looks for a colleague who tried it and got a different result
- A department that shares research findings and invites outside speakers, rather than relying solely on internal consensus about what works
