# Include parents, teachers, or mentors in any self-regulation intervention and support their own skills and coaching capacity

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 18 claims (10 for, 8 mixed) · 20 studies (13 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review, 1 design), `q2`–`q4` · 9 of 20 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns one narrower relationship inside self-regulation: **who else the intervention works on**. For an `adolescent` or young `adult` (roughly 14 to 25, the brief's range) in a programme meant to build self-regulation (planning, persisting, managing strong emotions and stress, decision-making, help-seeking; a `metacognitive-strategy` and `attitude-motivation` target), adding a component for the adults who see the young person every day (a parent or carer, a teacher, a mentor, a programme facilitator) is expected to make the young person's gains larger and more likely to be used outside the sessions, **if** that component (a) helps the adults regulate themselves in hard moments, (b) coaches them, through rehearsal and feedback rather than information alone, to co-regulate: keep a warm, responsive relationship, structure the setting so self-regulation is manageable, and cue and coach the young person's own skills, and (c) hands control back to the young person as the skill appears. The outcomes in question are the young person's observed or reported regulation and conduct (`behavioural`, `self-report`), and more weakly `general-achievement`.

**What is a synthesis here and what is the brief's own guidance.** The recommendation itself is a guideline in a practice brief, Murray & Rosanbalm (2017), which summarises a government-commissioned review of self-regulation interventions; the wiki records that brief's statements as `review` evidence coded `q2` with no effect sizes. **No claim in this wiki tests the recommendation as such**: none compares a youth self-regulation programme with and without an adult component, for adolescents or young adults. The syntheses and trials this page leans on test neighbouring relationships, each in another population: coaching parents through practice beats giving them information (a meta-analysis of 77 parent-training evaluations and a randomised trial, both with parents of children aged 0 to 7); parent-mediated interventions help children with autism (two meta-analyses); a brief exercise changing teachers' mindset about discipline reduced suspensions in middle school (two randomised field experiments); universal SEL programmes improved achievement only when school staff delivered them (a meta-analysis); and warm teacher–student relationships go with engagement (two correlational meta-analyses). Facilitators' and adolescents' own accounts of co-regulation strategies in one evaluation of adolescent programmes come closest to the setting, and they are perceptions, not outcomes. So the model below is a transported expectation and the default design is a proposal.

How this page sits beside its converted neighbours. [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) owns the general relationship between teaching a learner to plan, monitor and act and the learner's regulation and performance; this page does not repeat it and takes its cautions as given (monitoring accuracy, a self-report of regulation is not regulation, achievement gains are smaller than regulation gains). The [Social-Emotional Learning pattern](../patterns/social-emotional-learning.md) owns the programme-level sequence for teaching named social and emotional skills in schools; this page adds the adult side to it. The theory behind the three adult roles is on [Co-regulation](../theories/co-regulation-framework-adolescents.md) and [its three-domain framework](../theories/co-regulation-framework-three-support-domains.md).

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The brief's guideline turned into steps a designer can act on and revise. Every step is an untested proposal unless a claim is named, and the doses are proposals to set locally.

1. **Choose the adults by where the skill must be used.** For each setting where the young person needs to regulate (home, class, a workplace or programme), name the one adult who is there most often and can respond in the moment: a parent or carer, a subject teacher, a mentor, a supervisor. One or two adults per young person, not every adult. With young adults, ask the young person whom to involve. Untested proposal; the academic gains of SEL programmes were significant only when school staff delivered them ([SEL and achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [~S], K–12 students).
2. **Start with the adult's own regulation.** In the first adult session (proposal: 60–90 minutes, in a group of adults where possible), each adult names their own early signs of stress in hard moments with the young person, practises one short pause routine (for example a slow breath before responding) and writes a plan for the moment they find hardest. From the brief; untested. A neighbouring randomised finding: changing teachers' own stance towards discipline, in one 45–70-minute online exercise, reduced middle-school suspensions, most for students at high risk of suspension ([empathic discipline](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]; the preregistered overall effect was small and marginal).
3. **Coach the co-regulation moves by rehearsal, not by handout.** Teach three moves the brief and the co-regulation framework name: keep the relationship warm and responsive (greet by name, specific praise, listen before fixing); structure the setting (predictable routines, fewer triggers, a calm place to pause); coach the skill (model it aloud, prompt it, praise its use). For each move: the coach demonstrates, the adult rehearses it in a role-play of a real recent situation, gets feedback, and plans one use before the next session. Evidence: coaching parents through active practice with feedback beat information-only formats, with parents of children aged 0 to 7 ([parent coaching](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M], carried to adults of adolescents untested); facilitators of adolescent programmes said the co-regulation strategies helped them build warm, trusting relationships ([co-regulation and relationships](../claims/co-regulation-builds-warm-trusting-relationships.md) [+W], their own accounts).
4. **Use the young person's skill names.** Give the adults the same names, steps and cue cards the youth sessions use, so a parent or teacher can say "this is a moment for the pause" in the programme's own words. Untested proposal, from the brief's call to integrate skills training with ongoing coaching and support.
5. **Run the adult component alongside the youth programme, over weeks, with support thinning.** A default arc (proposal): weeks 1–2, two adult sessions (steps 2 and 3, 60–90 minutes each) with the coach modelling and the adults rehearsing; weeks 3–8, one short coaching contact a week (15–30 minutes, in person, by phone or video), in which the adult reports one incident, what they did and what the young person did, and rehearses one move; weeks 9–12, the adult keeps a short log of uses (one line per incident) and the coach checks in every two weeks; after that, a booster contact each month or at a known stress point (exams, a transition, a new job). Move an adult to the thinner support when they report using the move in most of the opportunities they log over two weeks and can describe what the young person did next. Not tested as an arc; how support is withdrawn is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).
6. **Hand control back to the young person.** Across the same weeks, the adult cues less and asks more ("what's your plan for this?") once the young person uses a skill with a cue; the adult stops intervening on the task itself. A neighbouring caution: among middle-school students, parental help with homework was the one form of involvement associated with lower achievement, and expectations and talk about learning with the highest ([parent involvement](../claims/parent-involvement-improves-achievement.md) [~M], correlational).
7. **Match the intensity to need.** All adults get steps 1–4 in brief; adults of young people already showing difficulties get the full arc in step 5 or a trained self-regulation coach for the young person; where the carers themselves live with chronic adversity, reduce their stressors and support their own regulation first, and add a mentor or facilitator as the co-regulating adult. From the brief's guidance on tiered support ([tiered self-regulation support](../strategies/tiered-self-regulation-intervention-support.md)); untested here.
8. **Record whether the coaching happened.** Keep a line per adult per week: contact made, move rehearsed, use reported. In one review of teacher coaching studies, only half recorded whether coaching was delivered as planned ([coaching components](../claims/coaching-components-inconsistently-reported.md) [~W]), so an absent effect often cannot be read.

When a condition is not met: if no adult can be reached for weeks 3–12, keep steps 2–4 as a single session and expect less; if the young person does not want a given adult involved, choose another (step 1) rather than proceed.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **Which adult is present when the young person needs to regulate, and whether that adult is available and able.** A teacher can cue a skill in class, a carer at home, a supervisor at work; an adult under heavy stress may need support for their own regulation before they can co-regulate. If unstated, ask: in the moments that go wrong, who is there, and how much time and capacity do they have?
- **The young person's age and wishes.** At 14 the adults around them shape most settings; at 22 the young person chooses whom to involve, and an adult cueing them uninvited can read as control. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, and have they agreed to the adults being involved?
- **What counts as success, and in which setting.** Fewer incidents at school, coping at work, a self-reported skill and achievement are separate outcomes; the adult component should sit in the setting where the target outcome happens. If unstated, ask: what would the young person and the adults see differently, where, and by when?

| One facilitator for many young people, or scarce facilitator time (volunteers, a busy shift, a brief check-in) | Spend the adult time on the three or four adults who see the most young people (teachers, house staff); teach one move per session; replace weekly coaching with a 10-minute check on one logged incident; start with the welcoming routine, which facilitators found easiest | [Co-regulation implementation](../claims/co-regulation-implementation-factors-context-experience-strategy-ease.md) [~W] (facilitators' reports, adolescent programmes: welcoming easiest, breathing exercises hardest; class size and session length shaped use) |
| Mixed modes: some adults remote, some in the room, or phone-only | Hold the first adult session live (in person or video) so moves can be rehearsed and watched; run weekly coaching by phone or messaging around one logged incident; send the youth's cue cards as images | untested; the claim page on parent coaching says its evidence does not test remote coaching |
| A single session only | Spend it on the adults, not only the youth: one own-regulation routine and one co-regulation move, rehearsed; leave a cue card both sides share; do not promise lasting change | untested proposal |
| Adults have low literacy or low confidence | Teach by demonstration and role-play, not handouts; one move per session; let adults log by voice note or a tick; praise what they already do well | untested; coached practice beat written self-directed materials for parents at post-test ([parent coaching](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M], ages 0–7) |
| Adults in a work setting (supervisors of apprentices or trainees, `workplace-clinical`) | Make the supervisor the co-regulating adult; fit coaching into existing supervision meetings (10–15 minutes); use the workplace's own incident types as role-play scenarios | untested proposal |
| The brief names its own routine (its check-in, its breathing step, its behaviour card) | Keep it; train the adults to run that routine and add only what the evidence says matters: rehearsal with feedback and one specific prompt to the young person | untested; [parent coaching](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M] for rehearsal over information |
| The brief has its own categories, criteria or behaviour rubric | Use them as the skill names the adults cue and the incident log's categories; do not substitute the programme's | untested proposal |

| A fixed short time budget (for example 3 hours of adult time in a term) | Give minutes: 75 minutes for the first session (own regulation and one move, rehearsed), then six 15-minute coaching contacts; drop content, not rehearsal | untested proposal |
| The young person is to use the skill in a whole real performance (a match, a placement, a presentation to a real audience, a job interview) | Keep the performance whole; the adult rehearses with the young person a pre-performance routine and a debrief afterwards, rather than splitting the skill into exercises | untested proposal |
| Evaluation relies on the adults' or youth's self-report | Add an observed or recorded outcome (incidents, attendance, a rated observation) and a record of whether coaching happened | [Training facilitators](../claims/srae-co-regulation-training-increases-self-regulation-knowledge.md) [~W] (gains were in self-reported knowledge only); [coaching components](../claims/coaching-components-inconsistently-reported.md) [~W] |

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: an adult's account of an incident (what happened, what they did, what the young person did next), a logged use of a move, attendance at coaching, a teacher's or carer's rating, an incident or exclusion record, and the young person's own report of whether the adult helped. "The adult co-regulates well", "the young person has internalised the skill" and "the relationship is warm" are inferred states. An adult's report of a move is a report, not the move.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Adults attend and report knowing more, but incidents do not change | Knowledge without practice; the adults are not present at the moments that matter; the moves are used but too late | Ask for one incident in detail and rehearse it; check who was present in recent incidents; compare logged uses with incident times. |
| The adult reports using the moves; the young person says the adult "nags" | Moves delivered as control rather than coaching; the young person's age or wishes call for less adult cueing; the adult's own stress shows | Ask the young person privately what helps and what does not; have the adult switch from telling to asking for a week and compare. |
| Gains at school, none at home (or the reverse) | Only one setting's adult was coached; skill names differ between settings; stressors at home overwhelm the skill | Check whether both adults use the same skill names; ask the carer what happens in the hardest moment at home. |
| Adults stop attending after the first sessions | Time and travel cost; sessions felt like blame; the adult's own adversity | Offer a phone contact; ask what one change would make the next session worth it; check whether the first session started from what they already do well. |
| The young person regulates well with the adult present and not without | Skill carried by the adult's cues; no hand-over planned | Plan a week in which the adult asks rather than cues, and record what the young person does unprompted. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners or adults. A probe (asking a young person what the adult does) is itself an intervention in the relationship, so record each one.

## Evidence and qualifications

**Syntheses and trials (neighbouring relationships, other populations).**

- [Parent Coaching Outperforms Information Only](../claims/parent-coaching-outperforms-information-only.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 77 published evaluations of parent training for children aged 0–7 (Kaminski et al. 2008, `q4`) found that requiring parents to practise new skills with their own children during sessions was one of the components consistently associated with larger effects on parenting behaviour and child externalising behaviour, while components limited to conveying information were associated with smaller effects; no pooled effect size is given for the component. A randomised trial with 305 preschoolers at high risk of conduct problems (Sanders et al. 2000, `q3`) found practitioner-coached versions of a family intervention ahead of a self-directed written version at post-test on parent-reported child behaviour, dysfunctional parenting and parental competence; by one year the three active versions had converged on observed behaviour, though coached versions stayed ahead on parent report. This is the synthesis that supports step 3 (rehearsal over information). It does not test adults of adolescents, nor self-regulation as the outcome, and its rigour is `r?` on both entries. Capped at M here because it is carried to another age.
- [Parent Implemented Intervention Improves Outcomes](../claims/parent-implemented-intervention-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of parent-mediated interventions for children with autism: 19 randomised trials with children aged 1–6 (small effects, average g = 0.23, dose of parent training not a significant moderator) and 18 studies with school-age children (g = 0.79 for group designs). It supports training the adult to deliver practice at home; it is about children with autism, not adolescents' self-regulation.
- [Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]: a randomised field experiment with 31 middle-school mathematics teachers found year-long suspensions at 4.8% among students of teachers given an empathic-mindset exercise against 9.6% in the control (`q3`); a preregistered replication with 66 randomised teachers and 5,822 students found a small, marginal overall effect and larger reductions for Black and Hispanic students and previously suspended students (`q4`). It is the nearest randomised evidence that changing an adult's own stance changes young adolescents' outcomes; it trained a mindset, not co-regulation skills.
- [Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [~S]: across 213 universal programmes (K–12), effects on conduct and achievement were small (ES 0.22–0.27) and academic gains were significant only when school staff delivered the programme; a later synthesis of rigorous designs found the achievement effect negligible (g = 0.08). It qualifies as well as supports: involving the school's own adults mattered for achievement, but the achievement effect is small.
- [Positive teacher–student relationships are associated with greater student engagement and, more weakly, with achievement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]: two meta-analyses of correlational and longitudinal studies (119 studies, mean r = .31 for learner-centred teacher variables; 99 studies, stronger in higher grades). It supports the warm-relationship role and does not show that improving the relationship raises engagement.

**The brief's own guidance and closest-setting accounts.**

- Murray & Rosanbalm (2017) is the source of the recommendation (Key Sources). Its claims recorded in this wiki are its summaries of research, coded `review` `q2` with no effect sizes: [interventions with high schoolers and young adults improve cognitive regulation](../claims/sr-interventions-improve-cognitive-regulation-young-adults.md) [+W] (the brief's summary of 60 studies), and the developmental and stress claims used in the situation table. None of them tests the adult component.
- [Using co-regulation strategies helped facilitators and youth build warm, trusting relationships](../claims/co-regulation-builds-warm-trusting-relationships.md) [+W]: facilitators' interviews and youth focus groups across nine sites of adolescent programmes (Tingey et al. 2023), consistently describing welcoming routines and praise as building relationships. Accounts, not outcome comparisons; the report states it does not address youth outcomes.

**What limits or qualifies the model.**

- [Supervisory teacher coaching shows mixed effects](../claims/supervisory-coaching-mixed-teacher-student-outcomes.md) [~M]: of 16 quasi-experimental and single-subject studies of teacher coaching, six reported improved teacher results and four improved student behaviour, and only six met all quality indicators. Coaching the adults does not reliably reach the young people.
- [Self-regulated learning improves achievement](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]: training effects were larger when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered SRL training. Handing delivery to the everyday adults can shrink effects unless they are trained and supported.
- [Parent Involvement Improves Achievement](../claims/parent-involvement-improves-achievement.md) [~M]: among middle-school students, help with homework was negatively associated with achievement while academic socialisation had the strongest association (correlational). Adult involvement is not uniformly helpful; its form matters.
- [Implementation of co-regulation strategies was shaped by classroom context, facilitator experience and mindset](../claims/co-regulation-implementation-factors-context-experience-strategy-ease.md) [~W]: the adults' context and capacity limit what is delivered.

Populations, comparisons and outcomes differ across these claims (parents of preschoolers, children with autism, middle-school teachers, K–12 programmes, facilitators' reports), so do not rank them by effect label, and do not read any as a forecast for 14–25-year-olds.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims bearing on this page that are not part of the model above; each says what it is and how far it bears.

- [Co-regulation strategies were reported to create a safe, positive classroom climate and to help youth manage emotions](../claims/co-regulation-supports-climate-and-youth-emotion-regulation.md) [+W]: facilitators' and youths' accounts from the same adolescent-programme evaluation; supports the structuring and coaching roles as perceived, not measured.
- [Facilitators reported that co-regulation strategies strengthened their facilitation and made their interactions with youth more intentional](../claims/co-regulation-strategies-strengthen-facilitation-intentionality.md) [+W]: the adults' own perception of the change in their practice.
- [Training SRAE facilitators significantly increased their self-reported knowledge of self-regulation](../claims/srae-co-regulation-training-increases-self-regulation-knowledge.md) [+W]: pre–post self-reported knowledge of trained facilitators; bears on whether adult training changes adult knowledge, not behaviour or youth outcomes.
- [Coaching components are inconsistently reported](../claims/coaching-components-inconsistently-reported.md) [~W]: a review of teacher-coaching studies (face-to-face feedback in 94%, fidelity recorded in 50%); used in step 8.
- [Mindfulness programs show substantial benefits across cognitive and emotional regulation](../claims/mindfulness-programs-benefit-adolescent-regulation.md) [+W]: the brief's summary of its review for adolescents and young adults; bears on the youth's skills component, not on the adults.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually the young person's self-regulation in a named setting (fewer incidents, coping with a stressor, finishing what they start), observed by someone other than the young person, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the young person wants: to be trusted, to stay in school or a job, to get on better at home, to be left alone by adults. Elicit too what the adults want (calm at home, a manageable class), since they are learners in this design. Agreement is an observation. A 17-year-old who values independence may welcome a mentor who asks and resent a parent who cues; a parent who values calm may want the young person managed rather than coached. When these diverge, the design keeps the young person's choice of adult (step 1) and makes the adult's goal explicit in coaching, so that a calmer household is not read as the young person's own skill.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if trials of adolescent or young-adult self-regulation programmes, with and without an adult component of the kind described, show no difference on observed regulation or conduct, or show gains only while the adult keeps cueing. If adults' own-regulation training adds nothing beyond coaching the co-regulation moves, step 2 should go. If information-only adult components (a handout, an evening talk) match coached ones for adults of adolescents, the rehearsal step is a preference, not a requirement, and the parent-coaching evidence did not carry to this age. If young adults do better with no adult involved, the age range should narrow. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "adults not engaged enough" after the fact.

Adult knowledge, adult practice, the young person's regulation with the adult present, without the adult, and in the setting the young person values are separate claims; the present evidence establishes none of them for 14–25-year-olds, nor any dose for the adult component.

## Description
The brief's third guideline holds that self-regulation interventions for adolescents and young adults should include parents, teachers, or mentors. It recommends providing "these caregivers and other supportive adults with assistance in building their own self-regulation skills in addition to coaching them on strategies to strengthen relationships and provide co-regulation for the youth."
