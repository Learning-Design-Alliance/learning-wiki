# Simulations & Immersive Virtual Environments

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 96 claims (36 for, 52 mixed, 8 against) · 66 studies (18 causal, 17 review, 11 quant-synthesis, 9 qualitative, 6 theoretical, 4 design, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 17 of 66 report an effect size · 91 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **practising inside a designed representation of a real task**: a screen simulation, a virtual lab, an immersive VR or AR environment, a mannequin, an AI-driven virtual patient or a simulated conversation. For a learner preparing for a performance that is costly, risky, rare or invisible in the real setting (a clinical procedure, a hazardous lab, a safety drill, a difficult conversation, a system whose dynamics cannot be seen), repeated attempts in a simulation that represents the task's decisions and cues are expected to improve performance on that task (`skill-performance`, `immediate-performance`, `near-transfer`) more than no simulated practice or passive instruction (not, on present evidence, more than active practice of another kind), **when three conditions hold**: learners are guided while they explore (a goal for each run, prompts or a facilitator who responds to what they do); each run is followed by feedback or a debrief that connects what happened to the real task; and the simulation keeps the parts of the task the learner must attend to, not only its look. More immersion or visual realism is not expected to add learning by itself.

How far this is tested. **Part of it is tested; the whole relationship is not.** A meta-analysis of 609 studies in the health professions found technology-enhanced simulation training beat *no intervention* on knowledge, skills and behaviours, with large pooled effects, but it does not compare simulation with equally long non-simulated practice, and its subgroup analyses could not single out feedback, repetition or curricular integration as the active feature ([Simulation Based Education Improves Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]). A meta-analysis of 65 studies of simulation games, reported second-hand with no effect size printed, found higher skill-based knowledge (the practical application of knowledge) in simulation conditions than in non-game conditions, which is a comparison with instruction rather than with nothing, though the claim does not say what that instruction was ([The review reports that participants in simulation-game conditions had significantly higher skill-based knowledge outcomes than those in non-game conditions](../claims/simulation-games-higher-skill-based-knowledge.md) [+M]). The same meta-analysis, read directly, splits that comparison by what the comparison group did: simulation games beat no substitute instruction (d = .61) and passive instruction (d = .38), but **learners taught with active methods learned slightly more than the simulation-game group** (d = −.19) ([The advantage of simulation games shrinks or reverses when the comparison group learns via active instructional methods](../claims/active-comparison-instruction-reverses-simulation-game-advantage.md) [~M]); and its published studies averaged d = .52 against d = −.10 in unpublished ones ([Published studies of simulation games report substantially larger effects than unpublished studies, indicating publication bias](../claims/publication-bias-in-simulation-games-research.md) [~M]). So the expectation here is against no simulated practice or passive instruction, not against active practice of another kind, and pooled simulation effects are likely inflated. One experiment found that VR with **guided** exploration produced much larger gains than either the same VR explored freely or no VR, and that free exploration in VR was no better than no VR ([Assimilator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode](../claims/assimilators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [+M]; [VR (non-guided exploration) did not differ significantly from Non VR](../claims/non-guided-vr-no-better-than-non-vr.md) [~M]). No claim in this wiki compares a simulation with and without a debrief, varies the number of runs, or compares levels of fidelity directly, so those parts of the design below are proposals.

Virtual, remote and online laboratories are one family of simulations with questions of their own (can one stand in for a hands-on lab, for which outcomes, and who guides a learner working alone); they are treated in a marked subsection at the end of the default design, with their own rows in the situation table and their own evidence entries.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's expertise on the task (`novice` learners need staged complexity and support during the run), on the kind of skill (gains are reported for communication confidence, structured knowledge and procedural skill; tactile psychomotor skills practised on a screen did worse than practice with a standardized patient), on the horizon (near transfer and immediate tests are where gains are reported; delayed and far transfer are weaker), and on access (devices, motion sickness, teacher preparation time).

How the converted siblings sit beside this one. [Experiential Learning](experiential-learning.md) owns the general relationship (an experience becomes learning when reflection, a concept and a further attempt follow); a simulation is one kind of experience. [Debriefing](debriefing.md) owns the facilitated conversation after the run and its four moves; this page uses it and does not repeat it. [Deliberate Practice](deliberate-practice.md) owns targeted refinement against a standard once a learner can perform roughly; simulation-based mastery training is one of its settings. [Game-Based Learning](game-based-learning.md) owns learning through a game whose winning move is the target reasoning; simulation games sit between the two pages. [Guided Practice](guided-practice.md) and [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) own support during attempts and its withdrawal. [Digital Learning](digital-learning.md) owns the argument that a medium is not a method, which applies here to VR and AI: what is expected to matter is the practice, guidance and feedback the simulation carries.

## Default design

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Simulation against no simulation and guided against free exploration have claim support; every dose, sequence and threshold below is a proposal, labelled as such, to be set locally and recorded.

1. **Choose a task worth simulating, and name the decisions and cues it must keep.** Use a simulation when the real performance is risky, costly, rare, slow or invisible; write down the three to five decisions or cues a learner must attend to, and check the simulation represents those, whatever its look. Evidence: simulation practice beat no intervention in the health professions ([Simulation Based Education Improves Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]); higher perceptual fidelity does not reliably improve learning and can add extraneous load ([Increased perceptual fidelity fails to reliably improve serious game learning outcomes](../claims/fidelity-fallacy-serious-games.md) [~M]). Make those cues easy to notice (highlight them, slow events down, remove distractors) instead of adding visual detail: an editorial reading the higher-education simulation meta-analyses reports that how closely the simulation's functions match the task, together with how noticeable the relevant information is, explains more of the variation in outcomes than physical resemblance does ([Functional correspondence predicts simulation learning outcomes better than physical resemblance or appeals to authenticity](../claims/functional-correspondence-predicts-outcomes.md) [+M]; a second-hand reading of Chernikova et al., not an independent test, no effect size printed). The list of decisions and cues is an untested proposal.
2. **Prime the parts and terms before the first run (5–10 minutes).** For learners new to the system or tool, give a short primer naming its key parts and what each does before entering the simulation. Evidence: in one randomized VR experiment, a pretraining video on the parts of a micropipette produced better knowledge scores and fewer errors on a real-world transfer task than the same VR lesson without it ([Pretraining learners on a system's key parts and terms before the main lesson improved knowledge and real-world transfer in one VR experiment](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M]; its table counts 4 distinct studies). The length is an untested proposal.
3. **Give each run a goal and guide the exploration.** Before each run, state what the learner is trying to do and what to watch for; during it, use built-in prompts or a facilitator who responds to what the learner actually does (a cue, then a more specific hint, then a demonstration). Do not leave novices to explore freely. Evidence: guided VR exploration beat free VR exploration and no VR for both learning-style groups in one experiment ([Accommodator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode](../claims/accommodators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [+M]); contingent support beat fixed or absent support, mostly in one-to-one tutoring ([Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M], carried from tutoring; its table counts 20 distinct studies). In a head-mounted display, do not assume spoken prompts beat printed ones: in one meta-analysis of the modality effect, studies using VR headsets showed larger effects of narration over on-screen text (d = 1.34) than studies using computer screens (d = 0.55) ([In one meta-analysis, modality-effect studies using virtual reality head-mounted displays show larger effects than studies using computer screens or audio-tapes](../claims/modality-effect-stronger-in-virtual-reality.md) [~M]; a moderator comparison across studies, not a test inside simulations), but a later VR experiment with 61 learners found short printed text beat narration on recall ([In immersive VR, visual-only text yields higher recall than audio-visual text](../claims/vr-reverse-modality-effect-recall.md) [-M]). Pilot both on the brief's own test, and keep any printed text short and anchored to the object it names (see [Modality](modality.md)).
4. **Stage the complexity for novices.** Early runs use a simplified scenario (fewer variables, slower events, one decision at a time); add variables and time pressure as the learner succeeds, and end the unit with the whole scenario. Evidence: part-task practice reduced load for novices on high-interactivity tasks ([Part-task practice reduces cognitive load for absolute novices during initial skill acquisition.](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M]); the case for ending on the whole task is a design argument, not a tested comparison ([A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~W]).
5. **Debrief every run, and give it as much time as the run.** Plan roughly as long for the debrief as for the run: what happened, the reasoning at two or three decision points, what the real task would have done differently, and one change to try next. Evidence: none here compares a run with and without a debrief (see [Debriefing](debriefing.md)); trainees in tabletop exercises consistently wanted more debriefing time than scheduled ([Trainees consistently wanted more debriefing time than scheduled](../claims/reflection-debriefing-time-underscheduled.md) [+W]). The time ratio is an untested proposal.
6. **Run again straight after the debrief.** A second attempt at the same or a varied scenario lets the change be tried. Evidence: simulation built around deliberate practice (repetition, mastery standards, immediate feedback) beat traditional clinical education on procedural skills in a meta-analysis of 14 studies, d = 0.71 ([Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice Improves Clinical Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]); it tests the bundle, not the second run alone. Between sessions, leave the simulation open for learners to rerun as often as they like: across simulation-game studies, the advantage over comparison groups was larger where trainees had unlimited access to the game (d = .68) than limited access (d = .31) ([Trainees learn more from simulation games when they have unlimited access to the game](../claims/unlimited-access-enhances-simulation-game-learning.md) [+M]; a moderator comparison between groups of studies, not a test of access within one).
7. **Build the bridge to the real task explicitly.** End each unit with an activity that names how the simulated case maps onto the real setting, then a check in a non-simulated or real setting (a real instrument, a standardized patient, a paper case, a supervised workplace task). Evidence: games and simulations support near transfer more robustly than far or long-term transfer, and learners "struggle to generalize" without explicit connections ([The review reports that simulation games support near transfer of knowledge but evidence for long-term or far transfer is less robust](../claims/simulation-near-transfer-robust-far-transfer-weak.md) [~M]); bridging activities that connect game learning to classroom instruction are reported to increase learning ([Students learn more from games when bridging activities connect implicit game learning with formal, explicit classroom instruction](../claims/bridging-activities-connect-game-learning-to-instruction.md) [+M], one report, no study detail); and in one study of 50 middle-school students, virtual labs did not help learners carry what a teacher had demonstrated into new inquiry contexts ([Virtual labs showed a limitation in helping students transfer what they learned from teacher demonstration to new inquiry contexts](../claims/virtual-lab-transfer-from-demonstration-limited.md) [-W]; no statistic printed for that finding), so a demonstration in the simulation is not itself the bridge. In vocational simulator training, teachers were observed using "a real machine" as the benchmark when settling how a simulated task should be done ([VET teachers use the real machine as the benchmark of legitimacy for simulator instruction and shift between teacher and future-colleague positions.](../claims/real-machine-benchmark-and-colleague-positioning.md) [+W]; one ethnographic study, a practice observed, not an outcome), which is one way to make the mapping explicit during the run as well as after it.
8. **Keep tactile skills on physical practice.** Where the skill depends on touch or force (palpation, injection, assembly by hand), use a mannequin, model, standardized patient or real equipment for the skill itself, and use the screen or AI simulation for the decisions and communication around it. Evidence: AI-powered nursing simulations showed short-term gains in communication confidence and structured knowledge but limited or inferior effects for tactile psychomotor skills compared with standardized patients ([AI-powered simulations show short-term gains in communication confidence and structured knowledge in RCTs, with limited or inferior effects for tactile psychomotor skills](../claims/ai-simulation-short-term-gains-nursing.md) [~M]; [One RCT found AI-assisted simulation inferior to standardized patient simulation for breast self-examination skills](../claims/ai-simulation-inferior-bse-skills.md) [-M]).
9. **Plan across sessions, not one event (proposal).** A default arc for a skill that matters weeks later: sessions 1–2, primer, simplified scenarios, prompts available throughout the run, facilitator-led debrief; sessions 3–4, full scenarios, prompts only on request, learners lead part of the debrief against the stated criteria; sessions 5 onward, unfamiliar variants and mixed cases with no prompts during the run, then a check in the real or non-simulated setting. A typical session of 45–60 minutes: 5–10 minutes briefing, 15–20 minutes run, 15–20 minutes debrief, 10 minutes second run. Move a learner on when they meet the stated criterion on two consecutive unfamiliar scenarios without prompts and pass the non-simulated check; add a refresher run after a gap, because gains reported at immediate tests have not always lasted ([AI-generated and prebuilt simulations produced higher immediate conceptual-assessment scores than physical laboratory work, but the advantage did not persist in later examinations](../claims/simulation-advantage-immediate-not-sustained.md) [~M]). All numbers here are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if no one can guide or debrief (self-paced use), build the run's goal, prompts and a written debrief (what happened, why, what next time) into the simulation itself, and add a short non-simulated check before the learner moves on; if the simulation cannot represent the decisions that matter, do not use it for that objective.

### Virtual, remote and online laboratories (added 2026-10-17)

*Scope.* A **virtual lab** is simulated apparatus or a simulated phenomenon on a screen (an interactive science simulation, a virtual bench, a virtual patient's test results); a **remote lab** is real apparatus operated at a distance over a network; an **online lab exercise** is any lab activity run in an online course, including one done with a home kit while an instructor is online; a **technology sandbox** is a resettable software, network or system environment that learners configure and break. The wiki's claims test the first and the third. **No claim in this wiki tests a remote lab, a home lab kit or a technology sandbox**, so what follows about those is carried from virtual labs and labelled.

*Stand in, supplement, or keep hands-on: decide by the outcome.*

- **Conceptual knowledge: a guided virtual lab can stand in.** In one study of 234 undergraduates learning heat and temperature, physical, virtual and combined manipulative experimentation were equally effective and all beat instruction with no experimentation; the authors conclude that manipulation, not physicality, mattered ([Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning](../claims/physical-experience-enhances-science-learning.md) [~M]; random assignment not stated, and a nonsignificant difference is not shown equivalence). In university physics, AI-generated and prebuilt simulations beat physical lab work on an immediate conceptual test but the difference was gone by the midterm and final ([AI-generated and prebuilt simulations produced higher immediate conceptual-assessment scores than physical laboratory work, but the advantage did not persist](../claims/simulation-advantage-immediate-not-sustained.md) [~M]). An older review found no clear advantage for *pure* discovery in a simulation over expository teaching ([Studies comparing pure simulation-based discovery learning with expository teaching show no clear and univocal outcome in favor of simulations](../claims/no-clear-simulation-advantage-over-expository.md) [~M]). Two later trials point the same way or further. In two randomised comparisons with secondary students on marine ecology, learning with an interactive simulation produced higher content knowledge than hands-on experiments, slightly in a 90-minute school lesson (443 students, d = 0.19) and by a wide margin in a 180-minute student-laboratory session on a more complex, multidimensional topic (367 students, d = 1.05) ([Secondary school students learn more complex marine ecology content from interactive computer simulations than from hands-on experiments, with a larger advantage for the more complex multidimensional topic](../claims/simulations-beat-experiments-complex-content-knowledge.md) [+M]; post-test timing not stated on the claim page, so no delayed result). With fourth- and fifth-graders taught to design unconfounded experiments by the same instruction on physical ramps or a computer simulation, both gained equally, including in explicit verbal knowledge of the strategy ([Both physical and virtual training conditions produced equal gains in children's explicit verbal knowledge of the control of variables strategy](../claims/explicit-cvs-knowledge-equal-conditions.md) [~M]; [Physical and virtual instructional materials are equally effective for teaching fourth- and fifth-graders to design unconfounded experiments when instructional method is controlled](../claims/physical-virtual-materials-equal-cvs-learning.md) [~M]; no difference found, equivalence not tested; see [Digital Learning](digital-learning.md)). And manipulating a model beat watching one: undergraduates who explored nanoscale phenomena with agent-based models gained more explanatory knowledge than those who watched visualization videos of the same phenomena (27 students, η² = .15) ([Undergraduate students learning nanoscience with agent-based models show greater gains in explanatory knowledge than students learning with visualization videos](../claims/abm-greater-explanatory-knowledge-gains-nanoscience.md) [+W]; a small randomised trial). Read together: substitution is defensible for concepts when the virtual lab is guided and a delayed check is planned, the simulation should be one learners operate rather than a recording of one, and where the content is complex and multivariate the simulation may do better than the bench. Hold the comparison honest: in immersive-VR comparisons where learning activities were not controlled, the VR arm came out ahead in 14 of 17 ([When activities were not controlled, the confound favored the IVR condition in 14 of 17 comparisons](../claims/activity-confounds-favor-ivr-condition.md) [~M]). Where the concept is about a felt force, the same claim page records experiments in which a brief physical experience of angular momentum improved understanding, so keep a short physical experience there if one can be had.
- **Procedural and manipulative skill: supplement, do not substitute.** No claim in this wiki compares a virtual science lab with a bench lab on manipulative skill. The nearest evidence points one way: for a tactile clinical skill, a screen-based AI simulation did worse than a standardized patient ([One RCT found AI-assisted simulation inferior to standardized patient simulation for breast self-examination skills](../claims/ai-simulation-inferior-bse-skills.md) [-M]); virtual patients showed small or negative effects against active comparators such as mannequin-based learning, against large ones over lectures and reading, in an exploratory reading of one meta-analysis ([Virtual patients show larger advantages over passive traditional learning than over active learning](../claims/vp-passive-versus-active-comparison.md) [~M]); and across higher-education simulation studies, mannequin simulators (g = 1.07) and VR (g = 0.85) were associated with larger effects than screen-based simulations (g = 0.68), a comparison between groups of studies rather than within them ([Higher levels of technology support during simulation are associated with greater effects on learning outcomes](../claims/technology-support-associated-with-greater-effects.md) [~M]). Use the virtual lab to prepare (parts, procedure, predictions) and the hands-on session for the manipulation itself; a primer before a VR procedure improved a real-world transfer task in one experiment ([Pretraining improves transfer](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M]).
- **Inquiry skill (planning an experiment, testing a hypothesis, reading data): a virtual lab is a cheap place to practise it, but only with guidance.** Guidance during inquiry learning improved learners' use of inquiry skills during the inquiry (d = 0.66) and learning outcomes afterwards (d = 0.50), with no difference found between types of guidance ([Guidance during inquiry learning produces a medium-to-large improvement in learners' use of inquiry skills](../claims/guidance-improves-inquiry-learning-activities.md) [+M]; [Guidance during inquiry learning improves post-inquiry learning outcomes](../claims/guidance-improves-inquiry-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]; inquiry learning generally, carried to virtual labs). Left alone in a simulation, learners design poor experiments (as few as 22% effective) and hold on to disconfirmed hypotheses ([Learners design poor experiments](../claims/poor-experiment-design-in-discovery.md) [~M]; [Learners have difficulty generating and adapting hypotheses](../claims/hypothesis-generation-difficulties.md) [~M]); structuring the experiment into before, during and after steps helped up to two to four times as many students distinguish central concepts ([Structuring the discovery process can outperform unstructured environments](../claims/structuring-discovery-process-effects.md) [+M]); successful discoverers plan across experiments and monitor progress ([Successful discoverers regulate learning more systematically](../claims/regulation-distinguishes-successful-discoverers.md) [~M]). Framing the same inquiry task in concrete, familiar terms lets learners use what they already know: in a randomised experiment with 57 first-year students, the concrete version of an isomorphic simulation task produced higher performance success than an intermediate version (r = .32), while the intermediate and abstract versions did not differ significantly ([Concrete-task participants achieved higher performance success than intermediate-task participants in simulation-based inquiry, while intermediate and abstract conditions did not differ significantly](../claims/concrete-task-higher-performance-success-than-intermediate.md) [~W]; the pattern is not a simple concrete-to-abstract gradient). Opening variables in stages helped in complex domains and not in a simple one ([Model progression benefits learning only in sufficiently complex simulations](../claims/model-progression-complexity-boundary.md) [~M]; [Multiple-hypothesis strategies improve performance only when simulation complexity is low](../claims/multiple-hypothesis-strategy-low-complexity.md) [~W]).
- **Attitudes and engagement: expect favourable reports, and do not read them as learning.** After a guided-inquiry lesson with a collision-carts simulation, most of 64 students rated it enjoyable and valuable and their self-rated knowledge rose ([After a guided-inquiry lesson with the collision-carts simulation, students' self-reported knowledge increased and most rated the lesson enjoyable and valuable](../claims/collision-carts-simulation-lesson-positive-student-feedback.md) [+W]; [Students reported that the virtual-lab lesson made learning fun and helped them figure out concepts themselves](../claims/students-report-simulation-lesson-fun-and-concept-figuring.md) [+W]); some found the worksheet "just copy values" ([Some students found the guided-inquiry worksheet activities insufficiently thought-provoking](../claims/students-criticize-inquiry-worksheets-low-thinking.md) [~W]). VR and AR in K-12 STEM most often improved achievement and motivation in a review, but sometimes did no better than conventional technology ([VR/AR most often improved academic achievement and motivation, but sometimes performed no better than conventional technologies](../claims/vr-ar-advantages-achievement-motivation-with-null-contrast.md) [~M]). When courses moved online suddenly, undergraduates named the loss of hands-on experiences such as labs among their main challenges ([Staying motivated was the most pervasive challenge of learning remotely, and students missed instructor feedback, peer collaboration, and hands-on experiences](../claims/motivation-and-lost-interaction-challenges-remote.md) [~M]).

*Guidance in an online lab: who does it.* Students saw online lab exercises as more effective when the instructor was present online and used pre-lab, during-lab and end-of-lab questions to direct attention to the key steps ([Students perceived online lab exercises as more effective when instructors were present online and used scaffolds and probes](../claims/online-labs-better-with-instructor-scaffolds.md) [+W]; student interviews only); teacher-led inquiry outperformed student-led inquiry by about .40 across studies ([Teacher Guided Inquiry Outperforms Student Led](../claims/teacher-guided-inquiry-outperforms-student-led.md) [+M]). In higher-education simulations, examples helped learners with less prior knowledge and reflection phases helped those with more ([Prior knowledge moderates scaffolding effectiveness](../claims/prior-knowledge-moderates-scaffolding-effectiveness.md) [~M]), and prompts alone added nothing while examples combined with prompts did well in a few medical studies ([Presence versus absence of individual scaffolding types did not support the added-value hypothesis, but scaffolding combinations partly did](../claims/scaffolding-added-value-partly-supported.md) [~M]). An AI assistant can take part of this load, with limits: answering student questions about an undergraduate biology lab, ChatGPT was rated acceptable but inconsistent and less accurate than human teaching assistants, and weaker on voice, understanding and helpfulness, though students preferred its answer 40% of the time ([SMEs rated ChatGPT's content accuracy acceptable but inconsistent and below human TAs](../claims/chatgpt-lab-accuracy-acceptable-inconsistent-below-human-tas.md) [-M]; [Human TAs demonstrated stronger instructor voice, understanding, and helpfulness than AI](../claims/human-tas-stronger-teaching-effectiveness-than-chatgpt.md) [-M]; [Students rated human TA responses more favorably overall but preferred ChatGPT responses 40% of the time](../claims/students-prefer-human-ta-responses-but-40-percent-prefer-chatgpt.md) [~M]); in a 35-student trial, instructor feedback on lab assignments raised scores more than AI feedback ([Students receiving instructor feedback showed significantly greater improvements in lab assignment scores than students receiving AI-generated feedback](../claims/instructor-feedback-greater-lab-score-improvements-than-ai-feedback.md) [-M]); and personalised ChatGPT feedback inside an augmented-reality physics lab improved outcomes in a trial reported by a review ([Personalized ChatGPT-supported feedback in an augmented-reality quantum physics laboratory improved learning outcomes](../claims/chatgpt-ar-lab-feedback-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]); and in a randomised trial with 81 undergraduates learning Internet-of-Things tasks in VR, ChatGPT-integrated feedback produced higher scores on memory, understanding and application tasks than the VR's ordinary feedback, and improved problem solving and critical thinking but not creativity ([Undergraduates learning IoT tasks in VR with ChatGPT-integrated feedback reached significantly higher cognitive levels](../claims/gpt-feedback-vr-higher-cognitive-levels.md) [+M]; [GPT feedback in VR markedly improved problem-solving and critical thinking but had limited impact on creativity](../claims/gpt-feedback-hots-problem-solving-critical-thinking-limited-creativity.md) [~M]; no effect sizes printed, immediate tests only). The pattern across these: AI feedback inside the run, compared with a simulation's fixed feedback, has helped; AI in place of a person's feedback on written lab work has done worse. Logs can show who needs help: detectors trained on simulation logs inferred a student's inquiry skill after three runs ([Machine-learned detectors of science inquiry skill made accurate inferences after a student ran a simulation three times](../claims/inquiry-detectors-accurate-after-three-runs.md) [+W]). For dividing the work between an AI tutor and a person, see the [AI Tutoring](../patterns/ai-tutoring.md) and [Mentor Support in Self-Paced Online Learning](../patterns/mentor-support-in-self-paced-online-learning.md) patterns.

*Safety and access.* Virtual-first practice is how science teachers described handling risky or inaccessible phenomena: try the materials virtually, then use the real ones ([Teachers report AI tools enable authentic, contextualized science experiences](../claims/ai-enables-authentic-contextualized-science-experiences.md) [+W], interviews only). Immersive versions bring cybersickness and cost (see the situation-table row on headsets); a browser or phone version keeps access open where devices and bandwidth are limited (proposal). No claim in this wiki measures lab-safety outcomes (incidents, safe-handling behaviour) after virtual pre-lab practice.

*A default online lab (proposal, untested as a whole).* (1) Pre-lab, 10–15 minutes: a primer on the apparatus and terms and two prediction questions answered in writing. (2) Guided run, 20–40 minutes: a goal for each run, a data table, a structured plan (hypothesis, variable to change, what would count against it) checked before the learner runs, prompts during the run, variables opened in stages if the system is complex. (3) Post-lab, 15–20 minutes: explain the result with the simulation off; a short write-up that a person (instructor or mentor) reads and answers, with an AI assistant allowed for procedure questions but not as the only reader. (4) Where a manipulative skill is an objective, a hands-on session (campus, local lab, residency or skills lab, or a home kit) assessed on real equipment before the skill is credited. (5) A delayed conceptual check two to four weeks later. Across a course: early labs fully scaffolded with worked examples of an experiment plan; middle labs with the plan template only; late labs with an open question, the learner's own plan, and the reviewer's feedback only after submission. Move a learner on when two consecutive lab write-ups meet the stated criterion without prompts.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner must do in the real setting, and whether the simulation represents it.** Decisions and communication travel well to a screen; tactile skill does not. If unstated, ask: what will the learner do in the real setting, with what hands-on component, and which decisions and cues must the simulation keep?
- **The learner's starting point on this task.** A novice needs a primer, simplified runs and guidance during the run; a learner who already performs the task needs varied, harder scenarios and less prompting. If unstated, ask: can they name the parts of the system, and can they carry out one run of the simplest scenario now?
- **Who can guide and debrief, and for how long.** Most of the support evidence comes from a facilitator or tutor responding to one learner. If unstated, ask: who will see each run, who leads the debrief, and how many minutes are there for both?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a run under stated conditions: the scenario and its settings, the prompts given, the actions taken and their order, the time taken, the errors, what the learner said in the debrief, and what changed on the next run. "Understands the system", "can transfer to the real setting" and "is overloaded" are inferred states. Success inside the simulation shows what the learner can do in that representation; it does not show what they can do in the real setting.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Succeeds in the simulation, fails on the real equipment or a standardized patient | The skill is tactile and the simulation did not represent it; the real setting adds cues or pressure the simulation lacked; success relied on in-run prompts | Give a matched real-setting task with no prompts; ask what was different; compare with a prompt-free simulated run. |
| Explores freely, clicks through, cannot say what happened | No goal for the run; the interface is consuming attention; the learner lacks the terms to make sense of what they see | Repeat with a stated goal and a primer on parts; ask the learner to narrate one decision. |
| Rates the simulation highly, shows no gain on the check | Enjoyment or presence without the target practice; the check does not match what the simulation practised; novelty | Compare a check aligned to the simulated decisions with one aligned to the real task; ask which decision the learner would now make differently. |
| Stops early, reports nausea or discomfort | Cybersickness; anxiety; an inaccessible control | Offer a seated or desktop version and see whether the learner continues and performs. |
| Repeats the same error across runs | The debrief did not reach the reasoning behind it; a misconception the simulation does not expose; the scenario rewards the error | Ask the learner to explain the decision before the next run, and give a scenario where the error produces a visible consequence (as a forestry-machine simulator does when a load falls from a wrongly opened grapple: [Simulator feedback responds authentically to student error](../claims/simulator-authentic-error-feedback-log-drops.md) [+W], an observation, not a test). |
| In a pair, group or multiuser VR run, one learner acts while the others watch or drift | No roles were assigned; the headset or interface isolates each person; the others lack the confidence or the controls to join; they are engaged but not thinking about the concept | Assign and rotate roles, then see whether the quieter learners act; ask each person separately to predict the next result and explain it afterwards. Joint activity alone did not ensure conceptual change in one study of pairs ([CSCL with simulation programs fostered conceptual change for students who were cognitively engaged](../claims/cscl-simulation-conceptual-change-cognitive-engagement.md) [~W]). |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself practice and changes the state it measures, so record each one.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Simulation Based Education Improves Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 609 studies (35,226 health-professions trainees) found technology-enhanced simulation beat no intervention, with large pooled effects on knowledge (g = 1.20), skills (about 1.09–1.18) and behaviours, and a moderate effect on patient outcomes (g = 0.50). The comparison is with no intervention, not with matched non-simulated practice; heterogeneity was large; subgroup analyses found no consistent interaction with feedback, repetition, distributed practice or curricular integration. It supports using simulation over nothing; it does not say which design features matter.
- [Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice Improves Clinical Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 14 studies found simulation-based medical education with deliberate practice beat traditional clinical education on clinical skill acquisition, d = 0.71, mostly procedural and psychomotor skills. It tests the bundle (repetition, mastery standards, immediate feedback) against ordinary training.
- [Assimilator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode than in VR (non-guided exploration) or Non VR modes](../claims/assimilators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [+M] and [Accommodator learners gained significantly more in the VR (guided exploration) mode](../claims/accommodators-gain-most-vr-guided-exploration.md) [+M]: one experiment (Chen et al. 2005) with pre-test as covariate; guided VR had the highest adjusted gains, with η² = 0.284 and 0.134 for the mode effect in the two groups. [VR (non-guided exploration) did not differ significantly from Non VR](../claims/non-guided-vr-no-better-than-non-vr.md) [~M]: free exploration in VR was no better than no VR (a nonsignificant difference, not shown equivalent). [In the VR (guided exploration) mode, assimilator and accommodator learners did not differ significantly in gain score](../claims/guided-vr-no-learning-style-difference.md) [~M]: equivalence was not tested. Together they support guidance, not immersion, as what made the difference in this one study; the learners' ages and the domain are not stated on the claim pages.
- [AI-powered simulations show short-term gains in communication confidence and structured knowledge in RCTs, with limited or inferior effects for tactile psychomotor skills](../claims/ai-simulation-short-term-gains-nursing.md) [~M] and [One RCT found AI-assisted simulation inferior to standardized patient simulation for breast self-examination skills](../claims/ai-simulation-inferior-bse-skills.md) [-M]: a mixed-methods systematic review of AI-powered nursing simulation (3 RCTs). This is the main limiting evidence: for a tactile skill, a screen-based AI simulation did worse than a standardized patient.
- [Controlled GenAI virtual-patient and communication studies reported short-term skill gains, but positive findings were not universal](../claims/genai-virtual-patient-short-term-gains-mixed.md) [~M]: a scoping review in medical education; several controlled studies reported short-term gains in history taking, communication and OSCE practice; some found no objective change, and human cases were sometimes rated more educational or authentic.
- [AI-generated and prebuilt simulations produced higher immediate conceptual-assessment scores than physical laboratory work, but the advantage did not persist in later examinations](../claims/simulation-advantage-immediate-not-sustained.md) [~M] and [The review reports that simulation games support near transfer of knowledge but evidence for long-term or far transfer is less robust](../claims/simulation-near-transfer-robust-far-transfer-weak.md) [~M]: both limit the horizon. Plan delayed checks and refreshers, and do not read an immediate gain as lasting.
- [Increased perceptual fidelity fails to reliably improve serious game learning outcomes (the fidelity fallacy)](../claims/fidelity-fallacy-serious-games.md) [~M]: a book-chapter review reporting, from earlier systematic reviews, that more realism does not reliably improve learning and can raise extraneous load and cost. [Conventional training received significantly higher ratings for perceived realism and immersion than VR-based training (r=0.774)](../claims/conventional-training-higher-realism-immersion.md) [~M]: in one within-participants comparison of 20 students, conventional radiopharmaceutical training was rated more realistic than the VR module; a VR environment is not automatically the more realistic one. [Immersive technologies are rarely adopted in robotics GBL/gamification but studies using them report heightened engagement, improved spatial reasoning, and enhanced perceived realism](../claims/immersive-tech-underused-positive-outcomes-robotics.md) [~W]: a robotics-education review whose immersive studies report positive ratings and some performance gains, with no comparison of immersion levels; it does not show that immersion, rather than the practice, made the difference.
- [Pretraining learners on a system's key parts and terms before the main lesson improved knowledge and real-world transfer in one VR experiment](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M]: 93 participants randomized; the benefit appeared on transfer to the real task, not on performance inside VR. Its table counts 4 distinct studies.
- [The review reports that participants in simulation-game conditions had significantly higher skill-based knowledge outcomes than those in non-game conditions](../claims/simulation-games-higher-skill-based-knowledge.md) [+M]: Sitzmann's meta-analysis of 65 studies, cited by a later review with no effect size printed; the comparator is non-game instruction of unstated form. It extends the comparison beyond no intervention, for applied knowledge, but does not isolate any design feature.
- [In one meta-analysis, modality-effect studies using virtual reality head-mounted displays show larger effects than studies using computer screens or audio-tapes](../claims/modality-effect-stronger-in-virtual-reality.md) [~M]: a moderator in Ginns's meta-analysis (VR d = 1.34, screens d = 0.55); it bears on how a headset simulation presents explanations, not on whether simulation helps. Marked mixed since 2026-10-19 (wave 2), for consistency with [Modality](modality.md): a later VR experiment found the reverse ([In immersive VR, visual-only text yields higher recall than audio-visual text](../claims/vr-reverse-modality-effect-recall.md) [-M]; Albus & Seufert 2023, 61 learners), so step 3 no longer tells a designer to narrate in a headset.
- Learners' and teachers' reports, used for single rows and steps and not as tests: learners valued safe, repeatable practice ([Learners valued safe, repeatable, nonjudgmental AI practice environments that reduced anxiety and bridged the theory-practice gap](../claims/ai-simulation-safe-practice-environments.md) [+W]); learners reported robotic interactions, delays and missing non-verbal cues in AI simulations ([Persistent technical limitations constitute an authenticity gap in AI-powered nursing simulations](../claims/ai-simulation-authenticity-gap.md) [~M]); teachers described virtual-first, then real, practice ([Teachers report AI tools enable authentic, contextualized science experiences](../claims/ai-enables-authentic-contextualized-science-experiences.md) [+W]) and named preparation time and confidence as barriers ([The review reports that insufficient prep time, curriculum-alignment difficulty, and low teacher confidence with new technologies are barriers to implementing simulations in K-12](../claims/teacher-time-confidence-barriers-simulation-implementation.md) [~M]); about 42% of VR users are reported to have cybersickness symptoms ([Cybersickness remains a persistent practical challenge](../claims/cybersickness-prevalence-vr-ar-immersive-learning.md) [~M]); trainees wanted more debriefing time than scheduled ([Trainees consistently wanted more debriefing time than scheduled](../claims/reflection-debriefing-time-underscheduled.md) [+W]); bridging activities between game and instruction are reported, without study detail, to increase learning ([Students learn more from games when bridging activities connect implicit game learning with formal, explicit classroom instruction](../claims/bridging-activities-connect-game-learning-to-instruction.md) [+M]); and vocational XR showed positive effects on procedural competence without a pooled effect ([Intelligent Extended Reality interventions in VET show consistent positive effects on procedural competence, practical skills, and learner motivation](../claims/ixr-vet-positive-effects-procedural-motivation.md) [+M]). Contingent scaffolding is carried from tutoring (step 3).

**Simulation-based learning in higher education ([Chernikova et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654320933544)), added 2026-10-17.** One meta-analysis of 145 studies (10,532 participants, mostly medical and teacher education) supplies the moderators below; each is a comparison between groups of studies, not a test inside a study, and all come from one source.

- [Simulation-based learning has a large positive overall effect on complex skills in higher education (g = 0.85)](../claims/simulation-based-learning-large-positive-effect-complex-skills.md) [+M]: the effect held against no intervention (g = 1.02), against differently instructed controls (g = 0.82) and against baseline (g = 0.88), so it extends the health-professions meta-analysis above beyond a no-intervention comparator.
- [Simulation effects vary by complex skill: highest for technical performance, lowest for communication skills](../claims/simulation-effects-vary-by-complex-skill.md) [~M]: g = 1.06 for technical performance, 0.82 diagnostic, 0.88 problem solving, 0.50 collaboration and 0.44 communication. It tempers the communication gains reported for AI virtual patients above.
- [High authenticity simulations show higher effects than low authenticity simulations](../claims/high-authenticity-simulations-higher-effects.md) [~M] (g = 0.86 against 0.58) and [Higher levels of technology support during simulation are associated with greater effects on learning outcomes](../claims/technology-support-associated-with-greater-effects.md) [~M] (mannequin simulators 1.07, VR 0.85, screen-based 0.68): both qualify the fidelity statement in the conditional relationship. Authenticity here means how fully the situation represents the real one, which is close to "keeps the decisions and cues" in step 1, and the technology contrast is between categories of studies whose tasks also differ; neither isolates visual realism, so the fidelity-fallacy reading stands for appearance and the decisions-and-cues reading is strengthened.
- [Live simulations with real patients showed the greatest effects among simulation types](../claims/simulation-type-moderates-effectiveness.md) [~M]: document-based simulations (working on X-rays, student homework and similar documents) were the least effective type (g = 0.31).
- [Longer simulation duration is associated with higher effects, but evidence for simulations over one month is inconclusive](../claims/longer-simulation-duration-higher-effects.md) [~M]: under an hour g = 0.65; several hours to a day 0.81. It bears on step 9's arc; it does not set a dose.
- [Prior knowledge moderates scaffolding effectiveness](../claims/prior-knowledge-moderates-scaffolding-effectiveness.md) [~M] and [Presence versus absence of individual scaffolding types did not support the added-value hypothesis, but scaffolding combinations partly did](../claims/scaffolding-added-value-partly-supported.md) [~M]: examples helped learners at lower education levels, reflection phases those at higher levels, and prompts on their own were associated with smaller effects (g = 0.65 with prompts against 0.92 without). This complicates step 3's prompts: pair them with an example for novices, and replace them with a reflection phase for advanced learners.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually performance in the real setting: a procedure done safely, a decision made well, a conversation handled, a concept used to predict. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the simulation: to stop feeling anxious before the real thing, to pass an assessment, to try equipment they cannot otherwise reach, or to enjoy it. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values a safe place to make mistakes may want many runs with no score shown; a designer who needs certification needs a criterion and a non-simulated check. Learners' high ratings of a simulation are evidence of what they value, not of the capability.

For example, a nursing student may want to feel confident talking to a distressed patient, while the course objective is a correct breast examination. An AI virtual patient serves the first; the second needs a standardized patient or model. Record both, so that confidence gained in conversation is not read as the examination skill.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if simulation practice shows no advantage over equally long non-simulated practice with the same feedback (the one moderator in the wiki that comes close, active-method comparison groups in simulation-game studies, already shows none), on aligned measures in the real setting; if gains seen at immediate tests regularly disappear at a delay even with refreshers; or if free exploration in a well-designed simulation matches guided exploration in further experiments. It should strengthen, and become more specific, if experiments vary one feature at a time (debrief or none, guided or free, high or low fidelity, one run or several) and find that feature matters. If immersive VR proves better than a desktop version of the same scenario with guidance held equal, the fidelity statement above should change.

For laboratories, the substitution rule above (a guided virtual lab may stand in for concepts, not for manipulative skill) should change if a trial comparing a guided virtual lab with an equally guided hands-on lab finds no difference on a real-equipment skill test, or finds the conceptual equivalence fails at a delay; it should change for remote labs and technology sandboxes only once claims about them exist in this wiki.

Success inside the simulation, immediate performance outside it, delayed retention, transfer to the real setting and effects on patients or clients are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of runs, no debrief length and no fidelity level.

## Description
Simulations and immersive virtual environments let learners practice decisions, procedures, and interpretations inside a designed representation of reality. Their value is not novelty by itself. They are useful when they make otherwise inaccessible experiences visible, repeatable, safer, or more manipulable than the real setting would allow. Good simulations help learners coordinate knowledge, perception, and action under conditions that resemble authentic performance.

These environments can support motivation and transfer because learners are doing something closer to the eventual task, not merely hearing about it. At the same time, simulation quality varies widely. If the interface is confusing, the scenario is unrealistic, or the task lacks debriefing and feedback, the medium can add distraction without improving learning.

## Implications
Simulations and immersive environments work when they let learners rehearse meaningful whole performances under conditions that are safer, cheaper, or more repeatable than real life [A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~M]. They are especially useful for novices when complexity is staged and support is available [Part-task practice reduces cognitive load for absolute novices during initial skill acquisition.](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M], and when coaching or debriefing responds to how the learner actually performed [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]. High fidelity alone is not enough: if the interface is confusing or the scenario misrepresents the real task, the medium adds distraction rather than transfer.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A task worth simulating**: The simulation should represent decisions, dynamics, or constraints that matter in real performance.
- **Guidance and debriefing**: Learners need framing before the experience and reflection or feedback after it.
- **Usable interface and access**: Technical barriers should not consume the cognitive resources needed for learning.
- **Alignment with real practice**: The environment should preserve the important structure of the task, not only its surface features.
#### Constraints
- **Technology overload**: Rich media can increase extraneous load if navigation, controls, or visuals are poorly designed.
- **False authenticity**: Simulations that oversimplify key variables can mislead learners about real practice.
- **Equity and access barriers**: Devices, bandwidth, motion sensitivity, and disability access all shape usefulness.
- **No transfer bridge**: Learners may enjoy a simulation without generalizing from it unless instructors make the connections explicit.

### Target Learners
- **Learners preparing for complex applied performance**: Useful when practice in the full real setting is costly, risky, or unavailable.
- **Novices who need safe rehearsal**: Simulations can provide repeatable attempts before real-world consequences are introduced.
- **Learners benefiting from multimodal representation**: Spatial, procedural, and interactive tasks may become easier to perceive and test.
- **Adult and professional learners**: Strong fit for workforce, technical, clinical, and community-based scenarios.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Applied decision making**: Practicing judgments under realistic constraints.
- **Procedural fluency in context**: Coordinating multiple steps or cues inside a meaningful scenario.
- **Transfer to performance settings**: Moving from training conditions to authentic application.
- **Reflection and revision**: Using debriefing to improve later decisions or strategies.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Experiential learning perspectives — learning strengthens when learners act, observe consequences, and reflect.
- Situated learning perspectives — performance is easier to transfer when it is learned in context-rich conditions.
- Multimedia and cognitive load perspectives — simulations can support learning when information is integrated clearly and unnecessary complexity is controlled.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- More immersion is not automatically better; fidelity only helps when it supports the learning objective.
- Simulations should often be sequenced from simpler to more complex cases, especially for novices.
