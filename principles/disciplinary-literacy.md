# Disciplinary Literacy

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (7 for) · 11 studies (6 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 11 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **reading, writing and reasoning with a discipline's own texts as that discipline does**: sourcing and corroborating documents in history, reasoning from sets of texts to an explanatory model in science, justifying claims in mathematics. For a learner who reads adequately in general but meets the texts of a discipline as a `novice` (they summarise a primary source as if it were a textbook, take an account at face value, or treat a scientific text as a list of facts), instruction that names the discipline's reading and reasoning practices, models them on the discipline's own texts and has learners practise them on new texts over a semester or longer is expected to improve comprehension of new texts in that discipline and the practised kind of disciplinary thinking (`near-transfer`), compared with textbook-led or usual instruction in the same subject.

**What the evidence here tests.** Two studies in the wiki compare a disciplinary-literacy curriculum with usual instruction ([Disciplinary Literacy Instruction Improves Comprehension](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]): a cluster-randomised trial in 24 high schools, in which a semester of text-based investigation in ninth-grade biology produced small gains in comprehension of new science texts (effect sizes 0.26 and 0.32) and **no gain in students' explanatory essays**; and a quasi-experiment in five urban high schools, in which a six-month document-based history curriculum improved eleventh graders' historical thinking, factual knowledge and general reading comprehension over textbook instruction (no effect size in the abstract read). A world-history curriculum evaluation found a gain in one of six historical thinking skills and comparable results on the other five (below). So the relationship is tested for `adolescent` learners in `classroom` settings, against usual instruction, at the end of a semester or school year.

**What it does not test.** No claim in the wiki compares disciplinary-literacy instruction with well-taught **generic** comprehension-strategy instruction, which is the comparison the page's name implies; none tests the teacher's disciplinary knowledge as a moderator, younger learners, adults, or disciplinary writing as an outcome (the one writing measure showed no gain). The default design below is therefore a proposal built around the tested curricula, with each step's evidence stated.

**A limit worth keeping in view.** Disciplinary practices are specific. In an expert–novice comparison recorded in the wiki, PhD historians judging live websites stayed on the page and were misled by logos and domain names, while fact checkers who left the site did better ([One-session checklist instruction ...](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [~M]). Sourcing a historical document and judging an unfamiliar website are different practices, and expertise in one did not carry to the other. Expect gains in the practice taught, on the kind of text practised, not a general rise in "critical reading".

**How the converted siblings sit beside this.** [Epistemic Cognition](epistemic-cognition.md) owns the standards for judging a kind of evidence in any domain; this page applies them to a discipline's own texts and genres. [Evaluating Sources](evaluating-sources.md) owns judging unfamiliar web sources by lateral reading, which is a civic practice rather than a disciplinary one. [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md) and the [Cognitive Apprenticeship pattern](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) own how modelled support is withdrawn; this page uses them for the hand-over and does not repeat them. [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and [Cognitive Load Management](cognitive-load-management.md) own how a difficult text is made workable.

## Default design

The page's earlier guidance and the folded history-imagination page, made concrete as a default a designer can act on. Each step is a proposal unless a claim is linked beside it; the tested curricula support the package (explicit practices, the discipline's texts, a semester or more), not each step.

1. **Name one disciplinary practice at a time, and choose the texts for it.** Pick the move the unit will teach (in history: sourcing, corroboration, contextualisation, continuity and change; in science: reasoning from several texts to an explanation; in mathematics: justifying a claim to a sceptic) and collect the discipline's own texts that call for it (primary documents, data, short research reports, proofs), not a textbook summary of them. Evidence for the package: [Disciplinary Literacy Instruction Improves Comprehension](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Teach and assess each practice on its own: sophistication in one second-order concept does not imply it in another ([Dimensions of historical thinking are conceptually decoupled](../claims/historical-thinking-dimensions-conceptually-decoupled.md) [~M], a review reporting British progression research).
2. **Model the practice aloud on a real text (10–15 minutes).** The teacher reads a document or text set as an insider would, saying the questions asked (who wrote this, when, for whom, what else says so) and marking the text where each move happens ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), [Annotating](annotating.md)). Untested as a step; from the earlier page.
3. **Set an expert reading beside a novice one (10 minutes).** Show two readings of the same text, one that takes it at face value and one that sources and corroborates it, and ask learners what the second did that the first did not. Evidence, carried from general case comparison: comparing cases side by side improved learning and transfer by a moderate average amount over studying them one at a time ([Comparing cases side by side ...](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]; 57 experiments pooled, plus two experiments; not tested on disciplinary reading, and in one algebra study comparison raised procedural knowledge, not conceptual).
4. **Guided practice on a new text set (20–30 minutes per lesson).** Learners apply the practice to two to four short texts in pairs, using a question card for the practice (for history, two question sets: probing questions that mine the source, check its creator's bias and seek corroboration, and general questions on what the author was thinking and what was left out), and recording each answer with a supporting quotation. From the folded page: [the two-question-set strategy for imagining the past](../strategies/strategy-imagining-past-two-question-sets.md) (a strategy page; no claim cited there).
5. **Anchor every interpretation in the sources (the folded page's recommendation).** When learners reconstruct what happened or what someone thought, require that each part of the account cite a source and a place and time, so another reader could check it; an account with no link to evidence is returned as speculation, not marked wrong for imagining. Basis: theoretical only, from Collingwood as reported by one paper: the historian's imagined picture is legitimate when localised in real space and time and tied to evidence ([Historical imagination ... constrained by evidence](../claims/historical-imagination-evidence-constrained-reconstruction.md) [+W]); the same paper argues this gives grounds for constructivist history teaching ([Collingwood's ideas ... constructivist approaches](../claims/collingwood-supports-constructivist-history-teaching.md) [+W]).
6. **Argue from the texts in the discipline's genre.** End each text set with a short written or spoken argument (claim, evidence from the texts, reasoning, a reply to a counterclaim) in the form the discipline uses ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)). Evidence, carried from argumentation generally: online scientific argumentation activities had a medium positive effect on argument skill and content learning, and multiyear dialogic argumentation transferred to better individual essays on new topics ([Argumentation Improves Reasoning](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]). Plan explicit support for moving from discussion to independent writing: the science trial above found no gain in essays and its authors attribute that to too little such support.
7. **Plan the arc across a semester, not a lesson.** A proposed arc (untested as a schedule; the tested curricula ran a semester or six months): weeks 1–3, one practice modelled every lesson on short teacher-chosen texts, with the question card and the teacher's think-aloud; weeks 4–8, learners lead the practice in pairs on longer or less familiar text sets, the teacher prompting only at the step where pairs stall; from week 9, learners choose or find the texts, run the questions without the card, and write the argument alone. Add the next practice once most learners apply the current one unaided on a new text set; move an individual learner on after two consecutive unaided text sets in which every interpretation is tied to a source. Withdrawing support follows [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md).
8. **Assess on new texts, at the end of the unit and again later.** Check comprehension and the practised skill on texts the learners have not seen, scoring each practice separately, and keep a writing measure separate from the comprehension measure. From the tested studies' designs; the horizon beyond a semester is untested here.

When a condition is not met: if the teacher cannot yet model the discipline's reading, start from a published curriculum's modelled lessons and annotated texts rather than improvising generic strategies under a disciplinary label (untested proposal; the earlier page warned that superficial "disciplinary-flavoured" activities without real epistemic work produce little benefit); if learners still labour over individual words, give fluency and vocabulary support alongside, not instead (row below).

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **The learner's starting reading.** A learner who cannot yet decode the text fluently has nothing left for disciplinary reasoning; one who reads well in general but treats sources as neutral is the learner the tested curricula served; one already reading as an insider needs harder texts, not more modelling. If unstated, ask: given one short primary text from this discipline, can the learner read it aloud, summarise it, and say who wrote it and why it matters?
- **Who can model the discipline's reading.** The practices are taught by someone who uses them. If unstated, ask: who will read a text aloud as a historian, scientist or mathematician would, and do they have the texts and lessons to do it?
- **Which practice, on which texts, measured how and when.** Gains appeared in the practice and the kind of text taught (one skill of six in one evaluation; comprehension but not writing in another). If unstated, ask: which disciplinary practice should learners be able to use unaided, on what new texts, assessed how, and when?

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is a learner's response to a text under stated conditions: which text, which question was asked, whether the question card or a model was available, what the learner wrote or said, and which sources they cited. "Reads like a historian" and "has the disciplinary practice" are inferred states, and they are inferred per practice: a learner can source well and contextualise poorly ([conceptually decoupled](../claims/historical-thinking-dimensions-conceptually-decoupled.md) [~M]).

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Summarises the document's content and never asks who wrote it | The practice was not taught or not yet transferred; the learner treats all texts as textbooks; the text was too hard to get past its content | Ask the sourcing question directly on a short, easy text; if they answer it there, the issue is transfer or load, not the concept. |
| Judges past people as foolish or immoral by present standards | Presentism, a common starting state ([novice presentism](../claims/novice-presentism-common-sense-historical-understanding.md) [~M]); missing knowledge of the period; the task invited a verdict | Give one source on what people at the time believed, then ask the question again; compare the answers. |
| Writes a vivid reconstruction with no citations | Imagination unanchored by evidence; learner did not know citing was required; could not locate supporting text | Ask them to mark, in the source, where each part of their account comes from; the parts they cannot mark show which explanation holds. |
| Good discussion of the texts, weak essay | The writing step was not supported; genre conventions unknown; time | Give the same argument as a spoken answer, then a writing frame for the genre; compare. |
| Applies the history question card to a website, or the web checklist to a primary source | Practice learned as a routine without its purpose; the two kinds of text were not distinguished | Give one of each and ask which questions fit which, and why. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself practice, so record it, and keep the remaining explanations open.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Disciplinary Literacy Instruction Improves Comprehension](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]: two studies. Goldman et al. (2019): cluster-randomised trial, 24 high schools, 964 ninth-grade students; Project READI biology for a semester against business as usual; on texts about topics neither group had studied, effect sizes 0.26 (multiple-choice comprehension) and 0.32 (multiple-text comprehension); no gain in the concepts or connections in explanatory essays. Reisman (2012): quasi-experiment, 236 eleventh graders in five San Francisco high schools; six-month Reading Like a Historian curriculum against textbook lessons; significant effects on historical thinking, transfer to contemporary issues, factual knowledge and general reading comprehension; effect sizes not recorded (abstract only). The claim page's Discussion still says the page "has no curated evidence entries", which is stale. Marked M, below its cap, because one study is a quasi-experiment read from the abstract and the other found small effects on one outcome and none on writing.
- [Students learning from World History Project ... continuity and change over time](../claims/whp-students-continuity-change-outcomes.md) [+M] and [comparable competency ... in the other five historical thinking skills](../claims/whp-comparable-other-five-skills.md) [~M]: one evaluation of one curriculum, 1,509 rubric-scored work samples from 25 teachers; gains in continuity and change over time (g = .60 summative, .36 everyday work), none detected for argumentation, causation, comparison, contextualisation or sourcing. Rigour coded r1. It supports expecting gains practice by practice, not across the board.
- [British empirical research concluded that students do not naturally progress in historical understanding with age but because of targeted instruction](../claims/targeted-instruction-drives-historical-thinking-progression.md) [+M]: a narrative review attributing the conclusion to decades of British research (SCHP, CHATA). It supports teaching the practices explicitly rather than waiting for maturity; second-hand.
- [Dimensions of historical thinking are conceptually decoupled](../claims/historical-thinking-dimensions-conceptually-decoupled.md) [~M]: the same review, reporting Lee & Ashby; sophistication in one second-order concept does not imply it in another. It qualifies any single "historical thinking" score.
- [Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]: three studies of teaching professional fact-checkers' moves (a district-wide matched-control field study, a four-section randomised pilot with a five-week posttest, and a single-group study). It shows an expert practice can be taught in two to eight lessons, but the practice is civic web evaluation, not reading a discipline's texts. The earlier version of this page linked it as evidence that "expert historians use discipline-specific sourcing and corroboration heuristics that novices lack"; the claim page does not say that.
- [One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction ... did no better than the alternative methods](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [~M]: its Wineburg & McGrew (2019) entry, an expert/novice think-aloud with 10 PhD historians, 10 fact checkers and 25 undergraduates, records that historians stayed on the site and were misled by on-page features. The limit on this page: disciplinary reading expertise did not carry to a different kind of text.
- [Comparing cases side by side improves learning and transfer by a moderate average amount ...](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 57 experiments (d = .50) and two experiments; supports the expert-beside-novice contrast as a general move, untested on disciplinary reading; in algebra the gain was procedural, not conceptual.
- [Argumentation Improves Reasoning](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 46 studies of online scientific argumentation (g ≈ 0.6) and a multiyear dialogic-argumentation quasi-experiment with transfer to essays; supports the argument step, in science and social issues.
- [Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]: carried from cognitive-load studies to the decision to reduce modelling for advanced readers; gives no crossover point.
- [Historical imagination ... legitimate rather than fictional when constrained by evidence](../claims/historical-imagination-evidence-constrained-reconstruction.md) [+W] and [Collingwood's ideas ... sound reasons for constructivist approaches](../claims/collingwood-supports-constructivist-history-teaching.md) [+W]: one theoretical paper (Lemisko 2004), coded q1. They give a rationale for the evidence-anchored reconstruction step; they test nothing.

Learners, disciplines and outcomes differ across these claims (ninth-grade biology, eleventh-grade history, world-history work samples, web evaluation), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a gain beyond a school year, and none compares disciplinary with generic strategy instruction.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually unaided use of a named disciplinary practice on new texts of that discipline, assessed by a stated task at a stated time. Ask separately what the learner wants from these texts: to pass the course's exam, to find out what really happened in a story that matters to their family or community, to read research in the job they want, to argue a case well. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants the exam may want the practice reduced to a template; one who wants the story may resist sources that complicate it, and that resistance is where corroboration earns its place.

For example, a history class's objective may be sourcing and corroborating primary documents on a migration, while a student wants to know why their grandparents left. A text set that includes a family letter or oral-history excerpt (with the family's consent) serves both, and the sourcing questions apply to it as to any document; record whether the learner uses them there too.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with well-taught generic comprehension-strategy instruction, on the same texts and outcomes, show no advantage for disciplinary instruction; if gains on new texts vanish at a delay beyond the course; or if teachers without deep disciplinary knowledge, using a published curriculum, produce the same gains (then teacher knowledge is not the boundary the earlier page assumed). If disciplinary writing improves only when explicit writing support is added, the default design's writing step becomes required rather than recommended. If the expert–novice reading contrast adds nothing to modelling alone, drop it. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "superficial implementation" after the fact.

Comprehension of new texts, the practised disciplinary thinking skill, disciplinary writing, factual knowledge and use outside the course are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose per practice and no criterion for moving on.

## Examples

### Illustrative

**[Historical Document Analysis (Stanford History Education Group)](https://sheg.stanford.edu)** — The Reading Like a Historian curriculum models sourcing, corroboration, and contextualization with primary documents, then has students apply the same heuristics to new documents. Breakstone, Smith & Wineburg report gains in historical thinking relative to textbook-based instruction.

**[Civic Online Reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — Teaches the lateral-reading and source-evaluation strategies that professional fact-checkers use, with documented improvements in learners' evaluation of online information [+M].

**Science notebooks and claim–evidence–reasoning frameworks** — Learners record observations, construct claims, and justify them with evidence in the genre conventions of scientific writing, practicing disciplinary argumentation rather than generic summary writing.

**Mathematical justification routines** — Learners critique and construct mathematical arguments ("Convince a skeptic"), reading and producing proofs as disciplinary texts rather than only solving exercises.

**Annotated journal-article reading in undergraduate STEM** — Students read a primary research article with instructor annotations explaining how a scientist reads (methods critique, results interpretation), then apply the same moves to a second article — a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)-then-practice structure.

## Description
Disciplinary literacy is the principle that literacy instruction in subject areas should teach the specialized ways of reading, writing, thinking, and communicating that define each discipline. Instead of treating "reading strategies" as content-neutral tools applied uniformly across subjects, it positions learners as apprentices to disciplinary practices: interrogating sources like historians, weighing evidence like scientists, or evaluating arguments like literary critics. The recommendation is to make these discipline-specific practices explicit, model them, and give learners supervised practice in authentic disciplinary tasks.

## Implications

Disciplinary literacy reframes content-area literacy from a set of generic strategies into apprenticeship in a discipline's epistemic practices, drawing on [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) and [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md). Shanahan & Shanahan (2008) showed that literacy demands become increasingly specialized as learners move into advanced study, so generic strategies lose traction precisely where disciplinary ones matter most [+M]. Expert-novice comparisons in history demonstrate that discipline-specific heuristics — sourcing, corroboration, contextualization — distinguish experts from even strong general readers [Expert historians use discipline-specific sourcing and corroboration heuristics that novices lack.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M], and instruction in these heuristics measurably improves learners' evaluation of documents [+M]. Because disciplinary practices are socially constituted, teaching them well requires making expert reasoning visible through [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) and structured [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md), not merely assigning texts.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructors with genuine disciplinary knowledge who can model expert reading and reasoning ([Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md))
- Authentic disciplinary texts and tasks — primary sources, data sets, journal articles — rather than textbook summaries ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](authentic-audiences-purposes.md))
- Explicit modeling of disciplinary heuristics ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)) paired with guided practice ([Guided Practice](guided-practice.md))
- Text scaffolds that manage difficulty without replacing the discipline's texts ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md), [Annotating](annotating.md))
- Imaginative reconstruction must be anchored in primary source evidence and referenced so others can re-imagine the account

#### Constraints
- Less effective when teachers lack deep disciplinary knowledge; generic strategy instruction is a safer default in that case [-M]
- Can overload novices if authentic texts are used without scaffolding, since disciplinary texts carry high intrinsic load [~M]
- Inappropriate when the goal is broad general literacy rather than disciplinary competence — generic strategies remain useful for everyday texts [~M]
- Requires instructional time that competes with content coverage; superficial "disciplinary-flavored" activities without real epistemic work produce little benefit [-W]
- Imagination unmoored from sources is treated by Collingwood as fantasy rather than historical knowledge

### Target Learners
- Upper secondary and postsecondary learners entering specialized coursework
- Learners who read adequately in general terms but struggle with discipline-specific texts and conventions
- Advanced learners for whom generic comprehension strategies have plateaued
- Less suitable for early readers still building foundational decoding and general comprehension skills
- K-12 social studies and history students

### Target Learning Objectives
- Discipline-specific comprehension: reading primary sources, data, proofs, or criticism as an insider would
- Epistemic practices: sourcing, corroborating, evaluating evidence, constructing discipline-appropriate arguments ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Disciplinary writing and communication in authentic genres
- Developing a disciplinary identity and disposition toward evidence
- Constructing evidence-based understandings of the past
- Historical thinking and critical inquiry

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../theories/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — disciplinary literacy is apprenticeship in expert practices: modeling, coaching, articulation, and fading within authentic disciplinary work
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — disciplinary practices are learned through legitimate participation in the discipline's discourse community, not through decontextualized strategy drills
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — learners build disciplinary understanding by actively doing epistemic work (interpreting sources, arguing from evidence) rather than receiving summaries

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — authentic disciplinary texts impose high intrinsic load; without [Cognitive Load Management](cognitive-load-management.md) and scaffolding, novices may flounder rather than apprentice

## Examples

### Illustrative

**[Historical Document Analysis (Stanford History Education Group)](https://sheg.stanford.edu)** — The Reading Like a Historian curriculum models sourcing, corroboration, and contextualization with primary documents, then has students apply the same heuristics to new documents. Breakstone, Smith & Wineburg report gains in historical thinking relative to textbook-based instruction.

**[Civic Online Reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — Teaches the lateral-reading and source-evaluation strategies that professional fact-checkers use, with documented improvements in learners' evaluation of online information [+M].

**Science notebooks and claim–evidence–reasoning frameworks** — Learners record observations, construct claims, and justify them with evidence in the genre conventions of scientific writing, practicing disciplinary argumentation rather than generic summary writing.

**Mathematical justification routines** — Learners critique and construct mathematical arguments ("Convince a skeptic"), reading and producing proofs as disciplinary texts rather than only solving exercises.

**Annotated journal-article reading in undergraduate STEM** — Students read a primary research article with instructor annotations explaining how a scientist reads (methods critique, results interpretation), then apply the same moves to a second article — a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)-then-practice structure.
