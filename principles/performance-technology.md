# Performance Technology

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)

## Description
Performance Technology (also called Human Performance Technology, HPT) holds that instructional designers should not assume training is the answer to a performance problem until they have analyzed the gap between desired and actual performance and identified its cause. HPT emerged in the 1960s–70s out of behaviorism — its "father," Thomas Gilbert, was a student of B.F. Skinner — and is grounded in general systems theory: any solution must be understood as operating inside a larger system of people, processes, and organizational culture, not in isolation (Stefaniak, 2018).

Where instructional design assumes instruction is the answer and designs it well, HPT starts a step earlier by asking whether instruction is the right answer at all. A **performance analysis** compares the desired and actual states of performance to locate the gap; a **cause analysis** then identifies why the gap exists — is it a skills/knowledge deficit (which instruction can fix), or a deficit in environment, incentives, tools, or feedback (which it cannot)? Gilbert's (1978) **Behavioral Engineering Model** is the classic tool for this diagnosis, separating causes into environmental supports (information, resources, incentives) and the individual's own repertoire (knowledge/skill, capacity, motives).

When the cause is not a knowledge/skill gap, the appropriate intervention is **non-instructional**: job aids, workplace redesign, electronic performance support systems, feedback systems, communities of practice, or process re-engineering, rather than a training course.

Rossett and Schafer (2007) offer a concrete taxonomy for one of these non-instructional interventions, Performance Support Tools (PSTs), which they distinguish sharply from instruction: "Instruction is not performance support. It is planned experience that enables an individual to acquire skills and knowledge to advance the capability to perform" (p. 5). PSTs split into **sidekicks** (real-time, in-the-moment job aids used concurrently with a task, like GPS turn-by-turn navigation) and **planners** (used in advance to access community-curated knowledge before starting a task, like planning a driving route before a trip). Effective PSTs are characterized by four factors: **convergence** (proximity — the support is situated where the task and performer actually are), **simplicity** (focused on the here-and-now task, not a broader curriculum), **relevance** (the right tool for the performer's specific goal), and **personalization** (the learner can adjust the level of support to their own experience, and may contribute back user-generated content that improves the tool for others).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to organizational data (or the ability to gather it) sufficient to compare desired vs. actual performance and identify root cause
- Willingness to recommend a non-instructional intervention when that is what the cause analysis indicates, even if the client requested "training"
#### Constraints
- Most directly evidenced and practiced in workplace/organizational settings; the systems-level analysis is harder to apply where the designer has no authority over surrounding organizational conditions (e.g., a single K-12 classroom)
- Does not replace instructional design where the cause genuinely is a knowledge or skill deficit — HPT is a broader diagnostic frame, not a substitute for good instructional design once training is the right call

### Target Learners
- Employees or performers embedded in an organizational system where environmental factors (resources, incentives, feedback, tools) may be limiting performance as much as or more than skill

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not learning objectives per se — performance objectives: closing a measurable gap between desired and actual organizational or individual performance, by whatever intervention (instructional or not) actually closes it

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Rooted in behaviorism and general systems theory (Stefaniak, 2018); a systems view is "holistic," focuses on interactions among elements rather than the elements themselves, and treats systems as nested within larger systems (Foshay, Villachica, & Stepich, 2014)
