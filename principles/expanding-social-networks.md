# Expanding Social Networks

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 27 claims (18 for, 8 mixed, 1 against) · 34 studies (13 qualitative, 8 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 3 associational, 2 design, 2 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 34 report an effect size · 22 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

This page owns **deliberate network-building around a learner**: adding ties to peers in the same situation, to mentors, and to people and organisations who hold the information, resources or opportunities the learner is after. For a learner, usually `adult` and often in transition (an older immigrant learning English, a job seeker or career changer, someone re-entering education, a professional isolated in their role), whose starting response is that they name few people, other than family, whom they could ask about their goal, a programme that builds repeated, structured contact with the same peers, has staff or mentors reach out to them rather than wait, and brokers introductions to relevant people with a follow-up step is expected to produce **new ties the learner actually uses**, less reported isolation and, more slowly and less certainly, `persistence` and access to the opportunities they came for. The comparison is leaving networking to the learner, or a one-off mixer. The knowledge type is mostly `attitude-motivation` and the practical `complex-skill` of asking for and following up help; the settings are `classroom`, `online-instructor-led` and `adult-workplace` programmes; the duration that matters is `days-weeks` to `term-plus`.

**No claim in the wiki tests this relationship.** No claim here compares a structured network-building programme with no programme, or one way of running it with another, on ties formed, isolation or later outcomes. What the wiki holds is descriptive and qualitative: adults in transition saying people and connections were the support they used most, teens in a library learning space not joining workshops until adults reached out, professionals reporting that repeated convenings built trust and new connections, and doctoral students judging a tutor alone insufficient against isolation. One randomised trial of a programme meant partly to build parents' social support found no lasting effect on it. So the default design below is an untested proposal, kept concrete from the earlier page and from the page folded into it on 2026-10-07 (an older-learner ESL classroom used as a remedy for social isolation).

Three conditions limit the expectation. Ties have to be relevant and trusted to be usable; networks left at information sharing produce contact without change. Programmes that "network" tend to serve those already connected, so who gets a mentor or an introduction has to be watched. And network effects are slow and indirect, so a single event or a single term should not be judged on employment or settlement outcomes.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: [Communities of Practice](communities-of-practice.md) holds sustained participation in one shared practice over a term or more, and the change in norms and identity that brings; this page is about widening *whom* a learner can reach, of which a community is one source. [Ask Experts](ask-experts.md) holds what happens inside one consultation with someone more expert; this page is about getting the learner to that person. [Check-ins](check-ins.md) holds the brief, regular contact that lets a facilitator see who is not connecting. [Cultural & Life Experiences Connections](cultural-life-experiences-connections.md) holds building from the communities learners already belong to. How a mentor's support is withdrawn is modelled in [Scaffolding and Fading](scaffolding-and-fading.md); this page does not repeat it.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier pages' guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Every quantity below is an untested proposal unless a claim is named beside it.

1. **Start from the learner's own goal and current ties (first session, 10–15 minutes).** Ask what they want access to (a job, a service, someone to practise English with, a colleague who has solved the same problem) and whom they turn to now. Record both. Untested proposal; adults in transition named people and connections as the support they used most ([People and connections were the most-used learning transition supports among HSE participant-advisors](../claims/people-connections-top-transition-support.md) [+M], interviews and co-design workshops, no comparison).
2. **Turn the goal into a specific contact goal for each period.** For example: "by the end of week 4, talk to one person who works in a hospital kitchen", rather than "network more". [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M] (a narrative review of goal-setting research on task performance, carried here to contact goals it did not test).
3. **Begin with peers in the same situation, and keep the group stable.** Put learners in small groups of three to five that meet with the same members every session for the first several weeks, with 15–20 minutes of structured exchange: each brings one problem or question and one resource or tip. Repeated contact with the same people is what professionals credited for trust and candid critique ([Interacting repeatedly across convenings built trust that enabled districts to give and receive critiques of one another's plans](../claims/repeated-convenings-trust-enables-critique.md) [+M], interviews, district leaders meeting three times a year); a facilitated community gave isolated directors a place for authentic problems ([A facilitated Community of Practice helped isolated state American Indian Education Directors build relationships that broke down silos and combated professional isolation](../claims/cop-relationships-combat-aie-director-isolation.md) [+M], participant report). Group size and minutes are untested proposals.
4. **Reach out; do not wait for learners to join.** In the first two or three weeks, a staff member or mentor makes personal contact with each learner who has not joined the structured activity, as a guide or collaborator rather than a recruiter. [In Year 1, teens left on their own did not automatically connect with structured workshops; adult mentors reaching out socially changed this](../claims/youmedia-teens-needed-mentor-outreach-to-connect.md) [+M] (observation and interviews in one library learning space for teens; carried to adults untested).
5. **Pair newcomers with veterans, and have mentors respond to what the learner actually asks.** Veteran learners mentor new arrivals; classmates help peers who are struggling; the teacher praises gains openly and plans many chances to succeed each class (from the folded older-learner ESL page; the author's own class reports gains on a standard test and citizenship passes with no comparison group: [In one practitioner's report on her own older-beginner Step One class, with no comparison group, students gained an average of 128 BEST Plus points over 6 months and two former absolute beginners passed the citizenship test](../claims/older-beginner-esl-outcome-gains.md) [+M]). A mentor's help should follow the learner's current need rather than a fixed script: [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (one-to-one tutoring of children and adolescents on academic tasks, carried here to mentoring it did not test).
6. **Broker introductions outward from about week 4.** Introduce learners to people beyond the group (a community organisation, an employer, an alumni panel, a professional association, an affinity group), one introduction at a time and matched to each learner's goal. Before each, the learner rehearses a two-sentence statement of who they are and what they are asking for. Untested proposal; participants in technology training programmes reported networking among their gains ([Participants report career transitions, certifications, networking gains, financial and social gains from tech-centered education and training programs](../claims/tech-program-accomplishments-gains.md) [+W], thematic analysis, no comparison).
7. **Build the follow-up into the design.** After each new contact the learner notes what was offered and one next step, and sends a thank-you or follow-up within a week; at the next session the facilitator asks what happened. Untested proposal; networks that stop at information sharing were reported to produce less tangible change ([Core to College network interactions were primarily information sharing and making connections between K–12 and higher education](../claims/core-to-college-interactions-primarily-information-sharing.md) [~M]).
8. **Hand initiative over across sessions.** Early sessions (about weeks 1–4): the facilitator makes introductions and is present for them. Middle sessions (about weeks 5–8): the learner initiates with a rehearsed script and the facilitator debriefs afterwards. Late sessions (week 9 on): the learner initiates unaided, brings a contact or resource back to the group, and mentors a newer learner. Move a learner on when they have made two contacts on their own initiative and followed both up. The thresholds are untested proposals; the general case for withdrawing support is [Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S], which tests worked-example fading in problem solving, not mentoring.
9. **Check monthly who has no new tie, and broker for them first.** Keep a simple list of who has a mentor, a peer partner and an outside contact. Untested proposal; access to mentors was found unequal by school poverty among principals ([Access to mentors and coaches is inequitably distributed, with high-poverty schools far less likely to have supported principals](../claims/mentor-coach-access-inequity-poverty.md) [~M], survey).

When a condition is not met: if learners are online or far apart, run the stable peer group in a channel with set participation norms (a weekly post answering one prompt and one reply to a peer) rather than an open forum; if there is no facilitator time for introductions, use veteran learners as brokers and keep step 7's follow-up as the one thing staff check.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the learner wants the ties for.** Someone practising English, someone looking for a job, and a principal looking for colleagues who solved the same problem need different people and different first contacts. If unstated, ask: what would the learner be able to do, or reach, with the right person to ask?
- **Whom the learner can already turn to.** A learner with family nearby but no one in their target field needs brokered introductions; one who is isolated altogether needs a peer group first. If unstated, ask: whom did they last ask for help about this goal, and how did it go?
- **How long contact can last, and who can broker it.** One event can make an introduction but not a tie; trust came from repeated contact with the same people. If unstated, ask: how many sessions, over how many weeks, and who has time to make introductions and check on follow-up?

| Learners are `adolescent`, in a drop-in or out-of-school space | Have adults reach out socially to each young person, as guides and collaborators, before expecting them to join workshops; do not rely on posters or sign-up sheets | [In Year 1, teens left on their own did not automatically connect with structured workshops; adult mentors reaching out socially changed this](../claims/youmedia-teens-needed-mentor-outreach-to-connect.md) [+M] (one library learning space, observation and interviews) |
| Adults in work settings: job seekers, career changers, workers in training | Tie each contact to the job goal: informational interviews with a rehearsed two-sentence ask, alumni or employer panels followed by one individual introduction each, and a written follow-up within a week | untested proposal; [Participants report career transitions, certifications, networking gains, financial and social gains from tech-centered education and training programs](../claims/tech-program-accomplishments-gains.md) [+W] reports networking as a gain, without comparison |

| Time is one session only | Make one introduction per learner to a named person or organisation that fits their goal, exchange contact details on the spot, and set one follow-up step with a date; do not claim a network was built | untested proposal; [Core to College network interactions were primarily information sharing and making connections between K–12 and higher education](../claims/core-to-college-interactions-primarily-information-sharing.md) [~M] (contact without deeper collaboration) |

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed: whom the learner names as someone they could ask about their goal (at intake and again later), the contacts they make and whether they initiated them, whether they followed up, what each contact led to, attendance at the peer group, and what they say about feeling alone. "Is isolated", "lacks social capital" and "has a network" are inferred states. A count of contacts is not a count of usable ties.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Attends sessions but makes no outside contact | Does not know whom to ask; lacks the language or the script; fears rejection; the goal is not yet clear; practical barriers (time, transport, care duties) | Ask them to name one person they would like to reach and what stops them; rehearse the ask with a peer; offer a facilitator-made introduction and see whether they take it up. |
| Makes many contacts, none followed up | Contacts were not relevant to the goal; no follow-up routine; the learner treats contact as the goal | Look at whether any contact matched the stated goal; ask what the next step with one contact would be. |
| Does not join the group at all | The activity does not look meant for them; no one has reached out personally; shame, depression or bereavement; timing | A personal invitation from a staff member or peer; ask privately about timing and what would make it easier. |
| Reports feeling less alone, but nothing has changed in access to work or services | The peer group meets the social need but brokers nothing outward; outside links not yet made; outcomes lag | Check whether any outside introduction has been made; record the outcome later rather than now. |
| Relies on one mentor for everything | Trust is limited to one person; the mentor has not passed the learner on; the learner's goal is narrow | Ask the mentor to introduce the learner to one other person and see whether the learner follows it up alone. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Asking about someone's ties is itself personal; ask in private and record only what the learner agrees to.

## Evidence and qualifications

No claim in the wiki tests a network-building design against an alternative. The claims below describe what learners and participants report, or limit the expectation.

- [People and connections were the most-used learning transition supports among HSE participant-advisors](../claims/people-connections-top-transition-support.md) [+M]: interviews (31) and co-design workshops with learners and workers grouped over 80 supports they used during transitions into eight categories; people and connections (friends, family, peers, mentors, networks) came first. It shows what adults in transition lean on; it does not show that adding ties changes outcomes.
- [In Year 1, teens left on their own did not automatically connect with structured workshops; adult mentors reaching out socially changed this](../claims/youmedia-teens-needed-mentor-outreach-to-connect.md) [+M]: over 130 hours of observation and interviews with 20 teens and 15 staff in one library learning space. It supports reaching out rather than waiting. One site, adolescents, qualitative.
- [Interacting repeatedly across convenings built trust that enabled districts to give and receive critiques of one another's plans](../claims/repeated-convenings-trust-enables-critique.md) [+M]: interview participants in three content communities, each convened three times a year, described repeated contact with the same people building trust and freer critique. Self-reported.
- [A facilitated Community of Practice helped isolated state American Indian Education Directors build relationships that broke down silos and combated professional isolation](../claims/cop-relationships-combat-aie-director-isolation.md) [+M]: directors from four states described the community as a place to share authentic problems and combat the isolation of their roles. An impact story drawing on a five-year evaluation; participant report, no effect size.
- [District leaders reported the working group created a valued cross-district network, with 96 percent forming new connections to colleagues](../claims/sel-working-group-cross-district-connections.md) [+W]: a post-convening survey; 96 percent agreed they had formed new connections and 46 percent had already been in contact. It shows contact was made and partly followed up, not what it led to.
- [The student-tutor relationship alone is judged insufficient: peer mixing and virtual communities are needed to counter isolation in online research supervision](../claims/virtual-communities-counter-supervision-isolation.md) [+M]: a consultation and case study on online supervision of research projects; consensus that students need peers in the same situation, which virtual communities make easier. A judgement, not a measured effect.
- **The limit:** [Offering FAST produced no meaningful, enduring effects on family functioning, family-school relationships, academic learning, or social-behavioral outcomes in a 60-school cluster RCT](../claims/fast-rct-no-meaningful-enduring-effects.md) [-M]: 60 Philadelphia elementary schools randomised (1,396 kindergarten pupils over two cohorts); no significant effect on parental social support, parent-school engagement or pupils' outcomes. It is the only randomised test in the wiki of a programme that sets out, among other aims, to build social ties, and it found none that lasted. It is about families of young children and one programme, so it limits rather than refutes this page. A companion claim, [Small isolated effects on family conflict (negative) and parent-teacher joining (positive) appeared but were not enduring and may be spurious](../claims/fast-isolated-small-effects-possibly-spurious.md) [~M], reports the one cohort-and-time-point effects the authors judged possibly spurious.
- **A qualification on what "belonging" work can do:** [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]: two randomised experiments with students entering college; a brief online belonging message raised first-year completion in the larger one, but only where students' groups were afforded opportunities to belong. Carried here, it suggests that a message or a mixer without real contact in the setting is unlikely to be enough.
- **Network shape:** [Network cohesion in NSI increased over time but remained low, with about 16 percent of possible school-to-school connections realized](../claims/nsi-network-cohesion-low-but-improving.md) [~M]: in a school-improvement network, connections rose over two years but most possible links were never made. Even a funded network leaves many members unconnected.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (adults in transition, teens, district leaders, state directors, doctoral students, families of kindergarten pupils), and none measures a learner's later employment, settlement or persistence against a comparison group. Do not rank them.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims the earlier page cited that are not part of the model above, each with what it is and how far it bears on this page.

- [Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — meta-analyses of self-explanation prompts while studying examples or solving problems. The earlier page used it for learners explaining their goals and problems to others; it tests understanding of academic content, not networking, and bears on this page only by analogy.
- [New network nodes were developed through field-based activities such as regional councils and faculty work groups](../claims/new-nodes-developed-through-field-based-activities.md) [+W] — a statewide college-readiness network grew new members through regional councils and faculty work groups; an evaluation observation about institutions, not learners. - [Successful program completion increased participants' self-confidence, self-esteem, and autonomy, which aided their settlement and integration into Canadian society](../claims/program-completion-increased-confidence-autonomy-settlement.md) [+W] — interviews with two immigrant women after a language-and-skills programme; it bears on confidence and settlement, and on workplace contact, not on network-building as such. Used in the low-confidence row above.
- [Participants report career transitions, certifications, networking gains, financial and social gains from tech-centered education and training programs](../claims/tech-program-accomplishments-gains.md) [+W] — thematic analysis of Black workers' and learners' accounts; networking is one of seven reported gains. Used in step 6 and the work-settings row.
- [SPICE learners report that the virtual Center reduced their rural isolation and made learning enjoyable](../claims/spice-learners-report-reduced-isolation.md) [+W] — testimonials from a family literacy programme; used in the online row.
- [HSE learners and workers most wanted more information, skills translation services, job training, mentorship, and money during transitions](../claims/top-wanted-transition-supports.md) [+M] — the same study as the people-and-connections claim: mentorship was among the five supports learners most wanted. It supports asking what the learner wants before brokering.
- [Despite strong academic qualifications, IB DP students often have limited access to the social capital needed to navigate college course selection and access faculty support](../claims/ib-dp-students-limited-social-capital.md) [+W] — a press release on a qualitative study: academic preparation did not supply the know-whom of college. It bears on why academic programmes may need a network step.
- [In the Tucson household ethnography, exchanges within households' social networks were often reciprocal and built mutual trust through shared practical activities.](../claims/household-exchange-networks-are-reciprocal-and-build-trust.md) [+W] — an ethnographic digest; trust grew through reciprocal exchange in shared practical activity. It bears on building from learners' existing networks, which [Cultural & Life Experiences Connections](cultural-life-experiences-connections.md) owns.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually a set of usable ties (a peer partner, a mentor, at least one outside contact relevant to the goal) and the learner's growing ability to make and follow up a contact unaided, recorded over weeks. Elicit separately what the learner wants: a job, company, someone who speaks their language, help with a form, colleagues who understand the work. Agreement and divergence are both observations. An older learner may value the class mostly for company and confidence, while the programme counts test gains and citizenship passes; a job seeker may want one introduction to one employer and find a peer group a waste of time. Record both, and do not read a learner's lack of interest in "networking" as a deficit: they may already have the ties they want.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of structured network-building with no programme, among comparable learners, show no difference in ties used, isolation or later outcomes; the one randomised trial in the wiki of a programme building parents' social support already points that way for families. It should change shape if one-off introductions turn out to be as useful as repeated peer contact, or if outreach to non-joiners makes no difference to who takes part. If ties formed in programmes are used mainly by learners who were already well connected, the equity step should become the centre of the design rather than a check. Do not protect the model by calling every null "too short to show network effects" after the fact; say in advance at what point, and on what outcome, the programme will be judged.

Ties formed, ties used, reduced isolation, persistence, and employment or settlement are separate claims, and the present evidence establishes none of them causally.

## Implications
Expanding social networks matters because access to opportunity is often mediated through people, not just through formal curriculum. New ties to peers, mentors, community organizations, and professional groups can increase belonging, information flow, and practical access to learning or work opportunities, especially for learners who start with limited institutional access.
