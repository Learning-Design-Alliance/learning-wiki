    Cognitive Activation¶

    Principle · All principles Evidence · 8 claims (3 for, 5 mixed) · 18 studies (6 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 associational, 1 design, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 3 of 18 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

    Conditional relationship¶

    For a learner working toward a concept or principle (a relation to be understood and then used, not a fact to be recalled), a task that can only be completed by generating something beyond what was presented (an explanation of why a step works, the difference between two cases, a prediction, a justification) is expected to improve conceptual-understanding and near-transfer more than a task of the same topic that can be completed by recall, copying or locating the answer. The best-recorded outcomes are immediate tests (immediate-performance); classroom implementation and delayed-retention are recorded much more weakly. The relationship is about the cognitive demand of the task, which is a property of what the task's output requires, not of how busy learners look or of the lesson format.

    Three conditions bound it. The learner must have enough knowledge of the relation to generate something correct: a novice asked to explain or discover with no prior explanation or example is expected to do worse, not better (see the minimal-guidance claim below). The generated output must point at the target relation; a comparison or explanation aimed at surface features consumes effort without the expected gain. And the challenge has to be supported: the gains on record are larger where learners were helped toward high-quality explanations. "Cognitive activation" as a rated dimension of teaching quality, the sense used in the classroom-observation studies listed under Key Sources, has no claim page in this wiki: nothing here tests whether lessons rated as more activating produce more learning. This page therefore states the task-level relationship and labels everything beyond it.

    Active Learning holds the broader, format-level model: replacing listening time with tasks in which learners generate, explain or apply, with feedback, raises course-end performance. That page treats the kind of engagement as one qualification among four; this page owns it as the main relationship, and it cuts across format. A lecture paused for one well-chosen "why" question can be activating; an active-format worksheet that can be completed by copying is not. Explaining Their Thinking and Analogical Reasoning cover single kinds of activating task; Cognitive Disequilibrium holds the narrower model for confronting a confident wrong explanation; Worked Examples and Scaffolding and Fading hold the guidance that makes activation productive for novices. No pattern page instantiates this principle.

    Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

    The page's earlier guidance, made concrete and kept as a default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal with its evidence status beside it; no claim here tests the sequence as a whole.

    1. Name the relation and write the probe first. State the concept or principle learners must use, and write one item a learner could answer only by applying it to a case not taught (a changed problem, an unfamiliar example, a "why" question). Untested here; from the earlier page's requirement that activation depends on work requiring thought.
    2. For learners new to the relation, explain or show it first. One worked example or a short explanation before any generative task. Evidence: unassisted discovery did worse than explicit instruction, while discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations did better (minimal-guidance claim, below).
    3. Choose one activating task per relation, from four kinds the earlier page named:
    4. Compare: two cases that differ on the target feature, side by side on one page, learners first asked what they share; state the principle after the comparison. Evidence: the case-comparison meta-analysis found larger gains when learners were asked to find similarities and when the principle came after the comparison.
    5. Explain: a "why" prompt at each solution step or after each section of text. Evidence: self-explanation prompts (below); the mathematics synthesis reports conceptual "why" prompts as more effective than procedural ones (Further evidence).
    6. Predict, then observe: a committed prediction before a discrepant event. Its model belongs to Cognitive Disequilibrium.
    7. Justify: an answer plus why it is warranted (justification). Untested here; from the earlier page.
    8. Check the task's output, not its activity. Ask: could a learner finish this by copying, highlighting, locating a sentence or recalling a phrase? If yes, it is at most "active"; rewrite it so the output has to go beyond the material. Evidence: the constructive-engagement claims (below), with the caveat that the ordering has not held in every setting.
    9. Support the quality of what learners generate. After each attempt, show a model explanation or the key difference, and have the learner compare it with their own. Evidence: the mathematics synthesis reports larger immediate effects when learners were scaffolded toward high-quality explanations.
    10. When most learners stall or produce wrong output, step back, not forward. Give a worked step or a fuller explanation of the same relation, then a matched task. Untested proposal; follows from step 2.
    11. Judge on the probe from step 1, at the horizon the objective sets. A good immediate result does not show the understanding lasts; plan a delayed, changed item if retention matters. Evidence: the self-explanation and case-comparison syntheses both report effects mainly or more strongly on immediate tests.

    Fitting the design to a situation¶

    The three facts that most change the decision:

    • Prior knowledge of this relation. Novices need an explanation or example before generating; learners who already hold the relation can be asked to compare or justify at once. If unstated, ask: can they solve or explain a routine case of this, unaided, today?
    • The kind of goal. Activation is aimed at concepts, principles and flexible use of procedures; for facts or vocabulary, retrieval with feedback is the more direct move. If unstated, ask: what should a learner be able to do with this on a case they have not seen?
    • The outcome and horizon that count. The evidence is strongest for immediate tests; if the brief cares about a delayed exam or use at work, plan the probe and the delay explicitly. If unstated, ask: when, and on what task, will anyone check whether this was learned?

    Observation, state and explanation¶

    Record what a learner produces for a stated task under stated conditions: the explanation, comparison, prediction or justification itself, what help was available, and what the learner then does on an independent probe of a changed case. "Cognitively activated" and "thinking deeply" are inferences, not observations. Effort, time on task, talk and confusion are behaviours; whether the learner generated an inference about the target relation is a hypothesis that the output can support and the behaviour cannot. Expertise is task-specific: a learner experienced in the subject may still be a novice for this relation.

    The rows below are proposals for reading responses to an activating task. They have not been tested with learners.

    Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
    Answers the task correctly but cannot explain or apply the relation on a changed case The task could be completed without the relation (copying, locating, pattern-matching on surface features); understanding during the task that was not retained; the probe changed more than the relation Ask whether the task's answer was present in the material; give a changed-case probe immediately and again later. Immediate success with later failure points to retention, not the task.
    Produces an explanation that restates the material ("because the formula says so") The prompt asks "what" rather than "why"; the learner lacks the relation; the learner holds it but cannot articulate it Rephrase the prompt to name the relation ("why does this step keep the equation balanced?"), then offer a choice between two explanations. Choosing correctly without producing one suggests an articulation limit.
    Stalls or abandons a comparison or explanation task Missing prior knowledge (novice); too many elements to hold at once (two cases differing on several features); unclear instructions Give a worked example of the same relation and a matched task; separately, reduce the cases to one differing feature. Which change helps points to knowledge or load.
    Generates confidently, but the output is wrong A misconception the task has surfaced; a correct idea poorly expressed; attention to the wrong feature Ask for a prediction on a case the wrong and the right idea decide differently, give corrective feedback, re-probe on a new case. (The narrower model is on Cognitive Disequilibrium.)
    A class's results improve after lessons are redesigned to be "more activating" The tasks' demand; more time on the topic; more feedback or probes that came with the redesign; a different assessment Record every feature that changed. Only a comparison holding time, feedback and assessment constant can credit the task demand.

    These probes can shift confidence among explanations; one contrast does not identify a cause, and a probe is itself a learning event. Record what remains unresolved.

    Evidence and qualifications¶

    No claim in the wiki tests cognitive activation as stated: none compares lessons or tasks rated higher and lower in cognitive demand, with topic and time held constant, on understanding and transfer. The claims below test kinds of activating task and the conditions on them.

    • Self-explanation prompts improve learning from worked examples [+S]: the most direct test of one activating task. A meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports found prompting learners to self-explain while studying or solving problems improved learning by g = .55 against no prompts, across task types, subjects and levels of education. A mathematics meta-analysis found small-to-moderate immediate gains in procedural knowledge, conceptual knowledge and procedural transfer, larger when learners were scaffolded toward high-quality explanations, with much more limited evidence for classroom settings or delayed tests. A small experiment with 24 eighth graders found prompted line-by-line explanation of a circulatory-system text gave larger pre-post gains than reading it twice. The meta-analysis entry passes the source check (abstract); moderator results are not reported on the page.
    • Comparing contrasting cases improves learning [+S]: a second activating task. A meta-analysis of 57 experiments (336 tests) found case comparison beat other ways of studying cases and traditional instruction (d = .50), more so when learners were asked to find similarities, when the principle came after the comparison, for perceptual content and on immediate tests; the comparison conditions are mixed. Undergraduates who compared two negotiation cases on one page transferred the principle to a new negotiation more often (48% against 19%) than those who studied them separately. Seventh graders comparing algebra methods gained procedural knowledge and flexibility but no more conceptual knowledge. Two of three entries pass the source check (abstract).
    • Constructive learning beats active and passive learning [~M]: the engagement ordering this page relies on to tell activating from merely active tasks. A theoretical review with an embedded lab study reports learning rising from passive to active to constructive to interactive engagement; a large multilevel field study in university classrooms supported only the end points, and not constructive over active, on perceived learning and affective outcomes. Neither reports a standardised effect. Use the ordering as a working hypothesis for classifying tasks, not as a settled ranking. Not yet checked against its sources.
    • Students learned significantly more in Constructive than Active activities [+M]: a classroom translation project in which teachers designed lessons at different engagement modes; students' pre-post learning was higher under constructive than active activities. No effect size or sample size is reported on the page. Not yet checked.
    • Teachers had minimal success designing Constructive and Interactive activities [~M]: from the same project, a limit on implementation. After professional development on the engagement framework, teachers had minimal success designing constructive and interactive lesson plans. "Activation is easier to claim than to design", as the earlier page put it, has this much support: a task's intended demand and its actual demand need checking. Not yet checked.
    • Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [~S]: the condition on challenge. Across 580 comparisons, unassisted discovery did worse than explicit instruction (d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), while discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations did better than other instruction; 112 children learned an experimental-design procedure far more often from direct instruction than from discovery. Generating with no guidance is not the activation this page means. Partly checked (1 of 3 entries pass, on abstract).

    Taken together: two kinds of activating task (explaining, comparing) have synthesis support on mostly immediate outcomes; the line between activating and merely active tasks rests on a framework whose middle steps have not held everywhere; activation without prior guidance does not help novices; and teachers find activating tasks hard to design. Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them by effect label. Retain the comparator, the instrument and its alignment, and the horizon when transporting them; a pooled average does not forecast one learner's response.

    Objective and learner-valued goal¶

    Ask what the learner wants from the task and why, separately from the designer's objective. A learner may want the answer quickly and see "explain why" as delay; a designer may want the learner to use the relation on cases the course never showed. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. Do not infer understanding from effort or from a fluent explanation of a taught case, and do not treat a wish for answers as a deficit; it may be a real goal that a fast route can serve once the relation is held.

    For example, an apprentice electrician may value getting a circuit working on site without delay, while the instructor's objective is that the apprentice can explain why a given fault trips a breaker and predict it on an unfamiliar layout. Completed wiring exercises cannot establish either aim. A prediction on an unfamiliar layout, the time taken on a routine job, and the apprentice's own account of when knowing "why" has helped on site can.

    What would revise this model?¶

    The expectation should weaken if tasks that demand generation beyond the material, compared with tasks of the same topic, time and feedback that do not, show no advantage on aligned changed-case probes. If the gains attributed to activating tasks are explained by extra time, extra feedback or test-like probes that came with them, credit those instead. If the constructive-over-active ordering keeps failing in authentic classrooms, stop using it to classify tasks and keep only the contrast between generating and not generating. If novices given explanation followed by ordinary practice do as well as novices given explanation followed by activating tasks, the claim narrows to learners who already hold the relation. If a dimension of teaching quality called cognitive activation is recorded here with its own studies, test this task-level model against it rather than assuming they agree. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as "not really activating" after the fact.

    Immediate understanding, near transfer, delayed retention and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish how much challenge is optimal, which kind of activating task is best for a given goal, or an effect for any individual learner.

    Related Principles¶

    • Active Learning — active learning becomes more valuable when the activity is cognitively demanding rather than merely participatory
    • Explaining Their Thinking — one of the strongest practical realizations of cognitive activation
    • Activation — activation of prior knowledge often serves as the entry point into deeper cognitive work

    Examples¶

    Illustrative¶

    Compare-and-contrast tasks — Learners examine two cases, representations, or solutions and explain the deeper relation or distinction between them.

    Discrepant Events — Learners encounter evidence that conflicts with their expectations and must explain the mismatch.

    Justification — Instead of giving an answer only, learners must explain why a claim, method, or interpretation is warranted.
