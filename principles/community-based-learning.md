# Community-Based Learning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 12 claims (8 for, 4 mixed) · 15 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 3 associational, 2 qualitative, 2 theoretical, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 15 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a learner whose coursework stays school-internal (completed for a grade, addressed to nobody else), giving the work a real role in a community (a partner's need, a shared responsibility, a public that receives it) may change what the learner attends to and values, and with it civic attitudes, self-reported self-efficacy and social responsibility, and the depth at which the learner connects the experience to course concepts. The relationship is proposed for `complex-skill` and `attitude-motivation` goals, mostly in `postsecondary` and `adolescent` `classroom`-plus-field settings over `days-weeks` or `term-plus`. Its conditions are that the community work is explicitly tied to the course's concepts, that reflection is structured rather than left free, that relationships with partners and mentors are supported and monitored, and that participation is equitable. Without those conditions the experience can become service without learning, or a shared activity that settles on the narrowest meaning the groups can coordinate around.

**No claim in the wiki compares community-based learning with the same content taught without it on a learning outcome.** The evidence below is associational, second-hand or a single case, and it bears on the conditions more than on the relationship itself. Content learning, transfer and delayed outcomes are not established here. No pattern page holds this policy yet. [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](authentic-audiences-purposes.md) holds the narrower conditional model of addressing a product to a real reader; this page owns the broader relationship of participation and contribution in a community, of which a real audience is one possible part, and does not repeat that model.

## Default design, while the relationship is untested

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

1. **Name the learning object before the activity.** Write down which course concepts the community work is meant to exercise, and put that connection in the syllabus and the brief. Evidence: one correlational survey found a syllabus that connects service to course content was the pedagogical item most strongly associated with civic competence (see below); a single case found a community project without a conceptual anchor converged on a narrow meaning. Neither tests the step as a design choice.
2. **Find a genuine need and agree the contribution with the partner.** The task should matter to a real group, issue or shared responsibility, and learners' contributions should matter beyond individual completion. Untested here; from the earlier page.
3. **Structure the reflection.** Give guided prompts that ask learners to analyse the experience and connect it to the concepts, not only to describe it. Evidence: one comparison of guided and free reflection in a graduate service-learning course (see below), on the reflections themselves, not on later learning.
4. **Support and watch the relationships.** Give learners a mentor or regular contact, and check how relationships with partners are going; act on conflict early. Evidence: associational only (see below).
5. **Hold a facilitated cross-group discussion** in which students, teachers and partners reflect together on what the activity is for. Evidence: the single case's own follow-up intervention, at one site.
6. **Set equitable norms and access.** Check that every learner can take part (time, transport, cost, safety, language) and that roles are not allocated by who is already confident. Untested here; from the earlier page.
7. **Assess the learning separately from the service.** Hours completed and partner satisfaction are not evidence of the learning object; score the concept use in a product or reflection against criteria. Untested here; follows from the earlier page's warning that community work can drift toward service without learning.

## Observation, state and explanation

What can be observed is what the learner does and says in and about the community work: attendance, the products delivered, the content of reflections (descriptive, analytic, integrative), what the learner says the work was for, interactions with the partner, and answers on attitude or self-efficacy surveys. "Belonging", "civic commitment", "ownership" and "transfer" are inferred states. Self-report after a chosen, enjoyable course could reflect the experience, the kind of student who enrols in it, or the wish to give a good answer. The rows below are proposals, untested with learners.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Completes the service, reflections only describe what happened | No link to the concepts was made explicit; free-form prompts invited description; the learner does not yet hold the concepts to connect | Give a guided prompt that names a concept and asks for analysis; separately, check the concept in a short low-stakes item. Compare the next reflection. |
| Group's project settles on a narrow aim (e.g. making money) instead of the intended one | No conceptual anchor; the narrow aim is easiest for students, teachers and partners to coordinate around; the partner's goal genuinely differs from the course's | Ask each group (students, teachers, partner) separately what the activity is for; run a facilitated joint discussion and see whether the framing changes. |
| Reports higher civic attitudes or self-efficacy after the course | Change from the experience; self-selection into a service course; socially desirable answering; mentor or partner relationship rather than the service itself | Compare with a pretest and with a comparable course; ask for a behavioural indicator (later participation) as well as a rating; record mentoring and partner contact. |
| Withdraws or reports conflict with the partner | Mismatched expectations; inadequate preparation for the setting; access barriers (time, transport, cost); a partner relationship that is genuinely difficult | Talk with learner and partner separately; check access conditions; see whether a changed role or added mentoring changes participation. |
| Engaged in the field, weak on the course assessment | The field work exercised different capabilities than the assessment measures; social and practical demands crowded out the concepts; the concepts are not yet learned | Compare the assessment's demands with what the field work required; probe the concept in the field context and in the assessment format. |

Any single contrast shifts confidence among these; it does not identify a cause, and asking learners to reflect is itself an intervention. Record what remains unresolved.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Service-learning within integrated projects is associated with improvements in students' self-efﬁcacy, self-esteem, social responsibility, and community participation](../claims/service-learning-improves-civic-and-personal-outcomes.md) [+W]: a university-level article's second-hand report of reviewed studies of integrated projects using service-learning. Students reported improvements in self-efficacy, self-esteem, attitudes to school and community-participation behaviours, and greater social responsibility, community awareness and teamwork. No effect sizes, sample sizes or comparators are printed; the outcomes are self-reported. The judge passed both entries on full text, which confirms the article says this, not that the underlying studies show it. It does not predict content learning.
- [A syllabus that clearly connects service work to course content shows the strongest relationship with civic competence outcomes](../claims/syllabus-service-content-connection-strongest-pedagogical-relationship.md) [+W]: Pearson correlations over a five-year course-evaluation dataset of community-based learning courses (`correlational`); the syllabus-service connection correlated with civic competence at r = .569, the strongest pedagogical item, with exploration of diversity at r = .552. It supports the content-link condition as an association with a self-reported outcome; course and student differences are not controlled. Not yet checked against its source.
- [Guided written reflection yields more analytic and integrative paragraphs than free reflection in service learning](../claims/guided-reflection-yields-more-higher-order-paragraphs-than-free.md) [+W]: journal and blog paragraphs from four sections of a graduate international service-learning course, coded by two coders. Guided reflections were 47% analytic and 27% integrative; more than half of free reflections were descriptive only and 11% off-topic. The outcome is the reflection text itself, at the time of writing; assignment to conditions and later learning are not reported. Not yet checked.
- [Service-learning students with a SLAM peer mentor scored higher on posttest civic action than non-mentored students, in a non-randomised comparison](../claims/peer-mentoring-increases-civic-action-service-learning.md) [+W]: a MANOVA of posttest civic-action scores, mentored (n = 60) against non-mentored (n = 65) groups formed by which courses had a mentor, with no pretest differences; civic action was higher with a mentor (M = 4.20 against 3.93, p = .037). Associational; it cannot attribute the difference to mentoring. Judge passed on full text.
- [Conflict and antagonism with community partners negatively correlate with social justice and diversity attitudes](../claims/community-partner-conflict-negatively-correlates-social-justice.md) [~W]: from the same study, student-partner conflict and antagonism correlated negatively with social-justice and diversity attitudes (r from −.36 to −.41). It limits the relationship: contact with a community is not uniformly positive, and a poor partner relationship travels with worse attitudes. Correlational, direction unknown. Not yet checked; its `q3` and `i1` codes look inconsistent with a correlational design and with r near .4.
- [Community projects need conceptual framing to avoid narrowing the learning object](../claims/community-projects-need-conceptual-framing-to-avoid-narrowing-the-learning-object.md) [~W]: one qualitative case (`qualitative`, `q1`) of a Finnish middle school's entrepreneurship course with local businesses and municipal partners. Without a disciplinary knowledge base the activity converged on "money-making" and left the critical dimensions the design implied unexamined; a researcher-facilitated mixed-stakeholder discussion surfaced a broader framing. It shows a mechanism at one site, not how often it happens. Not yet checked.

Taken together: the conditions (content link, structured reflection, supported relationships) have weak, mostly associational support; the outcomes are civic attitudes, self-reports and reflection quality, not tested content learning; and community contact can go badly. How far each may be carried: the two Harkins-study claims and the syllabus claim are correlational surveys of postsecondary service-learning courses and do not show that adding a mentor or a syllabus statement causes anything; the reflection claim compares reflection texts, not learning; the case study is one school. None argues against community-based learning, and none establishes it.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is a capability or understanding that the community work is meant to exercise (applying statistics to a local problem, analysing a policy, communicating with a client), judged by criteria and an instrument chosen locally. What the learner values may be the community itself: helping people they care about, being taken seriously by adults outside school, a line on a CV, or meeting a requirement with as little exposure as possible. The partner has a third goal. Ask the learner and the partner separately what the work is for; agreement and divergence are observations, and enthusiasm in the field does not show the learning object was reached.

For example, a student on a community nutrition project may value helping a food bank run its weekly distribution, while the designer's objective is analysing dietary data and the partner wants a clear leaflet. A well-received leaflet is evidence for the partner's goal, a reflection that analyses the data against the concepts is evidence for the designer's, and what the student says the project meant is evidence for theirs. Record all three rather than letting the most visible stand for the others.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of the same course with and without the community component, with comparable students, find no difference in civic or learning outcomes, or if the attitude gains disappear once self-selection and pretest differences are controlled. If explicit conceptual framing and guided reflection do not change what learners make of the experience, those conditions are not the active part and the model should say what is. If partner conflict or access barriers routinely reduce outcomes for identifiable learners, preparation, partner selection and equitable access become part of the relationship, not caveats. Do not protect the model by calling every disappointing project "tokenistic" after the fact.

Civic attitudes, self-reported self-efficacy, the quality of reflection, content learning and later participation in community life are separate claims. The present evidence touches the first three, associationally; it does not establish content learning, transfer, or how much community work is enough.

## Examples

### Illustrative

**Service-learning projects** — Learners apply course concepts through work tied to an actual community need, then reflect on both the content and the social context.

**Community partner briefs or deliverables** — Students create analyses, resources, or recommendations for an external partner, making quality and clarity matter beyond grading.

**Local inquiry with public-facing outcomes** — Learners investigate a neighborhood, organization, or social issue and present findings to a real audience rather than only to the instructor.

**[Humanizing Co-Design with Educators](../processes/humanizing-co-design-with-educators.md)** — Educators and researchers build shared community and responsibility across an extended co-design partnership.

## Implications

Community-based learning is strongest when learners are not only consuming content but participating in a setting where their work affects or serves others. The principle works by linking knowledge to belonging, contribution, and real social context. It can deepen meaning, motivation, and transfer because learning is tied to actual people, settings, and responsibilities rather than remaining purely school-internal [Whole-task performance improves transfer of complex skills to real-world settings.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+S].
