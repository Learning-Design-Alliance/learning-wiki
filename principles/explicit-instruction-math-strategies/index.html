Explicit Instruction: Math Strategies¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 20 claims (16 for, 4 mixed) · 28 studies (13 causal, 5 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 4 of 28 report an effect size · 13 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one part of explicit teaching in mathematics: teaching problem-solving strategies, including when a strategy fits and why. For a learner who can already carry out the computation and holds the basic concept (the starting response to look for is a correct calculation on bare numbers but a stalled, keyword-driven or piecemeal attempt at a word problem or non-routine problem), explicit modelling of a strategy that names its decision points, side-by-side comparison of two or more valid methods on the same problem, and guided practice in which the learner chooses and justifies a method, are expected to produce more flexible and more accurate skill-performance on new problems of the same kind (immediate-performance, near-transfer) than practising one method by rote or being left to find a method alone. The target knowledge is a procedure together with the principle that tells the learner when to use it.

The relationship is conditional on four things: whether the learner already has the conceptual footing (without it, strategy teaching tends to become rule-following, and a short problem attempt before instruction may build the concept better for older learners); which outcome counts (procedural flexibility, conceptual understanding and correct solutions behave differently in the evidence here); how many strategies are introduced at once; and the horizon.

No claim in the wiki tests this page's whole relationship, explicit strategy instruction against an alternative without it, with the learners and outcomes above. The nearest evidence tests parts of it: comparing solution methods side by side against studying them one at a time (seventh graders, algebra); one whole-problem strategy against a phrase-by-phrase translation strategy for algebra word problems (ninth graders, 1972); and a single-classroom, pre/post-only run of explicit language-focused strategy instruction for word problems that found no significant gain. The default design below is therefore a set of labelled proposals.

How the converted siblings sit around this page: Direct Instruction owns the general relationship (explain, model and guide practice for a learner with no working method, against minimally guided discovery) and its qualifications by expertise, age, outcome and horizon; this page applies that model to mathematical strategies and adds what it does not cover (teaching when and why, comparing methods, choosing among them). Productive Failure owns whether a new concept is attempted first or taught first; this page takes its answer as the rule for when to place a problem attempt before strategy teaching. Cognitive Flexibility owns the general case of holding knowledge in several forms; comparing methods here is one way to build it. Worked Examples owns studying a solution before attempting one, and the Direct Instruction pattern holds the lesson sequence as a reusable policy.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (and that of the folded page on Polya-style instruction for verbal problems), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. Session lengths and thresholds are untested proposals unless a claim is linked.

  1. Check the footing first, inside the work (a few minutes, session 1). Do not add a pre-test. Ask the learner to do one or two real items of the work in front of them and to say what the quantities mean: for a ratio or proportion strategy, ask what the ratio says ("for every 2 of this, 3 of that") and what happens to the other quantity when one doubles; for word problems, ask for the bare calculation and the same numbers in the story. If the brief says the concept must be checked first, do this before any strategy is modelled. If the calculation or the concept fails, teach it first and do not add strategies yet. Evidence: fourth graders scored much higher on computation than on word problems with the same numbers (Fourth-graders perform substantially better on computation tests than on word problems using the same numbers [+M]; one classroom of 24). Making this a task inside the work rather than a test is an untested proposal.
  2. For a new concept with secondary-age or older learners, let them try first (10–15 minutes). Give one problem the target strategy solves, let learners attempt it in pairs, and collect their methods, including wrong ones. Then teach the strategy by comparing it with their attempts. Evidence: Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S] (conceptual knowledge and transfer, not procedural skill; reversed for second to fifth graders) and PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features (contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions) are implemented [+M]. For a procedure, or for younger learners, skip this step and teach first; the decision is modelled in Productive Failure.
  3. Model one strategy, naming its decision points (10 minutes). Work a problem aloud and say, at each choice, what you looked at and why this strategy fits (for example, "the problem gives a total and asks for a part, so I write one equation for the total"). Annotate the written example with the reason, not only the step. Untested here; from the earlier page. Following the model with a closely related problem, rather than a problem first, has one supporting experiment (Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes [+M]; secondary-school novices, electrical circuits, carried to mathematics).
  4. For verbal (word) problems in algebra, teach a whole-problem strategy and keep translation as a sub-step. Teach learners to ask what is unknown, what relation links the quantities, to write one equation for the whole problem, solve it and check the answer against the story (a Polya-style approach: understand, plan, carry out, look back). Translating phrases into symbols is a step inside the plan, not the plan. Evidence: in one 1972 experiment, ninth graders taught the Polya method wrote correct equations more often than those taught the Dahmus phrase-by-phrase translation method (Polya Method students outscore Dahmus Method students on the equation criterion for verbal problems [+M]), and translation-method students often translated each phrase without combining them into one equation (Dahmus Method students translated phrases successfully but failed to combine them into a single solution equation [+W]); the two groups did not differ in solving the problems correctly (Polya and Dahmus methods do not differ on the problem solution criterion [~M]). The study's authors proposed a classroom synthesis of the two strategies, with the pure strategies kept as alternatives for students who struggle with the synthesis; that synthesis is an untested proposal.
  5. Compare methods side by side, and use the learners' own error as one of the cases (10–15 minutes, from the first session in which an error has been seen). Where an error has already been observed (a scaled recipe with one ingredient left unscaled, a ratio added instead of multiplied, one phrase translated without the others), put that attempt, anonymised, beside a correct method on the same problem and ask: where do they part, what in the problem shows which is right, and how would you check? Otherwise show two valid methods and ask which is quicker here and when you would use the other. Use at most two or three cases in one session. Evidence: Comparing cases side by side improves learning and transfer by a moderate average amount, though in one algebra study it raised procedural knowledge and flexibility but not conceptual knowledge [+S], Alternative-strategy practices are most effective for improving procedural flexibility, with weaker evidence for conceptual and procedural knowledge [+M], and, for comparing with learners' own attempts, PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features (contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions) are implemented [+M] (a review of problem-first designs, carried to an error seen during ordinary teaching). Using the learners' error as a compared case, and the cap on cases per session, are untested proposals.
  6. Prompt the reason, not only the answer. In guided practice, ask learners to say or write why they chose the method ("which method, and what in the problem told you?"). Evidence: Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] (self-explanation prompts in general; mathematics evidence is immediate and mostly not from classrooms).
  7. Set process goals early, answer goals later. In early sessions, mark and praise following the strategy's steps and stating a reason; move to accuracy and speed targets once the strategy is used reliably. Evidence: Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] (high-school writing revision and self-regulation research, carried to mathematics).
  8. Fade the support across sessions and mix the problems. A default arc over about six to eight sessions of 20–30 minutes (an untested proposal): sessions 1–2, one strategy modelled with a written plan card and full worked examples; sessions 3–4, two methods compared, completion problems with the last steps left for the learner; sessions 5–6, mixed problems where the learner must choose the method, plan card available on request; sessions 7–8, mixed problems in the form of the target assessment, no card, followed by a look-back question ("would another method have been quicker?"). Evidence for fading worked steps: Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+S] (near transfer; 7 distinct studies in its table, most outside school mathematics).
  9. Move a learner on when they choose and justify. A learner moves to the next strategy or to mixed independent work when they choose an appropriate method and give a correct reason on two of three new problems, unaided. Learners who already do this at the start skip to step 5 or 8. Untested threshold.
  10. Check with a short task inside later work, not with a test, and look at the plan as well as the answer. Add a test or a delayed check only if the brief asks for one. Otherwise, at the start of a later session, give one new problem as part of the work (a recipe to scale for tomorrow's service, a quantity to order) and look at the written plan or equation and the answer separately, because they can diverge. Evidence: in the 1972 study, solution scores on the more complex problems were higher at a four-week retention test than at the posttest (On the problem solution criterion, verbal problem solving scores do not decline over four weeks and rise on the more complex problems [+M]), and the equation and solution criteria gave different results (step 4); neither finding calls for a separate test.

When a condition is not met: if learners cannot do the computation or lack the concept, teach it first; if learners already choose and justify methods, use only comparison and mixed practice; if no one can see attempts (self-paced online work), put the plan card and worked reasons in the materials and use problems with automatic checking and a worked solution after each. When the brief gives its own explicit sequence (explain, model, practise) or its own setting, keep both and fit these moves into its steps; do not add sessions, tests or checks it did not ask for.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • The learner's footing. Can they do the computation, and do they hold the concept the strategy relies on (for example, what a ratio means)? A learner without either needs the concept taught (or, if older and the goal is conceptual, a problem attempt first), not more strategies. If the brief omits it, ask: on one or two real items of the work, what does the learner do, and can they say what the quantities mean? If the brief already reports an observed error, that error is the first observation to use.
  • Which outcome counts. Choosing among methods (procedural flexibility), writing a correct equation or plan, getting the right answer, and understanding why a method works did not move together in the studies here. If the brief omits it, ask: on what problems, with what aids, and judged how, must the learner succeed, and when?
  • Who sees the learner's choice and reason. The design depends on someone or something reading the learner's stated reason for a method, not only the answer. If the brief omits it, ask: during practice, who will look at the method a learner chose and why, and how soon?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is an attempt under stated conditions: the problem, the aids available (plan card, worked example, calculator), the method the learner chose, the reason they gave, the written equation or plan, the answer, and the time taken. "Has a strategy", "chooses by structure" and "lacks the concept" are inferred states. A correct answer does not show which method was used or why, and a method chosen during guided practice with the card visible does not show what the learner chooses alone.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Correct on bare calculations, wrong on word problems with the same numbers Reading or language demand; no plan for representing the story; the learner picks an operation by keyword Read the problem aloud and ask the learner to retell it, then ask for the unknown before any numbers; compare with a version with the keyword removed.
Translates each phrase into symbols but writes no single equation (or several unconnected ones) Translation taught as the whole strategy; the relation linking quantities not identified; the learner solves by arithmetic and treats equations as a formality Ask what single relation the story describes; give a problem where arithmetic alone is hard but one equation is easy.
Always uses the same method, even where another is much quicker Only one method known well; the alternative not trusted; the learner values safety over speed Show both methods on a problem where they differ greatly in effort and ask which the learner would choose and why; check whether the learner can carry out the alternative at all.
Chooses a method correctly but cannot say why The choice rests on surface features that happen to align; the learner lacks words for the reason; the reason is held but not verbalised Give a pair of problems with the same surface but different structure; allow a reason to be shown by pointing or drawing.

These rows are proposals for local observation, untested with learners. A probe is also practice and can change what it measures, so record each one, and keep open the explanations a probe has not ruled out.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares explicit strategy instruction with practice that lacks it after the same concept teaching, or varies the number of strategies taught. The claims below test parts of the relationship.

  • Comparing cases side by side improves learning and transfer by a moderate average amount, though in one algebra study it raised procedural knowledge and flexibility but not conceptual knowledge [+S]: three studies. A meta-analysis of 57 experiments found case comparison ahead of sequential or single-case study, non-analogous cases, traditional instruction and controls (d = .50), with larger effects when learners look for similarities, when the principle is given after the comparison, and on immediate tests; it pools several kinds of comparator. In the one mathematics study, 70 seventh graders who compared alternative algebra solution methods side by side gained more procedural knowledge and flexibility than those who reflected on the same methods one at a time, but no more conceptual knowledge (abstract only). It supports comparing methods; it does not support comparison as a route to conceptual understanding in mathematics.
  • Alternative-strategy practices are most effective for improving procedural flexibility, with weaker evidence for conceptual and procedural knowledge [+M]: a federal practice guide on algebra in middle and high school summarises its own evidence: teaching and comparing alternative strategies helped procedural flexibility most, with weaker evidence for conceptual and procedural knowledge. No effect size is printed.
  • Polya Method students outscore Dahmus Method students on the equation criterion for verbal problems [+M], Polya and Dahmus methods do not differ on the problem solution criterion [~M], Dahmus Method students translated phrases successfully but failed to combine them into a single solution equation [+W], Algebra ability level differences appear on both criteria with no treatment-by-ability interaction [~M] and On the problem solution criterion, verbal problem solving scores do not decline over four weeks and rise on the more complex problems [+M]: one 1972 study, 48 ninth-grade girls, 8 per cell, seven days of individual slide-tape instruction. Polya-method students scored higher on writing the equation; the groups did not differ significantly on solving the problems (equivalence not tested); translation-method students, in the authors' reading of the papers, translated phrases without combining them; ability mattered on both criteria with no treatment-by-ability interaction; and solution scores on the more complex problems rose at a four-week retention test. The authors called their implications tentative: the machine presentation left students unable to ask questions, and low attainment may have hidden group differences. It compares two explicit strategies, not explicit teaching against its absence. No effect sizes are printed.
  • Fourth-graders perform substantially better on computation tests than on word problems using the same numbers [+M]: in one class of 24 fourth graders, computation scores (mean 81.67) were far above word-problem scores with the same numbers (mean 47.5). It locates the difficulty in the word problem, not the arithmetic; it does not show what fixes it.
  • Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S] and PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features (contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions) are implemented [+M]: across 53 studies, a problem attempt before instruction beat instruction first on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), not on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03), and reversed for second to fifth graders (4 distinct studies in the claim's table); a review finds the benefit only when the design uses contrasting cases or builds instruction on students' solutions. This qualifies the earlier page's "problem solving and productive failure before direct instruction" to conceptual goals with older learners.
  • Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes [+M], Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+S], Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] and Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: neighbouring findings carried into the default design. Example-first sequences beat problem-first ones for 96 secondary-school novices in circuit troubleshooting (one study); fading worked steps favoured near transfer (7 distinct studies in the claim's table); self-explanation prompts improved learning by a moderate average amount (g = .55 across 64 reports), with mathematics evidence mostly immediate; process goals beat outcome goals for novices learning writing revision. None tests strategy teaching in mathematics as such.

The limiting claim. Three weeks of explicit language-focused strategy instruction produced no significant word-problem gain in this study [~M]: in one classroom, three weeks of daily 20-minute explicit strategy instruction raised the word-problem mean from 47.08 to 55.42, not a significant gain (t = −1.26), with no comparison group. It is weak evidence (one class, coded r1), and a non-significant gain does not show no effect, but it is the only claim in the wiki that runs explicit word-problem strategy instruction and measures the result, and it did not find a reliable gain. Do not promise that a short strategy unit will move word-problem scores.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (seventh-grade algebra, ninth-grade word problems in 1972, fourth graders, circuit troubleshooting, writing revision), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures delayed transfer of a taught strategy against no strategy teaching.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does teaching when and why to use a procedure, not only its steps, improve flexible use and understanding? Strategy Instruction Needs Conditional Knowledge [+M]
  • Does explicitly teaching a strategy or plan for word problems improve how learners set up and solve them? Three weeks of explicit language-focused strategy instruction produced no significant word-problem gain in this study [~M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S] — cited by the earlier page as a reason that strategy routines can organise problem solving and that too many strategies at once can overload; the claim is about working-memory limits in general (4 distinct studies in its table) and bears on this page only by inference, for example on limiting the number of methods per session.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually that the learner chooses an appropriate method for a new problem, writes a correct equation or plan, and reaches the right answer, unaided, on a stated set of problems at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to pass a test, to stop feeling lost in front of word problems, to work out a payslip or a discount, to help a child with homework. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values getting answers quickly may resist explaining a method; one who values understanding may resist a plan card.

For example, an adult in a basic-skills class may want to check whether a "30% off, then a further 10%" offer is really 40% off, while the designer's objective is writing one equation for a percent problem. One worked comparison of two methods on that offer, then an unaided attempt on a different offer, serves both; record whether the learner can now judge such offers, separately from whether they write the equation.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of explicit strategy instruction (with when and why) against practice without it, after the same concept teaching and with comparable learners, show no advantage on unaided new problems, or if gains at the end of a unit disappear at a delay. If comparing methods raises flexibility but never accuracy or solution rates, the page should say comparison serves flexibility only. If whole-problem planning keeps matching phrase translation on solving problems, as in the one study here, the case for it rests on equation writing alone. If problem-first designs keep beating strategy-first teaching on the outcomes a design targets, the default sequence should start with the attempt more often. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough conceptual footing" after the fact.

Choosing a method, writing a correct equation, solving correctly, explaining why, and delayed transfer are separate claims. The present evidence sets no number of strategies, no dose and no move-on criterion.

Related Principles¶

  • Worked Examples — strategy instruction often begins with explicit modeled solutions
  • Error Analysis — comparing failed and successful strategies can sharpen strategic judgment
  • Real-World Math — strategy flexibility matters most when problems are varied and contextualized
  • Explaining Their Thinking — strategy use becomes durable when learners justify choices aloud or in writing
  • Direct Instruction — the general model this page applies to mathematical strategies (explain, model, guide practice; qualified by expertise, age, outcome and horizon)
  • Productive Failure — decides whether learners attempt a problem before the strategy is taught
  • Cognitive Flexibility — comparing methods is one way to hold the same knowledge in several forms

Related Patterns¶

  • Direct Instruction — the explain–model–guide–release lesson sequence as a reusable policy

Examples¶

  • Direct Instruction: Problem-solving Strategies — explicit modeling and reflection on multiple solution methods
  • Comparing two or three valid methods for the same problem before independent practice
  • Worked examples annotated with why a strategy was chosen, not just how it was executed
  • Problem-solving notebooks where learners track method choice and reflect on effectiveness
