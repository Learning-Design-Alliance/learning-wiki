# Pre-reading Questioning

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 15 claims (4 for, 9 mixed, 2 against) · 24 studies (8 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 7 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 9 of 24 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

## Conditional relationship

For a reader about to meet an expository text (or a lecture or video segment) whose central ideas they do not yet know, attempting a written answer or best guess to a few questions that target those central ideas, then reading a text that answers them, then checking the attempts against it, is expected to improve later memory of the questioned content (`immediate-performance` or `delayed-retention` on items about the same ideas) compared with reading the same text for the same time without questions. The attempt does not have to succeed: in the recorded experiments almost every pre-question was answered wrongly or left blank, and the benefit came on those items.

The relationship is conditional on five things. **Alignment**: the gain is on what the questions ask about; factual pre-questions can reduce learning of material they do not ask about, and higher-order questions spread their benefit further. **Attempting**: reading the questions without answering them helped less than answering them. **Resolution**: the gain depends on the reading (or feedback) that follows supplying the answer; a pre-question with no answer to follow is a guess left standing. **The learner's response to failing**: in one online course, pre-tests reduced how many learners kept going, so a benefit to those who stay can sit beside a loss of those who leave. **What the question activates**: a question that asks for topic knowledge did not improve primary pupils' comprehension in one experiment, while one that activated knowledge about how to read did.

**One claim tests a narrow form of this relationship; none tests the broad form.** The tested form is undergraduates in a laboratory attempting pre-questions on an expository essay, with retention of the questioned concepts as the outcome. No claim in the wiki tests pre-reading questions in a classroom, with children or second-language readers, on `conceptual-understanding` or inference, against other pre-reading activities (a preview, a vocabulary pre-teach, a KWL chart), or as a way of "setting a purpose" in general. The default design below keeps the earlier page's guidance as labelled proposals.

[Retrieval Practice](retrieval-practice.md) holds retrieval of material already studied; a pre-question is an attempt on material not yet studied, where failure followed by the answer is the expected case, so the two should not be assumed to share moderators. ## Default design, while the relationship is untested

A concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Steps 1–3 and 5 rest on the laboratory evidence; the rest are proposals from the earlier page, labelled as such.

1. **Write two to four questions per text, aimed at the ideas the later check will test.** Pick the text's central concepts or claims, not its most quotable details, and ask about them in words the learner can read. Gains are on the questioned content ([Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M]); factual pre-questions can cost learning of content they do not ask about ([Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M]). The number is an untested proposal (the pretesting claim page's own discussion suggests "two or three" central questions, with no study behind the count).
2. **Make at least one question ask why, how or what follows, not only what.** Higher-order adjunct questions generalised to related and unrelated test material more than factual ones in the review on the question-prompts claim ([Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M]; abstract only, and it pools questions placed before and after text).
3. **Ask every learner to write an answer or a best guess before reading, privately, in about a minute a question (three to five minutes in all).** Attempting beat reading the questions without answering them ([Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M], Experiment 5). The timing is an untested proposal.
4. **Say plainly that the questions are not scored and that most guesses will be wrong.** In one online course, pre-tests reduced persistence ([Pretesting Can Harm Motivation](../claims/pretesting-can-harm-motivation.md) [-M]); whether framing prevents that is untested. Never grade the attempts.
5. **Read with the questions in view, then check.** Learners mark where the text answers each question, then compare their guess with the text, correct it in writing and note which guess changed. The answer must actually arrive: the recorded benefit came only where reading or feedback supplied it ([Retrieval Fails Without Encoding](../claims/retrieval-fails-without-encoding.md) [~M]). Allow five to ten minutes for the check (untested proposal).
6. **Ask how sure each guess was, and spend the check on confident wrong ones.** A confident wrong prediction that the text contradicts is the case to discuss explicitly: name the idea, say what in the text refutes it, and ask for a fresh prediction on a new case ([Instruction that confronts science misconceptions … produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition](../claims/misconceptions-interfere-with-new-learning.md) [~M]; [High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M], a review and one experiment on corrective feedback, not on reading). Untested as a pre-reading routine.
7. **Protect what the questions leave out.** Tell learners the questions sample the text, and end with one task on the whole text (a three-sentence summary, or "what else did the text say that the questions missed?"). Untested proposal against the narrowing cost on [Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M].
8. **Move the writing of questions to the learner across sessions.** A default arc, all thresholds untested proposals:
   - *Early sessions (first two or three texts):* the teacher writes all questions; learners attempt, read and check as above.
   - *Middle sessions:* the teacher writes two questions and learners write one more from the title, headings and first paragraph, using question stems ("Why does…?", "What would happen if…?", "How is … different from …?"); the class compares which questions the text answered.
   - *Late sessions:* learners write two or three questions of their own before reading and check them after; the teacher adds a question only for a text with a known trap.
   - *Moving on:* a learner works without teacher questions once, on two consecutive texts, at least two of their own questions are answered by the text's central ideas rather than its details.
   Training learners to generate their own questions improved comprehension across intervention studies ([Self-questioning improves comprehension](../claims/self-questioning-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]), and so did reciprocal teaching, where questioning is one of four rotated strategies ([Reciprocal Teaching Improves Reading Comprehension](../claims/reciprocal-teaching-improves-reading-comprehension.md) [+M]); those were trained over many sessions and mostly asked questions during reading, so the arc carries them to a pre-reading routine without a test.
9. **Check later, on both kinds of content.** At a delay of a week or more, include items on questioned and unquestioned content, so both the gain and any narrowing cost show up. Untested proposal; the recorded experiments do not report their delays on the claim page.

Dose, as a proposal: three to five minutes of questions before, five to ten minutes of checking after, per text, two or three texts a week in a reading-heavy course. When a condition is not met: if the text does not answer the questions, rewrite the questions or choose another text; if learners already answer most questions correctly before reading, the routine is a review, so switch to one higher-order question or to their own questions.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

- **What the later check will ask about.** If it tests the whole text, factual pre-questions on part of it may cost the rest; if it tests a few central ideas, pre-questions on those are the best-evidenced case. If unstated, ask: after reading, what must the learner be able to say or do, and about which parts of the text?
- **Whether the text resolves the questions, and whether someone checks the attempts against it.** The benefit on record depends on the answer arriving after the attempt. If unstated, ask: does this text answer each question, and when will learners compare their guesses with it?
- **How the learner will take being wrong.** Most pre-question attempts fail; a learner who reads that as proof of inability may stop. If unstated, ask: who are the readers, how confident are they, is anything scored, and can they leave (an optional course, a self-paced module)?

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Readers are `novice` on the topic and will know almost none of the answers | Keep the attempt-before-reading step; tell them most guesses will be wrong; keep to two or three questions | [Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M] (undergraduates, items answered at floor, laboratory) |
| Readers are `advanced` on the topic, or answer most questions before reading | Replace teacher questions with one higher-order question, or with the reader's own questions about what they still want to know | untested; the earlier page's caution against over-directing applies |
| Readers are primary-age children (`child`) | Ask two questions aloud and record answers for them; add one question about how they will check they understood ("What will you do if a part doesn't make sense?"), not only questions about the topic | [Advance organizers aided learning …, but activating prior topic knowledge did not improve primary pupils' text comprehension in one experiment](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M] (88 primary pupils: metacognitive activation helped, topic activation did not) |
| Readers are second-language learners or have low literacy | Write the questions in simpler language than the text; pre-teach the key words the questions use; accept guesses in the first language or as a drawing; move to learner-written questions only within a longer strategy programme | [Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension](../claims/reading-strategy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M] (English L2 meta-analysis g = 0.72; whole-classroom interventions d = .186 on standardized tests), carried from multi-strategy programmes to this routine; the wording moves are untested |
| The goal is understanding or inference (`conceptual-understanding`, `near-transfer`), not memory of facts | Make most questions why/how/predict questions; in the check, ask for the reasoning in the text, not just the answer | [Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M] (higher-order adjunct questions generalised further; abstract only); no claim tests pre-questions on inference |
| The later check covers the whole text | Use fewer factual pre-questions, add a whole-text summary after reading, or move some questions to after each section instead of before | [Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M] (factual pre-questions depressed learning of unquestioned content) |
| `online-self-paced` course or module, no teacher present | Keep pre-questions to two, framed as a puzzle with no score; show the answer straight after the relevant section; track drop-off at the pre-question screen and remove it if completion falls | [Pretesting Can Harm Motivation](../claims/pretesting-can-harm-motivation.md) [-M] (399 adults in a MOOC: pre-tests reduced persistence; post-test gains only among those who finished) |
| Mixed modes: some learners in the room, some remote, or some on phones only | Put the questions in one place everyone answers privately (a poll or a shared form) before the text opens; on phones, one question per screen with a short typed or multiple-choice answer; run the check in the same tool so remote learners' guesses are seen too | untested proposal |
| One teacher with a large class | Collect every learner's guesses at once (cards, mini-whiteboards, a poll), scan for the commonest confident wrong prediction, and spend the after-reading check on that one, with the text's refutation | untested proposal; the confront-the-misconception move is from [Instruction that confronts science misconceptions … produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition](../claims/misconceptions-interfere-with-new-learning.md) [~M] (science and reading syntheses, abstracts only) |
| One session only | Two questions, attempt, read, check, one whole-text summary; do not claim a lasting effect | untested beyond the laboratory sessions on [Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [~M] |
| Learners have low confidence, or a history of failing at reading | Keep answers private; offer forced-choice guesses instead of blank lines; open the check with what the class predicted well; never display individual guesses | untested; the low-confidence risk is the motivation claim's own speculation, not a finding ([Pretesting Can Harm Motivation](../claims/pretesting-can-harm-motivation.md) [~M]) |
| Adults in work settings reading a procedure, policy or case | Write questions as decisions they will face ("A patient refuses X: what does the policy let you do?"); check against the exact clause; ask what they do now that the text changes | untested proposal |
| Learners are reading sources for their own inquiry or for a product an audience will see | Let the learner's project question be the pre-reading question; ask what the source must show to be usable, and after reading, what it does and does not support | untested proposal; links to [Inquiry-based Learning](inquiry-based-learning.md) |
| The pre-questions are scored, or the course is high stakes | Remove the score from pre-questions; score only the later check | [Pretesting Can Harm Motivation](../claims/pretesting-can-harm-motivation.md) [-M] (persistence fell even unscored); scoring itself untested |
| Open brainstorming ("What do you know about…?") is planned instead of questions | Replace it with two specific questions the text answers | [Prior-knowledge activation instructions before study had little effect on retention in Experiment 2](../claims/prior-knowledge-activation-little-effect-on-retention.md) [-M] (1.5 minutes of talk produced about one relevant idea per topic) |

Eleven of the fifteen rows cite a claim, but only the novice and one-session rows carry the tested form (laboratory pre-questions on an essay, undergraduates); the others carry a neighbouring finding (adjunct questions, strategy programmes, a MOOC, conceptual-change syntheses, topic activation) to this setting and say so. The advanced-reader, mixed-mode, adult-workplace and inquiry rows are untested proposals.

## Observation, state and explanation

What a designer can observe: the written guess to each question, how sure the learner said they were, how long they took, whether they marked where the text answers, what they changed at the check, and how they did later on questioned and unquestioned items. "Has activated relevant knowledge", "holds a misconception" and "is reading with purpose" are inferred states. A blank answer before reading is the expected case and does not show disengagement; a correct answer after reading may come from copying the marked sentence.

| Response | Plausible explanations | Observation that could change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Blank or "don't know" on every pre-question | Nothing to guess from (expected for new content); reluctance to be wrong in front of others; the question's wording is the barrier | Offer a private forced-choice version; ask the learner to read the question aloud and say what it asks. |
| A confident wrong prediction that survives the reading | A misconception the text did not confront; the learner did not find the answering passage; the text is ambiguous on the point | Ask the learner to point to the passage that answers the question; give a refutation of the specific idea; re-ask on a changed case a week later. |
| Good on pre-questioned items later, weak on everything else in the text | Attention narrowed to the questions; the questions sampled too little; the rest of the text was harder | Compare questioned and unquestioned items of similar difficulty; add a whole-text task and see whether the gap closes. |
| Learner-written questions are all literal or about details | The learner cannot yet tell central from peripheral ideas; decoding takes the effort; they think easy questions are wanted | Ask them to rank their own questions by importance; check reading fluency on a short passage; show one central question from the teacher and ask for one more like it. |
| Fewer learners continue after the pre-question step (online) | Discouragement at failing; time cost; the step looks like a test | Compare completion with and without the step, or with a "no score, guesses expected" version; ask a confidence rating before and after. |
| Better later recall of pre-questioned items than of others | The attempt made the answer stand out when it arrived; the question flagged importance; extra time on those items | Compare items attempted with items whose questions were only read, at matched time, as the pretesting experiments did. |

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A pre-question is itself an intervention: asking it changes what the learner attends to, so record every question and probe given.

## Evidence and qualifications

- [Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+M]: across five laboratory experiments, undergraduates asked questions about an expository essay on human vision before reading it (nearly all answered wrongly or blank) did better on a later test of the same material than those given equivalent extra reading time; in Experiment 1, 75% against 56% correct (d = 1.1), counting only items failed at pretest. Emphasising the same concepts in italics or bold did not account for it, and reading the questions without attempting them helped less. A second set of experiments (trivia and word pairs, undergraduates) found failed attempts followed by the answer beat reading question and answer together (41% against 31%, d = 0.58). This is the closest test of this page's relationship. It does not show effects on unquestioned content, comprehension or inference, classroom use, children or second-language readers, and the delay is not recorded on the claim page. Not yet checked against its sources.
- [Question prompts improve learning](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [~M]: a review of adjunct (inserted) questions in prose finds that questions of all levels strongly help on the same questions later and weakly on related ones; higher-order questions generalise to related and unrelated higher-order items; and factual pre-questions can hurt learning of unquestioned material. Effects varied with text length, question density and format, not with learner age or delay. Abstract only; no pooled effect. **This is the limiting claim**: it sets the narrowing cost against the gain.
- [Pretesting Can Harm Motivation](../claims/pretesting-can-harm-motivation.md) [-M]: 399 adults in a museum's climate-change MOOC, randomly assigned to pre-tests (with no, basic or elaborate feedback) or no pre-test: pre-tests reduced persistence; across all students they did not change post-test learning; among completers they improved it. Abstract only; no effect size. The claim's title says motivation, but the measured outcome is persistence. It qualifies the default design: an unscored, quick pre-question is still a cost where learners can leave.
- [Retrieval Fails Without Encoding](../claims/retrieval-fails-without-encoding.md) [~M]: reads the same two experiment sets as showing that the failed attempt helps the encoding of an answer that follows it, not learning on its own; every trial ended with the answer shown or the passage read. It is why the default design requires a text that answers the questions and a check.
- [Advance organizers aided learning in a 1980 meta-analysis, but activating prior topic knowledge did not improve primary pupils' text comprehension in one experiment](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]: in a randomised 2 × 2 experiment, 88 primary-school students' text comprehension improved when metacognitive knowledge was activated, not when topic knowledge was, once prior knowledge was controlled; a meta-analysis of 135 studies found advance organizers helped learning and retention. It qualifies the earlier page's main rationale (activating background knowledge) for young readers' comprehension.
- [Self-questioning improves comprehension](../claims/self-questioning-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]: a review of intervention studies teaching students to generate their own questions about text reports a median effect of 0.36 on standardized comprehension tests and 0.86 on experimenter-developed tests; a review of 35 experimental studies with K–12 students with and without disabilities calls self-questioning effective. Both abstract only. It supports handing question-writing to learners over time; it tests trained generation, mostly during reading, not a pre-reading warm-up.

Learners, settings, materials and outcomes differ across these claims (laboratory undergraduates, MOOC adults, primary pupils, intervention programmes), so do not rank them by effect size.

## Objective and learner-valued goal

The designer's objective is usually that the learner can recall or use the text's central ideas on a later check, with the check's items, criterion and delay stated. The pre-question attempts and the corrected answers are means, not the outcome. Ask separately what the learner wants from the text, and record agreement or divergence as an observation. A learner who wants to finish quickly may write the shortest guess; one who fears being wrong may leave every line blank.

For example, a care assistant reading a revised infection-control policy may want to know what changes on her next shift, while the trainer's objective is recall of the policy's main requirements on a quiz. Pre-questions written as shift decisions ("A visitor arrives with a cough: what does the policy say you do?") can serve both; a quiz alone cannot show whether her practice on the ward changed, so record what she says she will do differently as well.

## What would revise this model?

The expectation should weaken if classroom studies with comparable readers and texts find no advantage of pre-questions over reading for the same time, or find that the gain on questioned content is matched by an equal loss on unquestioned content, so that whole-text learning is unchanged. It should be narrowed to retention of questioned facts if pre-questions do not help inference or comprehension. If unscored, framed pre-questions still reduce persistence in courses learners can leave, the online default should drop them. If topic pre-questions fail for young readers while questions about how to read succeed, the page should recommend the second for children. Do not protect the model by calling every null "badly written questions" after the fact.

The present evidence establishes no number of questions, no length of attempt, no delay over which the gain holds and no effect on comprehension of a whole text.

## Description
Pre-reading questioning is the instructional principle of using questions before a text, source, or reading task to activate prior knowledge, orient attention, and establish a purpose for reading. The value lies less in predicting the "right" answer than in helping learners notice what they already know, what they wonder, and what they should look for. When used well, pre-reading questions can improve comprehension by reducing aimless reading and by surfacing misconceptions early enough to address them.

## Implications
Pre-reading questioning improves comprehension when it gives learners a reason to read and a frame for what to notice. Orienting questions can reduce aimless attention by narrowing what must be held and tracked during reading [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~S], and they can support more deliberate monitoring as learners compare early predictions to what the text actually shows [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]. The key design risk is over-directing interpretation: questions should focus attention without predetermining every conclusion.

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A meaningful reading purpose**: Questions should help learners know what to watch for in the text or task.
- **Connection to prior knowledge**: Prompts should invite learners to activate existing knowledge, assumptions, or curiosity.
- **Alignment to the actual text demand**: Questions need to prepare learners for the kind of comprehension or analysis the reading requires.
- **Return to the questions after reading**: The questions should inform later discussion, annotation, or revision of understanding.
#### Constraints
- **Leading prompts**: Questions can narrow interpretation too much if they predetermine what learners are supposed to notice.
- **Shallow activation**: Generic warm-up questions may fail to connect meaningfully to the text.
- **Premature certainty**: Learners may become attached to initial predictions unless the post-reading phase invites revision.
- **Overload from too many questions**: Excessive prompting before reading can become distracting rather than orienting.

### Target Learners
- **Learners approaching unfamiliar texts**: Strong fit when the topic, vocabulary, or genre may otherwise feel opaque.
- **Learners developing strategic reading habits**: Pre-reading questions help shift reading from passive decoding to active inquiry.
- **Multilingual learners and developing readers**: Advance orientation can make comprehension demands clearer and more manageable.
- **Learners in content-heavy courses**: Questions can focus attention on the most important concepts, patterns, or claims.

### Target Learning Objectives
- **Activate background knowledge**: Bringing relevant prior understanding into the reading task.
- **Improve reading purpose and attention**: Helping learners know what they are reading for.
- **Surface misconceptions**: Revealing assumptions that need to be tested or revised.
- **Support later comprehension and discussion**: Creating a frame for note-taking, annotation, and post-reading synthesis.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Information processing views — orienting questions can direct attention and improve what gets encoded during reading.
- Constructivist perspectives — learners interpret new text through the lens of what they already know and believe.
- Expectancy-value and motivational views — questions can increase relevance and curiosity before the reading begins.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Pre-reading questions can constrain interpretation if they are too narrow or overly teacher-directed.
- Some texts are better entered first with open noticing before specific questions are introduced.

### Claims
- [Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~S] — orienting prompts can reduce the amount of undirected information learners try to manage at once
- [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M] — pre-reading questions help learners check what they already think and revise it as they read

## Examples
- **Prediction and purpose prompts**: Learners answer a small set of questions before reading and revisit them afterward.
- **Question-generation warm-up**: Learners create their own questions from a title, heading set, or opening image.
- **KWL-style framing**: Learners identify what they know, want to know, and later what they learned.
- **Source anticipation**: Before reading a document, learners predict what perspective, evidence, or problem the text might contain.
