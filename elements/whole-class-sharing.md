# Whole-Class Sharing

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Whole-class sharing brings individual or small-group thinking before the entire class for presentation, discussion, and synthesis. It functions as the public, collective phase of a learning sequence — consolidating private reasoning into shared knowledge and giving learners an authentic audience for their ideas.

## Design Implications

Whole-class sharing supports learning by requiring learners to articulate and organize their reasoning for others, which strengthens understanding through generative retrieval and explanation [Articulating explanations to others improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Its value depends on structure: unstructured sharing devolves into a few dominant voices, so protocols (timed turns, random reporters, talking pieces) and a clear synthesis role for the instructor are essential. Sharing also raises the stakes and visibility of ideas, increasing engagement when learners perceive the task as relevant and their contribution as valued [Perceived task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prior thinking or small-group phase so learners arrive with something to share ([Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md), [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md))
- Discussion norms and protocols that distribute airtime beyond volunteers
- Instructor facilitation that records, connects, and synthesizes contributions rather than evaluating each one
- A visible record (board, shared document) so collective ideas accumulate rather than evaporate

#### Constraints
- Public presentation creates social-evaluative threat for anxious or low-status learners; cold-calling without norms can suppress participation rather than elicit thinking [~M]
- Whole-class formats allow most learners to remain passive listeners; without individual accountability structures, few students actually produce language or reasoning during the segment [~S]
- Dominant voices and status effects can bias which ideas the class adopts, entrenching misconceptions if the instructor does not actively surface and test alternatives [~M]
- Inefficient for transmitting large bodies of new content — sharing time scales poorly with class size

### Target Learners
- Learners in K-12 through higher education and training contexts where participation and communication are goals
- Learners developing academic language and argumentation, who benefit from hearing and producing public reasoning
- Less suitable as a standalone format for highly anxious learners or novices who lack ideas to share — pair or small-group phases should precede it

### Target Learning Goals
- Communication skills: organizing and defending ideas for an audience
- Knowledge synthesis: integrating diverse group conclusions into collective understanding
- Metacognition and self-explanation: verbalizing reasoning exposes gaps [Articulating explanations to others improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Community norms: building a culture where ideas are shared, questioned, and built upon

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — sharing is the mechanism by which group work becomes collective knowledge; without a whole-class synthesis phase, small-group products stay fragmented
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — learners build understanding by articulating, defending, and revising ideas in response to peer perspectives rather than receiving conclusions
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — presenting and responding keeps learners producing ideas rather than receiving them, though only if protocols ensure broad participation
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — public sharing gives the instructor a real-time window into class-wide understanding and misconceptions to guide next moves
- [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md) — regular sharing rituals socialize learners into norms of public reasoning and mutual critique

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — the "share" phase is whole-class reporting of pair conclusions
- [Jigsaw](../patterns/jigsaw.md) — expert groups report back so the class assembles the full picture
- [Discussion-Based Learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md) — sharing structures the whole-class segment of discussion sequences
- [Collaborative Inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md) — groups present findings for collective critique and synthesis

## Examples
- **[Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md)** — pairs rehearse ideas privately before public sharing, lowering the risk of the whole-class phase
- **[Jigsaw Method](../patterns/jigsaw-method.md)** — each expert reports a piece of the content; the class cannot succeed without every share
- **Socratic Seminar** — students present and challenge interpretations in a whole-class circle governed by text-evidence norms
- **Gallery Walk** — written group products are posted and toured, converting simultaneous small-group sharing into a whole-class exchange
