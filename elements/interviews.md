# Interviews

## Description
An interview places learners in direct contact with a source of knowledge — a domain expert, practitioner, community member, or peer — whom they question using a prepared protocol. The learner acts as an active inquirer rather than a recipient of pre-packaged content, and the resulting material (notes, transcripts, recordings) becomes an artifact for analysis, synthesis, or presentation.

## Design Implications

Interviews make learning situated and purposeful: learners must activate prior knowledge to formulate questions, listen and adapt in real time, and reconcile answers with their existing understanding [Interview tasks that require question generation promote deeper processing than answering supplied questions.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Their value depends on preparation — learners need background knowledge to ask substantive questions and to interpret answers critically — and on a follow-up structure that converts raw conversation into analyzed knowledge rather than leaving it as anecdote.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient prior knowledge for learners to ask meaningful questions and evaluate answers ([Activation](activation.md) before the interview)
- A prepared question protocol, with training on follow-up probing and active listening
- Access to an appropriate interviewee whose expertise matches the learning goal
- A synthesis or analysis activity afterward — the interview is input, not the endpoint ([Application](application.md))

#### Constraints
- Interviews with poorly prepared learners yield superficial Q&A exchanges; without background knowledge, learners cannot probe or detect weak answers [-M]
- Unstructured "chat" formats drift from learning goals; the protocol and debrief carry the pedagogical weight [~M]
- Interviewees may present anecdote, opinion, or error as fact; learners need guidance on source evaluation and triangulation with other evidence [-W]
- Logistical barriers (scheduling, access, recording consent) make interviews costly relative to text or video sources; use when first-hand perspective is the point, not as a substitute for available material

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to formulate questions; complete novices benefit more from [Demonstration](demonstration.md) or direct exposition first
- Learners developing professional or research practices (journalism, qualitative methods, user research, oral history)
- Adolescents and adults; younger learners need heavily scaffolded protocols and role rehearsal

### Target Learning Goals
- Inquiry and information-gathering skills: question design, probing, active listening
- Perspective-taking and empathy: encountering lived experience and viewpoints different from one's own
- Authentic disciplinary practice: learning how professionals in a field actually elicit and use testimony
- Synthesis: integrating first-hand accounts with course material into an evidence-based product

### Affordances
- [Ask Experts](../principles/ask-experts.md) — interviews enact this principle directly: the expert is present and responsive, so learners can pursue their own lines of inquiry rather than receiving a fixed expert account
- [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) — a real interviewee and a real deliverable (article, report, oral history) give the task genuine stakes beyond the grade
- [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) — structured listening to personal narratives is a core mechanism of perspective-taking interventions; interviews operationalize it with real interlocutors
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — interviewing a practitioner exposes learners to how experts frame problems and narrate their own practice, a form of access to expert thinking that complements observation

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — exposure to expert practice through practitioner testimony
- [Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md) — interviews as the primary interface between learners and community partners

## Examples
- **Oral history projects (e.g., StoryCorps, https://storycorps.org)** — students interview family or community members using StoryCorps' question guides, producing archived first-person accounts; widely used in K-12 history and ELA curricula
- **Qualitative research methods courses** — learners design interview protocols, pilot them on peers, and code transcripts; the peer pilot interview is a standard rehearsal structure
- **User research in design education** — students conduct contextual interviews with users before prototyping, making the interview the front end of a design cycle
- **News literacy units** — students interview local sources and triangulate claims against documents, learning both interviewing craft and source evaluation
