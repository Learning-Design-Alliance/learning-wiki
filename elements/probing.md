# Probing

## Description
Probing is the instructional element of asking follow-up questions that press past a learner's first response — asking for justification, elaboration, counterexamples, or connections to prior knowledge. It functions as a form of [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): the probe diagnoses the learner's current understanding while simultaneously prompting the learner to reconstruct and extend it.

## Design Implications

Probing converts a surface-level answer into an act of retrieval and elaboration, which strengthens memory and reveals gaps that a correct answer alone would hide [Retrieval practice produces stronger long-term retention than restudying.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Probes that ask *why* and *how* elicit self-explanation, a mechanism with consistent positive effects on conceptual understanding [Self-explanation prompts improve conceptual learning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Effective probes are contingent on the learner's response — generic "any questions?" prompts do not function as probes; the question must engage the specific content the learner just produced [Question prompts improve learning outcomes.](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A safe climate where learners expect their ideas to be examined, not judged ([Check-In](check-in.md) and norms-setting help establish this)
- Wait time after each question — extending pauses to 3+ seconds increases response quality and participation
- The instructor's willingness to follow a wrong or partial answer with a counter-question rather than supplying the answer
- A model of good probing for peer use, since trained student questioners gain more than untrained ones [Guided peer questioning improves learning outcomes.](../claims/question-prompts-improve-learning.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- High-stakes or evaluative framing turns probing into interrogation; learners under perceived threat give shorter, safer answers [-M]
- Probing learners who lack the knowledge to elaborate produces guessing and frustration rather than construction [~M] — probes work best after some initial knowledge exists, not as a substitute for it
- Overly rapid probing sequences can exceed working memory capacity and degrade reasoning [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- In large groups, only a few learners are probed; unprobed bystanders may disengage unless structures like [Class Discussion](class-discussion.md) protocols or think-pair-share distribute participation [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with partial or fragile understanding, where probing exposes the gap between recognition and explanation [Self-explanation prompts improve conceptual learning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Learners prone to fluency illusions, who mistake familiarity for understanding
- Advanced learners benefit from probes demanding justification and transfer; novices need more scaffolded probe types (e.g., "what does this term mean?" before "why is this true?") [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: distinguishing deep structure from surface features
- Metacognitive monitoring: recognizing what one does and does not yet understand
- Argumentation and justification: articulating evidence and reasoning ([Argumentation](argumentation.md))
- Misconception repair: creating the cognitive conflict needed for conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — probing is formative assessment in its most immediate form: each probe yields diagnostic information that can redirect instruction within the same session
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — a probe forces generative processing; the learner must produce reasoning rather than receive it
- [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — well-aimed probes ("Is that always true?", "What would happen if…") create the productive struggle this principle describes
- [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — counter-probes that expose contradictions between a learner's answer and their other beliefs trigger the impasse that drives conceptual change

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Discussion-Based Learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md) — probing is the facilitator's primary move for deepening discussion beyond opinion exchange
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — probes push learners to justify diagnostic decisions and consider alternatives
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the "articulation and reflection" phases depend on probed questioning of learner reasoning

## Examples

**[Socratic questioning in case discussions](../patterns/case-based-learning.md)** — In Harvard Business School case teaching, the instructor probes a student's analysis ("Why that recommendation? What data contradicts it?") and then redirects the same probe to other students, building a chain of public reasoning.

**Reciprocal peer questioning (King, 1990s)** — Students are taught generic probe stems ("Explain why…", "How do … and … relate?") and question each other in pairs; trained questioners outperform control groups on comprehension measures.

**[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — Social annotation platforms operationalize probing asynchronously: instructor or peer replies to an annotation with a follow-up question, requiring the annotator to elaborate before the thread resolves.

**Clinical teaching (medical education)** — Attending physicians use structured probes ("What is your differential? What would change your mind?") to expose diagnostic reasoning rather than accepting a diagnosis at face value.
