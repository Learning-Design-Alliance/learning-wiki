# Expert Groups

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Expert groups are a cooperative structure in which learners first work in same-topic groups to master one segment of a topic, then return to heterogeneous groups to teach their segment to peers. Each learner becomes the sole source of a piece of knowledge, making the group's success depend on every member's contribution. The structure is the core mechanism of the Jigsaw classroom and related collaborative inquiry formats.

## Design Interactions

Expert groups promote deep processing because preparing to teach forces learners to organize, elaborate, and rehearse content rather than passively receive it [Explaining content to others improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The interdependence built into the structure — each member holds a unique piece — raises engagement and task value, since learners are accountable to peers, not just the instructor [When learners expect to teach, they study more deeply and remember more.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.07.001) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A topic that can be partitioned into meaningful, roughly equal segments
- Source materials at a difficulty learners can master with peer support
- Time for two phases: expert-group mastery followed by peer teaching
- Instructor monitoring of expert groups before teaching begins, since accuracy of peer instruction depends on the quality of first-phase mastery [Scaffolding that adapts to learner needs improves outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- If expert groups master their segment poorly, errors propagate through peer teaching; a comprehension check before the teaching phase is essential [-M]
- Ineffective when segments are trivially divisible or when one member dominates and others copy rather than learn [-M]
- Less effective for novices with no prior knowledge of the domain, who may lack the background to evaluate or correct peers' explanations [-W]
- Individual accountability must be assessed; without it, free-riding undermines the interdependence the structure depends on [-M]

### Target Learners
- Learners in upper elementary through higher education who have basic group-work skills
- Learners motivated by peer accountability and social interaction [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Less suitable for complete novices, who need more expert guidance before teaching others [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through elaboration and explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Communication and knowledge-synthesis skills: organizing material well enough to teach it
- Interpersonal and collaborative competence alongside content mastery

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — expert groups enact this principle by structuring positive interdependence: no learner can complete the task alone, so cooperation is built into the task rather than requested
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — learners actively construct understanding by reorganizing source material into teachable form, consistent with social constructivist accounts of knowledge building through explanation
- Distributed cognition — the structure distributes a knowledge domain across group members, so the group collectively knows more than any individual, and learning requires integrating distributed pieces

## Examples
- **Jigsaw Classroom** (https://www.jigsaw.org) — Elliot Aronson's original structure: material split into segments, expert groups master one segment, then teach their home group
- **Reciprocal jigsaw in STEM courses** — students each master one problem-solving strategy in expert groups, then teach it to base groups before tackling integrated problems
- **Fiorella & Mayer's learning-by-teaching studies** — students who studied material expecting to teach it, then actually taught, outperformed students who studied only for a test
