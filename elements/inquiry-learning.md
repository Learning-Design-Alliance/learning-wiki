# Inquiry Learning

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Inquiry learning places learners in the role of investigator: they pose or explore questions, gather and analyze evidence, and construct explanations, with the instructor acting as guide rather than primary explainer. It ranges along a continuum from open (learner-generated questions and procedures) to guided (teacher-structured tasks and prompts), and the evidence strongly favors the guided end.

## Design Implications

Inquiry can produce deep, transferable understanding when learners have enough structure to avoid unproductive search, but minimally guided discovery consistently underperforms explicit instruction for novices [Minimally guided instruction is less effective than explicit guidance for novices.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S]. The design task is therefore not "inquiry or not" but *how much* guidance: scaffolds, prompts, and worked models embedded in the investigation determine whether learners construct correct schemas or entrench misconceptions [Scaffolded guided inquiry produces learning gains comparable to or exceeding direct instruction.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+M]. Feedback during investigation should target hypotheses and reasoning processes, not just answers [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-defined question or problem that is genuinely investigable with available materials and time
- Embedded scaffolds: hypothesis templates, data-organization tools, prompts that direct attention to critical features
- Prior knowledge activation before exploration begins ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
- Structured opportunities to articulate and revise explanations ([Reflection](reflection.md), [Argumentation](argumentation.md))
- Instructor monitoring to catch and correct misconceptions early, before they consolidate

#### Constraints
- Unguided or minimally guided inquiry is less effective than explicit instruction for novices, who lack schemas to evaluate what they discover [Minimally guided discovery fails for novice learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [-S]
- Learners can construct incorrect or incomplete explanations and fail to notice; without verification against expert content, misconceptions persist [-S]
- High working-memory demands of simultaneous hypothesis generation, data handling, and reasoning can overload novices [Cognitive load during unguided problem solving interferes with schema construction.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [-S]
- Benefits reverse for advanced learners, who gain more from self-directed exploration than from heavy guidance [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Time-intensive; covers less content per hour than direct instruction, a real constraint in curriculum-dense settings

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who have enough schema to interpret evidence but still need scaffolds [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Novices only when inquiry is heavily scaffolded with models and prompts [+M]
- Advanced learners, who benefit from reduced guidance and open-ended investigation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and conceptual change, especially in science where learners confront their predictions against evidence
- Scientific practices: hypothesis formation, data interpretation, evidence-based argumentation
- Epistemic understanding: how knowledge in a discipline is generated and justified

### Affordances
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — guided inquiry is essentially scaffolded exploration; the design decision is which scaffolds to embed and when to [fade](fading.md) them as learners take over the investigative process
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — structuring the question, constraining variables, and providing data tools reduces the search burden that makes unguided discovery fail
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — inquiry enacts active learning at the deepest level: learners generate, test, and revise ideas rather than respond to prompts
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the investigation-explanation cycle mirrors expert practice in the discipline, especially when paired with [Coaching](coaching.md) and [Articulation](articulation.md)

## Related Elements

## Examples

**[BSCS 5E Instructional Model](https://bscs.org/bscs-5e-instructional-model/)** — Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate; places a scaffolded exploration phase before the explanation phase, sequencing inquiry and direct instruction deliberately.

**[WISE (Web-Based Inquiry Science Environment)](https://wise.berkeley.edu)** — Scaffolds inquiry projects with embedded prompts, data visualization tools, and reflection notes; extensively studied as a model of guided inquiry.

**[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)** — Teachers elicit and build on children's informal problem-solving strategies, a classroom-inquiry form in mathematics.

**[Case-Based Learning (Harvard Method)](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)** — Learners analyze real cases and defend recommendations, inquiry in a discussion format.
