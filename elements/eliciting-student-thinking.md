# Eliciting Student Thinking

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Eliciting student thinking is the practice of prompting learners to externalize their ideas, reasoning, and problem-solving approaches — through questioning, discussion, or explanation — so that both instructor and learner can examine them. It functions as a diagnostic and generative move: the instructor gains access to partial or faulty conceptions, and the learner deepens understanding by articulating it.

## Design Implications

Eliciting thinking converts passive reception into generative processing; verbalizing reasoning forces learners to reconstruct and evaluate their own understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The elicitation is only as valuable as the instructor's response: prompts should be open enough to reveal reasoning ("How did you decide that?"), and follow-up should build on what students actually say rather than steering immediately to the correct answer. Productive elicitation treats student errors as information to be worked with, not deficiencies to be corrected on the spot.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Open-ended prompts that target reasoning, not just answers ("Why?", "What if…?")
- Adequate wait time and a classroom norm that partial or tentative ideas are safe to voice
- Instructor responsiveness: interpreting student contributions and adapting instruction accordingly ([Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md)) [+M]
- A follow-up structure ([Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md), [Self-Explanation](self-explanation.md)) that lets students refine ideas after voicing them

#### Constraints
- Rapid-fire IRE questioning (initiate–respond–evaluate) elicits answers but suppresses reasoning [~M] — students learn to guess what the teacher wants rather than expose their thinking
- Elicitation without feedback or follow-through can entrench misconceptions: voicing an error and hearing only "not quite" leaves the misconception intact [-M]
- Low-verbal-fluency or high-anxiety students may perform poorly under public elicitation; private or written channels (whiteboards, clickers, journals) preserve the function [-W]
- If students lack baseline knowledge, eliciting "prior thinking" yields guesses rather than retrievable ideas [~W]

### Target Learners
- K–12 and higher education learners with enough prior knowledge to generate meaningful (if partial) ideas
- Novices benefit especially when elicitation is paired with structured prompts, since unguided self-explanation can overload working memory [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~M]
- Less effective as a standalone for complete novices, who may have little to externalize; elicit after minimal exposure rather than before any

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: surfacing and restructuring intuitive models
- Problem-solving flexibility: comparing multiple solution approaches
- Metacognitive development: monitoring one's own reasoning by articulating it ([Metacognition](metacognition.md))
- Formative diagnosis: informing instructional next steps ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))

### Affordances
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — elicitation enacts this principle by making learners' existing conceptions the raw material for instruction; new knowledge is built from what students already say and believe
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — a well-placed prompt is contingent support: it provides just enough structure to extend the learner's current reasoning one step further [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- [Metacognition](../principles/metacognition.md) — articulating reasoning makes monitoring visible; learners notice gaps in their own explanations as they produce them
- [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — open reasoning prompts push learners beyond recall toward elaboration, comparison, and justification
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — elicitation is a low-cost active-learning move; productive engagement (generating, explaining) predicts stronger outcomes than passive reception [~S]

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) for Math](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) — teachers elicit children's informal solution strategies and build instruction on them
- [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — elicited thinking is the evidence base for instructional adjustment
- [Discussion-Based Learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md) — sustained discourse norms that make student reasoning the classroom currency

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction** (Carpenter et al., University of Wisconsin) — teachers present a story problem, elicit children's own solution strategies before teaching procedures, and classify strategies to plan next instruction. [https://www.wcer.wisc.edu/cgi](https://www.wcer.wisc.edu/cgi)
- **Peer Instruction** (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — students commit to an answer via clickers, then convince a neighbor before revoting; the peer discussion phase elicits and tests reasoning. [https://peerinstruction4cs.org](https://peerinstruction4cs.org)
- **Accountable Talk** (Institute for Learning, University of Pittsburgh) — discourse routines with prompts ("Do you agree? How do you know?") that press students to articulate and justify reasoning. [https://ifl.pitt.edu](https://ifl.pitt.edu)
- **Think-Pair-Share** — a minimal elicitation structure: individual thinking, rehearsal with a partner, then public sharing; lowers the risk of public elicitation for anxious students.
