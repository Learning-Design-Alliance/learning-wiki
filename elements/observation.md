# Observation

## Description
Observation is the short-form canonical target for instructional activities in which learners watch people, processes, or contexts carefully in order to notice patterns, behaviors, or constraints.

Observation also functions as a teacher's primary *informal assessment* tool — continuously monitoring students' learning and motivation during instruction in order to decide whether to adjust teaching in the moment (Airasian, 2005). This is demanding in practice: beginning teachers find it harder than experienced ones, since it requires improvising responsively to student needs while simultaneously tracking the lesson's own goals and plan (Borko & Livingston, 1989). Observation-as-assessment covers both non-verbal behavior (who's looking out the window during a demonstration) and what students say (comments in a group indicating confusion), and feeds a range of in-the-moment decisions: who to call on next, whether to speed up or slow the pace, when more examples are needed, and whether a behavior problem is emerging. Moving around the room helps teachers observe more effectively from more vantage points, but the sheer pace and complexity of a real classroom limits how much any teacher can actually take in this way — a real constraint on how much can be inferred from observation alone, not a failure of technique.

#### Requirements

- **Prompts or criteria that focus attention**
#### Constraints
- **Unguided observation may stay impressionistic**
