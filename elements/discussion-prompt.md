# Discussion Prompt

## Description
A discussion prompt is a deliberately structured question, scenario, or statement designed to initiate substantive conversation among learners. Unlike generic invitations to "discuss," a well-designed prompt specifies a genuine problem or tension, is answerable from multiple defensible positions, and requires learners to articulate, defend, and revise their thinking in response to peers.

## Design Implications

Discussion prompts convert passive content exposure into active knowledge construction by requiring learners to articulate reasoning and respond to alternative perspectives [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Prompt quality is the primary determinant of discussion quality: open, contested, and personally relevant prompts elicit higher-order contributions, while closed or factual prompts produce short, convergent answers with little peer-to-peer exchange [~M]. Prompts work best when paired with [Moderation](moderation.md) that presses for evidence and elaboration rather than merely collecting opinions.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A question with genuine tension or multiple defensible answers — not a single retrievable fact
- Sufficient prior exposure to content (reading, case, demonstration) so all participants can contribute
- Clear norms and expectations for response depth and peer engagement ([Peer Interaction](peer-interaction.md))
- Instructor or peer [Moderation](moderation.md) to sustain momentum and press for justification

#### Constraints
- Closed or yes/no prompts produce convergent, low-level responses and suppress peer-to-peer exchange [~M]
- Prompts requiring knowledge learners do not yet have lead to speculation and off-task discussion rather than productive struggle [-M]
- In large or anonymous forums, prompts without accountability structures yield low participation and shallow agreement-seeking posts [-M]
- Poorly timed prompts — before learners have engaged with material — create social pressure without cognitive benefit [-W]

### Target Learners
- Learners with enough prior knowledge to hold a defensible position; complete novices benefit more from [Demonstration](demonstration.md) or direct instruction first
- Adolescents through adults; younger learners need more heavily scaffolded prompt structures and turn-taking routines
- Prompts that connect to learners' experience increase willingness to participate [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: articulating and testing explanations against peer reasoning [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Argumentation and critical thinking: constructing claims, giving evidence, responding to counterarguments
- Perspective-taking and communication: negotiating meaning in a group setting

### Affordances
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — a prompt is the minimal unit that converts a presentation into an activity; it forces retrieval, articulation, and evaluation rather than recognition
- [Social Learning](../principles/social-learning.md) — prompts structure the peer modeling and vicarious learning that occur when learners observe how others reason about the same question
- [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — a well-posed prompt creates the productive disequilibrium that drives deeper processing
- [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md) — recurring discussion prompts around authentic problems help learners adopt the discourse norms of a discipline

## Examples

**[Piazza](https://piazza.com)** — Q&A platform where instructor-seeded prompts structure threaded discussion; instructor endorsements surface high-quality student answers.

**[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — Social annotation platform; prompts anchored to specific passages of a reading generate localized, text-grounded discussion threads.

**Socratic Seminar (Paideia method)** — An open-ended prompt drawn from a shared text opens a student-led dialogue; follow-up questions probe assumptions and evidence rather than the facilitator's preferred answer.

**[Harvard Project Zero thinking routines](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines)** — Published prompt structures ("What makes you say that?", "Claim–Support–Question") that operationalize discussion prompts as repeatable classroom routines.
