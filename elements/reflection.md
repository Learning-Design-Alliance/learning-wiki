# Reflection

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Reflection is the deliberate act of analyzing one's own experiences, actions, or decisions after (or during) a learning task in order to extract lessons and adjust future behavior. It converts raw experience into usable knowledge by prompting learners to examine what happened, why it happened, and what they would do differently. Schön (1983) distinguished *reflection-on-action* (after the fact) from *reflection-in-action* (during performance), and both forms are designable instructional elements.

## Design Implications

Reflection deepens learning by engaging learners in self-explanation and self-monitoring rather than leaving experience unprocessed [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on structure: open-ended prompts like "reflect on your learning" produce vague responses, while prompts that direct attention to specific decisions, errors, and alternatives produce substantive analysis. Reflection is most powerful when paired with feedback, so that learners compare their self-analysis against external evidence [Hattie & Timperley, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8535.2007.00759.x) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A concrete experience or performance to reflect on — reflection without content becomes rumination
- Structured prompts that direct attention to decisions, evidence, and alternatives (e.g., "What did you try? What did you expect? What actually happened?")
- Psychological safety: learners must be able to surface errors without penalty for reflection to be honest
- A mechanism for acting on insights — reflection that never informs future practice decays quickly

#### Constraints
- Unstructured or purely emotional reflection can reinforce misconceptions rather than correct them [~M] — without evidence or feedback, learners may draw confident but wrong conclusions from experience
- Reflection on novel or low-knowledge tasks yields little, because learners lack the schema to interpret what happened [~M]
- Over-reliance on reflective journaling as assessment can produce performative writing aimed at pleasing the grader rather than genuine analysis [-M]
- Reflection consumes time that might otherwise go to practice; for procedural fluency goals, additional practice often outperforms additional reflection [~S]

### Target Learners
- Professionals and advanced learners with enough experience to have something substantive to analyze
- Learners developing self-regulation, where self-monitoring is itself a target skill [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less valuable for complete novices, who benefit more from direct guidance and worked examples than from analyzing their own unguided attempts [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive development: learning to monitor and evaluate one's own thinking
- Transfer: extracting generalizable principles from specific experiences
- Professional identity and judgment formation, especially in clinical, leadership, and practice-based fields
- Error learning: diagnosing why a performance fell short [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — reflection is the mechanism by which learners articulate and internalize expert reasoning after observing and practicing; it corresponds to the articulation and exploration phases of the pattern
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — reflection supplies the self-evaluation loop (forethought → performance → self-reflection) that drives strategy adjustment across study cycles
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — reflective self-assessment lets learners locate gaps themselves, making subsequent feedback actionable rather than merely received
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — reflection-on-action is how lessons from authentic, situated experience become portable knowledge rather than context-bound habit

## Examples

**[Activities for Student Self-Reflection](../strategies/activities_for_student_self-reflection.md)** — Structured post-task prompts (exit tickets, learning logs) that direct attention to strategies used and adjustments needed.

**[5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)** — Brief one-on-one exchanges in which instructor questions prompt learners to articulate and evaluate their own writing decisions.

**Debriefing in simulation-based medical education** — Post-simulation debriefs (e.g., the Debriefing with Good Judgment approach at the Center for Medical Simulation, https://harvardmedsim.org) use facilitator-led structured inquiry to surface reasoning gaps; a well-established professional reflection format.

**Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle** — Published experiential curricula (Kolb, 1984) position reflective observation as the mandatory stage between concrete experience and abstract conceptualization.
