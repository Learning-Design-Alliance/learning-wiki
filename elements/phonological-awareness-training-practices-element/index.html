Phonological awareness training practices: rhyme detection, blending, and segmentation activities¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 6 claims (3 for, 3 mixed) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Phonological awareness training is any practice targeting young children's ability to detect or manipulate the sounds in words independent of meaning. Practices "tend to focus on teaching children to rhyme or to detect alliteration in language" and include rhyme detection training (e.g., identifying which word in a series of three does not sound like the others), blending training (combining three spoken sounds into a word), and segmentation training (separating a short word such as "cat" into its sounds) at the phoneme, syllable, or word level. Training can occur in regular and special education classrooms, individually, in pairs, or in small groups, as core curriculum or a supplement. No single developer exists; the reviewed interventions were developed by the study authors.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Can be delivered by teachers or practitioners with children individually, in pairs, or in small groups, as part of the core curriculum or as a supplement

Constraints¶

  • No single developer provides materials; the reviewed interventions are not available for distribution through a common developer
  • Cost information is not available about teacher or practitioner training and implementation

Target Learners¶

  • preschool children with disabilities or developmental delays, including those with developmental delays and speech/language or learning disabilities

Target Learning Goals¶

  • phonological awareness: detecting, deleting, segmenting, and blending segments of spoken words; identifying or producing rhyme and alliteration

Claims¶

  • Blending-focused phonological awareness training produced statistically significant positive differences on blending outcomes in O'Connor et al. (1993) [+W]
  • Phonological awareness training has potentially positive effects on communication/language competencies for preschool children with learning disabilities, with a small extent of evidence [+W]
  • Of 225 reviewed studies of phonological awareness training, only four RCTs met WWC evidence standards without reservations [~W]
  • Tyler et al. (2003) found no statistically significant differences between phonological awareness training and a morphosyntactic intervention (indeterminate effect) [~W]
  • Tyler et al. (2011) found no statistically significant differences between a phonemic awareness intervention and a morphosyntactic intervention at the US site (indeterminate effect) [~W]
  • Phonemic Awareness Training Improves Reading [+M]
