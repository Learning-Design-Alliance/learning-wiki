Problem Solving¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 19 claims (9 for, 8 mixed, 2 against) · 26 studies (9 causal, 5 theoretical, 4 review, 3 design, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 qualitative, 1 associational), q1–q4 · 6 of 26 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Problem solving is the element in which learners identify, analyze, and respond to a challenge by generating and evaluating possible solutions. It is useful when the instructional goal is not only recall, but reasoned action in the face of constraints.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A meaningful problem or challenge
  • A process for evaluating possible solutions
  • A problem whose solution is not directly retrievable from instruction — learners must reason, not recall
  • Prior instruction or resources sufficient to make the problem solvable (Activation of relevant prior knowledge before the task)
  • Access to feedback: expert solutions, peer comparison, or instructor coaching after attempted solutions
  • A debrief or Self-Explanation prompt that helps learners abstract the underlying principle from their solution path

Constraints¶

  • Ill-structured problems may require stronger scaffolding for novices
  • Pure discovery with minimal guidance is less effective than guided problem solving, especially for novices Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [-M] — unguided search overloads working memory and can reinforce errors
  • Ill-structured problems without success criteria leave learners unable to evaluate their own solutions; provide rubrics or exemplars
  • High-difficulty problems can demotivate learners who lack self-efficacy; scaffold early success before increasing challenge
  • Experts gain little from structured problems and may benefit more from open or novel variants Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Develop analytical reasoning, strategy use, and solution generation.
  • Analytical reasoning: diagnosing, evaluating, and justifying solution strategies
  • Application and transfer: using concepts in novel, realistic contexts Whole-task practice supports transfer better than part-task drill alone. [+M]
  • Metacognitive skill: planning, monitoring, and revising an approach over an extended task

Affordances¶

  • Problem-based Learning
  • Cognitive Activation
  • Active Learning — problem-solving tasks enact this principle by requiring learners to generate and defend solutions rather than receive them, producing the retrieval and elaboration that passive formats omit
  • Cognitive Load Management — task sequencing (completion tasks, then near-transfer, then far-transfer) and on-demand hints keep intrinsic load within working-memory limits instead of dumping full complexity on learners at once
  • Collaborative Learning — group problem solving exposes learners to alternative solution paths and distributes the reasoning load, provided roles and accountability are structured
  • Situated Learning — embedding problems in authentic professional contexts connects the task to the conditions under which the skill will actually be used

Claims¶

  • Functional fixedness — treating an object's or idea's function as fixed — blocks solutions that require reinterpreting it. [+M]
  • Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [+W]
  • Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes [+W]
  • Usability testing with educators and developers identified chatbot strengths for independent challenge-solving but limited feedback for low-participation learners [+W]
  • Problem-based learning connecting coursework and practice is associated with stronger candidate skill development and self-efficacy [+W]
  • Evaluating GenAI-generated solutions produced no significant differences on midterm scores, final exam scores, course percentages, or normalized exam change relative to solving problems directly [~W]
  • QR faculty on several CSU campuses are incorporating active-learning and problem-solving pedagogies into redesigned entry-level math courses [+W]
  • VSDR helped students reflect on and adopt alternative problem-solving approaches to tasks [+W]
  • Most students solved BioWorld cases, and successful solvers took less time and fewer actions than unsuccessful solvers [~W]
  • Providing contrasting cases reduces the number of solution attempts students generate, both before and after instruction [-W]
  • Older adults describe less effective problem-solving solutions, producing fewer relevant steps than younger adults [~W]
  • Problem isomorphs with identical structure differ greatly in difficulty; Monster Change took 16 times as long to solve as the standard problem [~W]
  • MicroDYN is a stronger predictor of the in-game problem solving estimates than Raven's, with the rule-application facet more relevant than rule acquisition [~W]
  • Successful and unsuccessful complex problem solvers differ in transition frequencies between questions and decisions [~W]
  • Providing subgoals did not help on the Missionaries and Cannibals problem but facilitated performance on an extended 5-Missionaries and 5-Cannibals variant [~W]

The evidence by question¶

  • How much guidance do novices need inside a problem-centred design? Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [+S]
  • Should learners attempt a problem before the idea is taught, and what must the teaching afterwards do? Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [+M]

Related Elements¶

  • Solution Development
  • Decision-Making
  • Problem-Based Learning
  • Case-Based Learning — a problem-solving task anchored in a rich, realistic case narrative
  • Case Studies — published cases that supply the problem context and constraints
  • Coaching — the ongoing feedback mechanism that keeps problem solving productive rather than flailing
  • Collaboration — the social structure that turns individual problem solving into comparative reasoning

Patterns That Use This Element¶

  • Problem-Based Learning
  • Goal-Based Scenarios
  • Problem-Based Learning — the problem is the curriculum; tasks drive all content acquisition
  • Goal-Based Scenarios — simulated environments where learners pursue a goal and the system responds to their problem-solving moves
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the exploration phase, where learners tackle problems with decreasing support

Examples¶

  • Learners compare alternative solutions and justify which best fits the constraints of the problem.
