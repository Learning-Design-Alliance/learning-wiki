# Peer Explanation

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Peer explanation asks learners to verbalize their reasoning, solution methods, or conceptual understanding to a classmate. The act of articulating forces learners to reconstruct, organize, and monitor their own understanding, while the listener's questions expose gaps the explainer did not know they had.

## Design Implications

Peer explanation converts passive comprehension into generative processing: explaining to another person produces deeper learning than explaining to oneself or studying for a test [Explaining to another person yields greater learning than restudying or explaining to oneself.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.07.001) [+S]. The mechanism overlaps with self-explanation — verbalizing forces integration of new material with prior knowledge [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] — but the social setting adds accountability and the possibility of corrective feedback from the listener. Explanations must be substantive: high-quality elaborated explanations predict learning gains, while low-level responses do not [Learning in small groups depends on the quality and level of peer interaction, not mere participation.](https://doi.org/10.1016/0883-0355(89)90014-1) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task or problem with enough conceptual depth that explanation is non-trivial
- Structured prompts or sentence starters so explanations are elaborated, not restatements ([Articulation](articulation.md))
- A listener role with an active job: ask clarifying questions, identify gaps, paraphrase back
- Enough shared knowledge that the listener can follow — or pairing that lets the explainer benefit from reconstructing basics

#### Constraints
- Explainers with misconceptions can entrench them by rehearsing flawed explanations aloud, especially without expert correction [~M]
- Unequal participation: in unstructured groups, some learners explain and others receive little benefit [Learning in small groups depends on the quality and level of peer interaction, not mere participation.](https://doi.org/10.1016/0883-0355(89)90014-1) [-M]
- Listeners who passively accept fluent-sounding explanations gain little; benefits accrue mainly to the explainer unless the listener is given an active verification role [~M]
- Less effective when the task is purely procedural with nothing to reason about — explanation collapses into step recitation

### Target Learners
- Learners with enough baseline knowledge to generate a coherent explanation; complete novices may simply parrot material [~M]
- K-12 and higher education; effective across both when tasks are matched to level
- Learners who overestimate their understanding — the social demand of explaining exposes illusions of knowing

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: articulating why a procedure works, not just how
- Metacognitive monitoring: noticing gaps while attempting to explain [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Communication and disciplinary discourse: learning to use the language of the domain

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — peer explanation is the minimal unit of collaboration: two learners, one explaining, one probing; it structures interaction so both parties have generative roles rather than dividing labor
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — explanation is a generative activity that requires constructing output rather than receiving input, replacing listening time with meaning-making time
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — verbalizing forces learners to rebuild knowledge in their own words, revealing and resolving inconsistencies between their mental model and the target concept
- [Metacognition](../principles/metacognition.md) — the anticipation of explaining to a peer prompts self-monitoring during initial study, not just afterward

## Related Elements

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI for Math)](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) — students explain their solution strategies to peers and teacher, making diverse methods visible
- [POGIL (Process Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning)](https://pogil.org) — team roles include a "spokesperson" who reports and explains the group's reasoning, institutionalizing peer explanation

## Examples

**Peer Instruction (Mazur, Harvard University)** — Students answer a concept question individually, then explain their reasoning to a neighbor and re-answer. Widely adopted in physics and beyond; see [https://www.peerinstruction.net](https://www.peerinstruction.net).

**[POGIL](https://pogil.org)** — Structured group roles in guided-inquiry activities assign explanation and reporting duties, ensuring every student periodically articulates reasoning.

**Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — Students take turns explaining and questioning text segments in small groups; the explainer role rotates so all learners practice articulation.
