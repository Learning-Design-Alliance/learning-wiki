# Position Development

## Description
Position development asks learners to form, articulate, and defend a stance on a genuinely contested issue, then refine that stance in response to evidence and counterargument. The stance is treated as provisional: the learning happens in the cycle of claiming, justifying, and revising rather than in arriving at a "correct" answer.

## Design Implications

Position development works because it forces learners to retrieve, organize, and generate arguments rather than recognize them — the generative effort of constructing a case produces deeper processing than reading about the issue [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the issue being genuinely two-sided and on learners encountering strong opposing views; positions refined against counterargument are better warranted and more transferable than positions defended in an echo chamber [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A contested issue with legitimate multiple stances — not a question with a settled answer
- Access to evidence sources of varying quality, so learners must evaluate rather than merely collect support
- A structured opportunity to encounter and respond to opposing positions ([Peer Review](peer-review.md), [Structured Academic Controversy](structured-academic-controversy.md), or [Class Discussion](class-discussion.md))
- Time and expectation for revision — the position should be redraftable, not submitted once

#### Constraints
- Learners with strong prior commitments often engage in motivated reasoning, evaluating evidence by whether it supports their existing stance rather than its quality [~M] — structured perspective-switching (e.g., arguing the opposing side) mitigates this
- If the issue is not genuinely contested, the exercise collapses into guessing the instructor's preferred answer [-M]
- Adversarial formats can reward rhetorical dominance over epistemic quality; without rubrics that value evidence quality and responsiveness, debate skill can diverge from reasoning skill [-W]
- High-stakes public defense of a position raises anxiety and can entrench rather than revise initial stances [~W]

### Target Learners
- Advanced learners with enough domain knowledge to locate and evaluate evidence; novices tend to argue from assertion [~M]
- Students in law, debate, ethics, political science, and policy fields where constructing and defending positions is the target performance
- Learners who benefit from [Self-Efficacy](self-efficacy.md) building through public, defended commitment — successfully defending a position strengthens persistence beliefs [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Argumentation skill: claim–evidence–warrant structure, anticipating and answering objections
- Evidence evaluation: weighing source quality and discriminating strong from weak support
- Perspective-taking: understanding the strongest form of opposing views (steelmanning)
- Rhetoric and persuasion: adapting reasoning to an audience

### Affordances
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — position development is the generative application of argumentation: learners do not analyze others' arguments but produce and defend their own under challenge
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — the stance is not transmitted but constructed; learners build understanding through the disequilibrium of encountering counterargument and revising
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — paired or opposed positions create genuine communicative purpose: persuasion of a real interlocutor, not a performance for a grader
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — peer and instructor critique of a draft position provides actionable feedback at the point where the argument can still be revised

## Examples

**[Structured Academic Controversy](https://www.teachingworks.org/workshops/structured-academic-controversy)** (Johnson & Johnson, University of Minnesota cooperative learning tradition) — learners research one side, present, then switch sides and argue the opposition before synthesizing; the forced switch is the refinement mechanism.

**[National Speech & Debate Association](https://www.speechanddebate.org)** — competitive Lincoln-Douglas and Public Forum formats in which students develop a case, defend it across rounds, and adapt it in response to opponent rebuttals.

**Harvard Business School case method** — students commit to a position on a case before class, then defend and revise it under cold-calling and peer challenge; see [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md).

**[IB Theory of Knowledge](https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/core/theory-of-knowledge/)** — students write a sustained position essay on a knowledge question, evaluated on argument quality and counterclaim treatment rather than conclusion.
