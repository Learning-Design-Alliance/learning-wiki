# Model Revision

## Description
Model revision asks learners to articulate a working conceptual model, confront it with evidence and feedback, and deliberately revise it. The element treats the learner's initial understanding as a draft to be evaluated and improved rather than a fixed starting point, making the *process* of conceptual change visible and iterative.

## Design Implications

Model revision supports conceptual change by forcing learners to compare their predictions against evidence, which exposes gaps between their model and the target model [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. Revision is most effective when learners explain *why* the evidence warrants a change, not just that it does — self-explanation during revision produces deeper conceptual understanding than simply receiving corrective feedback [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- An explicit, externalized initial model (diagram, written explanation, or prediction) that can be compared against later states
- Evidence or feedback that is genuinely diagnostic — it must discriminate between the learner's model and the correct one
- A structured revision step in which learners state what changed and why ([Justification](justification.md), [Argumentation](argumentation.md))
- Time for at least one full revise-and-recheck cycle; a single pass rarely produces durable change

#### Constraints
- Learners with high confidence in a flawed model often dismiss discrepant evidence unless the task explicitly requires them to evaluate it [High-confidence errors improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M] — confidence must be surfaced and challenged, not ignored
- Vague or non-diagnostic feedback leads to cosmetic revisions (rewording) rather than structural change [-M]
- Repeated unguided revision cycles add extraneous load for novices; scaffolding the comparison (e.g., side-by-side model–evidence links) is needed [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [~S]
- Less effective when the target model is procedural or arbitrary, where there is no conceptual structure to revise

### Target Learners
- Learners in STEM disciplines who hold intuitive but incomplete models of mechanisms (physics, biology, climate science)
- Higher education students with enough prior knowledge to generate an initial model worth revising
- Learners prone to plausibility-based reasoning, who benefit from explicit evidence–claim linking tasks [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing or restructuring intuitive misconceptions
- Scientific reasoning: evaluating the evidential status of claims ([Scientific Reasoning](scientific-reasoning.md))
- Epistemic cognition: understanding that models are provisional and evidence-driven

### Affordances
- [Inquiry-Based Learning](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md) — model revision enacts inquiry by making evidence the arbiter of the learner's own ideas rather than the instructor's authority
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../principles/cognitive-load-theory.md) — externalizing the model offloads working memory, freeing capacity for comparing model and evidence rather than juggling both internally
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — revision requires learners to do evaluative work on their own output, not merely receive corrections
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — the model–evidence link structure is a temporary frame that can be faded as learners internalize the evaluate-and-revise habit

## Related Elements
- [Scientific Reasoning](scientific-reasoning.md) — supplies the evaluative standards learners apply when judging their model against evidence
- [Justification](justification.md) — the written or oral warrant for *why* a revision was made; without it, revision becomes guesswork
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — the broader discourse structure in which competing models and evidence are weighed
- [Cognitive Conflict](cognitive-conflict.md) — the discrepant-evidence moment that motivates revision
- [Coaching](coaching.md) — instructor feedback that makes revision cycles diagnostic rather than cosmetic

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Model-Evidence Link (MEL) Reasoning Pattern](../patterns/model-evidence-link-mel-reasoning-pattern.md) — the core pattern: learners draw links between scientific models and evidence, then revise their plausibility judgments

## Examples
- **[WISE (Web-Based Inquiry Science Environment)](https://wise.berkeley.edu)** — NSF-funded inquiry projects in which students make predictions, run simulations, and revise their explanations after evaluating evidence.
- **Model-Evidence Link (MEL) activities** (Lombardi et al.) — students diagram the strength of connections between alternative models and evidence lines, then re-rate model plausibility; used extensively in climate- and geoscience education.
- **Predict–Observe–Explain (POE)** tasks — learners commit to a prediction, observe a discrepant event, and revise their explanation; a lightweight classroom version of model revision.
