Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS): structured reciprocal peer-tutoring supplement for grades 2–6 reading and math¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

PALS is "a supplemental peer-tutoring program in which student pairs perform a structured set of activities in reading or math". During 30–35 minute sessions students take turns acting as tutor, coaching and correcting one another; pairs meet three or four times weekly for reading and twice weekly for math. Teachers form pairs and assign skills based on judgment of student needs and reassign pairs regularly. Developed by Lynn Fuchs and Doug Fuchs in 1997, it is distributed by the Vanderbilt Kennedy Center and has versions for kindergarten, first grade, and high school.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Teachers first train students in PALS procedures using training lessons from the teacher's manual; teachers attended a full-day workshop and received a manual with scripted student-training lessons

Constraints¶

  • The program is designed as a supplement to the general education curriculum, not a core replacement

Target Learners¶

  • students in grades 2–6 with diverse academic needs, including students with learning disabilities and English language learners

Target Learning Goals¶

  • reading fluency
  • reading comprehension
  • mathematics computation and concepts/applications

Claims¶

  • PALS effects were statistically significant for the full diverse sample but not for the learning-disability subsets analyzed separately [+W]
  • PALS shows no discernible effects on mathematics for students with learning disabilities [-W]
  • PALS shows potentially positive effects on reading comprehension for students with learning disabilities (average effect size 0.75, not statistically significant) [+W]
  • PALS shows potentially positive effects on reading fluency for students with learning disabilities (average effect size 0.36, not statistically significant) [+W]
