Practice Testing¶

Element · All elements
Evidence · 68 claims (33 for, 31 mixed, 4 against) · 42 studies (24 causal, 7 review, 6 quant-synthesis, 2 associational, 2 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 18 of 42 report an effect size · 64 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Practice testing (retrieval practice) asks learners to recall or apply information from memory — via low- or no-stakes quizzes, flashcards, free recall, or problem-solving — before or instead of restudying. The act of successful retrieval itself strengthens memory and reveals gaps, making testing a learning event rather than merely an assessment event.

Design Implications¶

Retrieval practice produces substantially larger and more durable learning gains than restudying or elaborative review at equivalent time on task Dunlosky et al. rated practice testing among the highest-utility learning techniques. [+S]. Its benefits depend on retrieval actually occurring: feedback after incorrect retrieval is essential, and tests that learners cannot answer at all yield little benefit. Spacing repeated tests over time multiplies the effect relative to massed testing.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Questions that require genuine retrieval (recall, application), not recognition of recently seen text
  • Feedback or answer-checking so errors are corrected rather than consolidated
  • Sufficient delay between study and test that retrieval is effortful but achievable — "desirable difficulty"
  • Repeated retrieval opportunities distributed over time (Spaced Practice where available)

Constraints¶

  • Testing before any instruction produces weak or negative effects when retrieval failure rates are high [~M] — pretesting helps mainly when learners can make plausible partial attempts
  • Repeated immediate testing of the same item in one session (massed retrieval) adds little beyond the first successful recall [~S]
  • Simple recognition formats (multiple choice with short exposure) can encourage cue-based guessing rather than retrieval [-W]
  • High-stakes use shifts the function from learning to evaluation and can induce anxiety that offsets gains [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Benefits are remarkably broad across ages and subject domains, including young children and learners with low prior knowledge Rowland's meta-analysis found robust effects across ability levels. [+S]
  • Learners prone to illusions of fluency from rereading benefit most, because testing corrects metacognitive miscalibration [~S]
  • Learners with high test anxiety need low-stakes framing to capture the benefit without the cost

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of factual and conceptual knowledge over delays of weeks to months
  • Transfer and inference: retrieval practice improves application to new questions better than restudying Karpicke & Blunt found retrieval outperformed concept mapping for meaningful learning. [+S]
  • Metacognitive accuracy: calibrating judgments of what is and is not yet known

Affordances¶

  • Assessment for Learning — low-stakes testing is the core mechanism: it generates evidence about learning while simultaneously producing learning
  • Cognitive Load Management — successful retrieval consolidates information so it no longer competes for working memory, freeing capacity for harder material
  • Spacing and Distributed Practice — tests are natural spacing events; scheduling retrieval at expanding intervals compounds the testing effect
  • Self-Regulated Learning — self-testing gives learners accurate feedback on their own state of knowledge, driving better study decisions

Claims¶

  • Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]
  • Repeatedly recalling a text without feedback or rereading produces the best one-week retention, while repeated studying leads on a final test at the end of the session [+M]
  • Learners Misjudge Retrieval Benefit [+M]
  • Spaced Retrieval Improves Retention [+M]
  • Practicing retrieval of some portions of an educational text can enhance retention of related nontested portions (retrieval-induced facilitation), but not when relational encoding is disrupted [~W]
  • Quizzing outperforms no/filler activity, fewer-question testing and restudying, with a larger advantage over no/filler activity (low reexposure) than over restudying (high reexposure); against other elaborative strategies its advantage is small and not significant. [+W]
  • Benefits of retrieval practice have persisted for 6 months in medical students and 9 months in middle-school students [~W]
  • A review reports that pretesting helps paired-associate learning only when correct-answer feedback is immediate, but helps trivia-fact learning even when feedback is delayed up to 24 hours [~W]
  • Retrieval practice produces reliable positive effects at short delays, although some forgetting must set in so that the final test is not at ceiling [+W]
  • Retrieval practice enhances learning across domains and the lifespan in applied educational settings [+W]
  • A math teacher whose students had typically prepared for tests by rereading notes implemented three retrieval-practice strategies after learning that rereading is not effective for long-term retention [+W]
  • Repeated successful retrieval during learning predicted final recall in both experiments [+W]
  • Pre-instruction testing enhances memory for directly tested text content relative to non-testing controls, at both immediate and one-week delayed posttests [+W]
  • Retrieval practice enhances performance on final inference and transfer questions relative to restudying, although a single-word fill-in-the-blank task did not [~W]
  • Retrieval practice instructions improved final recall of translations in Experiment 1 [+W]
  • Spaced retrieval practice (successive relearning) produced multi-year retention of foreign language vocabulary in adults, as reported by the review [+W]
  • Students given retrieval practice instructions spontaneously used a repeated retrieval strategy on new materials one week later without further instructions [+W]
  • Testing benefits retention over a conservative restudy control, establishing the testing effect as robust [+W]
  • Classroom pretesting improves high-stakes exam performance on pretested content by 8–9% over a multi-week course [~W]
  • Post-baseline practice condition has a significant multivariate effect on combined posttest performance and observed practice effort after adaptive AI-assisted pretesting [+W]
  • Transfer of test-enhanced learning is greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, to medical diagnosis problems, and to mediator and related word cues [+W]
  • Practice testing yields transferrable learning relative to a nontesting reexposure control condition (d = 0.40) [+W]
  • Effects of pre-instruction testing on untested but studied materials are highly inconsistent across experiments and materials [~W]
  • Transfer of test-enhanced learning is weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested materials seen during initial study, and to problems involving worked examples [~W]
  • Effects of instructions on some measures were inconsistent across experiments, and instructed students did not recall all items to criterion [~W]
  • Individual learners complete more flashcard practice cycles than paired learners, though inconsistently across experiments [~W]
  • Requiring overt retrieval eliminates the metacognitive calibration advantage of paired flashcard learning [~W]
  • Targeted retrieval via short-answer tasks improves metacognitive calibration more than holistic retrieval via free-recall [~W]
  • Paired flashcard learners show more accurate immediate metacognitive calibration than individual learners, who overestimate by roughly 20% [~W]
  • Practice retrieval performance did not differ significantly between the three retrieval strategy conditions [~W]
  • Test condition did not affect pre-post learning-outcome differences, but school type was a significant factor in the multivariate model [~W]
  • Taking a multiple-choice test boosts performance on later tests compared with nontested control conditions, including with complex SAT II materials [+W]
  • Multiple-choice testing improves later cued recall of prose facts, a benefit that persists though reduced after a one-week delay [+W]
  • More frequent practice-test participation predicts higher sensitivity (better detection of correct answers) in final-exam judgments, with and without feedback [+W]
  • The testing effect is largest at a retention interval of 1–6 days (g = 0.82) and robust across all intervals [+W]
  • The retrieval benefit occurs at the level of individual items, not through general practice at retrieval [+W]
  • The retrieval (TTST) advantage appeared at both short (5 min) and long (24 h) retention delays, with no reliable evidence that it differed between them [+W]
  • A single practice test produced a larger effect (g = 0.70) than two or more practice tests (g = 0.51) [~W]
  • When total learning time is fixed, permitting learners to skip feedback after retrieval improves delayed final recall compared with mandatory feedback [~W]
  • A systematic review of 50 classroom experiments found retrieval practice yields medium-to-large benefits in most cases, but only 6% were conducted in non-WEIRD countries [~W]
  • Practice with feedback, regardless of feedback type, improves metacognitive calibration relative to lecture, reducing overconfidence by about 13 percentage points [+W]
  • Self-regulating students choose to complete multiple-choice practice questions until each item has been answered correctly about one time during practice.   • Self-regulated multiple-choice students scored lower on final tests than experimenter-controlled groups but spent less time practicing, making their one-correct criterion comparatively time-efficient.   • In-person but not online self-regulated multiple-choice students completed fewer restudy trials before their first practice test than cued-recall students. [~W]
  • The spacing-induced practice decrement is confined to the first two quiz questions, with no significant difference on the third [~W]
  • Testing after studying four worked examples is not more effective for delayed test performance than restudying examples, with isomorphic or identical tasks (Experiment 3) [-W]
  • Testing via identical problem solving after studying identical worked examples did not significantly improve delayed test performance compared with restudying identical examples (Experiment 2) [-W]
  • The overt-retrieval advantage does not significantly differ by definition complexity (number of idea units), contrary to a retrieval-dynamics prediction [~W]
  • Across eight delayed-test comparisons, testing showed a small numerical advantage over restudy, but confidence intervals were wide and the combined effect imprecise [+W]
  • Taking an immediate plus delayed final test yields higher delayed test performance with less invested effort than taking only a delayed test (Experiment 2) [+W]
  • Testing after studying four worked examples does not enhance delayed test performance compared with restudy among vocational education students in probability calculation (Experiment 4) [-W]
  • Overt retrieval also outperforms covert retrieval for key terms, but with a smaller advantage than for definitions [~W]
  • Transfer across response formats is incomplete: practicing terms benefited definition recall, but practicing definitions showed no overt-over-covert advantage on a term test [~M]
  • Only individual performance feedback, not general instruction about the testing effect, produced long-term increases in retrieval practice use, and only for students who benefited from retrieval practice [~W]
  • Retrieval practice yields better delayed test performance than restudy (the testing effect) in both experiments [+W]
  • Students who benefited from retrieval practice rated it as more effective after receiving individual feedback, while other groups showed no statistically significant change (Experiment 1) [~W]
  • Individual feedback impacted perceived utility only to a limited extent, and general feedback lowered perceived utility of retrieval practice (Experiment 2) [~W]
  • Taking a fill-in-the-blank test is more time-consuming than restudying the same material [~W]
  • In the author's cited longitudinal classroom experiments, lessons with retrieval practice raised course grades from a C to an A level, and three spaced retrieval opportunities were optimal even nine months later [+M]
  • Retrieval practice mainly improves metamemory whereas generative activities mainly improve metacomprehension [~W]
  • Oral explaining yields more accurate meta-comprehension judgments than written explaining and retrieval practice [-W]
  • Retrieval practice improves delayed but not immediate performance, consistent with a desirable-difficulties explanation [+W]
  • Explaining was not more effective than oral retrieval practice overall in Experiment 1 [~W]
  • Practice effects on the Thorndike-McCall test over two or more hours of testing are very small [~W]
  • The overcoming-barriers intervention increases intention to use general retrieval practice methods immediately post-intervention but has no effect on reported use three weeks later [~W]
  • Stepwise MCQs, which delay answer options by 4 s, enhance vocabulary retention significantly compared to standard MCQs in three of four experiments, measured days after practice. The evidence by question¶

  • Does retrieving material (quizzes, practice tests, recall) improve later retention more than restudying it? Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]
  • Does the benefit carry to new questions, inferences and applications, or only to the items practised? Retrieval Practice Improves Transfer [+S]
  • Does testing learners before instruction, when they cannot yet answer, improve what they learn from it? Pretesting enhances learning [+S]
  • Will learners choose to test themselves on their own, and can they be taught to? Learners underuse effective learning strategies such as spacing and retrieval practice [+M]

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — practice testing is the memory-focused subset of practice; both depend on active production rather than review
  • Feedback — corrects errors surfaced by retrieval; testing without feedback risks consolidating mistakes
  • Spaced Repetition — schedules the retrieval events that maximize retention
  • Formative Assessment — the instructional pattern built on low-stakes checking

Patterns That Use This Element¶

  • Direct Instruction — frequent checks for understanding function as embedded retrieval practice
  • Mastery Learning — repeated testing with feedback until criterion is met
  • Gagné's 9 Events — "eliciting performance" and "assessing performance" events

Examples¶

Anki — Spaced-repetition flashcard software that schedules retrieval at expanding intervals; the canonical implementation of combined testing and spacing.

Khan Academy — Mastery-tracking exercise sets that require repeated successful retrieval across sessions before a skill is marked complete.

Retrieval Practice Guide — Agarwal and Bain's practitioner resource with classroom routines (brain dumps, two-things exit tickets) implementing free-recall testing.

Quizlet — Self-testing tools (Learn and Test modes) that convert study sets into retrieval trials with feedback. - Give students minimal direct instruction that retrieval practice beats restudying and that items should be recalled correctly three times before being dropped - Combine free-recall tasks followed by short-answer questions to cover both broad understanding and key information - Use frequent, low-stakes, cumulative tests in varied formats, including pretests, and encourage student self-testing
