# Child-Directed Speech

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Child-directed speech (sometimes called "parentese" or, informally, "baby talk") is a near-universal pattern adults adopt when addressing infants and toddlers: exaggerated vowel and consonant sounds, higher pitch, slower pace, and heightened facial expression relative to adult-directed speech. It increases infant attention and engagement, and helps infants more clearly perceive the phonemic boundaries of the language(s) around them. It also establishes an early reciprocal turn-taking rhythm between speaker and listener that anticipates conversational structure well before the infant can speak.

Infants begin life able to discriminate the phonemes of any human language, but by around 10 months this discrimination narrows to the phonemes actually present in their ambient language(s); contrasts not used in the ambient language(s) come to be treated as equivalent. Because this narrowing happens early and is influenced by the speech infants actually hear, child-directed speech during this window plausibly supports the phoneme discrimination that later underlies clear speech perception and production.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A responsive adult actually interacting with the infant — child-directed speech from a live, attentive speaker, not passive media exposure, is what the evidence supports
- An infant who is awake, alert, and available to attend to the interaction
#### Constraints
- Prolonged, overly simplified speech beyond the toddler years can delay exposure to more complex, age-appropriate vocabulary and syntax if it isn't gradually withdrawn as the child's own language develops
- Recorded media using exaggerated, child-directed-style speech (children's television, videos) does not substitute for responsive live interaction — the research support is specifically for reciprocal, adult-attentive speech

### Target Learners
- Infants (birth–12 months) during early phoneme discrimination and babbling
- Toddlers (12–36 months) during early receptive and productive vocabulary growth
- Infants and toddlers in multilingual homes, to support phoneme discrimination across more than one ambient language

### Target Learning Goals
- Phoneme discrimination within the ambient language(s)
- Early attention to and engagement with a speaking partner
- Foundational turn-taking patterns that later support conversational exchange

### Affordances
- [Support Early Language Development](../principles/supporting-early-language-development.md) — child-directed speech is one concrete practice within this broader principle
