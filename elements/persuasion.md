# Persuasion

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Persuasion tasks ask learners to construct and deliver arguments designed to change an audience's beliefs or actions, using logic, evidence, and rhetorical technique. Unlike [Argumentation](argumentation.md), which emphasizes reasoned justification, persuasion foregrounds audience awareness — tailoring claims, credibility, and emotional appeal to the listeners being addressed.

## Design Implications

Persuasion tasks integrate knowledge by requiring learners to select, organize, and defend evidence for a specific audience, which supports deeper processing than restating content [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. They work best when learners must argue positions they did not choose, since perspective-taking forces genuine engagement with opposing evidence rather than rehearsing existing views. Structured formats with explicit roles and turn-taking outperform open-ended "discuss and convince" prompts, because unstructured persuasion devolves into assertion and repetition [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M] — successful persuasion experiences also build communicative confidence that transfers to later advocacy tasks.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine, contestable issue with defensible positions on multiple sides
- An authentic or simulated audience whose characteristics learners must consider
- Explicit criteria for evaluating persuasive quality (evidence, reasoning, credibility, audience fit)
- Preparation time for research and drafting; impromptu persuasion favors confident speakers over careful reasoners

#### Constraints
- Learners may argue from conviction rather than evidence, entrenching misconceptions instead of examining them [-M]
- Competitive framing (winning the argument) can suppress genuine reconsideration of evidence and reduce willingness to update beliefs [-M]
- Dominant speakers can monopolize unstructured persuasion tasks, marginalizing quieter learners [-W]
- Weak prior knowledge of the topic produces persuasion built on rhetoric alone, decoupled from content learning [-M]

### Target Learners
- Intermediate to advanced learners who already have baseline content knowledge to draw evidence from
- Students in debate, leadership, business, law, and public speaking contexts
- Learners developing advocacy skills benefit from repeated low-stakes persuasion practice building communicative self-efficacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evidence selection and evaluation: choosing support appropriate to a claim and audience
- Audience analysis: adapting message, tone, and framing to listener characteristics
- Communication and advocacy: articulating positions clearly and responding to counterarguments
- Perspective-taking: understanding how the other side constructs its case

### Affordances
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — persuasion tasks require learners to generate and defend positions rather than receive them, producing the generative processing active learning depends on
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — structured persuasion formats assign roles and require responsive exchange, creating positive interdependence around a contested question
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — defending a position against live counterargument forces learners to reconstruct and test their understanding rather than retrieve it verbatim
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — persuasion is argumentation with an added audience dimension; strong persuasive tasks inherit the claim–evidence–warrant structure of argumentation frameworks

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) — formalized adversarial persuasion with assigned positions and timed turns
- [Structured Academic Controversy](../patterns/structured-academic-controversy.md) — learners argue one side, then switch, forcing engagement with both perspectives

## Examples

**Structured Academic Controversy (Johnson & Johnson)** — Pairs research and argue one position, then reverse sides and argue the opposite before reaching consensus; the side-switch is the mechanism that converts persuasion practice into perspective-taking.

**[Moot courts and mock trials](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/initiatives/mock-trial/)** — Law and civics learners construct persuasive arguments for a judge or jury, with audience (the bench) shaping formality and evidentiary standards.

**Model United Nations** — Delegates draft and deliver persuasive position papers and speeches to advance national interests, combining research, rhetoric, and real-time negotiation.

**[Toastmasters International](https://www.toastmasters.org)** — A structured practice community where members deliver persuasive speeches and receive criterion-based feedback on delivery and audience impact.
