## Description
Activating students' prior knowledge is usually beneficial, but it is a mixed blessing when that prior knowledge is itself misleading or wrong — a common situation at any grade level, not just among young children. A kindergartner may think the sun literally "rises" (echoing adult speech) or that the earth is flat because it looks flat from the ground; a high school student may believe a boulder falls faster than a pebble, or that an object dropped from a moving car falls straight down rather than continuing to travel alongside the car as it falls.

Because misconceptions are so common, effective teaching involves **anticipating** them wherever possible, which is a two-part task. First, know or guess likely preconceptions in advance so learning activities can be designed to counteract and revise them — many are well-documented by educational research and can be anticipated systematically (Tanner & Allen, 2005; Chiu & Lin, 2005), even though a busy lesson can still surprise a teacher with an unexpected one. Common documented science misconceptions include: stars/constellations appearing in the same sky position every night (counter: have students track a bright star's position weekly over several weeks); the world being flat like a pancake (counter: use a globe or software like Google Earth to show how a round Earth looks locally flat); dinosaurs and early humans coexisting (counter: construct an actual evolutionary timeline); rivers always flowing north-to-south (counter: identify real rivers flowing south-to-north, e.g. the Red River, and discuss that "south" isn't inherently "lower"); force being required to *keep* an object moving, not just start it (counter: explain inertia directly and demonstrate it with low-friction motion, e.g. a hovercraft or dry-ice puck); volume, weight, and size being identical concepts (counter: have students weigh objects of different sizes/volumes and compare); and seasons being caused by Earth's changing distance from the sun rather than its axial tilt (counter: model the tilt with a globe and light source, and demonstrate reduced heating at oblique angles) ([Chi, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1402_1); D. Clark, 2006; Slotta & Chi, 2006; Owens, 2003). Some misconceptions extend beyond content facts into beliefs about people — for instance, that girls are less talented than boys at math or science, a belief some students resist giving up even though research generally does not support it (Hyde & Linn, 2006).

Second, and just as important: **treat students' existing beliefs with respect**, even when they include misconceptions or errors, especially when a student persists with a misconception despite instruction. Most people, students included, hold their beliefs for reasons, and appreciate having those beliefs acknowledged rather than dismissed — even a religiously-motivated disagreement with a scientific theory taught in class (e.g., objections to evolution rooted in a more interventionist view of life's origins) needs respectful acknowledgment of the disagreement, however awkward that may feel, rather than simple correction-by-authority.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Advance knowledge (from research or prior teaching experience) of the misconceptions most likely to arise for a given topic, so counteracting activities can be planned rather than improvised
- A stance of respectful acknowledgment for a student's existing belief even while working to revise it, particularly when the belief is tied to identity, culture, or personal conviction
#### Constraints
- Some misconceptions are so deeply tied to other beliefs that students may resist revising them consciously or unconsciously — correction alone, without addressing why the belief is held, may not be sufficient
- Idiosyncratic, student-specific misconceptions cannot be anticipated from research alone and can only be caught by attentively listening to and watching individual students

### Target Learners
- Any learner population, since misconceptions occur at every grade level, not only among young children
- Learners whose misconceptions are tied to identity or personal conviction, who need acknowledgment of disagreement handled with particular care

### Target Learning Goals
- Accurate conceptual understanding that replaces, rather than merely sits alongside, a prior misconception
- Preservation of student trust and willingness to engage, even when a strongly held belief is being challenged

### Affordances
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — misconceptions are a direct consequence of learners actively constructing understanding from incomplete or misleading everyday experience, rather than passively absorbing correct information
