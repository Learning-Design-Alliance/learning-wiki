## Description
Performance-based assessment is the element in which learners demonstrate knowledge or skill by performing, producing, or solving something observable rather than only selecting answers. It is useful when the learning target involves application, integration, or authentic performance. Typically the teacher observes both the process (e.g., data collection during an experiment) and the product (e.g., the completed report), though for some tasks (a paper drafted partly outside class) only the product may actually be visible, and for others (group interaction skills) there may be no discrete product at all.

Two related terms are often used loosely as synonyms but mean something distinct (Linn & Miller, 2005): **alternative assessment** describes tasks that aren't pencil-and-paper, while **authentic assessment** describes tasks resembling those actually done in the "real world" — and authenticity is a matter of degree, not a binary. Conversing in Japanese with native speakers while actually in Japan is highly authentic; conversing in Japanese with native speakers in a U.S. classroom is still highly authentic; conversing with the teacher in Japanese during a lesson is moderately authentic; a matching test pairing Japanese and English vocabulary is much less authentic. A five-paragraph essay is a complex performance assessment, but not a highly authentic one, since that specific format is rarely used outside school; a letter to an editor or a memo to a principal is comparatively more authentic, since those formats are genuinely used in work contexts.

Performance assessments carry real, specific tradeoffs (Linn & Miller, 2005). Their advantages: they can measure complex learning outcomes other methods can't reach; they assess process as well as product, not product alone; and well-designed ones communicate the actual instructional goal to students clearly, which tends to give them strong content validity. Their central disadvantage is that they are typically very time-consuming for both students and teachers to complete and score — which means fewer of them can be given, so if they aren't carefully planned against the full range of intended learning goals, content validity actually suffers from *under*-coverage even though the individual assessment task itself has strong validity. State curriculum standards can help here by specifying concretely what a performance task in a given domain (e.g., a specific level of technical skill in a dance curriculum) should actually require.
