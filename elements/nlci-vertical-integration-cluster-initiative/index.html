North Lawndale Cluster Initiative (NLCI): a vertically-integrated neighborhood school cluster strategy¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 9 claims (3 for, 3 mixed, 3 against) · 2 studies (2 causal), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The NLCI was a foundation-funded initiative run by AUSL from 2013-14 through 2018-19 in four Chicago elementary schools and one high school. It aimed to advance "a cohesive, high-quality school and student support system by coordinating and aligning resources, programs, and partnerships to support students from pre-k through high school graduation." Its largest funding portion converted half-day pre-k to full-day pre-k; other supports included teacher professional development, City Year mentoring, social-emotional programs, a middle-to-high-school transition program, and college counseling partnerships.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Funding for full-day pre-k classes and supplementary supports across all grade levels
  • A managing organization (AUSL) to coordinate and align resources, programs, and partnerships across the cluster

Constraints¶

  • The evaluation assessed the impact of funding a vertical integration cluster strategy in one neighborhood and should not be taken as an assessment of the effectiveness of the schools overall or of individual programs
  • High student mobility and year-to-year variability in enrollment limited the strategy's ability to build over time

Target Learners¶

  • Pre-k through high school students in a low-income Chicago neighborhood, predominantly Black students from high-poverty census block groups

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improved attendance, academic achievement, school climate, high school transition success, and college enrollment and persistence

Claims¶

  • Elementary attendance and school climate improved over time at NLCI schools but not significantly more than at comparison schools [~W]
  • Eighth-grade graduates fared better in the transition to high school over time, but Freshman OnTrack rates were not consistently higher than at similar schools [~W]
  • Full-day pre-k funding significantly raised pre-k attendance at NLCI elementary schools relative to matched comparison schools [+W]
  • NLCI high school outcomes were lower than those of similar students at other CPS schools for most years of the initiative [-W]
  • High student mobility limited the vertical-integration strategy: only about one-third of the 2014-15 NLCI pre-k cohort remained at NLCI schools by second grade [-W]
  • Test scores in grades 2-8 at NLCI schools improved at rates similar to the district, so gains cannot be attributed to the initiative [-W]
  • Four schools that converted half-day pre-k to full-day showed attendance about 4 percentage points higher than matched comparison schools after expansion [+W]
  • In the first year of NLCI expansion, the attendance advantage over similar schools (3.8 percentage points) was not statistically significant [~W]
  • Students in full-day pre-k programs had significantly higher attendance rates than students in half-day programs across all study years [+W]

Examples¶

  • Districts supporting neighborhood schools should plan for continually shifting student populations and mobility-driven capacity demands
