# Peer Teaching

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Peer teaching places learners in the role of instructor: they explain, question, and elaborate material for classmates, then receive questions that probe their understanding. Preparing to teach and teaching itself force learners to reorganize knowledge, fill gaps, and articulate reasoning — a form of generative processing that benefits the teacher as much as the taught.

## Design Implications

Peer teaching works because explaining to another person demands deeper processing than studying alone: learners must select, organize, and integrate material and monitor their own comprehension when questions expose gaps [Explaining-to-others protocols produce better conceptual understanding than studying alone.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The effect is strongest when the teacher genuinely expects to teach and when the interaction is interactive — questions, feedback, and back-and-forth — rather than a one-way presentation [~M]. Structure matters: unstructured "work in pairs and teach each other" often degrades into low-level exchange, so roles, preparation time, and guiding questions should be designed in.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Learners must have sufficient baseline understanding to explain accurately; teaching from ignorance propagates error
- Preparation time or structured materials so the explainer organizes before speaking
- An interactive format — the listener should question and the teacher should respond, not just present
- Accountability for both roles (teaching quality and learning from being taught)

#### Constraints
- Explainers with misconceptions can entrench them in both parties; a verification step (instructor check, answer key, or expert group) is needed before teaching begins [~M]
- Low-structure peer teaching produces shallow, verbatim repetition rather than elaboration [-M]
- High-anxiety or low-efficacy learners may avoid the teacher role, gaining little; support or role rotation mitigates this
- Benefits the teacher more than the taught; learners who only receive peer explanations without expert instruction may underperform [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge — enough to explain, not so much that teaching is redundant
- Heterogeneous groups where more advanced learners consolidate by explaining (with safeguards against error propagation)
- Professional and clinical trainees who must articulate reasoning to colleagues

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and knowledge reorganization through explanation [Explaining-to-others protocols produce better conceptual understanding than studying alone.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive monitoring — questions from peers expose gaps the teacher did not know they had
- Communication and professional articulation of reasoning

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — peer teaching is the strongest form of role interdependence: each learner's explanation is a resource the other depends on, making individual accountability and group processing concrete rather than nominal
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — preparing and delivering an explanation is generative activity; the learner produces and organizes content rather than receiving it
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — explaining in one's own words forces chunking and reorganization of material into a coherent schema before delivery
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — peer questions function as formative assessment for the teacher, surfacing misconceptions in real time

## Examples

**Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — Students answer a concept question individually, discuss with neighbors, and revote; the peer discussion phase is peer teaching driven by a shared misconception. See [https://blog.peerinstruction.net](https://blog.peerinstruction.net) and Mazur (1997), *Peer Instruction: A User's Manual*.

**Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson)** — Each student masters one segment in an "expert group," then teaches it to their home group; interdependence makes teaching the only route to complete knowledge. See [https://www.jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org).

**Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — Students rotate roles (summarizer, questioner, clarifier, predictor) while reading, teaching each comprehension strategy to the group in turn.

**Supplemental Instruction (UMKC)** — Near-peer leaders who previously succeeded in a course facilitate study sessions in which students explain material to each other. See [https://info.umkc.edu/si](https://info.umkc.edu/si).
