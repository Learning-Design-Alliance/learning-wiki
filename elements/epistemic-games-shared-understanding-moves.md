# Epistemic Games: Shared-Understanding Moves

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Five recurring interaction moves that interdisciplinary teams use, often supported by a shared visual representation (a whiteboard, a sketch), to build a single, integrated problem statement out of individually held knowledge and perspectives:

- **Knowledge sharing**: making one member's knowledge (conceptual, procedural, contextual, experiential) explicit to the rest of the team, usually paired with an external representation.
- **Perspective-taking**: constructing what a different person or stakeholder (a customer, a future user, a policymaker) would experience, think, or need — flexibly projected across imagined past, present, or future.
- **Simulation**: creating and walking through a hypothetical scenario to surface cause-and-effect relationships, grounding an abstract issue in a concrete, relatable situation.
- **Knowledge validation**: negotiating what counts as trustworthy or "good enough" evidence — the move most likely to expose disciplinary differences in evidentiary standards (e.g., trusting industry reports vs. direct empirical observation vs. one's own replicated test).
- **Negotiation**: integrating individually proposed formulations into one consensual statement, typically through iterative, visibly-revised writing (voting, synthesizing, refining).

These moves are not sequential steps; a team can enter, exit, and re-enter any of them multiple times, and a team can transfer out to a different activity (individual research, consulting an outside expert) and back.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- At least two team members actively engaged, with the team open to revising its current understanding of the problem
- A shared visual/material representation the team can jointly attend to and modify (a whiteboard, sticky notes, a shared document)
#### Constraints
- Knowledge-validation disagreements often trace back to disciplinary background and can generate real, unresolved tension if left implicit — teams benefit from explicitly naming what counts as evidence for each discipline represented, rather than assuming everyone means the same thing by "good evidence"

### Target Learners
- Interdisciplinary teams working toward a single shared problem formulation, especially where members' disciplinary training gives them different default standards for what counts as valid knowledge

### Target Learning Goals
- Building an integrated, team-owned problem statement rather than defaulting to one member's framing or leaving disciplinary disagreements unspoken

## Examples
- A three-student interdisciplinary team (UX design, business, IT) building a fintech-trust problem statement moved through all five moves across a multi-week project, converging on: "How can consumers trust and give consent to third parties to use their data" — a statement visibly reflecting each member's disciplinary lens (data privacy/IT, trust-and-consent/business, transparency/design).
