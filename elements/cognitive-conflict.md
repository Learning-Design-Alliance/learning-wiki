# Cognitive Conflict

## Description
Cognitive conflict is the deliberate introduction of evidence, questions, or situations that contradict learners' existing conceptions, creating a state of [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) that motivates accommodation. Rooted in Piaget's equilibration model, it functions as the trigger for conceptual change: learners must notice the conflict, experience dissatisfaction with their current conception, and construct a replacement that resolves it.

## Design Implications

Cognitive conflict supports learning by making naive or partial conceptions visible and untenable, prompting learners to reason toward more robust understanding rather than merely adding new facts [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on learners actually resolving the conflict — pairing it with [Self-Explanation](self-explanation.md) prompts or structured discussion converts disequilibrium into conceptual change rather than confusion [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Presenting [Erroneous Examples](non-examples.md) — worked solutions containing deliberate errors — is a controlled form of cognitive conflict that builds conceptual knowledge when learners diagnose the flaw [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Accurate diagnosis of learners' prior conceptions, since conflict only arises when the challenge genuinely contradicts what they believe ([Activation](activation.md) of prior knowledge first)
- A conflict that is resolvable: learners must have, or be given access to, the conceptual tools to construct a better answer
- Structured resolution activity — discussion, [Self-Explanation](self-explanation.md), or guided [Inquiry-Based Learning](inquiry-based-learning.md) — rather than leaving learners in unresolved confusion
- Psychological safety: learners must be willing to expose and revise their ideas ([Class Discussion](class-discussion.md) norms, [Debate](debate.md) structures)

#### Constraints
- Conflict without resolution support produces anxiety, disengagement, or superficial accommodation rather than conceptual change [-M]
- Learners with low prior knowledge lack the schema to detect or resolve the conflict, so the technique fails or misleads [-M]
- Learners often dismiss anomalous data or reinterpret it to preserve existing beliefs (theory perseverance), so a single discrepant event rarely changes a conception [-M]
- High working-memory demands of simultaneously holding the old conception, the conflicting evidence, and candidate alternatives can overload novices [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners who hold well-formed but incorrect conceptions — the classic case in physics ("heavier objects fall faster") and biology — where conflict is the necessary trigger for change [~M]
- Intermediate learners with enough prior knowledge to notice and engage the contradiction; too little knowledge yields confusion, too much yields no conflict
- Less effective for novices encountering material for the first time, who have no stable conception to conflict with [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive or misconceived models with scientifically accepted ones
- Critical thinking and epistemic reasoning: evaluating evidence against claims
- Argumentation: articulating and defending positions under challenge

### Affordances
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — cognitive conflict enacts constructivist learning by forcing learners to actively restructure their own schemas rather than receive corrected content; the contradiction creates the need for construction
- [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — the element is the operationalization of this principle: it manufactures the disequilibrium state that Piagetian theory identifies as the engine of development
- [Cognitive Flexibility](../principles/cognitive-flexibility.md) — confronting multiple contradictory framings of the same phenomenon trains learners to represent knowledge from more than one perspective, the core goal of [Cognitive Flexibility Theory](../patterns/cognitive-flexibility-theory.md)
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — conflict demands a response (prediction, argument, revision), making it a reliable generator of the engagement active learning requires

## Examples

**Predict-Observe-Explain (POE)** — A widely used science-education routine: learners predict an outcome, observe a discrepant event (e.g., a heavy object and light object falling together), and explain the mismatch. The prediction step guarantees the conflict is personal.

**Refutation texts** — Expository texts that explicitly state a common misconception, refute it, and present the accepted conception; consistently outperform non-refutation expository texts for conceptual change in science reading.

**[Structured Academic Controversy (Johnson & Johnson)](https://www.co-operation.org/what-is-cooperative-learning/)** — Students argue one side of a controversial issue in pairs, then switch sides and argue the opposite, forcing engagement with contradictory evidence.

**[Erroneous examples in math tutoring](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-006-9002-3)** — Systems such as [ASSISTments](https://www.assistments.org) present incorrect worked solutions for learners to debug, converting error detection into conceptual learning.
