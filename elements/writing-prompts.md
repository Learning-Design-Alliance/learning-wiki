# Writing Prompts

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
A writing prompt specifies a task that requires learners to generate written text — an explanation, argument, reflection, or summary — rather than select or recognize a response. Prompts function both as learning activities (writing to consolidate understanding) and as assessment instruments (writing to reveal it), and their design determines which of these functions dominates.

## Design Implications

Writing prompts leverage the "generation effect": producing explanations and arguments in one's own words forces retrieval, organization, and elaboration that recognition tasks do not [Writing-to-learn tasks produce modest but consistent gains in content learning.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Prompt quality matters more than prompt presence — specific, purposeful prompts produce deeper reasoning than generic ones ("discuss X"), and prompts that ask learners to explain or argue outperform prompts that merely ask them to describe [Argumentation tasks improve reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]. Prompts should be calibrated to working memory demands: open-ended prompts on unfamiliar content can overload learners before they have anything to organize [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear task purpose (explain, argue, reflect, synthesize) stated in the prompt itself ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
- An audience or purpose beyond "the teacher will grade this" — authentic framing improves the quality of student writing [Authentic audiences improve the quality of student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]
- A follow-up mechanism: feedback, peer review, or self-assessment, since prompts without response loops produce practice without correction ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Sufficient prior knowledge to write about; prompts work best after [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of relevant knowledge

#### Constraints
- Generic prompts ("What did you think of the reading?") elicit summary and opinion rather than reasoning [-M]
- Prompts that exceed learners' current understanding produce fluent-sounding but conceptually empty text, especially for novices who lack the schemas to organize a response [~M]
- High-stakes grading of early writing prompts suppresses risk-taking and reduces the elaboration that makes writing-to-learn work [-M]
- Very short prompts (quickwrites) support retrieval but are insufficient for developing extended argumentation skill, which requires sustained practice with feedback [+M]

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough content knowledge to generate text but need structure to organize it; complete novices benefit more from [Demonstration](demonstration.md) and worked examples first [~M]
- Learners developing disciplinary habits of mind — prompts phrased in disciplinary language ("justify," "model," "critique") socialize learners into field-specific reasoning [+W]
- Reluctant writers benefit from low-stakes, frequent prompts over infrequent high-stakes ones [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual consolidation: explaining content in one's own words to expose gaps
- Argumentation: constructing and supporting claims with evidence
- Metacognition and reflection: monitoring one's own understanding and learning process
- Formative assessment: surfacing misconceptions that multiple-choice items cannot detect

### Affordances
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — a writing prompt converts passive reception (reading, listening) into generative processing; even a two-minute quickwrite mid-lecture enacts this principle
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — well-scoped prompts (one question, defined length, provided frame) constrain the task enough that working memory goes to reasoning rather than to figuring out what is being asked
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — prompts generate written evidence of thinking that instructors can diagnose and respond to, making them the raw material of formative feedback loops
- [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) — offering choice among prompts (or a constrained topic with free angle of attack) supports ownership without sacrificing task clarity

## Examples

**[3-2-1 Reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md)** — A structured quickwrite prompt (3 things learned, 2 connections, 1 question) that scaffolds metacognitive processing of a reading or lesson.

**[5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)** — Brief prompts paired with rapid individual feedback, making the prompt–response loop fast enough to function formatively.

**[Writing Across the Curriculum / WAC programs](https://wac.colostate.edu)** — Institutional programs built on discipline-specific writing prompts (lab justifications, policy memos, position papers) that use authentic genres and audiences.

**[AP and IB free-response items](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org)** — Highly specified exam prompts with explicit task verbs ("explain," "justify," "evaluate") and scoring rubrics; a mature example of prompts designed for valid assessment of reasoning.
