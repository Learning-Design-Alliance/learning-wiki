# Argumentation

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Argumentation asks learners to construct, defend, and critique claims using evidence and reasoning, rather than simply stating conclusions. It functions both as a learning activity and as a target skill: engaging in argument makes disciplinary norms of justification visible while deepening conceptual understanding through the need to justify and rebut [Engaging in argumentation improves conceptual understanding by requiring learners to articulate and defend reasoning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

## Design Implications

Argumentation supports learning by requiring learners to coordinate evidence with claims, anticipate counterarguments, and articulate the warrant connecting them — processes that expose gaps in understanding and drive knowledge restructuring [Collaborative argumentation promotes knowledge restructuring and conceptual change.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on structure: unstructured "discussion" often collapses into opinion exchange, so argumentation activities need explicit norms, roles, and evidence requirements.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely contestable question with multiple defensible positions
- Access to evidence or source material learners can cite in support of claims
- Explicit norms for critique — attacking the argument, not the arguer
- A structure that assigns positions or requires rebuttal ([Debate](debate.md), [Structured Discussion](structured-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Without scaffolds (sentence starters, evidence checklists, assigned roles), learners default to assertion and confirmation of prior beliefs rather than evidence-based reasoning [-M]
- Learners with weak topic knowledge produce arguments that are rhetorically fluent but evidentially empty; argumentation cannot substitute for content acquisition [-M]
- Competitive framing can suppress genuine consideration of opposing views — learners argue to win rather than to understand, reducing conceptual change [~M]
- Less effective when the question has a single uncontested answer; artificial controversy undermines the epistemic purpose

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough topic knowledge to marshal evidence but are still consolidating conceptual structure [~M]
- Learners in disciplines where justification norms are central — law, humanities, policy analysis, science
- Novices need substantial scaffolding (claim–evidence–reason frames) before open argumentation is productive [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical analysis: evaluating the strength of evidence and reasoning
- Conceptual change: restructuring beliefs through confrontation with counterarguments
- Disciplinary communication: learning field-specific norms of justification and rebuttal

### Affordances
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — argumentation enacts this principle by requiring learners to generate and defend positions rather than receive them; the cognitive work of justification is the learning mechanism
- [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — contestable questions and required rebuttals provoke elaboration and deep processing beyond recall
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — structured argumentation with peers exposes learners to alternative reasoning they must engage with, not merely tolerate
- [Perspective-Taking](../principles/perspective-taking.md) — constructing a rebuttal requires genuinely modeling the opposing position, which supports more nuanced understanding of the issue space

## Related Elements

- [Structured Discussion](structured-discussion.md) — conversational scaffolds that keep peer exchange argumentative rather than merely social

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) — argumentation as the core activity within a competitive structure
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — learners argue diagnoses, decisions, or interpretations of a shared case
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the articulation and reflection phases involve defending one's reasoning publicly

## Examples

**[Kialo Edu](https://www.kialo.com/edu)** — A structured argument-mapping platform where learners build collaborative trees of claims, counterclaims, and evidence; the visual hierarchy makes argument structure explicit.

**Structured Academic Controversy (Johnson & Johnson)** — Learners are assigned to argue one position, then switch sides and argue the opposite, forcing genuine engagement with both cases before reaching consensus.

**Project Zero's [Claim–Evidence–Reasoning routines](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines)** — Harvard Project Zero thinking routines that scaffold argument construction across subjects.
