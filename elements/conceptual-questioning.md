# Conceptual Questioning

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 associational, 1 design), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 5 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Conceptual questioning poses open, "why" and "what-if" questions that target learners' underlying conceptions rather than recall of facts. The goal is to surface assumptions, create productive cognitive conflict, and prompt learners to explain, justify, and revise their mental models.

## Design Implications

Conceptual questions work because generating an answer requires learners to elaborate and restructure their knowledge rather than recognize it — explaining one's own reasoning improves conceptual understanding even without corrective feedback [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Questions should be few and deep, sequenced from prediction to justification to generalization, and followed by structured comparison of answers so that divergent responses become the object of discussion.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions that target known misconceptions or pivotal concepts, not surface details
- Adequate wait time and a norm that partial or tentative answers are acceptable
- A mechanism for making thinking visible — [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md), clicker voting, or written responses — so answers can be compared and contested
- Instructor follow-up that connects learner answers to the canonical concept
- A task with at least two plausible, mutually exclusive interpretations of an unspecified condition or term, each leading to a different but individually correct answer
- Individual work time before any peer discussion, so that a genuine diversity of interpretations emerges rather than converging early on one interpretation
- If a task's ambiguity may not spontaneously surface multiple interpretations on its own, present candidate solutions as explicit options (e.g., "is this solution correct?") to prompt students to consider alternatives they might not generate spontaneously

#### Constraints
- Open conceptual questions impose high extraneous load for novices with little prior knowledge; unguided questioning can leave them floundering [~M] — pair with worked examples or scaffolds for novice audiences
- Questions asked before learners have any relevant knowledge base produce guessing, not reasoning [~M]
- Whole-class questioning dominated by a few volunteers gives the rest shallow rehearsal; anonymous response or [Peer Discussion](peer-discussion.md) structures mitigate this [-M]
- If the instructor evaluates every answer immediately, learners stop generating explanations and revert to answer-hunting [-M]
- Too much closely-related prior knowledge can undermine the task: if students already know many ways a condition could vary (e.g., many function types), they may conclude prematurely that the task is simply unanswerable rather than engaging in rich comparison of specific interpretations
- Without the task-revision step, students can recognize that assumptions matter (Learning Goal 1) without ever practicing the harder skill of stating one precisely (Learning Goal 2)

### Target Learners
- Learners with enough prior knowledge to generate a meaningful prediction or explanation [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Intermediate learners whose intuitions conflict with the canonical model — the question makes the conflict explicit
- Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge to elaborate on; give a model or example first
- Both elementary (studied at grade 5) and secondary (studied at grade 9) students — the pattern's core mechanism did not require secondary-level content

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: exposing and revising misconceptions
- Deep comprehension: connecting principles to cases rather than memorizing procedures
- Critical thinking and argumentation: justifying claims and evaluating others' reasoning

### Affordances
- [Inquiry-Based Learning](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md) — conceptual questions are the driving mechanism of inquiry: they define the gap learners investigate and give the investigation direction
- [Constructivism](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — by requiring learners to articulate their own conceptions, questioning forces knowledge to be actively built rather than transmitted
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — every learner must generate an answer, not merely receive one; the question converts passive listening into retrieval and elaboration

## Examples

**[Peer Instruction](https://mazur.harvard.edu/research/peerinstruction)** (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — ConcepTests pose a conceptual multiple-choice question; students vote individually, discuss with neighbors, then revote. Widely adopted in physics and beyond.

**Socratic Seminar** — A text-based seminar in which the facilitator asks open conceptual questions and students respond to one another, building interpretation through argument.

**[PhET Interactive Simulations](https://phet.colorado.edu)** — Research-based conceptual questions embedded alongside simulations prompt prediction and explanation before and during exploration.
- Grade 9 students, given two data points (2,18) and (3,12) and asked to find y when x=6, produced both y=6 (assuming inverse proportion) and y=-6 (assuming a linear relationship); when a student proposed a step function that also passed through both points but gave a third answer, the class concluded that even a third data point wouldn't guarantee a unique answer, and converged on the need to state the functional relationship explicitly as an assumption.
- Grade 5 students, asked to divide a rectangle into "four same parts," were shown two candidate solutions (four congruent triangles; two congruent triangles plus two equal-area rectangles) — 17 of 22 initially rejected the second, but discussion of "same" as area vs. shape led several students to accept it as equally valid, and the class then revised the task wording to "divide into four congruent triangles" to eliminate the ambiguity.

# Productive Ambiguity Task Design for Assumptions

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 design), `q3` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Mathematics tasks conventionally specify all needed conditions up front, so students never have occasion to notice that a conclusion's truth depends on an assumption. This pattern breaks that convention on purpose: a task leaves a local assumption ambiguous — either a task condition (e.g., what kind of function relates two given data points) or the meaning of an everyday word used in a mathematical instruction (e.g., what "same parts" means when dividing a shape) — so that students working independently produce genuinely different, individually defensible answers. A structured discussion then surfaces why the answers differ, and a follow-up task asks students to revise the original wording themselves so that only one answer remains valid, which requires them to articulate the assumption explicitly.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Present a task with a deliberately unspecified local assumption (a task condition, or the meaning of an ordinary word used in the instructions).
2. Have students work individually for several minutes without peer discussion, so genuinely divergent interpretations can emerge.
3. If needed, present candidate solutions as explicit options rather than relying on students to generate alternatives spontaneously.
4. Facilitate whole-class discussion: surface the different answers, ask why they differ, and guide students toward naming the underlying assumption ("what would make the answer unique?").
5. Give a follow-up sub-task: ask students to revise the original task's wording so that only one answer remains correct, requiring them to state the previously-implicit assumption explicitly.
