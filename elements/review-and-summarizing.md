# Review And Summarizing

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Review and summarizing is an instructional element in which learners revisit previously encountered content and condense it — orally, in writing, or graphically — into their own words. It functions both as a consolidation activity, strengthening and reorganizing memory traces, and as a formative check that surfaces misconceptions and gaps for the learner and instructor alike.

## Design Implications

Summarizing is effective because it forces generative processing: learners must select the most important ideas, organize them, and restate them, which is more demanding and more productive than rereading [~S]. Its benefits depend on training — learners taught explicit summarization rules (delete redundancies, identify topic sentences, invent superordinate statements) outperform untrained summarizers [+M]. Reviews should be spaced and cumulative rather than massed at the end of a unit, and summaries should be followed by feedback or comparison against a model so errors are corrected rather than consolidated [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Source material that has a discernible structure or hierarchy of ideas
- Explicit instruction in summarization strategies, especially for younger or novice learners
- A feedback mechanism: instructor review, [self-assessment](assessment.md) against a rubric, or comparison with an expert summary
- Sufficient time — summarizing is effortful, and rushing it degrades it into copying

#### Constraints
- Untrained learners often produce "delete nothing" summaries — verbatim copies that yield little learning benefit [-M]
- Summarizing poorly structured or incoherent source material can impair learning relative to rereading [-M]
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, forced summarization of familiar content can be redundant and off-task [~W]
- Copying text verbatim or transcribing slides produces near-zero consolidation benefit [-S]

### Target Learners
- Novices and intermediate learners who need to impose structure on new material
- Struggling readers, who benefit most from explicit summarization strategy training [+M]
- Less valuable for experts, who already hold well-organized schemas and gain little from restating known content

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation of declarative knowledge and key concepts
- Identifying main ideas vs. supporting detail (selective attention)
- Metacognitive monitoring: gaps in a summary reveal gaps in understanding
- Preparation for transfer by abstracting the gist rather than surface details

### Affordances
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — summarizing enacts chunking by requiring learners to compress many discrete details into a small number of organized idea units, reducing working-memory load on later retrieval
- [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) — a good summary makes the underlying structure of the material explicit; instructors can use learner summaries to diagnose whether the intended structure was communicated
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — summarizing converts passive exposure (lecture, reading, video) into generative activity, and works as a low-cost active-learning closure routine
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — summaries are rapid formative evidence; a one-minute summary at the end of class reveals what stuck and what did not, informing the next session's review

## Examples
- **[Reciprocal Teaching](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/comprehension/articles/reciprocal-teaching-text-structure)** — Palincsar and Brown's routine trains small groups to alternate summarizing, questioning, clarifying, and predicting while reading; summarizing is the anchor strategy.
- **[ Cornell Note-Taking System](https://cil.cornell.edu/learning-strategies/cornell-note-taking/)** — Learners divide notes into cues and a summary section, forcing a written summary within 24 hours of the lecture.
- **[Retrieval-enhanced "brain dump" + summary](../strategies/exit-ticket.md)** — End-of-class routine: learners write everything they remember, then condense it into a three-sentence summary; the instructor reviews these as formative data.
- **[Edpuzzle](https://edpuzzle.com)** — Embedded questions in video lessons can require learners to summarize a segment in their own words before proceeding.
