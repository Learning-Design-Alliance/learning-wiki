# Decision-Making

## Description
Decision-making as an instructional element places learners at a genuine choice point within a structured activity: they must weigh options, justify a selection, and experience the consequences of that selection. Rather than being told the correct course of action, learners construct the reasoning that connects evidence, constraints, and goals to a commitment — making the quality of reasoning, not just the choice itself, the object of instruction.

## Design Implications

Decision-making tasks support learning because they require learners to actively construct and defend reasoning rather than receive it, producing deeper processing than passive reception of the same content [Active learning produces better outcomes than lecture-only formats.](../principles/active-learning.md) [+S]. The design burden falls on the option set: choices must be plausible, consequential, and traceable to criteria learners can articulate. Requiring learners to explain *why* an option was chosen — before or after committing — substantially improves the conceptual understanding that transfers beyond the specific scenario [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Consequences matter: scenarios in which decisions produce observable outcomes (feedback, changed conditions, downstream problems) create the disequilibrium that drives revision of naive reasoning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A scenario with a real choice point: multiple defensible options, not one obviously correct answer
- Explicit or discoverable criteria (constraints, goals, evidence) that make evaluation possible
- Consequences that follow from the choice — feedback, changed scenario state, or expert comparison
- A justification step: learners articulate the reasoning behind their selection ([Articulation](articulation.md))
- A debrief or expert comparison so learners can calibrate their reasoning against expert judgment

#### Constraints
- Unguided decision-making with minimal prior knowledge often fails: learners lack the schemas to evaluate options and default to surface cues [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than structured guidance.](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [-S] — decision tasks need scaffolds (criteria lists, expert heuristics) for inexperienced learners
- Ill-structured scenarios with no consequential feedback can reinforce confident but flawed reasoning; high-confidence errors persist when never surfaced [High-confidence errors improve retention when corrected.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M]
- Group decision-making can drift toward premature consensus or dominance by the most confident member, suppressing the evaluation of alternatives
- If the "correct" answer is easily guessed or the options are trivially distinguishable, the task collapses into recall and the reasoning work disappears

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who hold enough domain knowledge to evaluate options but not enough to generate solutions unaided [Whole-task practice improves transfer when learners have baseline task knowledge.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [~M]
- Professional and pre-professional learners (law, business, medicine, leadership) who must build judgment under realistic constraints
- Novices benefit only with heavy scaffolding — criteria, worked decision examples, or constrained option sets [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Analytical reasoning: evaluating evidence and trade-offs against explicit criteria
- Problem-solving and judgment: selecting and defending a course of action under uncertainty
- Metacognitive monitoring: recognizing when evidence is insufficient and revising decisions [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Process skill over outcome: for novices, emphasizing *how* the decision was reasoned outperforms emphasizing whether it was "right" [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — decision tasks create the occasion for expert modeling of judgment: comparing learner decisions against expert decisions and articulated heuristics makes tacit professional reasoning visible
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) — decisions embedded in realistic scenarios with consequences situate reasoning in the context of use, supporting transfer to professional practice
- [Case-Based Learning](../principles/case-studiescase-based-learning.md) — a case supplies the evidentiary base and constraint set that decision-making requires; the decision point is what converts a case from narrative into analysis
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — requiring a group to commit to one decision forces negotiation of criteria and surfaces disagreements that individual work hides

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Problem-Based Learning](../patterns/problem-based-learning.md) — learners must decide on diagnoses, interventions, or recommendations before comparing them to expert practice
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md) — the Harvard method centers each session on a decision the case protagonist faced
- [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) — assigned positions force evaluation of options from a constrained perspective before commitment

## Examples

**[Goal-Based Scenarios](../patterns/goal-based-scenarios.md)** — Schank's design pattern: learners pursue a goal within a simulated environment and must make repeated decisions (hiring, budgeting, diagnosing) whose consequences reshape the scenario. Cover Story and other simulations at the Institute for the Learning Sciences were built on this pattern.

**Harvard Business School case method** — Each class session requires students to take and defend a decision position ("What would you do as the CEO?") before the instructor reveals the protagonist's actual choice, forcing commitment and justification prior to expert comparison. (https://www.hbs.edu/mba/academic-experience/Pages/the-case-method.aspx)

**[Moral Machine](https://www.moralmachine.net)** (MIT Media Lab) — Learners make forced-choice ethical decisions in autonomous-vehicle dilemmas and then compare their judgments against aggregate data, making the criteria behind their choices visible.

**[SimVenture](https://simventure.co.uk)** — A business startup simulation in which learners make sequential decisions about product, finance, and staffing, with market consequences feeding back into subsequent decision rounds.
