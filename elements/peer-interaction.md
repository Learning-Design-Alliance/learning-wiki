# Peer Interaction

## Description
Peer interaction places learners in direct engagement with one another through discussion, debate, peer teaching, or collaborative task work. It functions as a social engine for learning: learners articulate ideas, encounter alternative perspectives, and negotiate shared understanding rather than receiving instruction passively.

## Design Implications

Peer interaction supports learning when it requires learners to actively construct, explain, or defend ideas rather than merely co-occupy a space. Structured formats that assign roles, prompts, or accountability outperform unstructured "discuss with your neighbor" prompts [~M]. The mechanism is elaborative: explaining to a peer forces reorganization of one's own understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Interaction quality matters more than interaction quantity — groups without individual accountability or positive interdependence often produce unequal participation and shallow talk [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task genuinely suited to joint work — one with genuine discussion potential, not a worksheet that can be completed silently
- Individual accountability so that no member can free-ride on group effort
- Prompts or roles that structure the interaction (e.g., explainer/listener, devil's advocate)
- Sufficient shared background knowledge for peers to engage meaningfully with each other's contributions

#### Constraints
- Unstructured group talk can reinforce misconceptions when no member has sufficient knowledge to detect them [-M]
- Grouping low- and high-ability learners without role structure often yields unequal participation, with the stronger student doing the cognitive work [-M]
- Social dynamics (status, anxiety, groupthink) can suppress dissenting ideas, degrading the quality of discussion [-W]
- Interaction overhead consumes time; for simple factual content, direct instruction is often more efficient [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners at similar levels who can productively explain and question each other; mixed groups benefit most when roles are assigned
- Learners with some prior knowledge — complete novices lack the material to contribute or evaluate peer explanations [~M]
- Learners building communication, argumentation, and perspective-taking skills alongside content

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through explanation, elaboration, and resolving discrepancies between ideas [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Communication and argumentation skills: articulating reasoning, responding to counterarguments
- Social and collaborative competencies: negotiation, shared decision-making, giving and receiving feedback

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — peer interaction is the operative mechanism of collaborative learning; the principle specifies the conditions (positive interdependence, individual accountability) under which interaction produces learning rather than mere activity
- [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) — peer discussion supplies the social presence and cognitive conflict needed to sustain a community of inquiry, where ideas are tested against others' perspectives
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — peer formats (think-pair-share, peer instruction) convert passive listening into generative activity, with consistent positive effects on achievement [~S]

## Examples

**Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — Students answer a concept question individually, discuss with neighbors, then re-answer. Widely adopted in physics and beyond; the peer discussion phase produces substantial gains on concept inventories.

**[Acting / Role Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md)** — Learners take assigned positions and interact in character, structuring peer interaction around perspective-taking and negotiation.

**Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson)** — Each group member masters a piece of the material and teaches it to peers, building positive interdependence and accountability into the interaction structure.

**Piazza (https://piazza.com)** — Online Q&A platform where students answer each other's questions with instructor endorsement, extending peer interaction to asynchronous settings.
