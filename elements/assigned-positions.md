# Assigned Positions

## Description
Learners are assigned a specific stance on an issue — often one they do not personally hold — and must construct and defend arguments from that perspective. Because the position is imposed rather than chosen, learners must actively search for, organize, and articulate justifications they would not otherwise generate, forcing engagement with the strongest reasoning available on "the other side."

## Design Implications

Assigned positions work because arguing a case requires deeper processing than reading or hearing about it: learners must retrieve relevant knowledge, anticipate counterarguments, and justify claims [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Arguing an assigned (especially counter-attitudinal) position promotes perspective-taking and can reduce confirmation bias, but only when learners are required to argue *both* sides or encounter strong opposing arguments — arguing only one side can instead entrench attitudes [~M]. The assignment should be framed as an intellectual exercise, not a personal identity, so learners can commit to the argument without committing to the belief.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely contestable issue with defensible arguments on multiple sides
- Sufficient source material or preparation time so learners can build substantive arguments rather than improvise
- A structure that exposes learners to opposing arguments — e.g., a [Debate](debate.md) format or [Structured Academic Controversy](structured-academic-controversy.md) with a mandatory switch to the opposing side
- Psychological safety: learners must be able to argue a position without it being attributed to them personally

#### Constraints
- Arguing only one assigned side can produce biased assimilation and attitude polarization rather than perspective-taking [~M] — learners selectively credit evidence supporting their assigned stance
- Weak or superficial preparation reduces the exercise to rhetoric and performance rather than reasoning
- Learners with strong prior attitudes may resist counter-attitudinal positions or argue them sarcastically, undermining the goal
- Less effective for novices who lack the domain knowledge to generate or evaluate arguments at all

### Target Learners
- Intermediate to advanced learners in humanities, law, and social sciences who already have enough domain knowledge to construct arguments
- Learners prone to one-sided reasoning who benefit from forced engagement with opposing views
- Less suitable for novices, who lack the knowledge base to argue persuasively or evaluate counterarguments

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking: constructing, defending, and attacking arguments
- Perspective-taking: understanding the reasoning and values behind positions one does not hold
- Cognitive flexibility: holding and switching between multiple framings of the same issue

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Flexibility](../principles/cognitive-flexibility.md) — arguing an assigned position requires learners to reconstruct the issue from an unfamiliar conceptual vantage point, which is the core mechanism of flexibility training
- [Perspective-Taking](../principles/perspective-taking.md) — the assignment operationalizes perspective-taking behaviorally: learners must inhabit a stance, not merely acknowledge it
- [Social Learning](../principles/social-learning.md) — learners observe peers constructing arguments for other positions, providing models of reasoning they can appropriate
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — assigned positions give argumentation a concrete task structure: claim, evidence, warrant, rebuttal

## Related Elements
- [Debate](debate.md) — the most common delivery format; assigned positions are the role structure within it
- [Structured Academic Controversy](structured-academic-controversy.md) — extends the element by requiring learners to switch sides and reach consensus, guarding against one-sided entrenchment
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — the underlying skill the element exercises
- [Class Discussion](class-discussion.md) — the forum in which assigned positions are typically performed and challenged

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) — positions are assigned to teams or speakers
- [Structured Academic Controversy](../patterns/structured-academic-controversy.md) — pairs advocate a position, then reverse perspectives before synthesizing

## Examples
- **Structured Academic Controversy (Johnson & Johnson)** — Students in foursomes argue an assigned position in pairs, then switch sides and argue the opposite, then drop roles and seek consensus. The side-switch is the design feature that converts advocacy into perspective-taking.
- **Mock trials and moot courts** (law school curricula) — Students are assigned roles (prosecution, defense, judge) and must argue or evaluate a case from within that role, with preparation from case files.
- **Classroom deliberation formats such as [Talking Points](https://www.thersa.org) or structured deliberation protocols** — Learners receive a position card and must voice its arguments before open discussion, ensuring minority or unfamiliar positions are represented.
