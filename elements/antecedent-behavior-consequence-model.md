# Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence (ABC) Model

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Applied behavior analysis — using behaviorist techniques to change socially important behavior (Lovitt, 1994) — begins by analyzing a behavior into three components: **antecedents** (the environmental events or stimuli that trigger a behavior), **behaviors** (what the person actually does, says, or observably does), and **consequences** (the outcome that either encourages or discourages the behavior's recurrence). If a behavior reliably occurs when a specific antecedent is present, that pattern implies a favorable outcome has been reinforcing it, making the antecedent a genuine "trigger" for the next occurrence.

Only observable actions qualify as "behaviors" in this sense — attitudes, values, beliefs, feelings, and self-images are not behaviors, however important they are to a student's school life. The **"Hey, Dad! Watch me…" test** distinguishes the two: "Watch me ride a bike" (or "do a handstand," or "solve this problem") passes, since it names something observable; "Watch me feel good about myself" (or "have a positive attitude," "be motivated," "have an interest in science") fails, since none of these describe anything a bystander could actually see.

**Antecedents** acquire their power to trigger behavior through repeated association with what has followed that behavior in the past. Common classroom antecedents include: **seating arrangement** (sitting near a particular peer is an antecedent to talking and not completing work; sitting near a window or door predicts inattention; sitting near the teacher predicts on-task work); **teacher proximity** (students are more likely to go off-task the farther the teacher is from them, and to work better during independent seatwork when the teacher circulates); **style of asking questions** (attention is higher when a teacher asks a question, pauses, scans the whole class, then calls on someone); **activity transitions** (disruptive behavior is more likely during transitions between activities); **the nature of the activity** (some students attend during whole-group discussion but disrupt during individual seatwork, or vice versa); **who is leading the lesson** (students may attend for the regular teacher but misbehave for a substitute or student teacher); and **the teacher's manner toward students** (harsh criticism, mockery, or an unjust accusation predicts talking back). Antecedents matter practically because they point toward low-profile, non-intrusive prevention: rather than interrupting a lesson to stop two students talking, a teacher can simply change their seats beforehand; rather than repeatedly calling out names from a desk, a teacher can walk the room to prevent misbehavior before it starts.

**Consequences** can be positive (a pleasant outcome that increases the likelihood of recurrence) or negative reinforcement (removing an aversive condition after a desired behavior, which also increases its likelihood — as when Thorndike's puzzle-box cats learned to pull a cord that opened an escape door more quickly on each trial). Teachers rarely deliberately arrange negative reinforcement, since it requires first putting a student in an unpleasant situation, which runs counter to a general preference for positive, nonaversive techniques (Donnellan & LaVigna, 1990) — but teachers frequently trigger it **inadvertently**. A student who wants to escape difficult work, a dull exercise, or a teacher perceived as unfair may complain, refuse work, or disrupt class to delay or avoid it; if the teacher responds by changing the assignment or removing the student to the hallway, that response has just negatively reinforced the avoidance behavior, making it more likely next time. This **"negative reinforcement trap"** is one applied behavior analysts (e.g. Iwata, 1987) suspect accounts for more learned inappropriate behavior than positive reinforcement does — students more often learn to escape or avoid something undesirable than to be rewarded for something appropriate.

**A five-step procedure for changing classroom behavior**, drawn from applied behavior analysis: (1) identify precisely both the inappropriate behavior and the appropriate behavior meant to replace it, stated positively rather than as a prohibition — "Complete your assignments," not "No talking or staring out the window" (the negatively-stated version fails the so-called **Dead Person's Rule**: if a dead person could satisfy the stated goal by definition, it isn't a properly stated behavioral goal); (2) identify the antecedents to both behaviors and change them — new seating, an overhead projector so the teacher's back never faces the class, reviewing rules before an activity, warm-up tasks to eliminate dead time; (3) identify what is reinforcing the inappropriate behavior and stop supplying it — ignoring attention-seeking misbehavior (extinction), ensuring peers don't attend to it, holding students accountable for incomplete work rather than shortening assignments in response to complaints; (4) set up procedures to reinforce the replacement behavior, preferring reinforcers natural to the school setting — extra homework time, lunch with a favorite teacher, extra recess, an educational game, library time, computer access — since these are available at nearly no cost and so get used more consistently than purchased rewards (see [Conditioning Natural Reinforcers](../strategies/conditioning-natural-reinforcers.md)); and (5) use punishment only as a last resort, after the above have been tried, and under appropriate professional guidance — options in roughly increasing severity include brief time-out (10-30 minutes, appropriate only when the behavior's goal is attention rather than escape), loss of privileges, restitution (repairing, cleaning, or paying for damage), and positive practice (writing about why the misbehavior was a poor choice, or practicing the correct behavior instead).

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Ability to state both the problem behavior and its intended replacement in strictly observable terms
- Systematic observation of what precedes and follows a target behavior, rather than assuming its cause from a student's presumed personality or attitude
#### Constraints
- Only applies cleanly to behaviors that pass the observability test — the model has nothing directly to say about attitudes, feelings, or internal states
- A teacher can easily create or sustain a negative-reinforcement trap without realizing it, simply by responding in the way that provides the most immediate relief from a disruption
- Negative reinforcement (removing an unpleasant condition to strengthen a behavior) is rarely deliberately arranged by teachers, since it requires first placing a student in an aversive situation

### Target Learners
- Students exhibiting a specific, observable, recurring problem behavior amenable to antecedent and consequence analysis

### Target Learning Goals
- Increasing well-defined desirable behaviors and decreasing well-defined undesirable ones, through altering triggers and consequences rather than relying on general encouragement or generic punishment

### Affordances
- [Reinforcement Theory](../principles/reinforcement-theory.md) — the ABC model is the diagnostic front end that identifies what a reinforcement or punishment plan should actually target
- [Functional Behavior Assessment](../principles/functional-behavior-assessment.md) — shares the antecedent-identification logic, organizing triggers into physiological, environmental, and instructional categories
