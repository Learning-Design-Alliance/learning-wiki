# Resource Evaluation

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Resource evaluation asks learners to judge the credibility, accuracy, and relevance of information sources before using them. Rather than treating evaluation as a checklist delivered by the instructor, learners apply criteria themselves — comparing sources, probing authorship and evidence, and justifying their judgments — which builds transferable information literacy.

## Design Implications

Resource evaluation works when learners actively apply criteria to real, contrasting sources rather than memorizing a rubric. Professional fact-checkers outperform academics by *lateral reading* — leaving a page to investigate the source elsewhere — and teaching this strategy explicitly improves students' evaluative judgments [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [+S]. Evaluation tasks also deepen content learning: prompting readers to attend to source features (author, purpose, corroboration) improves their ability to integrate multiple documents [Braasch et al., 2013] [+M]. Structured comparison of credible and non-credible sources supports abstraction of the underlying criteria [Multiple contrasting cases help learners abstract general principles.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A source set that includes genuinely contrasting quality — credible, biased, and unreliable sources on the same topic
- Explicit evaluation criteria co-developed or operationalized by learners ([Criteria Development](criteria-development.md)), not just handed over
- A product that requires justification of judgments (annotated bibliography, source memo, verdict with evidence)
- Instructor feedback on evaluative reasoning, since novices often overweight surface features like domain extension or design polish [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [~M]

#### Constraints
- Checklist approaches (e.g., applying the CRAAP test mechanically) can produce false confidence: learners "pass" manipulated or biased sources that satisfy surface criteria [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [-M]
- Ineffective when all provided sources are pre-vetted and credible — learners never practice discrimination
- High working-memory demand when learners must evaluate content *and* source credibility simultaneously; scaffolds or chunking of the task are needed [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]
- Prior topic knowledge can bias evaluation — learners rate congenial sources as more credible regardless of quality [Braasch et al., 2013] [~M]

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and graduate students in research-heavy disciplines and academic writing courses
- Secondary students in media literacy programs, who show large gains from explicit strategy instruction [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [+S]
- Less effective for learners with no baseline content knowledge, who cannot judge plausibility of claims and rely entirely on surface cues [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: judging source credibility, bias, and provenance
- Critical analysis: evaluating evidence quality and argument support
- Epistemic cognition: understanding how knowledge claims are produced and contested
- Multiple-document comprehension: corroborating and reconciling conflicting accounts [Braasch et al., 2013] [+M]

### Affordances
- **Metacognition** — evaluating sources forces learners to monitor their own reasoning ("Why do I trust this?"), and articulating those judgments improves conceptual understanding of both the criteria and the content [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- **Inquiry-Based Learning** — evaluation is the quality-control step of inquiry; without it, learner-generated questions lead to uncritical source dumping
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — instructor modeling of expert evaluation moves (lateral reading, source triangulation) makes invisible disciplinary judgments observable before learners attempt their own
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — learners produce verdicts and justifications rather than receiving a list of approved sources

## Examples

**[Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — Free curriculum built on lateral-reading research; students evaluate live websites and social media posts, with lessons progressing from single-source checks to full corroboration tasks.

**[SIFT method (Mike Caulfield)](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/)** — A four-move strategy (Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims to origin) designed as a fast, teachable alternative to checklist evaluation.

**CRAAP Test (Meriam Library, CSU Chico)** — Widely used currency/relevance/authority/accuracy/purpose checklist; useful as a starting scaffold but should be paired with lateral reading to avoid surface-cue errors.

**Annotated bibliography assignments** — Learners summarize, evaluate, and justify the role of each source in an emerging argument, making evaluative reasoning assessable.
