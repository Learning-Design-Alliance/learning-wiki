# Peer Collaboration

## Description
Peer collaboration places two or more learners in joint work on a shared task, requiring them to explain, question, negotiate, and build on each other's thinking. It functions as a social engine for learning: the value comes not from proximity but from the elaboration, conflict, and mutual regulation that genuine joint work provokes.

## Design Implications

Collaboration supports learning when it forces learners to articulate reasoning and resolve discrepancies, producing elaboration that solitary study rarely generates [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Structured cooperative methods with individual accountability and promotive interaction outperform unstructured group work [~S] — the effect depends on design, not on grouping per se. Tasks must be genuinely joint: if the work can be divided and reassembled without discussion, collaboration adds coordination cost without cognitive benefit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task with a shared goal that cannot be completed efficiently alone (ill-structured, multi-perspective, or resource-distributed)
- Individual accountability — each learner's contribution is visible and assessable
- Sufficient prior knowledge to contribute; learners need something to bring to the interaction
- Group norms and roles that distribute participation rather than allowing free-riding

#### Constraints
- Collaboration on well-structured tasks that a competent individual could solve alone often yields no gain and adds coordination overhead [-M]
- Groups with heterogeneous participation — one learner dominating, others passively following — produce shallow learning for non-contributors [-M]
- Novices collaborating without guidance can reinforce misconceptions through consensus on error [-M]; low-knowledge dyads benefit more from expert modeling than from peer discussion
- Free-riding and social loafing increase as group size grows and accountability blurs [-S]

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who have enough schema to contribute and enough gaps to benefit from peers' explanations [~M]
- Learners who benefit from verbalizing reasoning to consolidate understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge to evaluate peer contributions and may encode errors

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through explanation, argument, and resolving discrepant interpretations
- Problem-solving and transfer on complex, whole tasks [Whole-task performance improves transfer.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+M]
- Teamwork, communication, and perspective-taking as outcomes in their own right
- Social regulation of learning — planning, monitoring, and revising jointly

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — peer collaboration is the direct enactment of this principle: knowledge is built through social interaction rather than transmitted
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — negotiation with peers surfaces and challenges naive conceptions, forcing reconstruction of understanding that solo work leaves unexamined
- [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) — sustained peer dialogue builds the cognitive and social presence needed for deep, sustained inquiry
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — collaboration converts passive reception into generative activity: explaining, questioning, and deciding together

## Examples

**Jigsaw method (Aronson, 1978; [jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org))** — Each learner masters one piece of the material in an "expert group," then teaches it to their home group. Resource interdependence makes collaboration structurally necessary rather than optional.

**[Piazza](https://piazza.com)** — Peer Q&A platform for courses where students answer each other's questions, with instructor endorsement to correct peer errors — a design that directly mitigates the misconception-reinforcement constraint.

**Peer Instruction (Mazur, Harvard) ([peerinstruction4cs.org](https://peerinstruction4cs.org))** — Students answer individually, discuss with neighbors, then re-answer; the peer discussion phase is where conceptual change concentrates.

**Project-Based Learning (e.g., [PBLWorks](https://www.pblworks.org))** — Teams carry sustained, authentic projects with defined roles and public products, embedding accountability into the task structure.
