The freshman on-track indicator¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 2 studies (2 associational), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The on-track indicator is a binary baseline measure of acceptable freshman performance developed by the Consortium in 1999 and since adopted into the Chicago public high school accountability system. A student is on-track if two criteria are met: "The student has accumulated five full course credits" needed for tenth-grade promotion, and "no more than one semester F" in a core subject (English, math, science, or social studies). It combines credit accumulation across all credit-bearing classes with core-course failures counted by semester, and provides timely information about performance within students' first year, unlike dropout rates or test scores.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to student transcript data on credits earned and core-course grades at the end of freshman year

Constraints¶

  • It is only an indicator of adequate freshman performance and provides little information about whether students are acquiring skills needed for advanced classes, college, or work
  • Students are more likely to be counted as off-track if course-taking increases, even if pass rates improve

Target Learners¶

  • First-time ninth-grade high school students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Progress toward on-time high school graduation

Claims¶

  • On-track status predicts graduation better than eighth-grade test scores [+W]
  • Freshman on-track students are over three times as likely to graduate in four years as off-track students in CPS [+W]
  • The on-track–graduation relationship remains strong after accounting for student background characteristics [+W]
  • The freshman on-track indicator predicts five-year graduation equally or more strongly for students with disabilities than for students without disabilities, with on-track students two to six times more likely to graduate [+W]
  • Citywide freshman on-track rates improved from 48 to 58 percent over a decade, with declines tied to increased course-taking [+W]

  • Monitor freshman-year grades and intervene early, treating D students as needing attention comparable to F students
