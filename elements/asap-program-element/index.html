Accelerated Study in Associate Programs (ASAP)¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 quant-synthesis), q4 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

ASAP is a three-year City University of New York program designed to remove barriers to college success and completion for students seeking associate degrees. It combines financial supports (tuition waivers, free textbooks, free public transportation), academic supports (tutoring, early registration, blocked courses in a cohort model, a structured pathways model), and personal supports (advisors with average caseloads of 150 students, seminars). Students must enroll full time and are encouraged to complete developmental education courses in the first year.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Students must complete the FAFSA and TAP, need no more than two developmental courses, enroll full time, complete a mandatory summer institute, and sign a contract agreeing to ASAP terms; continuing students need a GPA of at least 2.0 and no more than 15 college credits.

Constraints¶

  • The WWC notes the effects of ASAP are not known for college attendance, academic achievement, and labor market outcomes within the Supporting Postsecondary Success topic area.

Target Learners¶

  • Low-income, full-time community college students in need of developmental coursework

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Associate degree completion within three years
  • College persistence and credit accumulation

Claims¶

  • ASAP costs about $14,029 per student over three years, with no direct program costs billed to students [+M]
  • The evidence base for ASAP rests on two randomized controlled trials meeting WWC standards without reservations, out of eight identified studies [+M]
  • ASAP likely increases college access and enrollment rates [+M]
  • ASAP likely increases credit accumulation and persistence rates [+M]
  • ASAP likely increases community college students' graduation and degree attainment rates [+M]
  • ASAP showed no statistically significant effect on enrollment in any college at the sixth semester in the Scrivener et al. (2015) trial [~M]
