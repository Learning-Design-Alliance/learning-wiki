# Analogies

## Description
Analogies connect a new idea to a more familiar situation, structure, or experience so learners can reason about the unfamiliar in terms they already understand.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A source example learners actually know well enough to use
- Explicit explanation of which features map across the analogy
- Follow-up clarification about where the analogy stops being accurate

#### Constraints
- Weak analogies can create misconceptions if surface similarities hide important structural differences
- Learners may overextend an analogy unless limits are named directly
- Analogies work best as a bridge into understanding, not as a substitute for formal explanation

### Target Learners
- Learners encountering abstract or technical ideas
- Learners who need support connecting prior knowledge to new concepts
- Learners who benefit from concrete representations before formal terminology

### Target Learning Goals
- Activating prior knowledge
- Building an initial mental model of a new concept
- Improving explanation, recall, and transfer

### Affordances
- Makes abstraction more approachable
- Supports comprehension and retention through meaningful comparison
- Helps instructors expose structure, not just vocabulary

## Examples
- Explaining electrical current through the analogy of water moving through pipes
- Comparing a thesis statement to a roadmap that previews where the argument is headed
- Introducing computer memory through a desk-workspace analogy to distinguish short-term and long-term storage
