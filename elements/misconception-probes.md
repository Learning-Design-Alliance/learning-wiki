# Misconception Probes

## Description
A misconception probe is a carefully constructed question, scenario, or two-tier item (answer plus reasoning) that targets a known, documented misconception. Learners commit to an answer *before* instruction, making their intuitive model visible to themselves and the instructor. The probe functions as diagnosis, not assessment — its purpose is to activate the faulty schema so that subsequent instruction can directly challenge it.

## Design Implications

Probes work because learners cannot revise a belief they do not realize they hold; surfacing the misconception creates the cognitive conflict that motivates conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. Probes should be drawn from research-identified misconceptions in the domain (e.g., force-and-motion errors in physics, natural-selection teleology in biology), not invented ad hoc — a probe that misses the actual misconception diagnoses nothing. The reveal-and-discuss sequence matters more than the item itself: learners must compare their answer against evidence or peer reasoning, not simply be told they are wrong [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A documented misconception inventory for the domain (e.g., [Force Concept Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_Concept_Inventory)-style distractors grounded in research)
- Two-tier or explanation-requiring format, so the probe captures *why* learners answer as they do, not just what they answer
- A follow-up instructional move: contrasting evidence, peer discussion, or [Analogies](analogies.md) that offers a replacement model — a probe without a planned response is wasted diagnosis
- A low-stakes climate; learners must answer honestly rather than guess what the instructor wants

#### Constraints
- Simply telling learners their answer is wrong rarely changes the underlying intuition; refutation without replacement leaves the misconception intact [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [~M]
- Probes administered after instruction can *strengthen* the misconception by reactivating it, if the corrective discussion is weak
- In domains without a well-mapped misconception literature, item construction is unreliable and probes may test vocabulary rather than understanding
- Public reveal can embarrass learners and suppress honest answering in low-trust classrooms

### Target Learners
- Learners with prior informal experience in the domain (everyone has intuitions about falling objects, heat, evolution, fractions) — the more entrenched the intuition, the more valuable the probe
- Novices who do not yet know their intuitions are nonstandard; experts rarely benefit and may find probes trivial
- Reluctant responders benefit from anonymous formats (clickers, polls) that let them commit without social risk

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect models with scientific ones
- Diagnostic formative assessment: informing the instructor's next instructional move ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Metacognition: helping learners recognize the gap between what they think they know and what they can justify

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — a probe deliberately creates the conflict between prediction and evidence that drives conceptual restructuring; it is the cleanest instructional trigger for this principle
- [Activation](../principles/activation.md) — probes activate prior knowledge, including the faulty prior knowledge that would otherwise operate invisibly during instruction
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — every learner commits to an answer rather than passively receiving correction; commitment is what makes the subsequent discussion consequential
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — probes are formative instruments: the distribution of answers tells the instructor what to teach next, and tells learners what they don't yet know

## Examples

**[Peer Instruction](https://mazur.harvard.edu/research/instructional-methods)** (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — ConcepTests pose a conceptual probe, students vote individually, discuss with neighbors, then revote. The peer discussion between votes is where misconception revision happens.

**[PhET Interactive Simulations](https://phet.colorado.edu)** — Prediction-first activities built around sims (e.g., predicting circuit behavior before testing) surface intuitions against immediate visual evidence.

**[Force Concept Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_Concept_Inventory)** (Hestenes, Wells, & Swackhamer, 1992) — The canonical research-based probe set for Newtonian mechanics; its distractors are each mapped to a specific documented misconception.

**[Diagnostic Questions](https://diagnosticquestions.com)** — Multiple-choice items where each wrong answer corresponds to a specific misconception, giving teachers item-level diagnostic data.
