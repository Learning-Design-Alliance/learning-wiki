# Structured Questioning

## Description
Structured questioning is an instructional element in which the instructor sequences purposeful questions — moving from factual retrieval toward explanation, application, and evaluation — to guide learners through inquiry without giving answers directly. The questions function as scaffolds: each one directs attention, elicits reasoning, and prompts learners to construct and articulate understanding themselves.

## Design Implications

Structured questioning promotes deeper processing by requiring learners to generate explanations rather than receive them; eliciting self-explanations in response to prompts measurably improves conceptual understanding [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Effectiveness depends on question design and sequencing: questions must be within reach of learners' current knowledge while pressing beyond it, and the sequence should fade as learners internalize the questioning routine [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Teaching learners to ask the questions themselves — as in reciprocal questioning routines — extends the benefit and transfers responsibility to students [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A planned question sequence with a clear cognitive trajectory (recall → explanation → application → evaluation)
- Adequate wait time and a norm that learners attempt answers before the instructor intervenes
- Instructor responses that probe ("Why do you think that?") rather than evaluate and move on
- Content for which learners have sufficient prior knowledge to reason about; gaps must be activated first ([Activation](activation.md))

#### Constraints
- Pure discovery through questioning with minimal guidance is less effective than guided questioning, particularly for novices who lack the knowledge to generate productive hypotheses [-S] — unguided inquiry imposes unproductive search costs similar to those seen in problem solving [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]
- Questions pitched above learners' zone of proximal development produce guessing and frustration rather than reasoning
- Overly rapid-fire or evaluative questioning turns the routine into recitation, suppressing the elaboration that makes questioning effective
- Less effective when learners lack the vocabulary to articulate reasoning; pairing with [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) mitigates this

### Target Learners
- K–12 and higher education learners building conceptual understanding in content-rich domains [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Novices benefit when questions are heavily scaffolded and sequenced; the approach shifts toward open facilitation as expertise grows [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]
- Professional training contexts where diagnostic reasoning and justification are target skills

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: explaining why, not just what
- Metacognition: monitoring one's own reasoning in response to probing questions
- Inquiry skills: eventually generating one's own questions [Teaching students to generate questions improves comprehension.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — structured questioning enacts this principle by making learners actively construct explanations rather than receive transmitted content, while the structure prevents the failure modes of unguided discovery
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — questioning serves the coaching and articulation phases: the instructor elicits and shapes learner reasoning the way a master probes an apprentice's decisions
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — a question sequence is scaffolding in dialogue form; the design decision is when to fade prompts toward learner-generated questions
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — every learner response is generative cognitive work; questioning structures that work into a predictable routine

## Examples

**POGIL (Process Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning)** (https://pogil.org) — Published activity sets for chemistry, biology, and other disciplines use sequenced questions on a "key questions" ladder that guides student teams from data interpretation to concept construction, followed by application problems.

**Reciprocal Questioning (ReQuest)** — King's guided reciprocal-peer-questioning procedure trains students to ask each other "how" and "why" questions using prompt cards, producing gains in comprehension and knowledge construction in college classrooms.

**[Talk Science / Accountable Talk](https://inquiryproject.terc.edu)** — TERC's professional learning materials give teachers researched question stems and "talk moves" (revoicing, probing, pressing for reasoning) for orchestrating structured whole-class inquiry.
