# Feelings Vocabulary

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
A feelings vocabulary is an explicitly taught lexicon of emotion terms — ranging from basic labels (happy, sad, angry) to granular distinctions (frustrated, disappointed, anxious, relieved) — that learners use to identify, differentiate, and communicate their internal states. It functions as a prerequisite component for emotional regulation, help-seeking, and social collaboration: learners who cannot name a state cannot strategically manage or report it.

## Design Implications

Emotion labeling supports self-regulation and communication, and affective granularity — the ability to make fine-grained distinctions among emotional states — is associated with better regulation outcomes than broad positive/negative categorization [~M]. Explicit instruction in emotion words works best when terms are introduced in context (during [Check-Ins](check-in.md), literature discussion, or conflict resolution) rather than as isolated word lists, and when learners practice applying labels to their own experience, not just to hypothetical scenarios [~W]. Because emotion words are abstract and often conflated (e.g., angry vs. frustrated vs. annoyed), teaching should contrast near-neighbors explicitly, much like [Non-Examples](non-examples.md) sharpen conceptual boundaries.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A developmentally appropriate, progressively expanding set of terms (start with 4–6 core emotions, add granular terms as learners consolidate)
- Repeated, low-stakes opportunities to use the vocabulary ([Check-Ins](check-in.md), journaling, [Act It Out](act-it-out.md))
- Explicit contrast between similar terms so learners build discriminations, not just word recognition
- A classroom culture where naming emotions is safe and normalized

#### Constraints
- Word lists taught in isolation, without contextualized practice, produce recognition without use [-W]
- Emotion labeling can feel performative or intrusive if mandated publicly; forced disclosure may increase threat for some learners [~W]
- Vocabulary alone does not produce regulation — labeling must be paired with strategy instruction (what to *do* about a feeling) or it remains descriptive only [-W]
- Direct translation across languages and cultures is unreliable; emotion categories are not universal, so multilingual classrooms need care with assumed equivalence [~M]

### Target Learners
- Early childhood and elementary learners, for whom emotion labeling predicts later social competence and reduces behavioral incidents [~M]
- Learners with language delays, autism, or alexithymia, for whom explicit vocabulary instruction substitutes for incidental acquisition [~M]
- Adolescents and adults in conflict-prone or high-stress contexts (negotiation, clinical training), where precise affective language improves communication
- Second-language learners, who may lack emotion terms in the target language entirely

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: identifying a state as the first step toward managing it
- Communication and help-seeking: being able to tell a teacher or peer what is wrong
- Social perspective-taking and empathy: naming others' states from cues [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Literary and media interpretation: analyzing characters' motivations and emotional arcs

### Affordances
- [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) — a shared feelings vocabulary gives learners the words to represent others' internal states, which is a precondition for empathic response rather than mere sympathy
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — organizing emotions into families (e.g., anger-family: irritated, frustrated, furious) reduces the memory burden of a long list and supports discrimination [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) — feelings vocabulary instruction is a direct application: deliberately teaching the words learners need before requiring them to use them
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — a stable, shared lexicon removes the need to improvise descriptions of internal states during emotionally charged moments, freeing attention for the situation itself

## Examples
- **RULER Approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — teaches a "Mood Meter" with dozens of graded emotion terms organized by pleasantness and energy; learners plot their state and select regulation strategies. [https://ei.yale.edu](https://ei.yale.edu)
- **Zones of Regulation** — color-coded framework grouping emotions and arousal states into four zones, with explicit term instruction per zone. [https://www.zonesofregulation.com](https://www.zonesofregulation.com)
- **[What's My Emotion? game check-in](../strategies/whats_my_emotion_game_check-in.md)** — a game-based check-in in which learners match or guess emotion terms from cues, building discrimination through play.
- **Second Step (Committee for Children)** — SEL curriculum with explicit feelings-vocabulary lessons in early grades, embedded in scenario discussion. [https://www.secondstep.org](https://www.secondstep.org)
