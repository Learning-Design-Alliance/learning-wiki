# Collaborative Problem-Solving

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (8 for, 2 mixed) · 14 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 theoretical, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 14 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

## Description
Collaborative problem-solving (CPS) places a small group of learners around a problem that no single member can solve alone, requiring them to pool knowledge, divide cognitive labor, negotiate approaches, and converge on a shared solution. It functions simultaneously as a content-learning activity and as practice in the social-cognitive skills — perspective-taking, task regulation, and joint reasoning — that the OECD treats as a distinct competency domain.

## Design Implications

CPS improves learning when the group's discussion forces learners to externalize and defend their reasoning; the explanatory and argumentative exchanges, not mere co-presence, drive the benefit [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Groups outperform individuals on complex tasks when the task is genuinely divisible and high in cognitive load, because members share the burden of holding and coordinating information [A cognitive load approach to collaborative learning.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-008-9095-2) [+M]. Task design matters more than group formation: ill-structured, whole-task problems with a single deliverable produce the strongest outcomes [Whole-task performance improves transfer.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A problem that is genuinely interdependent — solvable only through coordinated effort, not parallel solo work stitched together
- Group size of roughly 3–5; larger groups dilute individual accountability
- Structured roles or protocols (e.g., recorder, skeptic, coordinator) that distribute participation and prevent free-riding
- Instructor monitoring and [Coaching](coaching.md) during group work, with prompts that push groups to justify and evaluate rather than merely divide labor
- Time for groups to reflect on *how* they worked together, not only on the solution

#### Constraints
- Groups of low-knowledge learners working on complex problems without guidance can flounder; unstructured collaboration is not a substitute for [Scaffolding](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [~M]
- Unequal participation: high-ability members often do the cognitive work while others watch, producing no learning for the passive members [-M]
- Group discussion can entrench shared errors — groups converge on plausible-but-wrong answers more confidently than individuals [~M]
- Social friction and status differences suppress contributions from lower-status members, reducing the diversity of ideas that makes collaboration valuable [-M]
- Assessment that grades individuals on group products undermines accountability and motivation structures [-M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with some prior knowledge of the domain, who can contribute to and benefit from peer explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Learners developing teamwork and communication competencies alongside content knowledge
- Less effective for complete novices on high-complexity tasks, who lack the knowledge to contribute meaningfully to joint reasoning [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through explanation, argument, and resolution of disagreement
- Complex, ill-structured problem-solving where multiple approaches must be evaluated
- Teamwork competencies: negotiation, role coordination, shared regulation
- Transfer of knowledge to novel, authentic situations [Whole-task performance improves transfer.](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — CPS is the problem-centered enactment of this principle: the group structure exists to serve joint reasoning about a task, not just discussion
- [Constructivism](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — learners actively construct solutions through negotiation and consensus-building rather than receiving expert solutions
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/scaffolding-and-fading.md) — peer [Articulation](articulation.md) and [Reflection](reflection.md) during group work make reasoning visible in the same way expert modeling does, but from near-peers
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — dividing the coordination of a complex task across members reduces individual working-memory burden on high-element-interactivity problems

## Examples

- **[PISA Collaborative Problem-Solving Assessment](https://www.oecd.org/pisa/)** — OECD's 2015 assessment measured CPS as a distinct competency through chat-based simulated group tasks; its framework is the standard reference for defining the skill
- **Problem-based learning in medical education (e.g., [Maastricht University's PBL curriculum](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl/education/problem-based-learning))** — small tutorial groups analyze patient cases, with a tutor facilitating rather than lecturing
