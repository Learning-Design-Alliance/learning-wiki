# Evidence-Based Reasoning

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Evidence-based reasoning asks learners to evaluate two or more competing models or explanations against real-world data, deciding which model best accounts for the evidence and articulating why. Rather than receiving a canonical explanation, learners weigh alternative claims, coordinate evidence with theory, and revise their understanding — a process that targets epistemic cognition as much as domain content.

## Design Implications

Evidence-based reasoning supports conceptual change by requiring learners to confront the inadequacy of intuitive or naive models against data, which is more effective than simply presenting the correct model [~M]. The activity gains its power from genuine competition between plausible alternatives; when one model is obviously correct, learners engage in pattern matching rather than evidential reasoning. Structured argumentation supports — such as evidence–claim linking frames — improve the quality of learners' reasoning, especially for novices [Argumentation quality improves when learners are given explicit scaffolds for connecting claims to evidence.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Two or more genuinely plausible competing models, each with some evidential support
- Real or realistic data that learners can interpret and that discriminates between the models
- A structure for articulating justification — e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning frames ([Argumentation](argumentation.md))
- Opportunities to revise initial commitments after evaluating evidence ([Cognitive Conflict](cognitive-conflict.md))

#### Constraints
- Unguided "discovery" of which model fits the evidence often fails; learners with low prior knowledge may not notice evidential inconsistencies without scaffolds [~S]
- Learners frequently reason backward from prior beliefs, discounting disconfirming evidence (belief bias); simply presenting contradictory data rarely produces conceptual change [-M]
- Heavy data-interpretation demands can overwhelm working memory, leaving no capacity for the reasoning itself; pre-chunking data or providing interpretation supports mitigates this [~M]
- Less effective when the domain has no genuine evidential ambiguity or when models differ only in terminology rather than substance

### Target Learners
- High school and higher-education students who hold intuitive models that conflict with scientific accounts
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge — enough to interpret evidence, but with misconceptions worth confronting
- Advanced learners and research trainees developing disciplinary norms of evidential justification; novices need substantially more scaffolded data interpretation

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive models with evidence-consistent ones
- Epistemic cognition: understanding what makes a claim warranted and how evidence bears on theory
- Critical evaluation: assessing competing explanations and articulating justified judgments
- Argumentation skill: constructing and critiquing evidence-linked claims

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../principles/cognitive-load-theory.md) — competing-model tasks must be designed so data interpretation is manageable; chunking evidence and providing interpretive schemas preserves working memory for the reasoning that matters
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — learners generate judgments and justifications rather than receiving conclusions, producing deeper processing than passive presentation of the correct model
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — making expert evidential reasoning visible (how a scientist weighs conflicting data) lets learners appropriate disciplinary norms of justification

## Related Elements
- [Argumentation](argumentation.md) — the discourse structure through which evidence-based reasoning is expressed and refined
- [Cognitive Conflict](cognitive-conflict.md) — the mechanism by which evidence contradicting prior beliefs opens the door to conceptual change
- [Case Studies](case-studies.md) — a common vehicle for supplying realistic evidence against which models are tested
- Justification — the explicit articulation of why evidence supports one model over another; often scaffolded with claim–evidence–reasoning frames

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Model-Evidence Link (MEL) Reasoning Pattern](../patterns/model-evidence-link-mel-reasoning-pattern.md) — the canonical pattern: learners connect competing scientific models to lines of evidence and rate the strength of each link
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — cases supply the realistic evidence base that competing explanations must account for

## Examples

**Model-Evidence Link (MEL) activities** — Developed for climate science education (e.g., through the University of Colorado Boulder and Penn State's MEL projects), learners link evidence such as ice-core data or spectral measurements to competing explanations of climate change and rate the plausibility of each connection. See https://mel2.meteorology.psu.edu

**[Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md)** — Published case curricula such as [National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science](https://sciencecases.lib.buffalo.edu) present data-rich scenarios where learners must adjudicate between competing diagnoses or mechanisms.

**Structured scientific argumentation curricula** — Osborne and colleagues' argumentation toolkits for secondary science provide claim–evidence–reasoning frames that scaffold learners in evaluating alternative explanations of phenomena.
