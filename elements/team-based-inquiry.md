# Team-Based Inquiry

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Team-Based Inquiry places students in small, structured groups that work through carefully sequenced questions or problems, constructing answers collectively rather than receiving them directly. Each team member typically adopts a role, and the group's reasoning — not just its answer — is the object of instruction. The structure distinguishes it from generic group work: tasks are interdependent, roles are assigned, and reflection on the group's process is built in.

## Design Implications

Structured team inquiry improves achievement and peer interaction quality compared with unstructured group work, provided tasks require genuine interdependence [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability outperforms purely competitive or individualistic structures.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. The critical design lever is accountability: if any member can complete the task alone, stronger students dominate and weaker students disengage. Well-designed inquiry sequences also produce gains in process skills — communication, critical evaluation of evidence — beyond content mastery [Active learning improves examination performance and reduces failure rates relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tasks with genuine interdependence — problems no single member can solve efficiently alone
- Individually accountable outcomes (individual quizzes, role reports, random member reporting) so participation is not optional
- Assigned or rotating roles (manager, recorder, skeptic, presenter) to distribute cognitive labor
- Instructor as facilitator who circulates, probes reasoning, and intervenes on group process rather than delivering answers
- A reflection step where teams examine how they worked, not only what they concluded

#### Constraints
- Poorly structured tasks devolve into division-of-labor work where members complete isolated pieces without joint reasoning [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability outperforms purely competitive or individualistic structures.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Free-riding and status effects (dominant members, marginalized members) emerge without individual accountability and role structure
- Less efficient for foundational factual learning, where direct presentation is faster; inquiry costs time that may not pay off for simple objectives
- Groups without sufficient prior knowledge can entrench misconceptions collectively; a [Guided Inquiry](guided-inquiry.md) sequence with scaffolded questions mitigates this
- Assessment pressure on individual grades can undermine willingness to help teammates; grading schemes must reward both individual and team outcomes

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and upper-secondary students in STEM, where the model originated (chemistry, biology, physics)
- K-12 learners, with heavier scaffolding and shorter inquiry cycles
- Humanities and professional programs using team analysis of texts, cases, or evidence
- Heterogeneous-ability classrooms — mixed groups benefit lower-achieving members through peer explanation, with modest gains for higher achievers who articulate their reasoning [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through collective meaning-making rather than transmission
- Process and teamwork skills: negotiation, division of cognitive labor, giving and receiving critique
- Evidence-based reasoning: constructing and defending claims within a group
- Metacognition through structured reflection on group process

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — team-based inquiry is a structured instantiation: the roles, interdependence, and accountability mechanisms are what convert "students in groups" into collaborative learning
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — the inquiry sequence itself is a scaffold; questions fade from highly directed to open as teams develop independent reasoning routines
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — students generate, argue, and revise ideas rather than receiving them, which is the mechanism behind the achievement gains documented for active learning [+S]
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — team reporting and reflection cycles give frequent low-stakes feedback on both content and process

## Patterns That Use This Element
- Process-Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning (POGIL) — the canonical pattern: teams of 3–4 with assigned roles work through guided-inquiry activity sets, followed by instructor-facilitated reporting
- [Collaborative Inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md) — teams pose and investigate their own questions with instructor guidance
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — teams analyze authentic cases, sharing the same interdependence and role structure

## Examples

**[POGIL Project](https://pogil.org)** — Published guided-inquiry activity sets for chemistry, biology, and other disciplines; teams of 3–4 with manager, recorder, and presenter roles work through model-and-question sequences.

**[SCALE-UP](https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/library/scaleup/index.html)** — Physics classrooms with round tables of three teams of three, integrating inquiry activities with immediate application; associated with reduced failure rates [+S].

**[Process Workshops in Chemistry](https://pogil.org)** — Mountain-style process workshops where teams alternate between exploring data/models and reporting conclusions to the class.
