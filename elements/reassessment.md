# Reassessment

> **Element** · [All elements](index.md)

## Description
Reassessment asks learners to revisit and revise their own answers after discussion, feedback, or additional instruction. It converts an initial response — right or wrong — into an object of reflection, making the gap between first thinking and improved thinking visible and revisable.

## Design Implications

Reassessment leverages the testing effect a second time: the initial response functions as a pretest that primes attention, and the revision pass consolidates corrected understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding when learners articulate why their revised answer differs.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Its distinctive value is exposing *change* — learners who compare their first and second answers see their own conceptual growth, which supports metacognitive calibration. Reassessment works best when the revision is consequential (graded, discussed, or built upon) rather than a perfunctory second click.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A preserved record of the learner's initial response to compare against
- New input between attempts — peer discussion, instructor explanation, or additional instruction — that gives revision a genuine basis
- Prompts that require justification of the change ("what made you reconsider?"), not just a new answer selection
- Low-stakes framing so learners are willing to commit to a first answer

#### Constraints
- Without meaningful intervening instruction or discussion, second attempts mostly reflect guessing or conformity rather than learning [-M]
- Learners may conform to peer majority answers without re-evaluating the reasoning, especially when confidence in the first answer was low [~M]
- When only the final answer counts, learners treat the first attempt as noise and skip the comparison that drives the benefit [-M]
- Reassessment of isolated facts adds little; the effect depends on items that probe conceptual understanding rather than recall [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with partial or unstable understanding, whose first answers reveal misconceptions worth revising
- High-confidence learners holding wrong answers benefit especially — confident errors are memorable and their correction is durable [High-confidence errors, when corrected, improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]
- Less valuable for learners who answered correctly with well-articulated reasoning; forced revision adds load without benefit [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: surfacing and correcting misconceptions
- Metacognitive calibration: aligning confidence with actual understanding
- Retention: the two-pass structure (respond, then revise) strengthens memory over single-response formats [Spaced repetition improves retention; the reassessment pass acts as a spaced second retrieval.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — reassessment enacts this principle by treating the first answer as diagnostic information for the learner, not just the instructor; the revision cycle is the "learning" half of formative assessment
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — the revise-and-justify step requires learners to actively reconstruct their reasoning rather than passively receive the correct answer
- [Constructivism](../principles/constructivism.md) — learners revise their own mental models in light of new evidence rather than overwriting them with transmitted answers

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — reassessment closes the feedback loop that formative assessment opens
- [Peer Instruction](../patterns/peer-instruction.md) — the classic ConcepTest cycle: individual vote, peer discussion, revote; the revote *is* reassessment

## Examples

**Peer Instruction (Mazur, Harvard Physics)** — Students answer a conceptual question individually, discuss with neighbors, then vote again. The revote after discussion is the canonical reassessment implementation. See [Peer Instruction](../patterns/peer-instruction.md).

**[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — Learners revise annotations and answers after reading peers' comments, with revision history preserved for credit.

**[IMathAS / MyOpenMath](https://www.myopenmath.com)** — Homework systems that allow re-attempts after viewing worked solutions, with prompts to explain what changed.

**Exam wrappers** — Post-exam reflection forms asking students to categorize their errors and plan revisions, then re-solve selected items.
