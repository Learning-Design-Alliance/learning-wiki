Feedback¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 175 claims (80 for, 82 mixed, 13 against) · 131 studies (49 causal, 22 review, 20 quant-synthesis, 15 associational, 9 qualitative, 9 design, 7 theoretical), q1–q4 · 34 of 131 report an effect size · 154 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Feedback is information provided to learners about their performance or understanding, intended to close the gap between current and desired performance. Effective feedback answers three questions: Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next? (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). It functions as the corrective mechanism in any instructional cycle that includes Practice or Assessment.

Design Implications¶

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable — it can improve or degrade performance depending on design Feedback effects on performance are highly variable and can be negative. [~S]. Feedback should focus on the task and the process, not the person; praise directed at the self carries little informational value and can undermine motivation. Feedback is most effective when learners have an opportunity to act on it — feedback delivered after grading, or without a revision opportunity, is largely wasted Formative feedback with opportunities to act improves achievement. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear success criteria so learners can interpret the feedback against a target
  • Timing that lets learners apply the information while the task is still active
  • Specific, actionable comments tied to the task or strategy, not global judgments
  • A revision or retry cycle — feedback without a subsequent action is inert (Formative Assessment)

Constraints¶

  • Feedback on performance level alone (grades, scores) without process information adds little and can reduce performance for struggling learners Feedback effects on performance are highly variable and can be negative. [-S]
  • Overly detailed feedback can overwhelm novices; too much guidance at once increases extraneous load [~M]
  • Feedback that threatens the ego (public correction, comparative ranking) triggers disengagement rather than improvement [-M]
  • Immediate feedback is not always superior — delayed feedback can benefit retention for some tasks [~W]

Target Learners¶

  • All learners, but the kind of feedback should shift with expertise: novices benefit from task-level corrective feedback, while advanced learners benefit more from process-level prompts that trigger Self-Explanation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Struggling learners need feedback framed around process goals rather than outcome goals Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]
  • Learners monitoring their own progress benefit when feedback builds self-assessment capacity Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Knowledge acquisition: correcting misconceptions and errors close in time to performance
  • Skill refinement: iterative improvement of procedural and compositional work
  • Self-regulation: developing learners' capacity to evaluate and adjust their own work

Affordances¶

  • Feedback Loops — feedback is the mechanism that closes the loop between performance and adjustment; the design question is loop latency (how quickly learners learn whether their attempt worked)
  • Mastery Learning — mastery models are unworkable without feedback; learners must know what remains unmastered and receive targeted input before re-attempting
  • Assessment for Learning — feedback converts assessment from measurement into instruction; the assessment itself becomes the feedback event
  • Scaffolding — feedback is a responsive scaffold that fades as learners internalize evaluative criteria Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]

Design Decisions¶

What should the feedback say?¶

  • Default: tell the learner what was right or wrong and how to improve on the task or the process, not just a score, a reward or praise. Across 435 education studies feedback averaged d = 0.48, rising with information content: reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24, corrective feedback d = 0.46, high-information feedback d = 0.99 — Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [+S], Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels [+S]
  • Changes when: the feedback turns attention to the learner rather than the task → expect it to help less or to hurt; more than a third of 607 feedback interventions lowered performance, and effectiveness fell as feedback moved toward the self — Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [-S]
  • Changes when: the feedback is praise for ability ("you must be smart") → children praised this way did worse after a later failure than children praised for effort; the harm is moderated by age and gender and is specific to person- or ability-directed praise — Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [-M]
  • Tested with: school and university learners across subjects (Wisniewski et al., 435 studies); laboratory and workplace tasks as well as education (Kluger & DeNisi); fifth graders for the praise experiments.
  • Not settled: the claims cannot separate task, process and self-regulation levels (the 2020 meta-analysis coded information content, not levels), and no claim here tests whether giving learners a required revision step after feedback changes its effect, although the page's Requirements assume it.

Which responses should get feedback?¶

  • Default: make sure errors are corrected; supplying the correct answer after a wrong retrieval raised one-week retention by 494% in a word-pair experiment, while feedback after correct answers made little difference even when learners were unsure — Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S]
  • Default: when practice is a quiz, attach corrective feedback; classroom quizzing with feedback gave g = 0.537 against g = 0.374 without (a between-study comparison; an earlier meta-analysis found no difference), and in the testing-effect meta-analysis g = 0.73 with feedback against 0.39 without — Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S], Retrieval practice improves long-term retention [+S]
  • Changes when: practice uses multiple-choice items → feedback is not optional, because students later reproduce the lures they chose; feedback roughly halved lure intrusions (20% to about 10%), and short-answer practice beat multiple-choice mainly when feedback followed it — Taking initial multiple-choice tests without feedback can lead students to later produce the incorrect lure answers they selected, even when an overall retrieval practice benefit occurs [-M], Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective [+M], Initial short-answer tests outperform initial multiple-choice tests mainly when feedback follows them; without feedback, the higher initial success of multiple-choice tests can favor multiple-choice [~M]
  • Changes when: the learner was confident and wrong → correction is more likely to stick (the hypercorrection effect), so confidence-rated items are worth correcting first; no effect size is recorded — High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors. [+W]
  • Tested with: undergraduates learning word pairs and prose in the laboratory; the classroom quizzing studies span school and university; the multiple-choice findings come to the wiki second-hand through one review chapter (Karpicke 2017).
  • Not settled: whether an explanation beats the bare correct answer after an error; no claim here compares elaborated with answer-only feedback.

When should feedback come?¶

  • Default: timing is not a strong lever in the evidence the wiki holds: in the one direct comparison here, immediate and delayed feedback after multiple-choice tests were equally effective at reducing lure intrusions — Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective [~M]
  • Changes when: second-language vocabulary is learned incidentally from reading → one study, reported second-hand in a review, found delayed feedback better than immediate for retention — Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems, with losses over time moderated by feedback timing and topic interest [~W]
  • Tested with: university students on laboratory retrieval tasks; L2 readers in one study cited by a review.
  • Not settled: no wiki claim with its own evidence tests immediate against delayed feedback for procedural skills, clinical simulation or school mathematics; the timing advice in several claims' Discussion sections is not backed by their evidence entries.

Who should give it: teacher, peer, software or the learner?¶

  • Default: peer and computer feedback are real options, not second-best. Structured peer feedback on university writing gave g = 0.91 against no feedback and did not differ reliably from teacher feedback; peer assessment across school and university levels gave g = 0.31 against no assessment and g = 0.28 against teacher assessment; feedback in computer-assisted language learning averaged g = 0.56 over 21 studies — Peer Feedback Improves Writing [+M], Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment [+M], Feedback in CALL has a significant moderate positive effect on student language learning outcomes (RE g = 0.56) across 21 studies [+M]
  • Changes when: peers are the source → stronger writers gave more helpful comments, though not more accurate diagnoses; with a well-specified rubric, peer rating accuracy did not differ between higher- and lower-performing schools; and a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on — In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems [~M], In two online comparisons, a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on (one study) and helped peer raters place writing in quality tiers, most for complex criteria (the other); the first study says the rubric restrained reflective thinking [+W]
  • Changes when: software is the source → marking an answer wrong without saying why was judged not beneficial by every participant in a small simulation pilot; the CALL effect also varied with educational level, provider, mother tongue, context, subject and target language — Students find computer-based simulation feedback insufficient because incorrect responses are marked without explanation [-W], Six of thirteen coded moderators significantly influence the effect of CALL feedback: educational level, intervention provider, mother tongue, research context, subject domain, and target language [~M]
  • Changes when: learners assess themselves → formative assessment that supported student self-assessment gave d = .61 against .29 overall in US K-12, but untrained self-assessment is inaccurate, and studying modelled examples of self-assessment made it more accurate — Assessment for learning improves achievement [+M], Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training [~M]
  • Tested with: higher-education writing (peer feedback), primary to tertiary students across subjects (peer assessment), language learners in CALL studies, US K-12 classrooms (formative assessment), secondary students (self-assessment training), 10 speech-language pathology graduate students (the simulation pilot).
  • Not settled: whether automated feedback in a self-paced mobile app matches a teacher's; the CALL meta-analysis does not say, and its moderators name who delivered it, not which is better.

For second-language errors, which kind of correction?¶

  • Default: correct errors; oral corrective feedback in 15 classroom studies had d = 0.74 against controls, and the effect held on delayed tests (0.63 immediate, 0.84 delayed) — Corrective feedback on second-language errors has a significant effect on L2 development that is maintained on delayed tests [+S]
  • Changes when: choosing between recasts and prompts → in classrooms, prompts (d = 0.83) and explicit correction (d = 0.84) had larger effects than recasts (d = 0.53), though all three worked — The literature is supportive of recasts, but recast effectiveness varies with the language area and learner knowledge, focus, and aptitudes [~M]
  • Changes when: the outcome is delayed rather than immediate → in one Iranian EFL study metalinguistic feedback beat recasts on the immediate test but not ten days later, because the metalinguistic group declined; a meta-analysis found implicit feedback better maintained than explicit, without saying how these two types were classed — Metalinguistic feedback outperforms recasts on the immediate post-test, but the two do not differ significantly on the delayed post-test [~M], Metalinguistic feedback's gains on wh-question performance decline over a ten-day delay while recast gains hold, leaving the two no different [~W]
  • Tested with: classroom learners, mostly children and adolescents: adults (mean age above 23) were 140 of 827 learners in the classroom meta-analysis, and younger learners benefited more; one Iranian EFL wh-question study. Population-specific: for adult self-paced learners this evidence is the least representative.
  • Not settled: corrective feedback delivered by an app rather than a teacher or interlocutor; treatment length (the two meta-analyses disagree).

Should learners choose when they get feedback?¶

  • Changes when: learners may request feedback → letting them decide after a trial whether to see knowledge of results improved 24-hour retention over decisions made before the trial and over yoked controls; deciding before the trial gave no benefit — Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results (Self-After, Self-Both) were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test than learners who decided before the trial (Self-Before) and than their yoked counterparts. [+W], Having control over the knowledge-of-results decision only before a motor trial (Self-Before) produced no statistically significant retention or transfer benefit over a yoked group receiving the same KR schedule. [~W]
  • Tested with: one laboratory study of university volunteers on a slider-positioning motor task. Population-specific: nothing here tests learner-requested feedback in cognitive, language or clinical tasks.
  • Not settled: a default. No claim here sets how often feedback should be given, or whether reducing its frequency helps retention.

Is feedback the active ingredient, or the practice around it?¶

  • Default: in simulation, pair feedback with repeated practice to a standard; simulation with deliberate practice (repetition, mastery standards, immediate feedback) beat traditional clinical education by d = 0.71 across 14 studies — Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice Improves Clinical Outcomes [+M]
  • Changes when: asking whether feedback itself drives the gain → across 609 health-professions simulation studies, feedback, repetition and mastery learning showed no consistent significant interaction with the simulation effect; and in one small tutoring study, tutors told to withhold feedback and explanations and only prompt produced as much learning — Simulation Based Education Improves Outcomes [~M], Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback [~W]
  • Tested with: physicians, nurses and other health professionals on procedural skills (simulation); 8th-graders with college tutors, 11 dyads per study (tutoring).
  • Not settled: debriefing as a feedback format; no claim here isolates it, and none tests medication-safety scenarios specifically.

The evidence by question¶

  • What should the feedback after a check say, and who or what should give it? Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [+S]
  • Does confirming or correcting each response add to learning from making the response? Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+S]
  • Does corrective feedback on second-language errors improve accuracy, and which form of correction (recast, prompt, metalinguistic clue, explicit correction with or without a rule) works for which target and learner? Corrective feedback on second-language errors has a significant effect on L2 development that is maintained on delayed tests [+S]
  • Can peers give the feedback, and how does peer feedback compare with none and with a teacher's? Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment [+M]

Related Elements¶

  • Formative Assessment — the assessment structure that generates feedback while learning is still underway
  • Coaching — the delivery mode in which feedback is embedded in ongoing, situated guidance
  • Scaffolding — feedback functions as a temporary support that should fade with competence
  • Practice — feedback without practice opportunities to apply it has little effect
  • Assessment — the formal structure through which much feedback is delivered

Patterns That Use This Element¶

  • Formative Assessment — feedback is the central mechanism of the formative cycle
  • Competency-Based Learning — feedback against explicit competencies drives re-attempt and progression decisions
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the coaching phase, where the expert diagnoses and corrects learner performance

Examples¶

Action-Oriented Feedback — Comments phrased as a next action ("add a counterexample here") rather than a judgment ("weak argument"), making the revision path explicit.

5-Minute Writing Conferences — Brief one-on-one conferences delivering targeted feedback on a single writing focus, keeping the loop short and actionable.

Khan Academy — Immediate item-level feedback on practice exercises with hints and linked explanations, allowing retry until mastery.

Draftback / Google Docs commenting — Inline, asynchronous teacher comments on drafts before final submission, preserving the revision cycle. - Provide timely automated feedback through an AI chatbot during EFL writing practice - Pair form-focused instruction with prompts that push learners to self-repair rather than recasts
