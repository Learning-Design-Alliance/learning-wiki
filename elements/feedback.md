## Description
Feedback is information provided to learners about their performance or understanding, intended to close the gap between current and desired performance. Effective feedback answers three questions: Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next? (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). It functions as the corrective mechanism in any instructional cycle that includes [Practice](practice.md) or [Assessment](assessment.md).

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable — it can improve or degrade performance depending on design [Feedback effects on performance are highly variable and can be negative.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.120.2.254) [~S]. Feedback should focus on the task and the process, not the person; praise directed at the self carries little informational value and can undermine motivation. Feedback is most effective when learners have an opportunity to act on it — feedback delivered after grading, or without a revision opportunity, is largely wasted [Formative feedback with opportunities to act improves achievement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654308317959) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear success criteria so learners can interpret the feedback against a target
- Timing that lets learners apply the information while the task is still active
- Specific, actionable comments tied to the task or strategy, not global judgments
- A revision or retry cycle — feedback without a subsequent action is inert ([Formative Assessment](formative-assessment.md))

#### Constraints
- Feedback on performance level alone (grades, scores) without process information adds little and can reduce performance for struggling learners [Feedback effects on performance are highly variable and can be negative.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.120.2.254) [-S]
- Overly detailed feedback can overwhelm novices; too much guidance at once increases extraneous load [~M]
- Feedback that threatens the ego (public correction, comparative ranking) triggers disengagement rather than improvement [-M]
- Immediate feedback is not always superior — delayed feedback can benefit retention for some tasks [~W]

### Target Learners
- All learners, but the *kind* of feedback should shift with expertise: novices benefit from task-level corrective feedback, while advanced learners benefit more from process-level prompts that trigger [Self-Explanation](self-explanation.md) [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Struggling learners need feedback framed around process goals rather than outcome goals [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Learners monitoring their own progress benefit when feedback builds self-assessment capacity [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Knowledge acquisition: correcting misconceptions and errors close in time to performance
- Skill refinement: iterative improvement of procedural and compositional work
- Self-regulation: developing learners' capacity to evaluate and adjust their own work

### Affordances
- [Feedback Loops](../principles/feedback-loops.md) — feedback is the mechanism that closes the loop between performance and adjustment; the design question is loop latency (how quickly learners learn whether their attempt worked)
- [Mastery Learning](../principles/mastery-learning.md) — mastery models are unworkable without feedback; learners must know what remains unmastered and receive targeted input before re-attempting
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — feedback converts assessment from measurement into instruction; the assessment itself becomes the feedback event
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — feedback is a responsive scaffold that fades as learners internalize evaluative criteria [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

## Design Decisions
<!-- Decision section (2026-09-30 pilot): drafted from the linked claim pages only; every choice
     cites the claims that settle it, with markers capped by each claim's recorded evidence. -->

### What should the feedback say?
- **Default:** tell the learner what was right or wrong and how to improve on the task or the process, not just a score, a reward or praise. Across 435 education studies feedback averaged d = 0.48, rising with information content: reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24, corrective feedback d = 0.46, high-information feedback d = 0.99 — [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S], [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- **Changes when:** the feedback turns attention to the learner rather than the task → expect it to help less or to hurt; more than a third of 607 feedback interventions lowered performance, and effectiveness fell as feedback moved toward the self — [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [-S]
- **Changes when:** the feedback is praise for ability ("you must be smart") → children praised this way did worse after a later failure than children praised for effort; the harm is moderated by age and gender and is specific to person- or ability-directed praise — [Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [-M]
- **Tested with:** school and university learners across subjects (Wisniewski et al., 435 studies); laboratory and workplace tasks as well as education (Kluger & DeNisi); fifth graders for the praise experiments.
- **Not settled:** the claims cannot separate task, process and self-regulation levels (the 2020 meta-analysis coded information content, not levels), and no claim here tests whether giving learners a required revision step after feedback changes its effect, although the page's Requirements assume it.

### Which responses should get feedback?
- **Default:** make sure errors are corrected; supplying the correct answer after a wrong retrieval raised one-week retention by 494% in a word-pair experiment, while feedback after correct answers made little difference even when learners were unsure — [Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+S]
- **Default:** when practice is a quiz, attach corrective feedback; classroom quizzing with feedback gave g = 0.537 against g = 0.374 without (a between-study comparison; an earlier meta-analysis found no difference), and in the testing-effect meta-analysis g = 0.73 with feedback against 0.39 without — [Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+S], [Retrieval practice improves long-term retention](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- **Changes when:** practice uses multiple-choice items → feedback is not optional, because students later reproduce the lures they chose; feedback roughly halved lure intrusions (20% to about 10%), and short-answer practice beat multiple-choice mainly when feedback followed it — [Taking initial multiple-choice tests without feedback can lead students to later produce the incorrect lure answers they selected, even when an overall retrieval practice benefit occurs](../claims/multiple-choice-lures-can-be-learned-as-false-knowledge.md) [-M], [Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective](../claims/feedback-after-multiple-choice-tests-halves-lure-intrusions.md) [+M], [Initial short-answer tests outperform initial multiple-choice tests mainly when feedback follows them; without feedback, the higher initial success of multiple-choice tests can favor multiple-choice](../claims/feedback-determines-whether-short-answer-retrieval-outperforms-multiple-choice.md) [~M]
- **Changes when:** the learner was confident and wrong → correction is more likely to stick (the hypercorrection effect), so confidence-rated items are worth correcting first; no effect size is recorded — [High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+W]
- **Tested with:** undergraduates learning word pairs and prose in the laboratory; the classroom quizzing studies span school and university; the multiple-choice findings come to the wiki second-hand through one review chapter (Karpicke 2017).
- **Not settled:** whether an explanation beats the bare correct answer after an error; no claim here compares elaborated with answer-only feedback.

### When should feedback come?
- **Default:** timing is not a strong lever in the evidence the wiki holds: in the one direct comparison here, immediate and delayed feedback after multiple-choice tests were equally effective at reducing lure intrusions — [Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective](../claims/feedback-after-multiple-choice-tests-halves-lure-intrusions.md) [~M]
- **Changes when:** second-language vocabulary is learned incidentally from reading → one study, reported second-hand in a review, found delayed feedback better than immediate for retention — [Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems, with losses over time moderated by feedback timing and topic interest](../claims/incidental-vocabulary-retention-problems.md) [~W]
- **Tested with:** university students on laboratory retrieval tasks; L2 readers in one study cited by a review.
- **Not settled:** no wiki claim with its own evidence tests immediate against delayed feedback for procedural skills, clinical simulation or school mathematics; the timing advice in several claims' Discussion sections is not backed by their evidence entries.

### Who should give it: teacher, peer, software or the learner?
- **Default:** peer and computer feedback are real options, not second-best. Structured peer feedback on university writing gave g = 0.91 against no feedback and did not differ reliably from teacher feedback; peer assessment across school and university levels gave g = 0.31 against no assessment and g = 0.28 against teacher assessment; feedback in computer-assisted language learning averaged g = 0.56 over 21 studies — [Peer Feedback Improves Writing](../claims/peer-feedback-improves-writing.md) [+M], [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M], [Feedback in CALL has a significant moderate positive effect on student language learning outcomes (RE g = 0.56) across 21 studies](../claims/call-feedback-medium-positive-effect.md) [+M]
- **Changes when:** peers are the source → stronger writers gave more helpful comments, though not more accurate diagnoses; with a well-specified rubric, peer rating accuracy did not differ between higher- and lower-performing schools; and a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on — [In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems](../claims/peer-feedback-accuracy-depends-on-expertise.md) [~M], [Rubrics changed what online peer reviewers commented on and improved their rating of complex criteria in two quasi-experiments, though one found less reflective feedback](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+W]
- **Changes when:** software is the source → marking an answer wrong without saying why was judged not beneficial by every participant in a small simulation pilot; the CALL effect also varied with educational level, provider, mother tongue, context, subject and target language — [Students find computer-based simulation feedback insufficient because incorrect responses are marked without explanation](../claims/simucase-feedback-insufficient.md) [-W], [Six of thirteen coded moderators significantly influence the effect of CALL feedback: educational level, intervention provider, mother tongue, research context, subject domain, and target language](../claims/six-significant-moderators-call-feedback.md) [~M]
- **Changes when:** learners assess themselves → formative assessment that supported student self-assessment gave d = .61 against .29 overall in US K-12, but untrained self-assessment is inaccurate, and studying modelled examples of self-assessment made it more accurate — [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M], [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~M]
- **Tested with:** higher-education writing (peer feedback), primary to tertiary students across subjects (peer assessment), language learners in CALL studies, US K-12 classrooms (formative assessment), secondary students (self-assessment training), 10 speech-language pathology graduate students (the simulation pilot).
- **Not settled:** whether automated feedback in a self-paced mobile app matches a teacher's; the CALL meta-analysis does not say, and its moderators name who delivered it, not which is better.

### For second-language errors, which kind of correction?
- **Default:** correct errors; oral corrective feedback in 15 classroom studies had d = 0.74 against controls, and the effect held on delayed tests (0.63 immediate, 0.84 delayed) — [Corrective feedback on second-language errors has a significant effect on L2 development that is maintained on delayed tests](../claims/corrective-feedback-effect-on-l2-development-is-significant-and-durable.md) [+S]
- **Changes when:** choosing between recasts and prompts → in classrooms, prompts (d = 0.83) and explicit correction (d = 0.84) had larger effects than recasts (d = 0.53), though all three worked — [The literature is supportive of recasts, but recast effectiveness varies with the language area and learner knowledge, focus, and aptitudes](../claims/recasts-supported-but-effectiveness-varies.md) [~M]
- **Changes when:** the outcome is delayed rather than immediate → in one Iranian EFL study metalinguistic feedback beat recasts on the immediate test but not ten days later, because the metalinguistic group declined; a meta-analysis found implicit feedback better maintained than explicit, without saying how these two types were classed — [Metalinguistic feedback outperforms recasts on the immediate post-test, but the two do not differ significantly on the delayed post-test](../claims/metalinguistic-beats-recasts-immediate-not-delayed.md) [~M], [Metalinguistic feedback's gains on wh-question performance decline over a ten-day delay while recast gains hold, leaving the two no different](../claims/recasts-more-enduring-than-metalinguistic-feedback.md) [~W]
- **Tested with:** classroom learners, mostly children and adolescents: adults (mean age above 23) were 140 of 827 learners in the classroom meta-analysis, and younger learners benefited more; one Iranian EFL wh-question study. **Population-specific:** for adult self-paced learners this evidence is the least representative.
- **Not settled:** corrective feedback delivered by an app rather than a teacher or interlocutor; treatment length (the two meta-analyses disagree).

### Should learners choose when they get feedback?
- **Changes when:** learners may request feedback → letting them decide after a trial whether to see knowledge of results improved 24-hour retention over decisions made before the trial and over yoked controls; deciding before the trial gave no benefit — [Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results (Self-After, Self-Both) were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test than learners who decided before the trial (Self-Before) and than their yoked counterparts.](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-after-trial-improves-motor-retention.md) [+W], [Having control over the knowledge-of-results decision only before a motor trial (Self-Before) produced no statistically significant retention or transfer benefit over a yoked group receiving the same KR schedule.](../claims/self-controlled-kr-decided-before-trial-shows-no-benefit-over-yoked.md) [~W]
- **Tested with:** one laboratory study of university volunteers on a slider-positioning motor task. **Population-specific:** nothing here tests learner-requested feedback in cognitive, language or clinical tasks.
- **Not settled:** a default. No claim here sets how often feedback should be given, or whether reducing its frequency helps retention.

### Is feedback the active ingredient, or the practice around it?
- **Default:** in simulation, pair feedback with repeated practice to a standard; simulation with deliberate practice (repetition, mastery standards, immediate feedback) beat traditional clinical education by d = 0.71 across 14 studies — [Simulation Based Education With Deliberate Practice Improves Clinical Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]
- **Changes when:** asking whether feedback itself drives the gain → across 609 health-professions simulation studies, feedback, repetition and mastery learning showed no consistent significant interaction with the simulation effect; and in one small tutoring study, tutors told to withhold feedback and explanations and only prompt produced as much learning — [Simulation Based Education Improves Outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-improves-outcomes.md) [~M], [Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback](../claims/tutoring-effectiveness-comes-from-scaffolding-and-feedback.md) [~W]
- **Tested with:** physicians, nurses and other health professionals on procedural skills (simulation); 8th-graders with college tutors, 11 dyads per study (tutoring).
- **Not settled:** debriefing as a feedback format; no claim here isolates it, and none tests medication-safety scenarios specifically.

## Related Elements

- [Coaching](coaching.md) — the delivery mode in which feedback is embedded in ongoing, situated guidance
- [Scaffolding](scaffolding.md) — feedback functions as a temporary support that should fade with competence

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — feedback is the central mechanism of the formative cycle
- [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md) — feedback against explicit competencies drives re-attempt and progression decisions
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the coaching phase, where the expert diagnoses and corrects learner performance

## Examples

**[Action-Oriented Feedback](../strategies/action-oriented-feedback.md)** — Comments phrased as a next action ("add a counterexample here") rather than a judgment ("weak argument"), making the revision path explicit.

**[5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)** — Brief one-on-one conferences delivering targeted feedback on a single writing focus, keeping the loop short and actionable.

**[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Immediate item-level feedback on practice exercises with hints and linked explanations, allowing retry until mastery.

**[Draftback / Google Docs commenting](https://www.google.com/docs/about)** — Inline, asynchronous teacher comments on drafts before final submission, preserving the revision cycle.
