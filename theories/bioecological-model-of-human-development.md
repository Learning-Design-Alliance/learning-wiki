## Description
Urie Bronfenbrenner's bioecological model holds that human development results from **proximal processes** — "stimulating, regular face-to-face interactions over extended periods with people, objects, or symbols" (Bronfenbrenner, 1994) — that realize a person's biological, psychological, and social potential. A parent shaping a child through parenting behaviors, modeling, and encouragement is a proximal process. Development is **transactional and bidirectional**: children affect their immediate contexts as much as contexts affect children — insecurely attached children are more emotionally demanding, and language-delayed children elicit less verbal exchange from adults.

The model is explicitly interdisciplinary, drawing on biology, behavioral genetics, neurobiology, psychology, sociology, cultural anthropology, history, and economics, and was developed partly to explain how macro-level events (e.g., an economic shock) propagate down through progressively more immediate layers of a person's environment to affect development. Development spans three broad, non-hierarchical domains: physical/biological/neuroanatomical, cognitive/language/academic, and social/emotional/behavioral.

### The Nested Systems
- **Microsystem** — "a pattern of activities, social roles, and interpersonal relations experienced by the developing person in a given face-to-face setting" (Bronfenbrenner, 1994, p. 39): families, neighborhoods, day care centers, schools, playgrounds.
- **Mesosystem** — "the linkages and processes taking place between two or more settings containing the developing person" (Bronfenbrenner, 1994, p. 40): the relationship between home and school, or peer-group norms that conflict with school behavioral expectations.
- **Exosystem** — "linkages and processes...between two or more settings, at least one of which does not contain the developing person, but in which events occur that indirectly influence" the person's immediate setting (Bronfenbrenner, 1994, p. 40): a parent's workplace, family social networks, and neighborhood-community contexts are the three most prominent exosystems.
- **Macrosystem** — "the overarching pattern of micro-, meso-, and exosystems characteristic of a given culture or subculture, with particular reference to the belief systems, bodies of knowledge, material resources, customs, lifestyles, opportunity structures, hazards, and lifecourse options embedded in each" (Bronfenbrenner, 1994, p. 40): laws, political economy, and broad cultural values, such as an American upper-middle-class emphasis on self-esteem and individualization.
- **Chronosystem** — the dimension of time across which proximal processes accumulate and unfold; described more thinly than the other four systems, as a temporal frame rather than a fully worked-out fifth "system" of its own content.

The model also emphasizes **experience**: "the scientifically relevant features of any environment include not only its objective properties but also the way in which these properties are perceived by the persons in that environment" (Bronfenbrenner, 1979, p. 22) — the same objective context can be experienced, and therefore act, differently depending on the surrounding macrosystem. For example, authoritative parenting generally outperforms authoritarian parenting (Baumrind, 1989), but authoritarian parenting was associated with *less* adolescent delinquency in a sample of African American and Latino American families in dangerous inner-city neighborhoods (Florsheim, Tolan, & Gorman-Smith, 1996) — the same parenting style functions differently depending on the macrosystem-level risk context surrounding the family.

## Implications

### Context
- Establishing that an exosystem-level event affects development requires a two-step causal chain: a link between the external event and a process in the child's microsystem, and a link from that process to an actual developmental change
- Criticized for weighting biological and cognitive aspects of development more heavily than socioemotional aspects; alternative frameworks put biological, cognitive, and socioemotional domains on more equal footing

### Target Learners
- Not scoped to a single age band — the model applies across the lifespan, though its clearest classroom applications concern children and adolescents whose immediate systems (family, school, peers) are still actively shaping development

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not an instructional-objective framework — it is a lens for understanding how the systems surrounding a learner (family, school, peer group, community, culture, policy) interact to support or constrain any given learning objective, rather than a framework for writing the objectives themselves

## Related Theories

- [Sociocultural Theory](sociocultural-theory.md) — both frame development as inseparable from social context; Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development operates within what Bronfenbrenner would call the microsystem
- [Situated Learning](situated-learning.md) — shares the claim that knowledge and development cannot be understood apart from the social, material, and cultural context in which they occur
- [Social Learning Theory](social-learning-theory.md) — Bandura's observational learning (e.g., parental role modeling) operates within the microsystem this model describes
- [Contextual Systems Model (CSM): child/family and school/schooling as interrelated systems](contextual-systems-model-csm.md)
