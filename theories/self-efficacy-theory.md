## Description
Self-efficacy is "people's beliefs in their ability to perform a course of action required to achieve a specific task" (Bandura, 1977). The theory's central claim is specificity: self-efficacy is not a general trait like self-confidence or self-concept, and it is not the same as documented ability. A learner's self-efficacy for "correctly subtracting fractions" is a distinct belief from their self-efficacy for "writing a persuasive essay," even though both might contribute to a broader (and much less predictive) sense of being "good at school" (Seifert & Sutton, 2018). Because self-efficacy beliefs are self-constructed rather than measured, they can diverge from true capability in either direction; the most functional level is at or slightly above actual capacity (Bandura, 1997).

Bandura (1977) identified four sources of self-efficacy, in order of typical strength (Pajares & Schunk, 2001, 2002): (1) **prior mastery experience** — the strongest source; succeeding at similar tasks raises self-efficacy, failing lowers it — but only if the success is genuine and the task actually meaningful, since success at trivial tasks, or praise for successes a student has not really had, does not raise self-efficacy (Erikson, 1968/1994); younger, elementary-age students in particular draw on a much shorter personal history when judging whether they can succeed again, so building a convincing history of success may require deliberately widening what counts as "the past" for them (Eccles et al., 1998); (2) **vicarious experience/modeling** — watching a similar peer succeed raises the observer's own expectation that they can succeed too, an effect that is strongest when the observer lacks direct experience of the task and when the model is respected or seen as genuinely comparable in ability (Schunk & Zimmerman, 1997) — overused without any real success of the student's own, or with a model perceived as "out of their league," vicarious mastery can backfire, since students may simply disqualify the model as irrelevant to their own potential; (3) **verbal persuasion** — a trusted person's encouragement can raise self-efficacy, though its effect is comparatively limited on its own, working mainly to reinforce direct or vicarious experience, and more so when persuasion comes from more than one person (Goddard, Hoy, & Hoy, 2004); and (4) **physiological/emotional states** — anxiety or bodily stress signals (sweating, racing heart) can be interpreted as evidence of incapability, lowering self-efficacy. Critically, these four sources don't operate directly — a learner interprets each event, and it is that interpretation, not the raw experience, that shapes the belief.

High self-efficacy for a task produces three effects, each with an upside and a downside: it makes learners **more willing to choose** tasks they feel confident about (which can mean avoiding valuable but unfamiliar challenges); it **increases persistence** at the task (which is beneficial unless it crowds out other priorities); and it **improves coping with stress and recovery from failure** (which generalizes to non-academic pursuits as readily as academic ones). When self-efficacy is very low and sustained, it can produce **learned helplessness** — the belief that effort makes no difference — a phenomenon Seligman (1995) first demonstrated behaviorally: animals repeatedly shocked with no way to escape later failed to escape even once escape became possible simply by moving to the other side of the cage. In people, learned helplessness produces a characteristic attribution pattern — attributing a problem to oneself, generalizing it across many areas of life, and seeing it as permanent ("I'm stupid; I never perform well on any schoolwork, and I never will") — contrasted with a more optimistic pattern that attributes problems to outside, specific, temporary causes ("The teacher made the test too hard this time; it doesn't prove anything about next time or other subjects"). The optimistic pattern closely resembles high self-efficacy's own framing of a setback as limited to a specific, temporary task rather than a stable trait of the self.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Opportunities for learners to actually succeed at meaningfully similar tasks (mastery experience is the strongest, most reliable lever)
- Visible peer models roughly similar to the learner in ability, since modeling works by the observer inferring "if they can do it, so can I"
#### Constraints
- Self-efficacy theory explains the *process* of motivation (how beliefs affect behavior) but says little about *content* — which tasks are intrinsically satisfying rather than merely achievable (Seifert & Sutton, 2018); pairing it with content-focused theories (interest, task value) fills this gap
- Persuasion-based interventions ("I know you can do this") are the weakest of the four sources and unlikely to work alone without genuine opportunities for mastery
- Self-efficacy beliefs can be miscalibrated (too high or too low relative to true capability), and large discrepancies create their own motivational problems

### Target Learners
- Learners facing a specific, definable task or skill, since self-efficacy is inherently task-specific rather than a global trait
- Learners at risk of learned helplessness after repeated failure, where breaking the pattern requires engineered opportunities for genuine, attributable success

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained engagement, task persistence, and resilience after setbacks
- Willingness to attempt appropriately challenging tasks rather than avoiding them

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Social Learning Theory](social-learning-theory.md) [+S] — self-efficacy is Bandura's own extension of social learning theory, and modeling (one of the four sources) is a direct application of observational learning
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Self-efficacy is often used interchangeably with "expectancies for success" in [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md), but the two are conceptually distinct: self-efficacy is task-specific, while expectancies for success are typically domain-specific (Park, 2018)

## Related Theories

- [Social Learning Theory](social-learning-theory.md) — self-efficacy is Bandura's own theoretical extension, adding a cognitive, belief-based mediator to observational learning
- [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) — both treat perceived competence as central to motivation, though SDT frames it as one of three basic psychological needs (alongside autonomy and relatedness) rather than a task-specific belief
- [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) — self-efficacy beliefs shape whether a learner persists with self-regulatory strategies after an initial setback
- [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md) — shares the "expectancy" component but frames it as domain-general rather than task-specific
- [ARCS Model of Motivational Design](arcs-model.md) — the Confidence category is a direct, practitioner-facing operationalization of self-efficacy-building tactics
- [Locus of Control](locus-of-control.md) — a related but distinct cognitive factor: self-efficacy is belief in one's own task-specific ability, while locus of control is a broader belief about whether outcomes generally are controlled by one's own effort or by external forces
