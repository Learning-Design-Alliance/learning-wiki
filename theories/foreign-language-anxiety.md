## Description

Foreign language anxiety (FLA) is the tension or apprehension a learner feels about learning and using a foreign language. MacIntyre and Gardner (1994) define it as tension related to the second language, including speaking and listening, and describe it elsewhere as an experience that can pose problems for acquiring, retaining and producing the language (MacIntyre & Gardner, 1991, as cited in Leichsenring, 2010). The articles distinguish two ways of conceiving it. One treats language anxiety as a manifestation of general anxiety that arises in many life situations. The other, following Horwitz, Horwitz and Cope (1986), treats it as a **specific anxiety** tied to language-learning conditions such as the classroom. It is distinct from trait anxiety. Leichsenring (2010) cites a study in which classroom anxiety in one language was independent of learners' trait anxiety.

Horwitz et al. (1986) name three performance anxieties underlying it: **communication apprehension**, **test anxiety** and **fear of negative evaluation** (social evaluation). The articles say the theory "is often cited" largely because it came with an instrument, the Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety Scale (FLCAS), which later studies revise and translate. Other work lists further sources, among them fear of making mistakes, limited vocabulary, lack of practice, classroom techniques and teacher-student interaction (Yang, 1991, and Liu, 2006, as cited in Sadighi & Dastpak, 2017). Leichsenring (2010) adds sociocultural factors such as fear of losing face in front of others, self-efficacy and motivation.

Most researchers, as Zhao et al. (2016) put it, agree that FLA "negatively influences foreign language performance" because it takes up processing capacity and draws attention away from the task (MacIntyre & Gardner, 1989, 1994). Zhao et al. then found the reverse for one kind of anxiety. Anxiety about learning words incidentally while reading positively predicted incidental vocabulary acquisition, and they read it as drawing attention to unfamiliar words. They describe this as "inconsistent with previous findings that foreign language anxiety usually has a debilitating effect". So the sources do not agree that anxiety always hurts learning. They do agree that anxiety tied to speaking in front of others hurts oral performance.

## Implications

### Context
- Speaking in class, especially in front of classmates and where what will be said is hard to predict, is the setting the sources most associate with this anxiety.
- Teacher-centred classrooms that give little speaking practice are named as a source of anxiety (Sadighi & Dastpak, 2017).

### Target Learners
- The fetched evidence comes from Iranian EFL institute learners, two Japanese adult EFL learners studying in Japan and the USA, and Chinese university students learning English.
- Woodrow (2006) found EFL learners from Confucian Heritage Countries (China, Korea, Japan) more anxious than other groups. Ohata (2005) linked Japanese students' anxiety abroad to fear of losing face. Both are cited in Leichsenring (2010).

### Target Learning Objectives
- Oral performance and willingness to take part in class.
- Vocabulary acquisition. Here the direction of the effect depends on the kind of anxiety (Zhao et al., 2016).
