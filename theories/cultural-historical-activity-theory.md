# Cultural-Historical Activity Theory

## Description
Cultural-historical activity theory (CHAT) holds that human thinking and learning cannot be separated from the culturally mediated activity in which they occur. Building on Vygotsky's insight that action is mediated rather than a direct stimulus-response link, CHAT analyzes learning through activity systems: a subject pursuing an object (a motive) using tools, within a community governed by rules and a division of labor. Object formation is treated as a joint, evolving achievement of participants rather than a fixed target defined in advance — the same activity can shift meaning as different groups (e.g., students, teachers, outside partners) bring different histories and interests to it. Two concepts extend the framework for settings that cross institutional boundaries: a **boundary object** is something plastic enough to adapt to each group's local needs yet stable enough to hold a shared identity across groups, and **dialogicality** describes how meaning is made by encounters between old (there-and-then) and new (here-and-now) meanings carried into the activity by different participants.

**Expansive learning, and its collective vs. system-level distinction.** Fourth-generation CHAT (Engeström) treats expansive learning as the production of "new material objects, practices and patterns of activity" in response to a systemic **contradiction** — opposing forces materialized in the activity's own practices, not mere individual friction. A **double-bind** (Bateson; Engeström & Sannino) is a specific, acute form of systemic contradiction in which participants face "pressing, equally incompatible, and unacceptable alternatives" — for example, having to choose between attending a participatory process that feels tokenistic or not participating at all. Meléndez (2021) refines expansive learning into two distinct, separable levels: **collective-level learning** — qualitative change in a group's agency to engage in a practice (e.g., participants shifting from passive attendance to actively voicing demands) — and **system-level learning** — change in practices mediated through new artifacts, roles, or activity structures that outlasts and generalizes beyond the original group (e.g., a new committee structure later adopted elsewhere). The key finding motivating this split is that expansive learning can occur at the collective level without ever materializing at the system level — resolving a double-bind durably requires deliberately introducing new mediating artifacts and roles, not just expanding who is present.

## Implications

### Context
- Most relevant when learning spans multiple activity systems — e.g., school and workplace, classroom and community — rather than occurring inside one self-contained setting
- Useful for analyzing *why* an intended learning objective can drift once real participants with different motives act on a shared task

### Target Learners
- Learners in project-based, community-partnered, or boundary-crossing designs where multiple groups (not just teacher and student) shape what is actually learned

### Target Learning Objectives
- Explaining how a "learning object" — the content or skill a design intends learners to construct — can be jointly reconstructed, narrowed, or contested in practice
- Diagnosing tensions between a design's intended conceptual content and the meaning system participants actually adopt

## Related Theories
- [Situated Learning](situated-learning.md) — shares the premise that cognition is inseparable from social and material context
- [Constructivism](constructivism.md) — CHAT extends constructivist ideas to joint, historically situated activity rather than individual meaning-making
- [Onto-Epistemic Heterogeneity](onto-epistemic-heterogeneity.md) — both frameworks treat participants' distinct histories and ways of knowing as constitutive of what gets learned, not as noise to be normalized away
- [Adaptive Cycles Framework](adaptive-cycles-framework.md) — both are systems-level frameworks for change, but adaptive cycles specifically models phase and timing dynamics rather than activity-system structure
- [Boundary Crossing Learning](boundary-crossing-learning.md) — shares CHAT's boundary-object concept but foregrounds the specific dialogical mechanisms (identification, coordination, reflection, transformation) of moving between activity systems
