# Piaget's Theory of Moral Development

## Description
Before Kohlberg extended and formalized the study of moral reasoning, Piaget studied it directly — partly by observing how children negotiate and enforce the rules of their own games, and partly through structured moral dilemmas. He described a two-phase progression driven by the same cognitive maturation that underlies his broader stage theory, tied to a shift from viewing rules as fixed and externally imposed to viewing them as constructed, revisable social agreements.

In the **heteronomous phase** (morality of constraint), typical of younger children, rules originate from authority — parents, teachers, or a generalized "elder" — and are treated as permanent and unchangeable. Children in this phase judge wrongdoing largely by its consequences rather than the actor's intent, and believe in **immanent justice**: the conviction that misbehavior is automatically and proportionally punished by the world itself, independent of whether anyone witnessed or reported it. This orientation is visible in young children's play, where rules (even ones they don't fully understand) are treated as sacred and unquestionable simply because "that's how it's always been done."

In the **autonomous phase**, typical of older children and adults, moral judgment shifts to weigh **intention** over consequence — a person's reason for acting matters more than the outcome alone. Children in this phase recognize that rules are not universal or eternal but are social agreements that can be renegotiated by the people bound by them (Piaget's own example: children revising the rules of a marbles game by mutual consent). They also stop believing in immanent justice, recognizing that wrongdoing sometimes goes unpunished and that punishment sometimes falls on the innocent — a recognition with real moral weight, since it underlies principled civil disobedience (e.g., someone breaking an unjust law with the deliberate intention of prompting its revision, as in Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her seat).

## Implications

### Context
- The heteronomous-to-autonomous shift parallels, and is partly explained by, the broader move from concrete to more flexible, decentered thinking described in [Stages of Cognitive Development](stages-of-cognitive-development.md)
- Because young children in the heteronomous phase judge wrongdoing by outcome rather than intent, punishing "bad results" without regard to intention reinforces rather than challenges this stage of reasoning
- Recognizing that rules are socially constructed and revisable is a precondition for later principled reasoning about when a rule itself is unjust

### Target Learners
- Children broadly, with the heteronomous phase most visible in early-to-middle childhood and the autonomous phase emerging through later childhood and adolescence
- Especially relevant wherever classroom rule-setting itself is a design choice (e.g., whether students participate in creating classroom rules, which is a concrete way to practice autonomous rather than merely obedient reasoning)

### Target Learning Objectives
- Shifting moral judgment from outcome-based to intention-based reasoning
- Understanding rules as negotiable social agreements rather than immutable decrees from authority
