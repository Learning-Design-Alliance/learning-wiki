Suitability and feasibility framework for deciding whether to use state tests in education experiments¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The paper organizes the decision of whether to use state assessment data in an RCT around two concepts from measurement theory. Suitability concerns whether the assessment yields accurate, useful information about intervention effects, and is further divided into validity (the degree to which the assessment measures the outcomes targeted by the intervention) and reliability (the degree to which scores are free of random measurement error). Feasibility concerns the practical task of gaining access to the necessary state test data. The authors note the two concepts are interdependent: an unreliable score is too noisy to be valid, and a reliable measure that misses the outcome of interest is not useful.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Researchers must obtain the test developer's or state department of education's technical manual to review the psychometric details of any assessment under consideration

Constraints¶

  • The framework is developed for randomized control trials, though the authors state many issues are also relevant for nonexperimental studies

Target Learners¶

  • Education researchers designing randomized experiments

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Accurate estimation of intervention impacts on student academic achievement

Claims¶

  • Reliability of state test scores is highest near cut-scores or the mean and much lower for very high- or low-performing students [+W]
  • Multiple-choice state tests are prone to ceiling and floor effects and do not measure higher-order skills well [+W]
  • Gaining access to state test data involves FERPA constraints and data-request processes typically taking one to six months for review and approval [+W]
  • Participation rates for state assessments are generally very high and exceed RCT minimum participation requirements [+W]
  • Unreliability in an outcome measure reduces the statistical power of treatment-control comparisons [+W]
