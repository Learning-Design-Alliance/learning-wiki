# Turiel's Social Domain Theory

## Description
Elliot Turiel proposed that stage-based models of moral development (Piaget's and Kohlberg's) share an assumption worth questioning: that a person reasons from one unified "level" of moral development that applies uniformly across every kind of social rule. Turiel's social domain theory argues instead that children differentiate at least three distinct domains of social knowledge from early in development, and reason differently within each:

- **Moral domain** — concerns fairness, equality, justice, and the welfare of others; violations in this domain (e.g., hitting another child) are treated as wrong independent of whether a rule against them exists, and independent of what an authority figure says.
- **Societal (conventional) domain** — concerns social conventions, group functioning, and traditions (e.g., raising a hand before speaking, forms of address); these rules are understood to be context-dependent and changeable by group consensus rather than inherently right or wrong.
- **Personal domain** — concerns matters of individual prerogative (e.g., choice of friends, personal appearance) that children come to see as not properly subject to group regulation at all.

Because these domains are differentiated rather than unified, a student's moral reasoning in one domain does not straightforwardly predict their reasoning in another — a student who reasons at a sophisticated level about fairness (moral domain) may reason quite differently about a dress code (societal domain) or a friendship choice (personal domain), and this is not a contradiction or a sign of inconsistent development. Decades of supporting research (spanning roughly 1980 onward) have found that even young children make this three-way distinction, well before they can articulate it explicitly, which challenges the assumption embedded in strict stage models that moral reasoning develops as a single, general-purpose capacity.

## Implications

### Context
- A student's apparent "inconsistency" — reasoning at a high level about one kind of rule but a lower level about another — may simply reflect that the two situations belong to different domains, not a developmental contradiction requiring correction
- Treating a societal-domain rule (a dress code, a seating chart) with the same moral weight as a moral-domain violation (harming another student) can undermine a student's ability to distinguish genuinely important ethical stakes from negotiable conventions

### Target Learners
- Learners across the developmental span — the domain distinction is present even in young children and persists into adulthood, unlike stage theories that describe a single trajectory of increasing sophistication

### Target Learning Objectives
- Distinguishing moral obligations (fairness, harm) from social conventions (context-dependent norms) and from matters of personal choice
- Recognizing that a rule's *source* (an authority, a group consensus, or an individual's own domain) affects whether it is properly treated as fixed or negotiable
