## Description
Metacognition is knowledge about, and the ability to regulate, one's own cognitive processes — realistically judging how difficult a task will be, monitoring whether a strategy is working, and adjusting accordingly. It is closely related to, but distinct from, [Executive Function Development](executive-function-development.md): executive function is the underlying capacity to control and coordinate cognition, while metacognition is specifically the learner's own knowledge *about* that cognition and the strategies available to direct it.

Metacognitive strategy use develops gradually. Bjorklund (2005) and Schneider, Kron-Sperl, and Hunnerkopf (2009) trace a steady increase in strategy use across elementary school, and identify three distinct ways a learner can fail to benefit from a strategy, which matter for diagnosing *why* a particular intervention isn't working rather than simply concluding "the strategy doesn't work":

- **Mediation deficiency** — the learner doesn't understand the strategy at all, even when taught it directly.
- **Production deficiency** — the learner understands and can use the strategy when prompted, but doesn't spontaneously produce it on their own without a cue.
- **Utilization deficiency** — the learner spontaneously uses the strategy, but it doesn't yet actually improve performance, often because executing the strategy itself consumes so much cognitive effort that little capacity is left over for the task it's meant to support; this is common early in learning a new strategy and tends to resolve with practice.

A rough age-8 inflection point has been observed in some longitudinal work: strategies acquired before around age 8 tend to show gradual, incremental improvement with practice, while strategies acquired after age 8 show more of an "all-or-nothing" jump once the child grasps them (Sodian & Schneider, 1999) — though this pattern should be treated as a descriptive tendency from a specific line of research rather than a firm developmental law.

## Implications

### Context
- A learner who fails to benefit from a taught strategy may be experiencing any of three distinct problems (not understanding it, not spontaneously using it, or using it without yet benefiting from it) — and each calls for a different instructional response: reteaching, prompting/cueing, or simply more practice
- Utilization deficiency in particular means a newly-taught strategy may show no visible benefit, or even a temporary cost, before it becomes beneficial — a discouraging early phase that shouldn't be mistaken for the strategy being ineffective

### Target Learners
- Elementary-age children, where strategy use (rehearsal, organization, visualization, mnemonics) develops most visibly across this age range
- Learners of any age newly acquiring an unfamiliar cognitive strategy, where utilization deficiency is a predictable early-stage phenomenon rather than a sign the strategy has failed

### Target Learning Objectives
- Realistic self-assessment of task difficulty and one's own current performance
- Spontaneous, self-initiated use of previously-learned strategies without needing an external prompt
- Efficient execution of a strategy so that it frees rather than consumes cognitive capacity
