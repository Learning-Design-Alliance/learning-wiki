# Critical Pedagogy

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Social reconstructionism was founded as a response to the atrocities of World War II and the Holocaust, aiming at social reform that would help prepare students to build a better world by instilling democratic values. Critical pedagogy emerged from that foundation.

Critical pedagogy is the application of critical theory to education. For critical pedagogues, teaching and learning is inherently a political act: knowledge and language are never fully neutral or objective, so issues of social, environmental, or economic justice cannot be separated from the curriculum. Its goal, following Paulo Freire, is to emancipate marginalized or oppressed groups by developing *conscientização* — critical consciousness — in students.

Critical pedagogy de-centers the traditional teacher-centered classroom. Curriculum and classroom practice under this stance are student-centered and aim to instill a desire to make the world a better place, focusing on controversial world issues and using current events as a springboard for thinking. Students are taught the importance of working together to bring about change, and teachers explicitly connect what is happening in the world with what is being learned in the classroom (Ganly, 2007).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Willingness to treat the curriculum itself, not just the delivery method, as carrying social, environmental, or economic-justice content
- Current events and controversial issues as legitimate, regular curricular material rather than a departure from "the real content"
#### Constraints
- The explicitly political framing can create real tension in contexts (or with stakeholders) that expect curriculum neutrality

### Target Learners
- Students from marginalized or historically oppressed groups, for whom the framework's emancipatory aim is most directly targeted
- More broadly, any student a teacher wants to prepare for active, collective participation in social change

### Target Learning Objectives
- Critical consciousness — the capacity to analyze and act on social, environmental, or economic injustice — rather than neutral mastery of disciplinary content alone

## Examples
- A unit built around a current social-justice issue, where students research the issue, discuss competing perspectives, and plan a collective response
