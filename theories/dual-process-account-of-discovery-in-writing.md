# Dual-Process Account of Discovery in Writing

## Description
The **problem-solving account** of writing (Flower & Hayes; Bereiter & Scardamalia's knowledge-transforming) holds that discovery — the deepening of a writer's own understanding through the act of writing — happens because writers deliberately adapt content to rhetorical goals through controlled, iterative problem-solving. On this view, discovery and text quality should rise together, since both stem from the same rhetorical-planning process.

The **dual-process account** (Galbraith) proposes instead that discovery arises from a *knowledge-constituting* process distinct from rhetorical planning: as a writer produces text, ideas are synthesized "on the fly" from patterns of activation across implicit (semantic) memory, guided by the writer's own dispositions rather than by an explicit rhetorical goal. This spontaneous text production generates genuinely new ideas — but because it is not rhetorically controlled, it tends to *reduce* the immediate coherence and quality of the text, which must then be repaired through a second, independent process: deliberate revision of the text's global structure. The theory predicts that discovery and text quality are not simply positively linked, and can even be negatively related, because they draw on two different processes with different — sometimes opposing — relationships to text quality.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies to open-ended writing tasks (e.g., argumentative essays) where a writer has not fully worked out their position before drafting
- Measured via keystroke-logged writing processes (pause patterns, revision patterns) rather than self-report alone

### Target Learners
- Writers at any level composing about a topic where their own understanding is still developing, rather than simply transcribing pre-formed knowledge

### Target Learning Objectives
- Deepening a writer's own understanding of a topic through writing (discovery), as distinct from producing a high-quality finished text
- Distinguishing writing-to-communicate from writing-to-think as separate instructional goals
