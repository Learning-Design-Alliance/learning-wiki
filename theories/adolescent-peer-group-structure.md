# Adolescent Peer Group Structure

## Description
Adolescent peer relationships operate through several distinct, overlapping structures, each studied somewhat separately in developmental psychology.

**Cliques** are small, face-to-face, interaction-based groups (Carstensen and others) — the friend group a student actually spends time with and coordinates activities with. Cliques evolve over adolescence from largely **homosocial** (same-sex) membership toward more **heterosocial** (mixed-sex) membership as romantic interest develops.

**Crowds** (Brown and colleagues) are larger, reputation-based groupings — "the jocks," "the band kids," "the theater crowd" — that a student may be identified with by peers without necessarily interacting face-to-face with every member. Crowd identity forms partly around shared status, socioeconomic background, and activities, and exerts **normative social influence**: a student's crowd affiliation shapes how peers interpret and respond to their behavior, independent of the student's actual clique-level friendships. Crowd structure typically becomes salient in middle school, grows more differentiated and rigid through early high school, and loosens again by the senior year as identity becomes less dependent on group labeling.

**Sociometric status** (Coie & Dodge, 1988) describes an individual's standing within the peer group as measured by peer nomination, typically sorted into five categories: **popular** (well-liked, low rates of being disliked), **rejected** (actively disliked by many peers), **controversial** (both liked and disliked by different peers — often visible, polarizing figures), **neglected** (rarely nominated either way — not disliked, but socially invisible), and **average**. Status correlates with factors including perceived attractiveness, social skill, and emotional regulation, and is not fixed — targeted social-skills intervention can shift a rejected or neglected child's status over time.

**Peer influence** operates through two distinct mechanisms that are easy to conflate. **Homophily** ("birds of a feather") describes the tendency for adolescents to select friends who already resemble them in behavior and attitudes — meaning similarity between friends can reflect selection, not influence. **Peer contagion** (Dishion & Tipsord, 2011) describes the separate, genuinely causal process by which spending time with peers actually shifts an adolescent's own behavior and attitudes toward the group's, including in problematic directions (e.g., escalating risk behavior within a group of already risk-prone peers). Distinguishing homophily from contagion matters for intervention design: grouping struggling or high-risk students together for intervention can inadvertently trigger contagion effects that offset whatever benefit the intervention was meant to provide.

## Implications

### Context
- Crowd labels can function as a self-fulfilling normative pressure independent of a student's actual close friendships — two students in different crowds may have very similar clique-level friend groups but experience very different peer expectations
- Sociometric status is measurable (via peer nomination) and responsive to intervention, which distinguishes it from more fixed personality traits
- Grouping high-risk peers together for behavioral intervention risks peer contagion effects that can offset or reverse the intervention's intended benefit

### Target Learners
- Middle school and high school students, where crowd structure and sociometric dynamics are most salient and well-studied
- Particularly relevant for students who are neglected or rejected by sociometric measures, where status is more responsive to intervention than commonly assumed

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not a content-objective framework — it informs classroom and group-formation design (e.g., how to form work groups without amplifying existing status hierarchies, or without triggering peer contagion among high-risk students grouped together)
