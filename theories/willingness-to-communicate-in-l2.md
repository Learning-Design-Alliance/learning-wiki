## Description

Willingness to communicate (WTC) began as a construct about first-language communication, and McCroskey and colleagues treated it as a personality trait: a person's intention to start talking when free to do so. The articles disagree about its origin. Altiner (2018) credits McCroskey and Baer (1985), and Khazaei et al. (2012) credit McCroskey and Richmond (1990). MacIntyre and Charos (1996) were the first to study WTC in an L2, adding motivation, personality and context to MacIntyre's (1994) model. In their data, perceived competence and anxiety directly affected WTC.

MacIntyre, Clément, Dörnyei and Noels (1998) proposed the comprehensive L2 model, which the articles describe as "the first attempt at a comprehensive treatment of WTC in the L2". It treats WTC as situational rather than trait-like: "the probability of engaging in communication when free to do so", a readiness to initiate communication at a particular time with a particular person. A pyramid of twelve variables mixes enduring influences with situational ones. Personality sits at the enduring base, and L2 self-confidence can be either. Self-confidence has two parts, a cognitive evaluation of one's L2 ability and the anxiety felt when using the L2, so more anxiety means less confidence and lower WTC. Enough motivation plus positive self-confidence yields **state communicative self-confidence**, the momentary sense of being able to communicate that leads to L2 use. The model also holds that "the proper goal of the L2 pedagogy should be to instill WTC in learners" (as paraphrased by Khazaei et al., 2012).

The articles call the model heuristic. They report that different studies find different routes from motivation to WTC. Jung (2011) found a direct path. Several tests of the model, including Yashima (2002) and Kim (2004), found only an indirect one, running through communication confidence (as summarised by Altiner, 2018). Altiner's own correlation suggests motivation matters either way. Khazaei et al. (2012) say future work should decide which affective variables belong in the model. Lepore (2014) extends it to pronunciation, which the original model did not address, by treating willingness to use one's pronunciation as a behaviour that follows from WTC.

## Implications

### Context
- The model is about situations where the learner is free to choose whether to speak. Class size is one such situational factor: in the fetched evidence, WTC was highest in the smallest of three EFL classes (Khazaei et al., 2012).
- In a foreign-language setting, the classroom may be the only place to use the language, so what happens in class carries most of the weight.

### Target Learners
- The fetched evidence comes from Iranian EFL institute learners, Turkish university preparatory students, and US introductory L2 learners. Nothing in it is specific to adults beyond university age.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Learners who start L2 communication and use the L2 more often, which the model treats as a goal of instruction in itself.
- Pronunciation use, in Lepore's (2014) extension.
