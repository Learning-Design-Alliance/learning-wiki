# Language Acquisition Theory

## Description
How children acquire language so quickly and reliably, without formal instruction, is a long-running theoretical debate with three main competing accounts.

**Learning theory** (Skinner, 1957) holds that children acquire language the same way they acquire any other behavior: through reinforcement, imitation, and shaping by caregivers who respond to and correct speech attempts. This account struggles with **generativity** — children routinely produce novel, grammatically patterned utterances they have never heard and could not simply be imitating (e.g., over-regularized past tenses like "I swimmed," which no adult models but which follows the regular past-tense rule). It also struggles to explain cases where children invent structured language with no model to imitate at all: deaf children raised without exposure to a conventional sign language have been observed spontaneously inventing gestural systems with language-like structure (Goldin-Meadow & Mylander, 1998), and the emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language among deaf children brought together for the first time in a school (Senghas, Senghas, & Pyers, 2005) shows a full, structured language arising from peer interaction alone, without adult teaching.

**Nativism** (Chomsky, 1965) holds that humans are born with an innate, biologically specified capacity for language — a "Language Acquisition Device" pre-loaded with universal grammatical structure — that environmental input merely triggers and fills in with the specifics of a particular language. This account is supported by the generativity problem above and by evidence of **critical periods**: language acquired early is qualitatively different from language acquired later, and severely delayed first-language exposure (as in extreme deprivation cases) produces permanent limitations no later intervention fully reverses (Lenneberg, 1967; Johnson & Newport, 1989). But strong nativist claims about universal grammatical structure have also been challenged empirically: Evans and Levinson (2009) catalog human languages that lack features (such as clear noun/verb phrase distinctions or grammatical tense) that a strict universal-grammar account predicts should be near-universal, suggesting the innate constraints are looser or more flexible than originally proposed.

**Social-interactionism**, most closely associated with [Sociocultural Theory](sociocultural-theory.md), holds that language develops through the child's social need and desire to communicate, accelerated by responsive interaction with more knowledgeable others rather than by exposure to input alone. On this account, the *quality* and *responsiveness* of interaction — not simply the presence of language in the environment — is what drives development; see the related claim on conversational turn-taking.

The current consensus among researchers is that no single account is sufficient on its own: language acquisition requires both a biological readiness that only humans of a certain age reliably have, and rich, responsive environmental interaction that gives that readiness something to work with (Gleitman & Newport, 1995; Stork & Widdowson, 1974).

## Implications

### Context
- Instructional or parenting advice that assumes language develops from either "just exposure" (a pure nativist reading) or "just correction and reinforcement" (a pure behaviorist reading) is oversimplified relative to the evidence
- Critical-period evidence means early language exposure — including early exposure to a signed language for deaf children, and early exposure to additional languages for multilingual development — has effects that are difficult or impossible to fully replicate with later intervention

### Target Learners
- Infants and young children during first-language acquisition
- Older children and adults acquiring an additional language, where the theory's critical-period claims predict (and evidence generally supports) an advantage for earlier exposure, though not an absolute cutoff

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not itself an instructional-objective framework — it explains the mechanism underlying language development that principles like supporting early language development and heritage-language maintenance build on
