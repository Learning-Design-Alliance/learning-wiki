Hypothesized model of noncognitive factors shaping academic performance¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 7 claims (5 for, 2 mixed) · 5 studies (3 associational, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article builds on a hypothesized model (Farrington et al., 2012, Figure 1) in which five categories of noncognitive factors—academic behaviors, academic perseverance, academic mindsets, learning strategies, and social skills—interact to produce academic performance. The authors hypothesized that "teachers' instructional practices and the conditions for learning that they create inside the classroom would directly influence students' academic mindsets and learning strategies... and indirectly influence downstream outcomes of academic perseverance, academic behaviors, and grades." The present study empirically tested three components of this model: classroom environments, selected noncognitive factors, and course grades.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Classroom environments are conceptualized as shaping mindsets and learning strategies, which in turn drive perseverance, behaviors, and grades

Constraints¶

  • The model is hypothesized; the present study tested only correlational within-student relationships among three components, and the authors note possible recursive effects in which grades reinforce mindsets

Target Learners¶

  • Students ages 10-20 in middle and high school

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Fostering academic mindsets, learning strategies, perseverance, and academic behaviors that support course performance

Claims¶

  • Classroom Environment Grades Mediated By Noncognitive Factors [+M]
  • Classroom environment is positively associated with students' self-reported motivation and other noncognitive factors within students [+W]
  • Student-by-classroom interaction accounts for a substantial share of variation in noncognitive factors, indicating context-specific malleability [+W]
  • Within-student differences in noncognitive factors are associated with differences in course grades across the same student's classes [+W]
  • Teachers who are effective at improving academic behaviors and mindsets are often not the same teachers who raise math test scores [+W]
  • Child and guardian reports add minimal predictive power beyond teacher reports of noncognitive skills [~W]
  • Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~M]
