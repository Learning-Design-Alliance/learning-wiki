# Gilligan's Ethic of Care

## Description
Carol Gilligan, a former student of Kohlberg's, argued that his [Stages of Moral Development](kohlberg-moral-development.md) were built on all-male original samples and, in centering abstract justice, undervalued an equally legitimate moral orientation: an ethic of **care** — a system of beliefs about responsibility, interdependence, and responsiveness to the needs of others. Gilligan proposed this morality of care not as a replacement for justice reasoning but as a complementary orientation; a fully developed moral reasoner draws on both. Subsequent research has generally found no consistent gender difference in which orientation people use, which tempers Gilligan's original hypothesis that care reasoning was distinctly more common among women — but the justice-versus-care framing itself has remained a live and useful distinction in moral education independent of that empirical question.

Rather than Kohlberg's strict sequential stages, Gilligan organized care reasoning into three **positions**, ranked by depth and sophistication but not treated as a rigid, universal sequence everyone must pass through in order:

1. **Caring as survival** — a person is primarily concerned with their own welfare; "morally good" means minimizing stress or harm to oneself. This is not simply immature selfishness — for a child in an abusive or otherwise threatening situation, prioritizing one's own safety is the healthy, appropriate response, even though it would not sustain a workable classroom norm as a general orientation.
2. **Conventional caring** — a person is concerned with others' happiness and welfare, and works to reconcile the sometimes-conflicting needs of multiple people; "morally good" means pleasing others. This can produce desirable traits — cooperativeness, eagerness to please — but if it is the *only* orientation a learner is encouraged toward, it risks neglecting the learner's own development, goals, and identity, and can undermine autonomy.
3. **Integrated caring** — a person coordinates their own needs and values with those of others; "morally good" accounts for everyone, including oneself. This is the most sophisticated and cognitively demanding position, and typically requires sustained opportunities to make real choices with real consequences to develop.

Gilligan's framework surfaces classroom dilemmas that a pure justice lens can obscure. A justice orientation asks "What is fair?" (e.g., should every student get equal time reading aloud?); a care orientation asks "What does this student, specifically, need right now?" Neither question alone is sufficient — a teacher who only asks about fairness may miss individual need, while one who only asks about care may produce inconsistency that itself feels unfair to other students.

## Implications

### Context
- Care reasoning develops through opportunities to actually reconcile competing needs — being told the "right" answer to a dilemma does not substitute for practicing the reconciliation itself
- Long-term, open-ended tasks that require balancing one's own goals against a group's needs (e.g., a self-directed inquiry project with a collaborative component) are well suited to activating and developing Position 3 (integrated caring); highly structured, single-right-answer tasks give less room for it to develop
- A teacher's own orientation matters: consistently emphasizing pleasing others (Position 2) as the classroom ideal can inadvertently discourage the more mature integration of self and others (Position 3)

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners across the developmental span; unlike Kohlberg's and Piaget's stage models, positions are not tightly age-bound
- Not gender-specific — despite the theory's origin as a critique of gender bias in Kohlberg's sampling, later research does not support a consistent gender difference in which orientation people default to

### Target Learning Objectives
- Balancing personal needs and values against the needs of others, rather than treating either as automatically overriding
- Recognizing that "fairness" (justice) and "responsiveness to need" (care) are both legitimate, sometimes competing, considerations in a moral decision
