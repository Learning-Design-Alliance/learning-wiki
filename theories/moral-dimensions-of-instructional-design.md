## Description
Osguthorpe, Osguthorpe, Jacob and Davies argue that instructional design is a moral endeavour in the sense teacher education already accepts for teaching. Following Fenstermacher, any human action undertaken in regard to other human beings carries questions of what is fair, right, just and virtuous. A designer is usually absent when learning happens, but that does not remove the moral dimension. It only makes it easier to overlook.

The argument turns on a distinction they take from Jackson (1986). The **mimetic** tradition transmits factual and procedural knowledge through an imitative process: test, present, perform and evaluate, reward or remediate, advance. The authors call that sequence "hauntingly similar" to ADDIE. The **transformative** tradition tries to change how learners see themselves in relation to others and the world, and cares for the learner "in their wholeness", including character. The authors do not reject the mimetic tradition. Their claim is that performance is not a sufficient criterion of success, and that designers who attend only to it produce instruction with unintended effects on learners.

"Moral" is scoped carefully. It does **not** mean professional ethics codes (such as not plagiarising), and it does **not** mean slipping lessons on virtue into content. It means how designers conduct and view their work in relation to the people who will use what they make.

The framework adds conscience formation, taken from Green's *Voices: The Formation of Conscience* (1999), on top of the standard designer competencies. It names five consciences:

- **Craft**: holding oneself to the often unstated standards of the profession, beyond what a client demands. The example given is a designer who builds a first prototype quickly and then seeks feedback relentlessly.
- **Membership**: asking what one owes the profession. The authors suggest the field's norms are weak partly because designers do not see their work as having moral aims.
- **Sacrifice**: truth-telling and promise-keeping, and acting beyond self-interest for learners and for the design team.
- **Memory**: rootedness, meaning drawing on the field's history and on the designer's own strengths, and giving learners ways to bring who they are to the topic.
- **Imagination**: leading rather than executing a preconceived product, and proposing approaches a client has not considered.

The consciences develop through **reflexive judgment**, Green's term for differentiating what is right and good from what is not, through reflective thought.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies wherever a designer's product reaches learners without the designer present: online courses, self-paced materials, corporate training, published curricula
- Most pointed where a model is applied mechanically. The chapter opens with a corporate training unit whose model aimed at "zero defects" and counted a trainee's wrong answer as a defect in the person

### Target Learners
- Every learner a design reaches. The framework is about the designer, but its test is the learner's experience, including effects nobody measured

### Target Learning Objectives
- Transformative outcomes alongside mimetic ones: how learners come to see themselves and their relation to others, not only test performance
- For the designer: a developed conscience of craft, membership, sacrifice, memory and imagination
