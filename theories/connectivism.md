# Connectivism

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Connectivism, proposed by George Siemens (2004/2005), argues that behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism were all developed before digital technology reorganized how people communicate and learn, and that the conditions underlying them have shifted too far for those theories to simply be revised. Where the earlier theories differ on *how* learning happens inside a person, they share the assumption that learning is knowable and occurs inside an individual. Connectivism drops that assumption: learning can reside outside the person entirely — in an organization, a database, or a network — and the central skill is not holding knowledge but forming and maintaining the connections needed to access it when needed.

Siemens frames this against the rapidly shrinking "half-life of knowledge" — much of what is known today did not exist a decade ago — and draws on chaos theory, network theory, and self-organization to argue that learning is "a process that occurs within nebulous environments of shifting core elements — not entirely under the control of the individual" (Siemens, 2004).

Principles of connectivism (Siemens, 2004):

- Learning and knowledge rest in a diversity of opinions.
- Learning is a process of connecting specialized nodes or information sources.
- Learning may reside in non-human appliances (databases, tools, organizations).
- The capacity to know more is more critical than what is currently known.
- Nurturing and maintaining connections is needed to facilitate continual learning.
- The ability to see connections between fields, ideas, and concepts is a core skill.
- Currency (accurate, up-to-date knowledge) is the intent of all connectivist learning activities.
- Decision-making is itself a learning process — the "right" choice today may be wrong tomorrow as the information landscape shifts.

Connectivism also draws on network science: nodes compete for connections, well-connected nodes ("hubs") are more likely to gain further connections, and "weak ties" — links between otherwise dissimilar networks — are disproportionately valuable for innovation and serendipitous discovery (Barabási, 2002; Granovetter's weak-ties idea as applied by Siemens). Personal knowledge is understood as a network that feeds into organizational networks and back again, so that staying current is a matter of cultivating and pruning connections rather than accumulating facts.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- An information environment rich enough to make network-navigation itself a meaningful skill — online courses, professional networks, rapidly evolving fields
- Tools or platforms that make connections visible and traversable (personal learning networks, social bookmarking, MOOCs, organizational knowledge bases)
#### Constraints
- Connectivism is a descriptive account of learning in networked environments rather than a tested, falsifiable psychological theory; it has drawn criticism for lacking the mechanism-level specificity of the theories it responds to
- Provides little direct guidance for foundational skill acquisition (e.g., novice procedural learning), where explicit instruction and practice still dominate

### Target Learners
- Learners who already have enough foundational knowledge to evaluate and integrate information from a network — the theory targets *continuing*, professional, and self-directed learning rather than initial skill acquisition
- Professionals in fields where the "half-life of knowledge" is short and staying current matters more than mastering a fixed curriculum

### Target Learning Objectives
- Building and maintaining a personal or organizational knowledge network
- Pattern recognition across disparate fields, ideas, and information sources
- Knowing "where" to find current, accurate information rather than only "what" is currently known

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Explicitly positions itself against [Behaviorism](behaviorism.md), [Cognitivism](cognitivism.md), and [Constructivism](constructivism.md) as theories built for a pre-networked era that cannot account for learning distributed across people, organizations, and technology

## Related Theories
- [Behaviorism](behaviorism.md) – predates networked learning; treats learning as entirely internal to the individual
- [Cognitivism](cognitivism.md) – also treats learning as an internal information-processing activity, which connectivism explicitly rejects as insufficient
- [Constructivism](constructivism.md) – shares connectivism's interest in learner-directed, socially situated learning, but still assumes knowledge is constructed inside the individual mind
- [Situated Learning](situated-learning.md) ~ both emphasize that knowledge exists in a context beyond the individual, though situated learning centers embodied practice rather than digital networks

## Examples
- Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) as an implementation of network-based, distributed learning
- Personal learning networks built through social media, RSS, and professional communities
