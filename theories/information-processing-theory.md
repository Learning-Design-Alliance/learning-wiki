# Information Processing Theory

## Description
Information Processing Theory treats learning as the movement and transformation of information through attention, working memory, encoding, storage, and retrieval. In broad terms, the theory asks what learners notice, how much they can actively process at once, how they organize new information, and how it becomes retrievable later.

This theory family is especially useful for instructional design because it highlights bottlenecks: limited working-memory capacity, the need for organization, the role of prior knowledge in encoding, and the importance of retrieval and feedback. Many common design moves such as chunking, worked examples, sequencing, and review are partly justified in information-processing terms.

**Sensory memory** is the system's entry point: it holds roughly four items for 0.5–3 seconds. The best-understood registers are iconic (visual) and echoic (auditory, 2–3 seconds); touch and smell/taste registers are much less understood. Information sensory memory doesn't capture is lost to **decay**.

**Encoding** can be semantic (meaning-based), visual, or acoustic; Craik and Tulving's (1975) levels-of-processing finding is that semantic encoding — connecting new information to existing meaning — produces the strongest recall of the three, and the closely related **self-reference effect** (Rogers, Kuiper, & Kirker, 1977) shows recall improves further when learners relate new material to themselves specifically. This distinction underlies a practical contrast between **maintenance rehearsal** (simply repeating information to keep it active in working memory, which the evidence base treats as inefficient for long-term retention on its own) and **elaboration** (actively relating new information to existing knowledge), which is what actually moves information into long-term memory.

Baddeley and Hitch's (1974) **working-memory model** offers a more differentiated alternative to Atkinson and Shiffrin's original single short-term store, splitting working memory into a central executive, a phonological loop (verbal/acoustic information), a visuospatial sketchpad (visual/spatial information), and an episodic buffer (integrating information across the other components) — a structure consistent with Miller's (1956) classic finding that working memory holds roughly 7±2 discrete chunks at once. Information in working memory that isn't consolidated is vulnerable to **interference** rather than simple decay.

Long-term memory is commonly split into **explicit** (declarative — semantic and episodic) and **implicit** (procedural) memory, with three modes of retrieval: recall, recognition, and relearning. A more fine-grained taxonomy adds **imagery** knowledge (mental representations of sensory experience), **strategic** knowledge (knowing which strategy to apply and when), and **conceptual** knowledge (organized relationships among ideas) alongside declarative, procedural, and episodic knowledge. Long-term forgetting is generally attributed to **retrieval failure** — losing the pathway to stored information — rather than true loss of the information itself, distinguishing it from the decay (sensory memory) and interference (working memory) mechanisms above.

Paivio's (1986) **dual coding** hypothesis — that information stored as both an image and a verbal label is better retained than information stored in only one format — is closely related and is covered in its own page, [Dual Coding Theory](dual-coding-theory.md).

### Metacognition and Metamemory
**Metamemory** is a learner's developing understanding of their own memory; **metacognition** is the broader awareness of one's own cognitive functioning generally (Miller, 2011). Two developmental gaps between having a strategy and using it productively are worth distinguishing: **production deficiency**, where a learner can use a strategy (e.g., rehearsal) if told to but doesn't spontaneously produce it unprompted, and **utilization deficiency**, where a learner does use a strategy but it fails to produce the intended benefit. Both matter for deciding whether to teach a strategy directly or simply prompt a learner to use one they already have.

### Criticisms
Information processing models are often criticized as overly linear and serial, in tension with evidence that some processing is parallel rather than strictly sequential. The model's rehearsal-dependent account of encoding struggles to explain single-exposure or emotionally charged (e.g., traumatic) memories, which can be strongly encoded without repetition. The computer-processing metaphor at the theory's core is imperfect and can obscure rather than illuminate certain phenomena, and the theory has little to say about the "hardware" changes underlying cognitive development itself (e.g., the emergence of abstract, formal-operational thought — see [Stages of Cognitive Development](stages-of-cognitive-development.md)), or about how environment, culture, and individual differences shape processing. It also generally does not account for the effect of emotional state on processing and recall.

## Implications

### Context
- Relevant whenever instruction depends on attention, working memory, schema formation, or retrieval
- Often used to justify sequencing, chunking, modeling, review, and explicit guidance
- Particularly useful for novice learners who have not yet organized content into efficient schemas

### Target Learners
- Learners encountering unfamiliar or cognitively dense material
- Learners who benefit from clear structure and manageable information flow
- Novices who need help organizing steps, concepts, or representations

### Target Learning Objectives
- Building durable schema and conceptual organization
- Reducing overload during initial learning
- Improving transfer from working memory to long-term memory
- Supporting later retrieval and application

### Educational Implications
Gain students' attention; ask students to recall prior relevant learning; point out important information; organize information from simple to complex; categorize related information; have students relate new information to what they already know; teach encoding techniques for memorizing lists (mnemonics, imagery); repeat and present information in multiple ways; use **overlearning** — practice continued beyond initial mastery — to build durable retention; make learning multi-modal by engaging more senses (e.g., physically acting out new vocabulary, video demonstrations); and avoid cognitive overload.
