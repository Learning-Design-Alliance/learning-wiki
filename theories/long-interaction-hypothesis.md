## Description

Ellis (eric-ed338037) summarises the interaction hypothesis, which he associates most clearly with Michael Long, as a three-part hierarchy. First, comprehensible input is necessary for L2 acquisition, which is Krashen's input hypothesis. Second, the modifications to conversational structure that happen while speakers negotiate a communication problem make input comprehensible. These are the confirmation checks, clarification requests, comprehension checks and repetitions that Long (1980) found much more often in native/non-native than in native/native conversation. Third, some conditions create more of this negotiation. Two-way tasks that require an exchange of information create more than one-way tasks (Long), and so do partners with equal status despite unequal proficiency (Pica 1987). Long drew teaching proposals from the hypothesis: fewer display questions, more group work, and two-way, closed tasks.

The hypothesis changed over time. By 1989, Ellis reports, Long still held that comprehensible input is necessary but accepted that it may not be sufficient. Drawing on Swain's output hypothesis, he also accepted that interaction helps by pushing learners to modify their output. On this view some moves do more than others: requests for clarification "stretch" the learner, while confirmation checks solve the problem for them. Later accounts put feedback at the centre. Rassaei and Moinzadeh (eric-ej1080713) cite Long (1996) for the view that recasts promote L2 development because they come during meaning-focused activity, giving learners both comprehensible input and a focus on form. Lv and Liu (eric-ej1361438) cite Long (2007) for the view that a recast can lead a learner to notice the gap. Shetye (eric-ej1435677) places the hypothesis inside Gass and Mackey's (2020) "interaction approach", in which negotiation draws learners' attention to input, output and feedback. Altiner (eric-ej1208383) reports Long (1985) as holding that interaction in the L2 is "the obligatory condition" for acquisition.

The articles disagree about how far interaction explains acquisition. Ellis found the most convincing evidence for the third part of the hypothesis, the conditions that produce negotiation. He found weaker evidence that interactionally modified input leads to acquisition, rather than to comprehension. His evidence includes Sato's two learners, who never acquired past-tense marking because interactional support made it unnecessary, and Loschky's comprehension gain with no retention gain. His revision (see [Ellis's revised interaction hypothesis](noticing-comparison-integration-revised-hypothesis.md)) says that interaction makes acquisition possible by helping learners notice features and compare them with their own output, but does not cause it. Ponniah (eric-ej1071042) reports Krashen's opposite view, that negotiating conversation "won't help us to acquire the language".

## Implications

### Context
- Two-way information-exchange tasks, and closed tasks with a limited set of correct solutions, are the task conditions the hypothesis favours (Ellis's account of Long).
- In EFL peer interaction the amount of negotiation depends on task type and proficiency pairing (eric-ej1269664). Unfocused communicative tasks drew more negotiation than focused ones in one Iranian study (eric-ej1348282).
- Ellis warns that the quality of modifications may matter more than their number, and that teacher-dominated discourse is not always free of negotiation.

### Target Learners
- Second language learners interacting with native speakers, teachers or peers. The fetched studies cover adult and university EFL learners (eric-ej1080713, eric-ej1348282) and primary-age EFL children (eric-ej1269664, eric-ej1361438).
- Ellis reports that negotiation helped the comprehension of learners rated lower in comprehension ability more than it helped others (Pica 1989).

### Target Learning Objectives
- Comprehension of L2 input, which is the best-supported link.
- Grammatical development through interactional feedback such as recasts. Here the fetched studies are quasi-experiments with short delays.
