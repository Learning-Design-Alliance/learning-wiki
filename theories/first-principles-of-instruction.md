## Description
First Principles of Instruction (Merrill, 2002, 2013) is M. David Merrill's answer to the question "what makes instruction effective, efficient, and engaging?" Reviewing a wide range of existing instructional design theories and models, Merrill argued that despite differing vocabularies and areas of emphasis, they converge on a small common set of principles. He deliberately avoided the term "presentation" in favor of "demonstration," on the grounds that showing (not just telling) is what most instruction is missing.

The five principles:

- **Activation** — learning is promoted when learners activate a relevant mental model of prior knowledge or experience as a foundation for new skills.
- **Demonstration** — learning is promoted when learners observe a demonstration of the skill to be learned (Tell–**Show**), not merely a presentation of information.
- **Application** — learning is promoted when learners apply their newly acquired knowledge or skill (Tell–Show–**Do**), consistent with the type of content being taught, rather than only being asked to remember it.
- **Integration** — learning is promoted when learners integrate new knowledge into their existing mental models by reflecting on, discussing, and defending their work through peer collaboration and critique.
- **Problem-centered** — learning is promoted when learners are engaged in solving a progression of whole, real-world problems or tasks, rather than only assembling isolated topics at the end of a course.

Merrill frames instruction as a progression of four learning-event types: **Tell** (present information), **Ask** (have learners recall it), **Show** (demonstrate the skill with examples), and **Do** (have learners apply it — first by identifying correct instances, then by executing the skill themselves). A Tell-only or Tell–Ask sequence is the weakest strategy; adding Show produces a Tell–Show sequence; adding Do produces the more effective Tell–Show–Do sequence. Merrill considered motivation an *outcome* of effective, engaging instruction rather than a precondition to design for directly: "the greatest motivation comes when people learn."

The **problem-centered** principle further restructures the typical topic-by-topic sequence (teach each topic in isolation, then apply everything in a final project) into one where a whole instance of the target problem is demonstrated first, component skills are taught and shown as they are needed to solve successive instances of the problem, and guidance is gradually withdrawn until learners can solve a new instance independently.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task or skill that can be organized around whole, realistic problem instances rather than only isolated topics
- Ability to sequence instruction so demonstration and application happen close to when each component skill is introduced, not only in a final capstone project
#### Constraints
- Adds the most value where existing instruction relies mainly on Tell–Ask (information plus recall testing); where demonstration and application are already present, the added benefit is smaller
- Requires more up-front design effort to build a progression of realistic problem instances than a topic-by-topic outline does

### Target Learners
- Learners acquiring problem-solving or complex-task skills, from novice to intermediate
- Applies across settings — corporate training, K-12, higher education, MOOCs

### Target Learning Objectives
- Applying knowledge or skill to solve real problems or complete real tasks, as opposed to recalling information
- Transfer of component skills to novel instances of a problem

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Presented as a synthesis of convergent principles across many named instructional design theories and models ([Merrill, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02505024)), rather than a wholly new theory
