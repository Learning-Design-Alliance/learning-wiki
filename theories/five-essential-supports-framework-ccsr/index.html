Framework of five essential supports and contextual resources for school improvement¶

Theory · All theories
Evidence · 51 claims (34 for, 17 mixed) · 41 studies (24 associational, 6 qualitative, 5 review, 3 design, 2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q1–q3 · 3 of 41 report an effect size · 49 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The CCSR framework holds that five in-school supports are all essential for improving student learning: leadership acting as a catalyst, parent-community ties, professional capacity, student-centered learning climate, and ambitious instruction. Leadership is inclusive, instruction-focused, and strategic, and it stimulates the other four supports; instruction is "the single most direct factor that affects student learning," but providing it depends on strength in the other supports. The supports are anchored in contextual resources: relational trust, structural factors such as school size and student stability, and local community social capital. The framework was developed with Chicago educators in the mid-1990s and used to guide CPS school-improvement planning and self-assessment.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Strength in multiple supports simultaneously; the report states each support facilitates the functioning of the others and a weakness in any element can undermine strengths elsewhere
  • Schools must be assessed across all five organizational domains rather than any single domain
  • Programs must be large enough to function as organizations, with the report treating three or more classrooms and at least six educators as the minimum for organizational structures to exist

Constraints¶

  • Developed and validated in the context of Chicago public elementary schools during 1990-1996 under the 1988 decentralization reform
  • Framework was established through research on public elementary schools
  • The framework was developed for K-12 schools and required adaptation and empirical testing to establish applicability in early education settings

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary school students in urban districts
  • students in public elementary schools
  • preschool children (three- and four-year-olds) in publicly funded early education programs

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Improved student engagement and academic learning in reading and mathematics
  • reading achievement
  • mathematics achievement
  • school organizational improvement
  • improving children's learning trajectories and kindergarten readiness through stronger organizational conditions in ECE programs

  • Ninety-five Chicago elementary schools showed substantial improvement in academic productivity, accumulating an extra half year of learning in reading and over 1.25 years in mathematics over eight grades [+M]

  • School climate functions as a setting-level indicator: students entering schools where they feel safe and supported attend more and earn higher grades, and Chicago's five essential supports survey responses are highly predictive of teacher and school performance [+W]
  • Schools with strong professional community show faster instructional pacing than schools with weak professional community, including among high-poverty schools [+W]
  • Schools small by design showed significantly higher teacher influence and all four professional capacity measures [~W]

  • Racial composition, student mobility, limited-English percentage, and low-income concentration are not independently related to reform implementation once school size is accounted for [~W]

  • A district math administrator reported that schools supported by coaches with mathematics expertise showed greater gains and stronger collaborative structures than schools without such support [+W]
  • Profiled New Mexico community schools showed improvement across attendance, graduation, achievement, climate, health access, and family engagement indicators [+W]

  • In a large California study, principals' preparation quality and professional development access predicted teacher retention and student achievement gains [+W]
  • Effective principals have positive effects on student outcomes including grades, test scores, attendance, discipline, and long-term attainment [+W]
  • A more positive school climate is related to improved academic achievement beyond socioeconomic status [+W]
  • Teachers in schools with better organizational and learning climates have higher value-added and observation scores, even compared with schools serving similar students [+W]
  • AI/AN students have comparatively low access to well-prepared teachers: 27 percent were taught mathematics by teachers with a mathematics degree, significantly lower than any comparison group [~W]
  • Middle grades principals have lasting effects on students' high school outcomes and young-adult life outcomes, beyond contemporaneous achievement effects [+W]
  • Parental support for student learning is substantially higher in top-quartile schools than in bottom-quartile schools [~W]
  • Teachers rate principal instructional leadership highly even in many bottom-quartile schools [~W]
  • Multiple dimensions of school climate were associated with lower school absences, with stronger relationships after the pandemic than before [+W]
  • Mistrust between teachers and parents remained a low-level roadblock in 2017, while lack of parental support had grown as a top roadblock for almost one-third of administrators [~W]
  • Teacher-principal trust is far more prevalent in top-quartile schools than bottom-quartile schools [~W]
  • Teachers in top-quartile schools hold their principals in high regard as facilitative, inclusive, committed leaders [+W]
  • Elementary GPA improved more in schools with strong 5Essentials Survey scores, a new outcome beyond the original validation [+W]
  • District-provided instructional resources supported strong practices only when instruction-ready and tied to professional learning; other resources showed a null or negative relationship [~W]
  • Freshman OnTrack rates improved by as much as 3.25 percentage points more in high schools with strong 5Essentials Survey measures [+W]

  • Teachers reporting higher leadership effectiveness and supports have lower predicted turnover (9.0% vs. 18.7% for low-leadership teachers) [+W]

  • Program content focused on instruction, collegial organizations, and data use is associated with positive school outcomes [+W]
  • Schools with high teacher turnover and large proportions of underprepared teachers are associated with lower achievement for all students and for students experiencing homelessness, after accounting for other factors [~W]
  • Among school-level factors, only principal leadership and school culture correlate positively with CMSE [~W]
  • Supportive leadership increased the likelihood of transitioning to the Ambitious profile [+W]

The evidence by question¶

  • Are schools strong on several of the five essential supports more likely to improve student learning than schools weak on them? Elementary schools strong in three or more of the five essential supports were ten times more likely to substantially improve student math and reading scores (attributed to Bryk et al., 2010) [+M]
  • Does principal leadership raise student outcomes, and by what route? Principals' instructional leadership practices are linked to improved student outcomes, largely through their support for teachers [+M]
  • Is a school's learning climate associated with achievement and attendance beyond the students it serves? A more positive school climate is related to improved academic achievement beyond socioeconomic status [+M]
  • Do community context and relational trust condition whether a school builds the supports and improves? Schools with strong essential supports were more likely to sit in communities with strong social capital, low crime, and low density of abused or neglected children [+M]
