# Critical Maker-Entrepreneurialism

## Description
Critical maker-entrepreneurialism names an identity and practice that emerges when youth-led STEM making is deliberately organized around justice rather than market logic, and youth then bring that orientation into mainstream entrepreneurial spaces built around competition and profit. It draws on four frameworks: **critical youth studies**, which positions youth as legitimate co-researchers and critics of the systems they navigate, not passive subjects; **identity-in-practice** (Holland et al.), under which identities form through engagement with a community's discourses and are stabilized (or contested) through recognition from more powerful others; **humanization / Afrocentric humanism** (Harvell), which centers the dignity and collective well-being of a community rather than individual advancement, and treats de-humanization as a structural, not individual, failure; and **funds of knowledge**, extended here to youths' community connections and lived knowledge of local hardship (e.g., a bus driver's knowledge of transit patterns) as legitimate design-relevant expertise. The synthesis also draws critically on **critiques of neoliberal entrepreneurialism** (Rhoads; Ogbor), which frame conventional entrepreneurship discourse as individualistic, competitive, and historically discriminatory. Under critical maker-entrepreneurialism, youth do not simply reject entrepreneurial platforms; they occupy them while re-narrating what entrepreneurship is for — reframing "profit" and "competition" language to argue for community benefit and solidarity, and collectively resisting misrecognition (e.g., judges dismissing high-tech nonprofit projects as lacking market "polish").

## Implications

### Context
- Requires sustained (multi-year, ideally) engagement with a community-based STEM-making program, not a one-off workshop, since the identity work documented took years to develop
- Best demonstrated when youth eventually engage a mainstream, market-oriented platform (a public entrepreneurial fair, a pitch competition) where the tension between justice-oriented and profit-oriented framing becomes visible and must be actively navigated

### Target Learners
- Youth from low-income backgrounds and Communities of Color engaged in community-based STEM-rich making, for whom conventional entrepreneurship narratives may be alienating or extractive

### Target Learning Objectives
- Developing and enacting an alternative professional identity ("critical maker-entrepreneur") that integrates technical STEM competence with community care and structural critique
- Recognizing and collectively resisting racialized, class-based misrecognition in professional/entrepreneurial spaces
