# Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

## Description
Abraham Maslow proposed that human motivation is organized into a hierarchy of five need levels, in ascending order of "prepotency": **physiological** needs (food, water, sleep — "the most pre-potent of all needs"), **safety** (structure, order, security, freedom from danger and chaos), **love/belonging** (friends, family, group identification, intimate relationship), **esteem** (both strength/achievement/confidence and earned respect/recognition from others — Maslow is explicit that esteem "must come from earned respect and not from fame or social status"), and **self-actualization** (growth and self-improvement, which Maslow described as rare). Maslow also discussed a **desire to know and understand** as a separate cognitive drive, prepotent in itself (the desire to know precedes the desire to understand), but he treated it as distinct from, not a sixth member of, the core five-level hierarchy.

Emergence of a need is gradual rather than all-or-none: Maslow illustrated this with rough satisfaction percentages for an "average citizen" — physiological 85%, safety 70%, love 50%, esteem 40%, self-actualization 10%. Needs are largely unconscious and are claimed to be universal across cultures even though their specific expression varies. A related but distinct set of **preconditions for need satisfaction** — freedom to speak, freedom of expression, freedom to investigate, freedom to defend oneself, justice, fairness, honesty, orderliness — are reacted to almost as strongly as direct threats to the basic needs themselves when they are threatened.

Maslow himself catalogued several **exceptions to the standard order**: some people's self-esteem outranks their need for love; innately creative people may keep creating despite unmet lower needs; long-term deprivation (e.g., extended unemployment) can permanently lower a person's aspirations; early, prolonged loss of love can produce a "psychopathic personality" that permanently forgoes that need; a need satisfied for a long time can be underestimated in importance; a person's conscious desires and their actual behavior can diverge; and self-sacrifice for an ideal is explained as increased frustration-tolerance built through early gratification. Behavior is typically multi-motivated — a single act can express several needs simultaneously — and not all behavior is need-driven at all.

## Implications

### Context
- Contemporary research treats motivation as more pluralistic than a strict ladder: Tay and Diener (2011), analyzing Gallup data from 60,865 people across 123 countries, tested six needs analogous to Maslow's against three wellbeing measures and found the needs themselves are cross-culturally universal, but the *hierarchical ordering* was not supported — people can benefit from "higher" needs without first satisfying "lower" ones
- Maslow derived the self-actualization traits from a biographical analysis of only 18 people he judged self-actualized, and his sample (Jefferson, Lincoln, Einstein, Gandhi, and a few women such as Eleanor Roosevelt) skewed heavily toward highly educated white men — a methodological limitation on the theory's claim to generalize
- Counter-examples exist to a strict reading of the hierarchy: people in severe poverty who still achieve love and belonging, and impoverished artists (Rembrandt, Van Gogh) who appear to have reached self-actualization without their lower needs being reliably met

### Target Learners
- Learners whose physiological, safety, belonging, or esteem needs are unmet or precarious — hunger, fatigue, insecurity, and low self-esteem are named as direct barriers to learning
- Applicable across ages, though classroom applications are most often drawn from K-12 contexts

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not objective-specific; the theory is a framework for the conditions (safety, belonging, respect) that make any learning objective attainable, and for self-directed growth as an aspirational long-term outcome

### Educational Implications
A tired and hungry student will find it difficult to focus on learning — physiological needs must be substantially met before cognitive/learning needs can be productively addressed. Students must feel emotionally and physically safe and accepted in the classroom to progress toward their potential; a teacher should visibly show students they are valued and respected. Students with low self-esteem will not progress academically at an optimal rate until their self-esteem is strengthened. Maslow (1971, p. 195) framed the goal of a humanistic educational approach as producing people who "would be stronger, healthier, and would take their own lives into their hands to a greater extent," actively shaping the society they live in rather than simply being shaped by it.

## Related Theories
- [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) — SDT's autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs partially echo Maslow's esteem, self-actualization, and love/belonging tiers, but SDT treats its three needs as co-equal and simultaneously active rather than hierarchically ordered
- [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md) — task-specific capability belief maps loosely onto the confidence component of Maslow's esteem tier, but is narrower and more directly testable
- [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md) — a contrasting motivational paradigm (expectancy × value predicting engagement) rather than a need-satisfaction account
- [Behaviorism](behaviorism.md) – the chapter this page draws on explicitly contrasts Maslow's holistic, needs-based view of the learner against reductionist stimulus-response accounts of motivation
