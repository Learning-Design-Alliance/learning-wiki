    Glasser's Choice Theory and Cooperative Learning¶

    Theory · All theories

    Description¶

    Glasser's (1990) cooperation-through-problem-solving approach — also called reality therapy — is the other half of the humanist tradition in classroom management, alongside Ginott's Congruent Communication; Glasser and Ginott "represent two sides of the same coin." Glasser holds that effective classroom managers create a learning environment students want to be in, develop mutually agreed-upon standards of behavior that must be followed to remain in that environment, and hold problem-solving conferences with students who violate those standards.

    Glasser advocates cooperative learning as the specific instructional approach that makes a classroom a place students want to be. In his view, whole-group instruction — where students compete with one another for limited rewards — inevitably leaves roughly half the class bored, frustrated, inattentive, or disruptive. Faced with the resulting misbehavior, Glasser argues, teachers commonly fall back on boss management: using reward and coercive power to manipulate and control learners. Boss management jeopardizes the development of self-control, teaches students to value external reward over the satisfaction of doing good work, and produces disruption, frustration, and inattentiveness once those rewards fail to materialize. Glasser (1990) contrasts boss management with lead management directly:

    Boss Leader
    Drives Leads
    Relies on authority Relies on cooperation
    Says "I" Says "We"
    Creates fear Creates confidence
    Knows how Shows how
    Creates resentment Breeds enthusiasm
    Fixes blame Fixes mistakes
    Makes work drudgery Makes work interesting

    For Glasser, dealing with a disruptive student is straightforward once students already experience belonging, power, and freedom in the classroom — in other words, once it is genuinely a classroom the student would regret leaving. When a student persists in violating rules the group considers essential, the teacher holds a brief private conference reviewing the rules, describing the disruptive behavior, asserting the need to follow the rules, and making clear the consequence for continued noncompliance (for example, removal from the room until the student chooses to follow the rules). Glasser cautions teachers not to accept excuses — including socioeconomic or sociocultural excuses — for a student's inability to control their own behavior, on the premise that there is no excuse for disrupting an environment genuinely designed to meet learners' needs: "students will soon discover that you have given them every chance. If they want to stay in class, they have no choice but to follow the rules... And if your students are satisfied most of the time, they'll want to stay" (Glasser, 1990, p. 142) — students choose to behave, rather than needing to be forced to.

    Applied concretely, Glasser's specific recommendations for taking over a disruptive class are to (1) develop workplace rules together with students, (2) get administrator support for an area to which disruptive students can be removed, (3) hold private conferences with disruptive students that stress correct choices and accept no excuses for wrong ones, and (4) follow through consistently when students must be removed, while always allowing them to return once they choose to obey the rules.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    • Depends on genuinely making the classroom rewarding enough that removal from it functions as a real, felt consequence — the approach does not work in a classroom students are indifferent to leaving
    • Requires administrative support for a removal space, and consistent follow-through on both removal and readmission

    Target Learners¶

    • Students who are bored, frustrated, or disruptive specifically because whole-group competitive instruction leaves them without a rewarding role in the classroom

    Target Learning Objectives¶

    • Sustained engagement and self-chosen good behavior, driven by wanting to remain in a valued environment rather than by fear of a boss-managed authority

    Theory¶

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • Boss management's reliance on reward and coercive power to control students parallels the risk documented under the overjustification effect [-M] and under the authoritarian profile in Teaching Styles as Warmth and Control

    Related Theories¶

    • Ginott's Congruent Communication — the other humanist-tradition approach; Glasser's private-conference protocol draws on similar communication-skills groundwork
    • Social Interdependence [+M] — the direct theoretical grounding for why cooperative learning succeeds where competitive whole-group instruction produces disengagement
    • Kounin's Classroom Management Research — the prevention-oriented tradition that, unlike Glasser's largely reactive problem-solving conferences, focuses on what effective managers do before misbehavior occurs

    Examples¶

    • Cooperative Learning — the concrete instructional strategy Glasser recommends as an alternative to competitive whole-group instruction
    • Low-Profile Classroom Control — cites Glasser's point that an effective consequence for rule-breaking is temporary removal from the classroom, provided the classroom is somewhere the student wants to be
