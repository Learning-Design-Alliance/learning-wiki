Baldwin's genetic logic as the elaboration of natural thought processes¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

In his four-volume Thought and Things, Baldwin differentiated three types of logic: formal, dialectical, and genetic. The paper states that "the genetic logic adopted by Baldwin concerned itself with the elaboration of natural thought processes," revealing themselves through individuals' adaptive responses to their environments — forming new habits, organizing new experiences, and learning about the world. This framework anticipates Piaget's constructivist genetic epistemology.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Understanding Baldwin's genetic logic requires situating it within his broader genetic epistemology of successive developmental stages

Constraints¶

  • The paper presents this as historical reconstruction based on Baldwin's 1906-1915 volumes and Cahan's (1984) analysis, not as an empirically tested model

Target Learners¶

  • historians of psychology

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • understanding the origins of genetic epistemology and constructivist developmental theory

Claims¶

  • Baldwin, not Piaget, first proposed progressive stage-by-stage development and a genetic epistemology founded on assimilation and accommodation [+W]
  • Both Baldwin and Piaget explained development through the complementary processes of assimilation and accommodation, with accommodation arising from conflict with habit [+W]
  • Baldwin's work receives little recognition today although his theories anticipated Piaget's, for reasons including lack of empirical basis and a 1908 scandal [+W]
  • Scholarly views differ on whether Piaget appropriated Baldwin's ideas or extended them with empirical support [+W]
  • Piaget denied Baldwin's influence, calling the theoretical similarities simple convergences, yet his mentor Janet had been strongly influenced by Baldwin [+W]
