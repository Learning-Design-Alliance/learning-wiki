# Social Learning Theory

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Social Learning Theory, developed primarily by Albert Bandura, proposes that learning occurs not only through direct reinforcement (as behaviorism holds) but also through observation of others and the cognitive processing of those observations. The core mechanism is *modeling*: learners observe a model performing a behavior, encode the behavioral pattern, and reproduce it — with reproduction mediated by attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation. Bandura's Bobo doll studies (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961) are the theory's signature demonstration: children who watched an adult model act aggressively toward an inflatable doll reproduced that aggression themselves. Observational learning can occur in at least three ways (Lefrançois, 2012): a learner acquires an entirely new response; a learner decides whether to imitate a model based on what happened to the model; or a learner extracts a general rule applicable beyond the specific modeled instance. Bandura distinguished three kinds of models: a **live model** demonstrates the behavior in person; a **verbal instructional model** describes or explains the behavior without performing it (e.g., a coach describing correct kicking technique); and a **symbolic model** is a real or fictional figure demonstrating behavior through media (books, film, television, video games, the internet).

Bandura (1986) later renamed and substantially expanded the theory as **Social Cognitive Theory**, foregrounding cognition rather than simple observation-and-reproduction. Its central architecture is **triadic reciprocal causation** (or reciprocal determinism): behavior, personal factors (cognition, self-efficacy), and environment are "equal, interlocking determinants of each other" (Bandura, 1986). A hostile act draws an unfriendly response roughly 75% of the time (Bandura, 1973), reshaping the actor's environment, which in turn reshapes further behavior — a concrete illustration of the loop. Bandura used this to argue against pure environmental determinism: people act as agents within and upon their environment, not merely as products of it (Bandura, 1977a).

Social Cognitive Theory names four **modes of human agency** (Bandura, 1986, 2006; Pajares, Prestin, Chen, & Nabi, 2009) — individual (a person's own influence on their environment), proxy (another's efforts on one's behalf), and collective (a group acting for shared benefit) — built on four properties: intentionality, forethought, self-reactiveness, and self-reflectiveness. It also names four foundational **human capabilities** (Bandura, 2002): symbolizing capability (perceiving and constructing solutions symbolically, rather than only through trial and error), self-regulation capability, self-reflective capability, and vicarious capability (learning from consequences observed in others or media, increasingly central given modern media saturation).

Modeling itself is more differentiated than simple imitation: its effectiveness depends on characteristics of the model (status, competence, power cues such as a lab coat or expertise signals), attributes of the observer, and the consequences the model experiences. Observers can recombine cues from multiple models into new, innovative behavior rather than only copying one model rotely, and modeling can *inhibit* as well as produce behavior — a teacher's glare can suppress off-task talk in students who merely observe another student being corrected. Observers don't expect the model's literal reward or punishment for themselves but form **outcome expectancies** — an anticipation of a similar consequence if they imitate — which are themselves shaped by environment and culture (e.g., DUI penalties, and their expected consequences, vary by country). In the Bobo doll experiment specifically, children who saw a model punished for aggression toward the doll decreased their own imitation of that aggression (**vicarious punishment**), while children who saw the model praised or simply left unpunished imitated the behavior, including the model's specific words (**vicarious reinforcement**) — motivation to imitate, not just attention or retention, depends on the consequence observed. The full modeling sequence Bandura described is therefore four steps: attention, retention, (motor) reproduction, and motivation.

Bandura later extended the theory into a dedicated account of **self-efficacy** — a learner's belief in their capacity to perform a specific task — as a central determinant of whether observed behavior is translated into action; this extension is covered in its own page, [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md). Self-efficacy develops across the lifespan: infants build early "personal agency" through learning they can cause predictable effects (shaking a rattle); young children have unrealistic self-efficacy and need adult guidance to calibrate it (Bandura, 1986, p. 414); school-age children develop "cognitive efficacy" partly through the ranking effects of grades and peer comparison, and a child low in social self-efficacy but high in coercive or aggressive self-efficacy is at risk of becoming a bully.

**Self-regulation** is a related but distinct mechanism: learners engage in self-reinforcement and self-punishment as internally generated complements to external reward and punishment (Bandura, 1977b, p. 129). The standards a learner judges themselves against are often relative (e.g., a weightlifter tracking their own totals over time) and are themselves typically acquired through modeling — children tend to model moderately competent rather than highly expert standards, keeping self-set goals within reach. Cumulative self-judgment against these standards shapes overall self-concept.

The theory sits between behaviorism and cognitive constructivism: it accepts that behavior is shaped by consequences, but insists that cognitive factors (observation, expectation, self-belief, self-regulation) are essential mediators. It also distinguishes **moral competence** (the capacity to reason about right and wrong) from **moral performance** (what a person actually does), since the two do not always align (Santrock, 2008).

### Criticisms
Social Cognitive Theory is not a fully unified theory: the literature offers no established mechanistic connection between observational learning and self-efficacy, leaving the framework broad but under-integrated. It is difficult to observe or quantify all forms of social learning, and the theory largely treats observational learning as undifferentiated across the lifespan, without a strong account of developmental or maturational differences between how children and adults learn from models.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies wherever learners can observe expert or peer performance — particularly powerful in skill-based domains (sports, surgery, teaching, craft)
- Social presence is not strictly required; recorded models (video demonstrations, worked examples) can be effective
- Self-efficacy effects are strongest at the boundary of competence — when a task is challenging but not overwhelming

### Target Learners
- Novices benefit most from watching competent models; the gap between model and observer should not be too large
- Peer models (slightly more capable) can be more effective than expert models because they feel attainable
- Learners with low self-efficacy may avoid modeling opportunities — design should provide early success experiences to calibrate beliefs

### Target Learning Objectives
- Procedural skills with visible performance
- Professional practices and norms
- Self-regulation and strategy use (cognitive modeling)
