# Social Learning Theory

## Description
Social Learning Theory, developed primarily by Albert Bandura, proposes that learning occurs not only through direct reinforcement (as behaviorism holds) but also through observation of others and the cognitive processing of those observations. The core mechanism is *modeling*: learners observe a model performing a behavior, encode the behavioral pattern, and reproduce it — with reproduction mediated by attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation.

Bandura later extended the theory to emphasize *self-efficacy* — a learner's belief in their capacity to perform a specific task — as a central determinant of whether observed behavior is translated into action. The theory sits between behaviorism and cognitive constructivism: it accepts that behavior is shaped by consequences, but insists that cognitive factors (observation, expectation, self-belief) are essential mediators.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies wherever learners can observe expert or peer performance — particularly powerful in skill-based domains (sports, surgery, teaching, craft)
- Social presence is not strictly required; recorded models (video demonstrations, worked examples) can be effective
- Self-efficacy effects are strongest at the boundary of competence — when a task is challenging but not overwhelming

### Target Learners
- Novices benefit most from watching competent models; the gap between model and observer should not be too large
- Peer models (slightly more capable) can be more effective than expert models because they feel attainable
- Learners with low self-efficacy may avoid modeling opportunities — design should provide early success experiences to calibrate beliefs

### Target Learning Objectives
- Procedural skills with visible performance
- Professional practices and norms
- Self-regulation and strategy use (cognitive modeling)
