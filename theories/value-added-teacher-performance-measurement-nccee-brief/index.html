Value-added measurement of teacher performance as the basis for defining highest-performing teachers¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 associational), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The brief defines value-added in ideal terms as "the contribution of the teacher to student achievement growth, holding constant the factors outside the teacher's control." It estimates value-added as average test-score growth for each teacher relative to the district average, with statistical controls for student background and entering achievement, using up to three years of data per teacher for precision. The top 20 percent of teachers within each district, subject, and grade span are labeled highest performing. The authors note active debate about high-stakes use but observe these measures resemble indicators districts use in Race to the Top, the Teacher Incentive Fund, DC's IMPACT system, and EVAAS.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Several school years of data per teacher (up to three years) to obtain more precise performance estimates
  • Statistical controls for students' background characteristics and entering achievement levels

Constraints¶

  • There is active debate about the interpretation and use of value-added measures for high stakes decisions
  • The top 20 percent cut point is described by the authors as arbitrary

Target Learners¶

  • K-12 students in the ten studied districts

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Equitable access to effective instruction as measured by student achievement growth

Claims¶

  • Highest Performing Teachers 4 To 14 Percentile Gains [+M]
  • The most extreme district disparity: District A's high-poverty middle schools had about 1 in 20 highest-performing math teachers versus about 12 in 20 in the lowest-poverty quintile [+W]
  • District G shows the opposite pattern: highest-poverty elementary schools were most likely to have highest-performing teachers [+W]
  • Across ten districts combined, elementary schools show no statistically significant difference from an equitable distribution of highest-performing teachers [+W]
  • Elementary under-representation of highest-performing teachers appears in 4 of 8 districts despite the aggregate null [+W]
  • Across ten districts combined, high-poverty middle schools have significantly fewer highest-performing teachers than low-poverty middle schools [+W]
