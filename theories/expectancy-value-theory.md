# Expectancy-Value Theory

## Description
Expectancy-Value Theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) proposes that achievement behavior is most immediately predicted by two beliefs: **expectancy for success** (whether the learner believes they will do well on an upcoming task) and **task value** (why the learner believes the task is worth doing). Expectancy for success is conceptually distinct from present-tense **ability beliefs** — expectancy is about future potential, ability belief is about current competence — though the two are closely related in practice.

Task value answers "why should I do this?" with four components (Wigfield & Eccles, 1992):

- **Intrinsic value** — the pure enjoyment of doing the task itself.
- **Attainment value** — the importance of doing well because the task reflects something important about one's identity.
- **Utility value** — the task's perceived usefulness for a future goal (e.g., taking a language class to get a specific job).
- **Cost** — what the learner has to give up (time, effort, competing opportunities) to engage in the task; high cost suppresses engagement even when expectancy and the other value components are favorable.

Expectancy and value are often described as combining *multiplicatively* rather than additively (Wigfield & Eccles, 2002; Wigfield, Tonks, & Eccles, 2004): motivation requires at least a modest expectancy **and** at least some positive value, so a learner with high expectancy for success but zero perceived value ("I could ace this, but why bother?") will be no more motivated than one with high value but zero expectancy ("this matters a lot to me, but there's no way I can do it") — either factor at zero drives the product to zero.

Numerous studies link both expectancy and task value independently to achievement outcomes, including in online learning: perceived competence and task value each significantly predicted learner satisfaction, persistence, and achievement in an online university sample (Joo, Lim, & Kim, 2013), and expectancies/task values more broadly predict achievement behaviors and outcomes across studies (Dennissen, Zarrett, & Eccles, 2007; Durik, Shechter, Noh, Rozek, & Harackiewicz, 2015; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Bong (2001) found that college students' perceived competence significantly predicted their performance, while perceived utility value separately predicted their intentions to enroll in future courses — evidence that expectancy and value operate as genuinely separate levers rather than one driving the other.

Because expectancy and value require different remedies, low motivation calls for diagnosing which one is missing before intervening. Raising **expectancy** means calibrating task difficulty so success is a realistic prospect (neither too hard nor too easy), backed by thoughtful planning, adjusted objectives, supportive materials, and help when needed; it also means addressing non-skill causes of low expectancy, such as a student's belief that fairness or favoritism — not their own effort — determines outcomes, or an external locus of control that makes effort feel disconnected from results, both of which may call for a clearer, more predictable path to success and encouraging feedback rather than more skill practice. Raising **value** (sometimes decomposed further into instrumentality — whether good performance is reliably rewarded — and valence — whether the available rewards are ones the student actually wants) means linking the task to students' existing interests and prior knowledge, making its utility toward future goals explicit, showing that people the student respects value it too, and — for instrumentality specifically — making sure any reward system tracks genuine performance and progress rather than being awarded meritlessly, since rewards perceived as unearned can erode the motivation of the highest performers.

The theory is explicitly integrative — it is often used as the organizing frame that ties together goal orientation, self-efficacy, interest, and self-determination theories into a single motivational-design framework, most visibly in **Keller's ARCS model**, where the *relevance* category maps closely onto task value and the *confidence* category maps onto expectancy for success.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- The ability to make a task's value legible to the learner along at least one of the four dimensions (why it's enjoyable, why it matters to identity, what it's useful for, or what it costs) rather than assuming value is self-evident
- Tasks calibrated so that a learner's expectancy for success is realistic and not chronically suppressed by consistent failure
#### Constraints
- The four value components can trade off against each other — a task can have high utility value but such high cost (time, competing demands) that overall engagement still suffers
- Expectancy and value are both learner beliefs, not task properties; the same task can carry very different expectancy/value profiles for different learners, so this is not a one-size-fits-all task-design checklist

### Target Learners
- Learners who can articulate or be observed to lack a sense of either "can I succeed at this" (expectancy) or "why does this matter" (value) — the theory implies different interventions depending on which is missing
- Learners weighing competing time/effort demands, where the cost component of task value is especially salient

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained engagement and enrollment/persistence decisions (e.g., choosing to continue in a subject area), which expectancy and task value have both been shown to predict
- Deeper engagement that depends on genuine valuing of the task, not just extrinsic compliance

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Integrates elements of [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md), [Attribution Theory](attribution-theory.md) (the stability dimension of an attribution shapes expectancy), and [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) (task value overlaps with the sense of relevance SDT-informed design also targets)
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Self-efficacy and "expectancies for success" are often used interchangeably, but conceptually self-efficacy is task-specific while expectancy for success tends to be domain-specific (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) — see [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md)

## Related Theories
- [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md) — the expectancy component overlaps heavily with self-efficacy, differing mainly in grain (domain-specific vs. task-specific)
- [Attribution Theory](attribution-theory.md) — the stability dimension of a learner's attributions for past outcomes directly shapes their expectancy for future success
- [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) — task value (especially attainment and utility value) overlaps with the sense of personal relevance and autonomy SDT identifies as motivating
- [ARCS Model of Motivational Design](arcs-model.md) — Keller's Relevance and Confidence categories directly operationalize task value and expectancy for success
