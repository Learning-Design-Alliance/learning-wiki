    Design Layers Theory¶

    Theory · All theories

    Description¶

    Gibbons (2003/2018) argues that instructional designs are not unitary objects but stacks of layers, borrowing the analogy directly from architect Stewart Brand's account of how buildings are actually structured — site, structure, skin, services, space plan, and stuff, each aging and changing at a different rate, each requiring deliberate "articulation" with the others so a change to one layer doesn't force a rebuild of the whole. Gibbons and colleagues found the same structure holds for instructional designs, identifying seven layers: model/content (subject-matter structure — concepts, rules, principles, tasks), strategy (event structures — problems, exercises, instructional periods), control (rules governing learner actions and system responses), message (main ideas, examples, feedback, explanations), representation (media selection and display structures), media-logic (the underlying program/logic implementation), and management (session control, record-keeping, evaluation planning). Each layer carries its own design goals, constructs, theoretic principles, tools, and specialized skills — which is why, over time, these skills harden into separate professional roles (a media producer, a strategy/instructional designer, a programmer).

    The theory's most distinctive claim is about designer cognition: designers predictably mature through a sequence of "centrisms" — media-centrism (thinking in the vocabulary of the medium — "the page," "the hyperlink"), message-centrism (shifting focus to telling the content better — examples, analogies, emphasis), strategy-centrism (recognizing structural patterns across messages that generalize across subject matter, and governing delivery through rules — Gagné's conditions of learning fit here), and model-centrism (entering the design through the subject matter's own system and model constructs first, then adding strategy, message, and media — the stance Gibbons identifies as underlying most "new-paradigm" approaches, including problem-solving-centered and simulation-based instruction). Each phase does not discard the previous one; it adds a new primary entry point while retaining the earlier layers' constructs as secondary considerations. Which layer a designer should enter through is not universally fixed — project constraints (e.g., an assignment specifically to produce a set of videotapes) can force representation/media-logic to the front regardless of the designer's own maturity level.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Enough design-team maturity or explicit training to recognize which layer a design problem actually lives in, since a symptom that looks like a message problem (an explanation "not landing") can actually originate in a missing or incoherent strategy or model layer
    • Deliberate "articulation" between layers when a design is revised — changing a representation/media choice should not require rebuilding the model or strategy layer underneath it, and a design that doesn't allow this is architecturally brittle

    Constraints¶

    • The theory describes the designer's own cognitive process and the design artifact's internal structure — it does not by itself prescribe which layer to prioritize for a given project; project constraints can override an individual designer's natural entry point
    • A design polished at the message or media layer can disguise a genuinely missing or weak strategy or model layer, producing content that reads well but doesn't hold together instructionally — the theory's main diagnostic value is spotting this mismatch, not preventing it outright

    Target Learners¶

    • Not learner-facing directly — this is a designer/instructional-design-team framework, in the same category as TPACK and Performance Technology: its downstream effect on learners runs through the quality and coherence of the resulting instructional artifact

    Target Learning Objectives¶

    • Not a learning objective — a diagnostic and pedagogical framework for instructional design education and design-team practice: explaining why novice designers over-index on media, and providing a vocabulary for locating where a design's incoherence actually originates

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Directly adapted from Stewart Brand's (1994) "shearing layers" analysis of building architecture, applied to instructional design by Gibbons, Nelson, and Richards (2000)
    • Model-centrism is described as the shared thread running through most new-paradigm, problem-solving-centered instructional approaches (Gibbons & Fairweather, 2000)

    Claims¶

    Related Theories¶

    • First Principles of Instruction — Merrill's problem-centered principles reflect a model-centered design stance in Gibbons' framework, entering through subject-matter and problem structure rather than media or message
    • Cognitive Load Theory — poor articulation between the representation/media-logic layer and the message layer is one route by which extraneous cognitive load enters a design

    Examples¶

    • A course visibly built around PowerPoint slide templates and video-hosting conventions (media-centric) versus one built by first mapping the domain's own concept/rule/task structure and only then choosing media to represent it (model-centric)
    • Revising a course's video platform (a representation/media-logic change) without needing to rewrite its underlying strategy or content structure — evidence of well-articulated layers
