# Historical Reasoning Framework

## Description
The historical reasoning framework (Van Drie & Van Boxtel, 2008) identifies six interrelated aspects of thinking and writing like a historian: answering historical questions about continuity/change and cause/consequence; making claims and constructing arguments; using sources as evidence; using substantive concepts (e.g., "empire," "patrician"); constructing historical context by considering chronology, geography, and social characteristics of a period; and using meta-concepts and heuristics such as corroboration and source reliability. **Historical contextualization** — reconstructing the chronology, geography, and social features of a time period to situate a source or phenomenon (Wineburg, 1991) — is one of the six aspects and the one this framework's originating study focuses on; Wineburg further decomposes contextualizing moves into spatio-temporal, social-rhetorical, biographical, historiographic, linguistic, and analogical types. The framework is used both generatively (to design instruction that targets a specific aspect) and analytically (as a rubric to score student writing on each aspect separately, so that overall improvement and aspect-specific improvement can diverge).

**Empirical expansion beyond source analysis.** A later interview study of academic historians (Kainulainen et al., 2025) identifies 107 epistemic processes in 14 categories from historians' own accounts of their research — a far larger and more empirically-grounded set than the original six aspects, which were derived primarily from studies of historians reading pre-selected source documents under researcher-designed tasks. Five of the 14 categories are largely absent from prior inquiry-based-learning (IBL) research and design: **collecting and archiving materials** (searching archives, deciding what to select amid an overwhelming volume of sources); **tools and languages** (multilingual reading/writing decisions, and "digital tool criticism" — asking who built a research tool and how it shapes what can be found); **virtues and affect** (curiosity, tolerance of ambiguity, pluralism, and emotional regulation as professional skills, not distractions from rigor); **broad approaches and methodologies** (explicit choice of research design — comparative, microhistorical, quantitative/qualitative — and a hermeneutic, non-linear inquiry process); and **explicitly social processes** (collaborative writing, audience-aware revision, and consultation with archivists or other historians). The study's authors are explicit that professional historiography's full complexity should not simply be miniaturized into every classroom — the finding's design implication is that history IBL should be *informed* by this broader empirical model, at a grain size and via pedagogies (including alternatives such as having students interview historians) appropriate to the setting, rather than replicating all 14 categories directly.

## Implications

### Context
- Requires source material and a writing task (typically a document-based question, DBQ) rich enough to distinguish the six aspects from one another in scoring
- Compatible with L2/multilingual history instruction when paired with explicit language scaffolding (e.g., a CLIL — Content and Language Integrated Learning — approach), since contextualization requires specific chronological and causal-connective language forms

### Target Learners
- Secondary and university students learning to write evidence-based historical arguments, including L2 learners for whom the language demands of contextualization (chronological and causal connective phrasing) are a separate hurdle from the historical content itself

### Target Learning Objectives
- Producing written historical arguments that integrate accurate background context with source-based evidence and an explicit claim, rather than treating contextualization, evidence, and claim as separately graded but disconnected components
