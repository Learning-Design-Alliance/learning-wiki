# Experiential Learning Theory

## Description
David Kolb's Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) is "a holistic model of the learning process and a multi-linear model of adult development" (Baker, Jensen, & Kolb, 2002, p. 51), rooted explicitly in Dewey, Kurt Lewin, and Piaget. It models learning as a four-stage cycle — concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation — organized around two underlying dialectical continuums: **apprehension** (concrete experience) versus **comprehension** (abstract conceptualization), and **intension** (reflective observation) versus **extension** (active experimentation). Kolb's central theoretical claim is that learning requires actively resolving the discomfort of whichever pole of each continuum a learner is *not* naturally comfortable with: "perception alone is not sufficient for learning; something must be done with it," and "transformation alone cannot represent learning, for there must be something to be transformed" (Baker, Jensen, & Kolb, pp. 56–67). This dialectical-resolution requirement is the theory's core explanatory mechanism, distinguishing it from a purely descriptive account of "learning by doing."

The theory targets **adult learners** specifically, framed as an inclusive model of adult development within a single framework, and underlies applications such as cooperative education, internships, and continuing-professional-development e-learning.

### A Rival Account: Dewey's Reflective Experience
Miettinen (2000) contrasts Kolb's model against Dewey's own philosophy of experience, on which Kolb draws but from which the model departs in two significant ways. First, Dewey held that **non-reflective, habitual experience is the dominant mode** of everyday functioning, and that reflective experience arises only when habitual experience is *contradicted* by something unexpected; Kolb's model, by contrast, does not address non-reflective experience at all, treating every turn of the cycle as if it begins from reflection. Second, Dewey held that **observation of an external reality is the starting point** of knowledge, whereas Kolb makes the learner's own subjective experience the starting point, disregarding the learner's prior objective reality.

### Criticisms
"Concrete experience" is under-theorized in Kolb's own writing — it is "nowhere defined or elaborated," leading some to argue the model is really about reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation rather than a genuine four-part cycle (Herron, cited in Yorks & Kasl, 2002). The very idea of "immediate and concrete" experience is itself philosophically contestable (Miettinen, 2000). Kolb's concepts are criticized as an eclectic blend of disparate theorists (Dewey, Lewin, Piaget) that does not fully cohere into a single logical account, and critics have argued the theory exists partly to justify Kolb's commercial Learning Style Inventory (Miettinen, 2000). The model has also been criticized for treating its four phases as potentially disconnected rather than truly integrated, and for having no account of the *social* dimension of experience or knowledge construction — the cycle is modeled as happening within a single learner, not within a group.

In response to the last two criticisms, some accounts propose a **revised experiential learning cycle**: starting from **subjective stimuli** (the learner's own prior observations and felt judgments, performed individually), through a **disruptive experience** (an explicitly Deweyan break from habitual experience), an **emotion inventory**, **reflective observation**, **conceptualization/hypothesizing**, and **addressing** (planning, experimentation, or cautious testing) — looping back via either expected or further disruptive experiences, situated within an encompassing environment, and explicitly able to occur either individually or within a social group.

## Implications

### Context
- Best suited to settings where learners can genuinely act, observe consequences, and revise understanding across repeated cycles — internships, cooperative education, service learning, simulations, and structured reflection practices
- The model's silence on non-reflective, habitual experience means it does not, by itself, explain everyday functioning that doesn't involve active reflection

### Target Learners
- Adult learners specifically — Kolb frames the theory as a model of adult development, and its signature applications (internships, cooperative education, professional continuing education) are adult/professional contexts

### Target Learning Objectives
- Integration of concrete experience with abstract understanding through repeated cycles of action and reflection
- Development toward more complete engagement with all four modes of the cycle, since the theory holds that skilled learning requires resolving discomfort with whichever pole (concrete/abstract, reflective/active) a learner is less naturally inclined toward
