Transfer for Future Learning (TFL): a three-phase assessment approach for measuring transfer within a learning progression¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 design), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Transfer for Future Learning (TFL) is an assessment approach measuring whether students transfer recently learned concepts to future topics within the same learning progression. Inspired by Preparation for Future Learning and grounded in Evidence-Centered Design, TFL provides instructional support ("bridging") to reduce transfer difficulty, adopting a three-phase protocol of eliciting prior knowledge, bridging instruction, and assessing performance on the target concept. The article developed TFL tasks within Cignition's microtutoring context.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Requires domain analysis and domain modeling to identify source and target concepts within a learning progression
  • Requires a tutor or instructor to deliver bridging instruction

Constraints¶

  • Designed for relatively near transfer within a learning progression, not far transfer across domains

Target Learners¶

  • fifth-grade students struggling with fraction concepts

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • transfer of fraction understanding to more advanced mathematical topics

Claims¶

  • Cignition Posttest Correlates Overall Tfl Score [+M]
  • Cignition posttest scores correlate significantly with prior knowledge (portion A) scores on both TFL tasks at p < 0.01 [+W]
  • Most students scored 50% or fewer of available points on the TFL tasks, indicating transfer was challenging [+W]
  • Cignition posttest scores correlate with transfer portion C scores for Fraction Division but not for Fraction Generalization [+W]
  • Prior knowledge (portion A) scores correlate with transfer (portion C) scores for Fraction Division but not for Fraction Generalization [+W]
  • Qualitative observations indicate Fraction Division transfer difficulty stemmed from weak connections between multiplicative/additive strategies and division, while Fraction Generalization difficulty stemmed from varied variable notation experience [+W]
