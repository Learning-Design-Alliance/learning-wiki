## Description

Usage-based approaches "explain language development as a gradual process of generalizing constructions through language experience" (Kim, Rah & Hwang, 2020). The unit of learning is the **construction**, defined in Construction Grammar as a form-meaning pairing whose form or meaning cannot be predicted from its parts (Goldberg, 1995). In first-language acquisition, children first build up concrete, highly frequent, item-based patterns. They lean on a few light verbs (*go*, *get*, *give*) that signal a construction's form and meaning. Through repeated exposure they extract statistical regularities and move to abstract constructions that generalise over many exemplars. Kim et al. adopt the view that L2 constructional development "is guided by their language experience, just as in L1 development". On this view the L2 mechanism is a subset of the L1 mechanism, and it operates "even in the EFL context where target language input is regarded as being impoverished".

Sáez (2014) presents Cognitive Linguistics as "a usage-based model of language acquisition" in which "meaning is the driving force behind form". Meaning grows out of embodied experience and extends by metaphor into abstract domains, and languages differ in how they conceptualise experience. Learning an L2 therefore includes learning its community's conceptual categories. Some patterns, such as the metaphoric uses of prepositions, differ from the L1 "in subtle ways that learners may never identify on their own". Sáez cites N. Ellis (2008) for the proposal that such patterns "may only be achieved through explicit instruction". This is a limit on learning from exposure alone that comes from inside the usage-based literature. Shetye (2024), evaluating Khanmigo, cites usage-based research (Crossley et al., 2016) showing that input made salient through word familiarity, meaningfulness and concreteness helps acquisition. She also questions whether a generative-AI tutor supplies such input to beginners.

The fetched sources do not agree on what "experience" should count. Usage-based accounts stress frequency. Jones, Dye and Johns (2017), writing about first-language lexical organisation and not SLA, argue that "it is not simply frequency but the diversity and distinctiveness of contexts" that organises the lexicon, and that repetitions within one context add little. If that holds for L2 learners, raw exposure counts would be the wrong measure of experience. None of the fetched sources tests this in an L2.

## Implications

### Context
- Learners need repeated, meaningful exposure to target constructions. In foreign-language settings input is thin, which the articles identify as a main source of difficulty with English constructions.
- In the fetched evidence, school grade stood in for language experience (Kim et al., 2020). How much exposure produces which stage has not been measured directly.

### Target Learners
- The fetched evidence comes from Korean EFL learners from elementary school to college, and from advanced L2 learners in a Cognitive Linguistics preposition study.
- The account predicts that beginners rely on individual verbs and frequent patterns, while advanced learners use abstract constructions.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Constructional (argument-structure) knowledge, with frequent and prototypical constructions acquired first. In Kim et al. (2020) that was the transitive, and the rare resultative stayed hard even for advanced learners.
- Using L2 forms in line with the L2's conceptual conventions (Sáez, 2014).
