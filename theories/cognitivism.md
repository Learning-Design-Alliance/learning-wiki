# Cognitivism

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Cognitivism emerged in the late 1950s as a reaction against behaviorism's refusal to look inside the "black box" of the mind. Rather than treating learning as a change in observable stimulus-response associations, cognitivists treat it as a change in internal mental structures — the way information is received, organized, stored, and retrieved. Learning is equated with discrete changes between states of knowledge, not with changes in the probability of a response, and the learner is viewed as an active participant who attends to, codes, transforms, rehearses, and stores information rather than merely reacting to it (Ertmer & Newby, 2013).

Cognitivism shares with behaviorism an interest in environmental conditions (explanations, demonstrations, examples, practice with feedback), but interprets their role differently: a behaviorist arranges the environment to shape observable responses, while a cognitivist arranges it to support internal mental processing. Memory is central — learning succeeds when information is stored in an organized, meaningful way, and forgetting is explained as a retrieval failure (interference, decay, or missing retrieval cues) rather than the "nonuse" of a habit.

Cognitivism is the broader theoretical paradigm; [Information Processing Theory](information-processing-theory.md) is the specific mechanistic model (attention, working memory, encoding, long-term storage, retrieval) most often used to operationalize it, and [Cognitive Load Theory](cognitive-load-theory.md) extends it with a working-memory-capacity account of instructional design.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instruction can name and organize its content structure explicitly (hierarchies, categories, relationships) so learners have something to encode it into
- Best suited to content or tasks with identifiable mental structure — concepts, procedures, principles — that can be related to what the learner already knows
#### Constraints
- Because it treats knowledge as something that can be organized and mapped onto the learner, cognitivism gives limited guidance for ill-structured, highly context-dependent problems, where [Constructivism](constructivism.md) offers a better account

### Target Learners
- Learners who already have some relevant prior knowledge to which new information can be connected via analogy, advance organizers, or hierarchical structure
- Novice-to-intermediate learners acquiring rule-, concept-, or procedure-based knowledge, where cognitive task analysis and explicit sequencing pay off

### Target Learning Objectives
- Complex forms of learning that a purely behavioral account struggles to explain: reasoning, problem-solving, classification, rule and procedure application
- Conceptual understanding and transfer that depend on how information is organized in memory rather than only on reinforcement history

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Grew directly out of dissatisfaction with behaviorism's inability to explain higher-level skills (language development, problem solving, inference, critical thinking) (Ertmer & Newby, 2013)
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Constructivism](constructivism.md) rejects cognitivism's assumption that knowledge is mind-independent and can be "mapped" onto a learner, arguing instead that learners construct their own meaning from experience

## Related Theories
- [Behaviorism](behaviorism.md) – the paradigm cognitivism reacted against; both value practice and feedback, but for different reasons (shaping observable responses vs. supporting mental encoding)
- [Constructivism](constructivism.md) – shares cognitivism's view of learning as mental activity but rejects the assumption that a fixed body of knowledge can be transmitted or "mapped" onto the learner
- [Information Processing Theory](information-processing-theory.md) + the specific memory-system model most commonly used to implement cognitivist instructional design
- [Cognitive Load Theory](cognitive-load-theory.md) + a working-memory-capacity account built on cognitivist assumptions about encoding and storage
- [Dual Coding Theory](dual-coding-theory.md) + a cognitivist account of how verbal and nonverbal information are represented in memory
