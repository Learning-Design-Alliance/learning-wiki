Long's interaction hypothesis¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 30 claims (8 for, 21 mixed, 1 against) · 9 studies (6 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 associational), q1–q3 · 2 of 9 report an effect size · 29 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Ellis (eric-ed338037) summarises the interaction hypothesis, which he associates most clearly with Michael Long, as a three-part hierarchy. First, comprehensible input is necessary for L2 acquisition, which is Krashen's input hypothesis. Second, the modifications to conversational structure that happen while speakers negotiate a communication problem make input comprehensible. These are the confirmation checks, clarification requests, comprehension checks and repetitions that Long (1980) found much more often in native/non-native than in native/native conversation. Third, some conditions create more of this negotiation. Two-way tasks that require an exchange of information create more than one-way tasks (Long), and so do partners with equal status despite unequal proficiency (Pica 1987). Long drew teaching proposals from the hypothesis: fewer display questions, more group work, and two-way, closed tasks.

The hypothesis changed over time. By 1989, Ellis reports, Long still held that comprehensible input is necessary but accepted that it may not be sufficient. Drawing on Swain's output hypothesis, he also accepted that interaction helps by pushing learners to modify their output. On this view some moves do more than others: requests for clarification "stretch" the learner, while confirmation checks solve the problem for them. Later accounts put feedback at the centre. Rassaei and Moinzadeh (eric-ej1080713) cite Long (1996) for the view that recasts promote L2 development because they come during meaning-focused activity, giving learners both comprehensible input and a focus on form. Lv and Liu (eric-ej1361438) cite Long (2007) for the view that a recast can lead a learner to notice the gap. Shetye (eric-ej1435677) places the hypothesis inside Gass and Mackey's (2020) "interaction approach", in which negotiation draws learners' attention to input, output and feedback. Altiner (eric-ej1208383) reports Long (1985) as holding that interaction in the L2 is "the obligatory condition" for acquisition.

The articles disagree about how far interaction explains acquisition. Ellis found the most convincing evidence for the third part of the hypothesis, the conditions that produce negotiation. He found weaker evidence that interactionally modified input leads to acquisition, rather than to comprehension. His evidence includes Sato's two learners, who never acquired past-tense marking because interactional support made it unnecessary, and Loschky's comprehension gain with no retention gain. His revision (see Ellis's revised interaction hypothesis) says that interaction makes acquisition possible by helping learners notice features and compare them with their own output, but does not cause it. Ponniah (eric-ej1071042) reports Krashen's opposite view, that negotiating conversation "won't help us to acquire the language".

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Two-way information-exchange tasks, and closed tasks with a limited set of correct solutions, are the task conditions the hypothesis favours (Ellis's account of Long).
  • In EFL peer interaction the amount of negotiation depends on task type and proficiency pairing (eric-ej1269664). Unfocused communicative tasks drew more negotiation than focused ones in one Iranian study (eric-ej1348282).
  • Ellis warns that the quality of modifications may matter more than their number, and that teacher-dominated discourse is not always free of negotiation.

Target Learners¶

  • Second language learners interacting with native speakers, teachers or peers. The fetched studies cover adult and university EFL learners (eric-ej1080713, eric-ej1348282) and primary-age EFL children (eric-ej1269664, eric-ej1361438).
  • Ellis reports that negotiation helped the comprehension of learners rated lower in comprehension ability more than it helped others (Pica 1989).

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Comprehension of L2 input, which is the best-supported link.
  • Grammatical development through interactional feedback such as recasts. Here the fetched studies are quasi-experiments with short delays.

Claims¶

  • Native-speaker conversations with learners show more interactional modification than native-speaker conversations in two-way information-exchange tasks, but not in tasks without information exchange [+M]
  • Interactionally modified input aids comprehension but shows no advantage for retention among beginner learners [~M]
  • Interactionally adjusted and premodified input yield statistically comparable comprehension overall, with negotiation benefiting lower-ability learners [~M]
  • Conversational interaction may facilitate acquisition of some grammatical structures but not others [~M]
  • The quality rather than the quantity of interactional modifications predicts comprehensible input [~M]
  • Comprehensible input alone is insufficient for acquisition of high levels of grammatical proficiency [~M]
  • There is no direct evidence that comprehensible input is necessary for L2 acquisition; the input hypothesis remains untested [-W]
  • Recasts facilitate child EFL learners' acquisition of the third person singular -s verb form in task-based lessons [+M]
  • Metalinguistic feedback's gains on wh-question performance decline over a ten-day delay while recast gains hold, leaving the two no different [+M]
  • Metalinguistic feedback outperforms recasts on the immediate post-test, but the two do not differ significantly on the delayed post-test [~M]
  • Metalinguistic feedback and recasts, but not clarification requests, significantly improve Iranian EFL learners' wh-question performance on an immediate post-test [~M]
  • Higher task complexity is associated with lower written production of the target form among child learners receiving recasts [~M]
  • Mixed proficiency dyads negotiate for meaning more than matched proficiency dyads, irrespective of time and language [~M]
  • EFL primary children's meaning negotiation during a spot-the-differences task is low in amount but significantly higher in the L2 than in the L1 [~M]
  • Unfocused communicative tasks elicit significantly more meaning negotiation than focused tasks among intermediate EFL learners [~M]
  • Task condition (decision-making vs. opinion-exchange) did not significantly moderate the amount of meaning negotiation [~M]
  • In high school groups, learners with negotiation of meaning obtained more comprehensible input in the match task than learners with pre-modified input only [+M]
  • In both high school and college groups, learners with negotiation of meaning scored higher on the delayed post-vocabulary test than learners with pre-modified input only [+M]
  • In high school groups, frequency of negotiation of meaning correlates positively with vocabulary score (r = 0.761) and with comprehensible input (r = 0.310) [+M]
  • In college groups, negotiation of meaning made no difference to comprehensible input obtained in the match task [~M]
  • In college groups, no correlation was found between frequency of negotiation of meaning and vocabulary score [~M]
  • Learner age/level moderates the benefit of negotiation of meaning: high school learners relied on it more than college learners [~M]
  • Non-native speaker dyads provided with subtitled videos before tasks produce a significantly higher percentage of negotiated turns in online TBLL than dyads without videos [~M]
  • Prior research on task familiarity and negotiation of meaning is inconsistent, with some studies finding more negotiation for unfamiliar tasks and one finding no difference [~W]
  • Completing meaning-focused tasks through CMC fosters negotiated interactions in all three modes (discussion board, text chat, videoconferencing) [+M]
  • Closed meaning-focused tasks foster more negotiation of meaning and negative feedback than open tasks in CMC [+M]
  • Videoconferencing was conducive to more negotiation of meaning than text chat and discussion board, measured as proportion of negotiated turns [~M]
  • Discussion board interactions generated no negative feedback at all, suggesting synchrony is required to foster corrective feedback [~M]
  • Unprompted self-correction dominates negative feedback in NNS-NNS CMC interaction, interpreted as a face-saving preference [~M]
  • Content-related problems trigger most negotiation routines, and syntactic triggers are absent from text chat where turns are short [~M]

Examples¶

  • For child beginner language learners, keep task complexity low or moderate so attention remains available for noticing recasts
  • Provide incidental corrective feedback through clarification requests, recasts, and a P.S.
