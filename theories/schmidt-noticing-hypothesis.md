## Description

The articles trace the concept of noticing to Schmidt (1990), who stressed the role of noticing in L2 input for acquisition (eric-ej1155586). As Ellis (eric-ed338037) reports it, Schmidt holds that noticing is necessarily conscious ("you can't learn a foreign language through subliminal perception") and that learners "who notice the most, will learn the most". Conscious noticing is not the same as deliberate attending. It can happen intentionally or by chance, and it does not need focal attention: a learner focused on the message can still pick up striking features at the edge of attention. Schmidt and Frota's (1986) diary study of Schmidt learning Portuguese is the case the articles cite. Schmidt often "noticed the gap" between what he said and what he heard, and forms he noticed and compared later appeared in his own speech.

Other theories took noticing in. Ellis made noticing and comparison the processes that interaction helps with, in his [revision of the interaction hypothesis](noticing-comparison-integration-revised-hypothesis.md). Swain's output hypothesis argues that producing output can also trigger noticing (eric-ej1155586). Research on feedback uses noticing to explain why input-providing feedback such as recasts and explicit correction works: the correct form makes the gap easier to see (Sato, eric-ej1412014, citing Schmidt 1995 and 2001). Vocabulary research treats noticing an unknown word as "the first and important step" in acquiring it from reading (eric-ej1117239, citing Schmidt 1993). Focus-on-form work cites Schmidt (1995) on limited attentional capacity: a learner absorbed in comprehension has little attention left for a new form (eric-ed507438).

The articles also mark the hypothesis's weak points. Barnawi (eric-ej930155) notes that "noticing", "attention", "awareness", "understanding" and "memory" are used almost interchangeably in the L2 literature, which makes studies hard to compare, as Schmidt (1995) himself observed. Geist reports that researchers operationalise noticing in different ways: as language-related episodes in think-aloud protocols, as learners' written notes, or through grammaticality judgements. Noticing is also not the same as uptake. Lv and Liu (eric-ej1361438) cite a study in which learners reported noticing 37% of recasts but took up only 14%.

## Implications

### Context
- Noticing needs attention to spare. The articles tie it to task demands (eric-ed507438, eric-ej1361438) and to the salience of the form: non-salient, redundant forms such as third-person -s are the least likely to be noticed.
- In the writing studies, the classroom routes to noticing are structured comparison tasks: note-taking while writing, comparison with a model or reformulation, and collaborative feedback (eric-ej930155, eric-ej1155586).

### Target Learners
- Second language learners in general. The fetched applications cover EFL college writers (eric-ej930155), German teenage learners of English (eric-ej1155586), primary-age EFL children receiving recasts (eric-ej1361438), and adult learners in the diary study Ellis reports.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Grammatical and lexical accuracy, especially for forms that communication alone leaves unnoticed.
- Vocabulary learned incidentally through reading.
