# Study-Phase Retrieval (Reminding) Account of the Spacing Effect

## Description
Study-phase retrieval accounts hold that a repeated item benefits from spacing because, at the second presentation, the learner retrieves the first one, and that retrieval strengthens memory. Retrieval alone predicts the wrong direction (retrieval is most likely at short gaps), so the accounts that work add that a retrieval helps more the more difficult it was (Bjork, 1988; [Pavlik & Anderson, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0000_14)).

Benjamin and Tullis (2010) set out what any account of distributed practice has to explain, with a meta-analysis of 829 conditions from 72 articles. Performance on twice-studied items rises above the level two independent study events would give (superadditivity), and the lag function is nonmonotonic: benefits rise with the gap and then fall. They argue that Stimulus Sampling Theory, the standard encoding-variability account, cannot produce either pattern, because it explains spacing by growing independence between the presentations. Deficient-processing accounts (inattention to an item repeated soon) explain why massed practice falls short but not why spaced practice exceeds independence. They propose a reminding model on three principles:

1. The first presentation is forgotten over time, following a power law.
2. The second presentation varies in its capacity to remind the learner of the first: a repetition reminds strongly, an associate less, an unrelated item hardly at all.
3. A successful reminding potentiates memory, more when it was difficult (after more forgetting or from a weaker cue).

The model produces superadditive, nonmonotonic lag functions. To capture the interaction of retention interval and lag (the best gap grows with the retention interval), it needed an added assumption: memories that were successfully reminded are forgotten more slowly.

## Implications

### Context
- Repeated study of verbal or pictorial material with a lag between repetitions and a test after a retention interval; Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesise 839 such assessments.
- Expanding schedules (gaps that grow) look better than equal gaps in Cepeda et al.'s synthesis, but they call the conclusion tentative.

### Target Learners
- Learners in the verbal-learning studies the meta-analyses pool, mostly adults.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Retention of repeated material; choosing the lag between repetitions for an intended retention interval.
