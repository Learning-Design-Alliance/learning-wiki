# Ginott's Congruent Communication

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Ginott's (1972) communication skills approach — one of the two humanist traditions in classroom management, alongside [Glasser's Choice Theory](glassers-choice-theory-and-cooperative-learning.md) — rests on a cardinal principle: learners can control their own behavior if teachers allow them to. Teachers foster this self-control by letting students choose how they wish to change their own behavior and how the class will be run, and by helping students express their inner thoughts and feelings through effective communication. In this tradition, communication skills are the primary vehicle for influencing students' self-esteem, which in turn is the primary force underlying acceptable behavior — congruent communication is what promotes that self-esteem.

Teachers communicate congruently, typically during private conferences with a misbehaving student (though it can also happen during whole-class problem-solving), through several specific practices:

- **Express "sane" messages** — messages that communicate a behavior is unacceptable without blaming, scolding, preaching, accusing, demanding, threatening, or humiliating, describing what should be done rather than scolding what was done. Example: "Rosalyn, we are all supposed to be in our seats before the bell rings," not "Rosalyn, you're always gossiping at the doorway and coming late to class."
- **Accept rather than deny feelings** — accepting a student's feelings about their circumstances rather than arguing about them. If a student says "I have no friends," the teacher accepts and identifies with the feeling of isolation ("So, you're feeling that you don't belong to any group") rather than trying to convince the student they've misperceived the situation.
- **Avoid using labels** — avoiding terms like "lazy," "sloppy," or "bad attitude" (and equally, "dedicated," "intelligent," or "perfectionist"), instead describing what the teacher likes or doesn't like about specific things a student does: "You have a lot of erasures and whiteouts on your homework," not "Your homework is sloppy"; "You form your letters correctly," not "You are a good writer."
- **Use praise cautiously** — Ginott holds that many teachers use praise excessively and manipulatively to control behavior rather than to acknowledge genuinely exceptional performance: praising judgmentally ("Horace, you are a good student"), confusing correctness with goodness (calling a student who makes few mistakes a "good child"), praising minimally acceptable behavior as a way of influencing other students ("I like the way Joan is sitting in her seat"), or praising so often the statements lose all significance. Ginott urges reserving praise for exceptional performance and phrasing it to separate the deed from the doer — "That essay showed a great deal of original thought and research," not a judgment of the student as a person.
- **Elicit cooperation** — once a behavioral concern is identified, offering the student alternatives for solving the problem rather than telling them what to do. Ginott's maxim: "Cooperate, don't legislate."
- **Communicate anger through "I-messages," not "you-messages"** — teachers get frustrated too, and Ginott holds they should express that through "I-messages" that focus on the teacher's own feelings about the behavior ("You talked when the guest speaker was lecturing, and I feel very unhappy and embarrassed by that") rather than "you-messages" that accuse and blame ("You were rude to the guest speaker"). I-messages are for use when the teacher owns the problem — is the one who is angry or upset — a distinction [Gordon's Teacher Effectiveness Training](../strategies/teacher-effectiveness-training-conflict-resolution.md) develops further as "problem ownership."

Applied concretely: taking over a disruptive class, Ginott's approach would have the teacher hold an open discussion drawing students' attention to the problem, invite their cooperation in developing mutually agreed-upon rules and consequences, and then hold individual conferences using congruent communication as further problems arise.

## Implications

### Context
- Congruent communication is primarily deployed in one-on-one private conferences with a student who has misbehaved, though whole-class problem-solving discussions use the same techniques
- Depends on a teacher's willingness to describe specific behaviors rather than judge character, and to name their own feelings rather than assign blame

### Target Learners
- Students whose misbehavior is driven at least partly by low self-esteem or unmet needs to be heard and understood, rather than by a need for firm external structure alone

### Target Learning Objectives
- Development of student self-control and self-esteem as the route to acceptable classroom behavior, rather than compliance driven by external reward or punishment
