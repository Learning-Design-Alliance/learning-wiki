# Pragmatism and Progressivism (Educational Philosophy)

## Description
Like Realism, Pragmatism requires empirical observation of the real world; unlike Realism and Idealism, however, that world is not an unchanging whole but is evolving and changing according to how thought is applied to action on a problem. Thought should not merely describe or represent reality — it should be applied practically to problems as they arise, so that the universe is always evolving according to newly applied thoughts turned into action. Pragmatism's founder, Charles Sanders Peirce, held that thought must produce action toward an ever-changing universe.

**Progressivism**, founded by John Dewey, follows an explicit pragmatic ontology: experience is central to explaining the world, and is itself what needs explaining. A learner needs practical experience and uses that experience to find models that fit a given problem or situation; as new experiences and explanations arise, reality evolves to new situations and problems. Educationally, Progressivism focuses on experiential learning and developing the whole child: students learn by doing rather than being lectured to, following something like the scientific method of questioning and searching for answers, working in cooperative or collaborative groups on project-based, expeditionary, problem-based, or service-learning activities, and pursuing their own interests. The teacher is a facilitator rather than the center of the educational process, sharing authority in planning and decision-making with students. Curriculum is usually integrated across content areas rather than siloed by discipline, current events are used to sustain interest in required subject matter, and evaluation relies on projects and portfolios rather than tests alone. Progressivism's stance stands in stark contrast to both Essentialism and Perennialism on nearly every one of these points.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Time and structure for extended, cooperative, project-based work, rather than a schedule built around lecture and discrete tests
- A teacher willing to act as facilitator and share planning authority with students, rather than remaining the sole source and arbiter of content
#### Constraints
- Integrated, project-based curricula can be harder to align cleanly to discrete, disciplinary content standards than a subject-siloed approach

### Target Learners
- Learners who benefit from hands-on, real-world problem work over lecture-based instruction

### Target Learning Objectives
- Practical competence in applying thought to real, evolving problems, rather than mastery of a fixed body of facts or ideas
