# The von Restorff effect in text marking

## Description
The von Restorff effect (also called the isolation effect) is the finding that an item that stands out from its surroundings — through distinctiveness in color, size, or form — is remembered better than items that blend in. Hedwig von Restorff first demonstrated the effect in 1933: when one item in a homogeneous list was made distinctive, recall for that item improved substantially. Applied to text marking, the effect explains how underlining and highlighting function: marked text is isolated against an unmarked background, and readers "tend to study and remember that information better than nonunderlined information."

The mechanism is attentional and encoding-based. Marking increases the probability that the isolated information is attended to during study and receives deeper processing at encoding. Crucially, the effect is relative, not absolute: marking does not add capacity, it reallocates it. What gains salience does so at the expense of the surrounding text, which becomes the "background" and is studied less.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies to any study or instructional material where learners select a subset of information for emphasis — textbooks, lecture slides, study guides, online course pages
- The effect requires a homogeneous background: if everything is marked, nothing is isolated, and the advantage disappears [~M]
- The effect's utility depends on whether the marked information is important or unimportant: marking high-relevance content improves test performance on that content, while marking low-relevance content can actively harm learning of the unmarked but important material [~M]
- Learner-generated marking (students underlining as they read) is a distinct case from experimenter-provided marking; self-generated marking functions partly as an active-selection strategy rather than a pure isolation effect [~W]

### Target Learners
- College freshmen and other novice readers of academic text, the population in which underlining effects were first studied systematically
- Less skilled readers benefit more from externally provided marking, because they are poorer at spontaneously distinguishing important from unimportant information [~M]
- Skilled readers may gain little from provided marking and may benefit more from generating their own selections

### Target Learning Objectives
- Recall and recognition of specific, discrete facts and key terms
- Text comprehension, when marking is restricted to high-relevance content
- Not well suited to integrative or inferential objectives: isolation emphasizes fragments, not relationships between them

## Claims

- [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+W] — the benefit of marking depends on whether the emphasized information is actually the important information; marking irrelevant content misdirects attention away from what matters
- [Irrelevant material hurts learning](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+S] — the complement of the isolation effect: unmarked background text is processed less, so marking the wrong material effectively deletes it from study
- [Highlighting and underlining are low-utility study strategies](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-M] — Dunlosky et al.'s review rated highlighting as low utility: it reliably improves performance on marked content but shows little benefit or even costs for unmarked content and for transfer

## Related Theories

- [Cognitive Load Theory](cognitive-load-theory.md) — marking reallocates limited attentional and working-memory resources; selective emphasis is a load-management device, but over-marking or mis-marking creates a redundancy/salience problem rather than solving one
- [Information Processing Theory](information-processing-theory.md) — the von Restorff effect is classically explained as an encoding phenomenon: distinctiveness increases the depth and distinctiveness of encoding, improving retrieval
- [Dual Coding Theory](dual-coding-theory.md) — visual marking (color, bolding) adds a nonverbal code to verbal content, potentially providing a second retrieval route for marked items

## Examples

- **[Headings and highlight strategy](../strategies/"headings_and_highlight"_strategy.md)** — applies the isolation effect by pairing structural cues (headings) with selective highlighting, constraining what learners mark to high-relevance content
- **[Attention](../elements/attention.md)** — marking is fundamentally an attention-direction device; the von Restorff effect describes the payoff of successfully capturing and allocating attention during study
- **[Advance organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)** — like marking, organizers pre-select what matters in a text; both work by biasing attention toward high-relevance information before or during reading
