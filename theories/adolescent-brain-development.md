# Adolescent Brain Development

## Description
The adolescent brain undergoes substantial restructuring between roughly ages 10 and 25, and the *sequence* of that restructuring — not just its extent — has direct implications for how adolescents learn, take risks, and regulate emotion. The **limbic system**, which governs reward-seeking and emotional response, matures years ahead of the **prefrontal cortex**, which governs executive functions such as impulse control, planning, emotion regulation, and sustained attention. Steinberg (2008) describes this as "a powerful engine before the braking system is in place" — adolescents have an adult-level capacity for reward-seeking and emotional intensity well before they have an adult-level capacity to regulate it.

Two neurotransmitter shifts accompany this restructuring: **dopamine** activity increases, heightening sensitivity to reward and novelty, while **serotonin** activity decreases, reducing its normal calming effect. Adolescents also show a natural shift in **circadian rhythm** — a later sleep onset and later natural wake time — that is biological, not merely behavioral, and that runs against typical early school start times, with direct consequences for daytime alertness and learning readiness. Neuroplasticity remains relatively high throughout adolescence compared to adulthood, meaning the period is simultaneously one of heightened vulnerability (to risk, substance exposure, stress) and heightened capacity for skill acquisition and identity formation.

Executive functions — impulse control, cognitive flexibility, working memory, planning, and sustained attention — do not reach adult maturity until the mid-20s, well after most other physical and cognitive milestones. This has direct instructional implications: adolescents are not simply "small adults" with fully formed self-regulation who occasionally choose poorly: the neural circuitry for regulating impulses is still under construction throughout secondary and much of postsecondary education.

## Implications

### Context
- Instruction that assumes adult-level impulse control, planning, or emotion regulation in adolescents will be miscalibrated for a meaningful share of the population, even among high-achieving students
- Reward-sensitive, novelty-seeking behavior is a predictable feature of this developmental period, not a discipline problem to be reasoned away
- Later natural sleep/wake cycles mean early class start times work against, rather than with, adolescent alertness

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults roughly ages 10–25, spanning middle school through early postsecondary education
- Particularly relevant for designing instruction that depends on sustained self-regulation, delayed gratification, or long time horizons

### Target Learning Objectives
- Not a content-objective framework — it informs *how* to scaffold objectives (e.g., building in external structure for planning and impulse control) rather than what the objectives should be
- Especially relevant to objectives involving self-directed work, long-term projects, or behavior/conduct expectations
