# Ecological Paradigm of Interdisciplinary Learning

## Description
The ecological paradigm contrasts with the field's dominant **cognitivist paradigm** of interdisciplinary learning (IDL) research, in which success is measured by an individual's cognitive integration of disciplinary insights (groundedness, integration, reflectiveness — Boix Mansilla & Duraisingh's three dimensions). The ecological paradigm instead studies IDL as a **group-level, socio-material process**: learners are treated as agents who interact with peers, artifacts, communities, institutions, and — depending on the activity — the natural environment, and success is measured by the interdisciplinary *practices* a group develops (epistemic practices, identity development, authentic research productivity), not only by what settles in an individual mind afterward.

This paradigm organizes design guidance around three recurring dilemmas, each with concrete design responses observed across a cluster of empirical studies:

1. **Content dilemma** (how to give learners access to disciplinary knowledge before integration is possible): resolved either through a **"sandwich" sequence** (cross-cutting theme → disciplinary "deepening" activities → integrative synthesis, repeated in short cycles for younger learners) or a **"human capital" approach** (rely on learners' own disciplinary networks and invited experts rather than pre-designed disciplinary content, more feasible in higher education).
2. **Pedagogy dilemma** (how to support the fine-grained social process of diverse learners actually integrating ideas together): resolved through simulations and role-play, a two-layer scaffold (first building each learner's awareness of their own epistemic strengths/gaps, then supporting the group in weaving perspectives together), and treating co-created artifacts as visible objects the group can negotiate and challenge.
3. **Organization dilemma** (how to build on, but also push past, existing institutional infrastructure): resolved through **infrastructuring** — bringing in collaborators from outside the course or institution, and in some cases negotiating entirely new infrastructure (e.g., an out-of-school program) when existing school structures cannot accommodate interdisciplinary work.

## Implications

### Context
- Applies to any interdisciplinary learning design question, but is most useful where the goal is understanding or designing group-level integration processes, not just measuring an individual's post-hoc disciplinary synthesis
- Most relevant where learners bring heterogeneous disciplinary backgrounds, expertise levels, or institutional homes into a shared activity

### Target Learners
- Groups of learners (K-12 through higher education and professional contexts) with differing disciplinary backgrounds, expertise, or institutional affiliations working toward a shared interdisciplinary outcome

### Target Learning Objectives
- Developing interdisciplinary practices (negotiating shared understanding, co-constructing artifacts, integrating knowledge across boundaries) rather than only individual cognitive synthesis
- Designing learning environments and infrastructures that make such group-level integration possible
