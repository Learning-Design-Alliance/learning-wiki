## Description

The articles present Krashen's theory as five hypotheses. The **acquisition-learning hypothesis** separates two ways of developing a second language: acquisition, a subconscious process like a child's first-language development, and learning, the explicit presentation of rules with error correction. The **natural order hypothesis** holds that grammatical structures are acquired in a predictable order. The **monitor hypothesis** makes acquisition the more important process, since it builds fluency, and gives conscious learning only the job of an editor, or monitor. Adamson's account adds that learned rules can change output only when there is enough time, as on a written test. The **input hypothesis** holds that learners acquire by understanding input that contains structures "a bit beyond" their current level (i+1). They go "for meaning" first and acquire structure as a result. Speech then emerges by itself after a silent period, and input should come from real communication rather than a grammatical sequence. The **affective filter hypothesis** holds that anxiety, low self-confidence and low motivation act as a mental block that stops learners from making full use of input.

The articles do not agree on what the monitor allows. Diaz reads the monitor as the heart of the theory and concludes that formal learning "has a role to play" in performance. Calderon's training packet cites Krashen for the view that repetitive drill and a focus on grammar are ineffective. Ponniah (eric-ej1071042) defends the later "Comprehension Hypothesis" as the strongest account in SLA. He reports Krashen's view that negotiating conversations "won't help us to acquire the language", that output and skill-building accounts wrongly make conscious knowledge necessary, and that vocabulary grows by i+1 through reading. The incidental-vocabulary review (eric-ej1051487) likewise cites Krashen (1989) for vocabulary acquired from comprehensible input, but notes researchers who argue that learners must attend to words.

The critiques are sharper than the defences. Adamson argues that the absolute split between conscious and unconscious monitoring is unnatural, and that Labov's continuum of attention fits the variation data better. Nunan's paper (eric-ed367161) quotes Gregg (1986) as the most trenchant, "as yet unanswered" critic of *The Input Hypothesis*. Ellis (eric-ed338037) reports that the input hypothesis "has not been proven", since no direct test had been run. He also reports that French immersion and intensive communicative programmes supplied plenty of comprehensible input and still produced low grammatical accuracy. Swain's output hypothesis was proposed against that result (eric-ej1095572, eric-ej420159). DeKeyser (eric-ej912917) cites Krashen's claim that "learning does not become acquisition" as part of the case made against practice, and argues for a role for practice.

## Implications

### Context
- Calderon's packet draws three programme requirements from the hypotheses: provide comprehensible input in the weaker language, maintain subject matter, and maintain the first language.
- Cheung (eric-ed512082) cites Krashen for delaying oral production: early emphasis on speaking takes time away from input, and forcing speech before learners have internalised the rules creates anxiety and errors.
- A monitor needs time to work, so learned rules show most in edited writing and tests, and least in conversation (Adamson).

### Target Learners
- Second language learners in general. The articles apply the theory to children in bilingual programmes (Calderon), to L2 writers (Diaz), and to adult ESL and EFL learners (Adamson, Cheung).
- Advanced adult learners are the case the critics raise most often. A diary study of one advanced adult learner (eric-ej420159) and the immersion findings both describe learners who received plenty of input and still did not reach native-like accuracy.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Fluent, meaning-focused use of a second language, and vocabulary grown by reading.
- The critiques all concern grammatical accuracy, the objective the input account is said to reach least well.
