## Description

Skill acquisition theory treats learning a second language like learning other complex cognitive skills, such as chess or geometry, driven by general learning mechanisms (Wedlock & Binnie, eric-ej1488626). It has three main concepts: declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge and automatization. Declarative knowledge ("knowing what") becomes procedural knowledge ("knowing how") through repeated, meaningful practice, especially in goal-oriented, structured tasks. With further practice, fine-tuning and feedback, procedural knowledge can become automatized: retrieved rapidly and used with minimal cognitive resources (eric-ej1488626, citing DeKeyser 1997, 2007 and 2020). The psychological source is Anderson's ACT* model. Aydinbek (eric-ej1344843) describes it as the most widely accepted account of how automaticity comes about: explicit "knowledge that" is turned into specialised procedural rules, "knowledge how", by analogy with examples. This change is called proceduralization. The rules are then fine-tuned, so reaction time and error rate gradually drop. Anderson also holds that declarative and procedural knowledge can coexist.

DeKeyser (eric-ej912917) says the theory is often misunderstood as one kind of knowledge *turning into* another. He argues instead that declarative knowledge plays a *causal role* in developing procedural knowledge. On his account, retrieving knowledge during language processing is a complex skill that needs much practice. This holds for the lexicon as well as grammar, whether automatization is seen as more fluid rule use or, following Logan's instance theory, as memory retrieval gradually replacing rules. Automatization is at best partial. Forms that are rare, not salient, or carry meanings the L1 does not mark (English plural marking for speakers of Chinese, Japanese or Korean, for example) need "a very large amount of practice" to proceduralize. Practice here does not mean mechanical drill. DeKeyser (2007) defines it as activities "engaged in systematically, deliberately, with the goal of developing knowledge of and skills in the second language" (eric-ej1488626). He also separates mechanical, meaningful and communicative drills, and only the last two require learners to process meaning. The theory predicts that practice is skill-specific. In DeKeyser's (1997) artificial-language study, comprehension practice improved comprehension and production practice improved production (reported in eric-ej1344843).

Sato (eric-ej1412014) links corrective feedback to the theory through Lyster and Sato (2013). Metalinguistic prompts give learners declarative knowledge and a chance to use it in their next utterance, so they may encourage more proceduralization than feedback that supplies the correct form, such as recasts. Related cognitive accounts make the same general argument without DeKeyser's terms. McLaughlin's (1987) cognitive theory, which Khatib and Fat'hi apply to L2 reading (eric-ej1066572), holds that practising component subskills until they are automatic frees controlled processing for comprehension. Zaccaron's task-repetition paper (eric-ed604148) rests on the same idea: repeated practice turns monitored, effortful processes into automatic ones. Robinson (eric-ed296585) uses "declarative" and "procedural" in a different, Widdowsonian sense. For him the terms mean knowing *that* a word has a meaning and knowing *how* to realise that meaning in discourse. That is a distinction about lexical competence, not a claim about how knowledge develops.

The articles do not agree. VanPatten (eric-ej912912) argues that applying skill theory to grammar confuses two different things. On his account, grammar is abstract, implicit mental representation, not a skill. The rules skill research trains, including those of DeKeyser's (1997) miniature language, are teaching shorthand that does not exist in learners' heads. He also argues that skill theory cannot explain two findings. First, learners acquire syntax they were never taught while staying insensitive to agreement they were taught and practised. Second, output develops in stages, as Processability Theory describes. Ellis (1993), as DeKeyser reports it, takes a middle position: practice focused on forms can automatize existing knowledge but cannot create new grammar. Sato's own results are mixed. Procedural memory predicted gains under metalinguistic prompts only on a measure of declarative knowledge, and the treatment was too short for much proceduralization.

## Implications

### Context
- DeKeyser (eric-ej912917) sequences activities so that declarative knowledge comes first, followed by proceduralization and at least partial automatization. He also notes that explicit knowledge is best retained when it answers questions arising from communicative interaction.
- Practice should involve form–meaning links and can take many forms: communicative drills, role plays, task-based and content-based teaching (eric-ej912917, eric-ej1488626). Aydinbek (eric-ej1344843) reports DeKeyser's (2003) two-phase proposal, in which a communicative main activity creates a need to repeat target utterances and a more controlled, still communicative, follow-up comes after it.
- Because practice is skill-specific, learners should practise the skill they need, whether comprehension or production (eric-ej1344843).
- The benefit of a type of practice depends on learners' aptitudes, age and cultural background, and DeKeyser notes that research on this is scarce (eric-ej912917).

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult second and foreign language learners who can use explicit, declarative instruction. The fetched applications cover Japanese university EFL learners (eric-ej1412014) and Iranian ESP undergraduates (eric-ej1066572).

### Target Learning Objectives
- Speed and accuracy in using L2 forms already known explicitly (fluency and accuracy in production and comprehension).
- Proceduralizing form–meaning mappings for low-salience or low-frequency features.
