Intelligence Testing: Uses and Limits¶

Theory · All theories
Evidence · 39 claims (17 for, 20 mixed, 2 against) · 29 studies (17 associational, 5 review, 4 theoretical, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 design), q1–q3 · 1 of 29 report an effect size · 38 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The first intelligence test, the Binet-Simon test, grew out of Alfred Binet's 1904 appointment as France's Minister of Public Education, where he sought a way to identify children with developmental disabilities so they could receive appropriate support — Binet believed "abnormality" could be remediated, and strongly supported special education on that basis. It was the first test to distinguish "normal" children from those with developmental disabilities, and its later revision became the Stanford-Binet, one of the most widely used intelligence tests (Zimmerman & Schunk, 2003). Binet's original purpose — identifying students who need support — sits in some tension with how intelligence-test scores are more often used today, as a general ranking or classification device (see Constraints below).

Most intelligence tests aim to measure "g," the general intelligence factor, and are judged by two standards: reliability (consistency over repeated administration) and validity (actually measuring intelligence rather than something else). Because what counts as an "intelligent" answer changes with age (a 3-year-old multiplying two two-digit numbers is precocious; a 25-year-old unable to do so is not average), interpreting a raw score requires standardization — testing a large, representative sample at each age level to establish population norms. Comparing a person's performance to these age norms yields their mental age; comparing mental age to chronological age yields the classic intelligence quotient (IQ) formula (mental age ÷ chronological age × 100), though most modern tests instead rank a person's raw score against same-age peers directly rather than computing this ratio literally.

Because population-wide intelligence-test performance drifts upward over time (see the Flynn effect [~M]), tests must be periodically re-standardized or their norms become stale and systematically overstate test-takers' relative intelligence. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is the most widely used adult intelligence test; its current version, the WAIS-IV, was standardized on 2,200 people aged 16-90, spans 15 tasks across four domains (verbal, perceptual, working memory, processing speed), and has very high reliability (>0.95) along with substantial correlations to real-world criteria — college grades, work performance, occupational level, and everyday functioning among people with intellectual disabilities. Downward and upward age-range adaptations exist for preschoolers (WPPSI-IV) and school-age children/adolescents (WISC-V).

Despite this psychometric strength, intelligence tests and the definitions of intelligence behind them have been heavily criticized since the 1970s on two related grounds. First, they have tended to favor Anglo-American, middle-class test-takers and to poorly capture non-academic forms of ability or talent. Second, what counts as "smart" is itself culturally variable in ways the tests don't accommodate: in dominant Western culture, speed is often equated with intelligence (the fastest correct answer is seen as smartest), whereas other cultures instead value a slower, more contemplative, thoroughly-considered answer as the mark of intelligence. A test built around one of these cultural assumptions will systematically undervalue test-takers operating under the other.

Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Periodic re-standardization of any intelligence or ability test against a current, representative population sample
  • Awareness of what a given test's format actually rewards (e.g., speed) versus what "intelligent" behavior looks like in a test-taker's own cultural context

Constraints¶

  • Contemporary use for general ranking or classification departs from Binet's own founding purpose of identifying students who need additional support, not of sorting students overall
  • Norms decay: a test standardized years or decades ago overstates the relative standing of current test-takers, since the population's average performance rises over time [~M]
  • IQ scores do not reflect a person's ability to change — a test-blind spot with real consequences for how the resulting number gets used or interpreted downstream
  • Tests calibrated to a speed-favoring or Anglo-American, middle-class conception of "smart" undervalue test-takers whose cultural background favors contemplative reasoning or values non-academic forms of ability

Target Learners¶

  • Learners being formally assessed for eligibility decisions (giftedness, intellectual disability classification) where the underlying test's cultural assumptions and standardization currency directly affect the decision
  • Learners from cultural backgrounds whose implicit definition of "smart" diverges from the speed-and-analytic-reasoning framing built into most standardized instruments

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Accurate, fair identification of a learner's actual capability, distinct from how well they happen to match a test's built-in cultural and format assumptions

Theory¶

Supporting¶

  • Triarchic Theory of Intelligence [+M] — analytical-intelligence-focused standardized tests are exactly the instrument type Sternberg argues fails to capture creative or practical intelligence

Contradicting / Qualifying¶

  • Multiple Intelligences Theory [~M] — if intelligence is genuinely multi-component, a single g-focused score cannot represent a learner's full range of ability

Claims¶

  • Average scores on intelligence tests have risen substantially across generations worldwide [~M] — the basis for the requirement that tests be periodically re-standardized
  • Teacher reports of noncognitive skills add predictive power beyond baseline IQ for most school outcomes [~W]
  • Test scores measure accumulated knowledge at a moment in time, not innate student ability [+W]
  • Children with ADHD perform worse than controls on WISC-IV Coding but not on Coding Copy, indicating deficits beyond graphomotor speed [~W]
  • Curve estimation shows linear decline in processing speed after age 30, quadratic trajectories for category fluency and IQ, and no age change in letter fluency [~W]
  • The set of executive-function and intelligence predictors of academic achievement differs across Grades 2, 4, and 6, with EF contributions appearing cumulative as students get older [~W]
  • Full-scale IQ is genetically related but not redundant with common cognitive variance [~W]
  • In predicting the change in achievement from 3rd to 4th grade, IQ is positively and study habits negatively associated with change, together predicting 12% [+W]
  • IQ, short-term memory and study habits each correlate positively and significantly with academic achievement in both 3rd and 4th grade [+W]
  • Full-scale IQ predicts reading comprehension more strongly than word reading, and working memory and processing speed remain significant predictors after IQ is controlled [+W]
  • High performance in old age is associated with higher education, foreign language use and IQ, but not physical activity or personality [+W]
  • Complexity of children's naturalistic memory reports predicts reading ability over and above IQ [+W]
  • When previous academic achievement is controlled, intelligence predicts only later reading comprehension and age-10 mathematics, and WMC predicts nothing additionally [+W]
  • Processing speed has a significant effect on both letter and category fluency in older adults, while IQ does not [~W]
  • In latent-variable SEM models, intelligence — not working memory — is the significant direct predictor of academic outcomes in both childhood and early adulthood [+W]
  • Specific processing speed measured at the start of Grade 7 predicts mathematics achievement on the high school entrance examination at the end of Grade 9 [+W]
  • Specific processing speed generalises beyond mathematics, significantly predicting Chinese and English achievement in both Grade 7 and Grade 9 [+W]
  • Fluid reasoning and self-control jointly explain far more variance in standardized test scores than in grades [+W]
  • Males show a statistically significant but practically negligible advantage on WISC Full-Scale IQ (d = 0.09), which becomes non-significant with newer WISC versions [~W]
  • Gender differences in fluid intelligence (gF) on the WISC are negligible, and newer fluid-reasoning subtests show no difference or favour females [~W]
  • Instrument type moderated the mental rotation gender-difference effect size but not the visualisation effect size [~W]
  • Cross-cultural studies show Eastern children can outperform Western ones on creativity measures, and children with little schooling can show high divergent thinking [+W]
  • Published form-board age norms differ markedly across investigators, yielding discrepant diagnoses [~W]
  • Group scales allow examining fifty or a hundred children in an hour, whereas individual Binet testing restricts use to special cases [~W]
  • The editor predicts NRC group tests will make intelligence testing a routine school measure and open the way for adjusting school work to pupil needs [~W]
  • The Binet-Simon scale measured intelligence development from ages seven to eleven with at least 96 per cent efficiency under the study's conditions [+W]
  • The twelve-year tests of the Binet-Simon scale were unsatisfactory, with several individual tests too difficult for the children tested [-W]
  • The Binet-Simon scale accurately graded fairly minute individual differences in mental ability within same-age groups, as validated against teacher and principal judgments [+W]
  • Yerkes-Bridges coefficients of intelligence of 1.20 or more are attained by very different percentages of children at successive ages (34.5% at ages 4-5 down to 0% at ages 16-17 and 18-on), so they do not represent the same degree of brightness at all ages. [~W]
  • Summing all test scores into one general-intelligence rating manufactures the appearance of a general factor [~W]
  • Verbal test segments correlate more highly with the composite intelligence criterion than non-verbal segments [+W]
  • In the Yerkes-Bridges alteration of the Binet Scale, equal point increments are assigned to digit-span steps whose growth intervals, as placed in the Binet Scale, are approximately 1, 3, 3, and 4 years. [~W]
  • Exceptional Binet-Simon test outcomes were rare (4 per cent) and mostly attributable to error in adult judgments rather than the scale [+W]
  • Correlation magnitude rises with the standard deviation of mental age of the group, so cross-group comparisons of the two traits are spurious [~W]
  • Arbitrarily assigned point values in the Yerkes-Bridges scale yield implausible growth-rate results, such as intellectual ability increasing more than fifty per cent during the fourth year and only approximately fifteen per cent during the seventh. [-W]
  • The low Alpha–school-standing correlation is difficult to explain and investigators report conflicting correlation results [~W]
  • Pupils characteristically 'Bright' or 'Slow' in the early elementary grades remained in the same category in high school, in both graduate and non-graduate groups [~W]
  • Easy opposites scores correlate with scholastic rank, faculty and graduates outscoring seniors and juniors [+W]
  • Binet's norms were worked out for Paris working-class children and may need revision for other social classes and regions [~W]

Related Principles¶

  • Supporting Gifted and Talented Students — giftedness classification is conventionally IQ-threshold-based, inheriting all of this principle's measurement caveats
  • Supporting Students with Intellectual Disabilities — intellectual disability classification likewise depends on IQ scores read alongside adaptive functioning, precisely because IQ alone is an incomplete and sometimes biased measure
  • Criterion- and Norm-Referenced Testing — tests historically called "intelligence tests" are now more often called learning-ability, cognitive-ability, or scholastic-aptitude tests, a terminology shift reflecting the same controversy this page describes over whether "intelligence" implies fixed, inherited capacity rather than developed ability (Linn & Miller, 2005)
  • Standardized Test Fairness and Bias — a parallel treatment of cultural and content bias in achievement and admissions tests specifically, distinct from but related to the IQ-test bias described here

Examples¶

  • Follow the French two-stage placement plan: pedagogical examination first, then mental tests for equal mental retardation
