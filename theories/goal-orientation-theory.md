## Description
Goal setting is itself a key motivational process (Locke & Latham, 1984, 2006) — the goals learners choose, and how committed they are to attaining them, shape motivation to learn (Wentzel, 2000). But beyond goal *content* (what a learner wants to achieve), goal orientation theory argues that the *reason* a learner pursues a goal has its own independent effect on learning and performance, explaining differences in how individuals approach and respond to achievement situations (Ames & Archer, 1988; Meece, Anderman, & Anderman, 2006).

The two most basic goal orientations are **mastery goals** (a focus on mastering new skills, gaining understanding, and improving competence, defining success as improvement and learning) and **performance goals** (a focus on doing better than others and demonstrating competence via social comparison, seeking favorable judgment) (Ames & Archer, 1988; [Dweck & Leggett, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.95.2.256)). The same distinction appears under other names in the literature: learning vs. performance goals (Elliot & Dweck, 1988), task-involved vs. ego-involved goals (Nicholls, 1984), and task-focused vs. ability-focused goals (Maehr & Midgley, 1991).

Both categories further split along an **approach/avoidance** dimension (Elliot & Church, 1997; Elliot & Harackiewicz, 1996; Elliot & McGregor, 2001), producing a 2×2 model:

| | Approach focus | Avoidance focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Mastery** | Improve knowledge/skills; errors are learning opportunities; judge performance against self-improvement standards | Avoid misunderstanding or failing to master the task; errors indicate failure; judge performance against a "not being wrong" standard |
| **Performance** | Outperform others, demonstrate superiority; judge performance against normative "being the best" standards | Avoid negative judgment or appearing inferior; errors indicate failure; judge performance against a "not being the worst" standard |

Mastery-avoidance is the least-studied and most contested of the four cells — despite confirmatory factor-analytic support for the full 2×2 structure (Elliot & McGregor, 2001), it remains the least accepted construct in the field, illustrated by cases like an athlete anxious specifically about falling short of their *own* past performance.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- An achievement context where a learner's implicit reason for pursuing a goal (mastery vs. demonstrating/protecting standing) can plausibly be shaped by classroom structure, framing, and feedback
#### Constraints
- Goal orientations rarely occur in pure form; a single learner typically holds a blend (e.g., enjoying an activity for its own sake while also wanting to look skilled to peers and fearing embarrassment), so intervention should shift the *balance* among orientations rather than assume a learner has exactly one
- Some degree of performance orientation is likely inevitable simply from the presence of classmates and social comparison, and cannot be fully eliminated by design
- Mastery-avoidance remains a contested, under-validated construct; treat claims specific to that cell with more caution than the other three

### Target Learners
- Any learner in an evaluated, socially comparative setting, since goal orientation concerns the reason behind engagement rather than a fixed trait tied to one population
- Learners showing signs of failure-avoidant self-handicapping (procrastination, deliberate under-effort, unrealistically high goals) — a byproduct of excessive performance-goal emphasis (Urdan, 2004) worth diagnosing and addressing directly

### Target Learning Objectives
- Deep, thoughtful processing and durable, transferable learning (favored by mastery-approach orientation)
- Short-term performance metrics like grades and test scores (favored, though sometimes at the expense of deep learning, by performance orientations)
- Reduced anxiety and self-handicapping behavior (favored by avoiding performance-avoidance and failure-avoidant orientations)

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Elliot & McGregor's (2001) confirmatory factor analyses support treating the 2×2 mastery/performance × approach/avoidance grid as measuring four genuinely distinct orientations, not just two
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- The mastery-avoidance cell remains the field's least accepted construct, weakening the 2×2 model's claim to being fully validated

Empirically, the four orientations diverge sharply in outcome. **Mastery-approach** goals are consistently associated with high effort, task interest, and use of deep learning strategies (Greene, Miller, Crowson, Duke, & Akey, 2004; Harackiewicz, Barron, Pintrich, Elliot, & Thrash, 2002; Wolters, 2004), and — because they are a form of intrinsic motivation — sustain interest in a subject well beyond a course's official end, predicting further enrollment in the same subject area. **Performance-avoidance** goals are consistently linked to poor persistence, high anxiety, superficial strategy use, and low achievement (Linnenbrink, 2005; Urdan, 2004; Wolters, 2003, 2004) — the most reliably detrimental of the four. **Performance-approach** goals show genuinely mixed results: some studies find modest positive links to achievement (Linnenbrink-Garcia, Tyson, & Patall, 2008), others find maladaptive outcomes like poor strategy use and test anxiety (Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Keys, Conley, Duncan, & Domina, 2012; Middleton & Midgley, 1997) — taken together, performance-approach students tend to achieve well but at the cost of negative emotionality such as test anxiety, and though they often get higher grades than mastery-oriented peers (both short-term and cumulatively), they tend not to learn the material as deeply or durably (Midgley, Kaplan, & Middleton, 2001), plausibly because performance metrics reward shallow memorization and because the competitive framing of performance goals discourages the peer help-seeking and help-giving that supports deeper learning. **Mastery-avoidance** goals are the least-studied orientation, but existing evidence links them to increased anxiety and reduced performance (Howell & Watson, 2007; Hulleman, Schrager, Bodmann, & Harackiewicz, 2010).

Two further goal categories affect achievement indirectly rather than being achievement goals themselves. **Failure-avoidant goals** are often a negative byproduct of performance-goal competitiveness (Urdan, 2004): when success feels unattainable or interest in the material itself has been crowded out, a student may shift to simply avoiding failure, deliberately underachieving via self-handicapping (procrastination, minimal effort, unrealistically high goals chosen specifically to make failure unsurprising rather than shameful). **Social goals** — most students value relationships with teachers and peers — cut both ways: a positive teacher relationship tends to motivate hard work, though this effect is closer to performance than mastery motivation since the student is primarily concerned with pleasing someone else (Dowson & McInerney, 2003); peer relationships help achievement when the underlying motive is genuine closeness (leading to mutual help-seeking and help-giving) but can hurt it when the motive is impressing peers, which reintroduces performance-goal competitiveness and discourages collaboration. Peer effects are also context-dependent: low peer achievement/motivation affects an individual's motivation more in elementary than high school, more in math than reading, and more in classrooms with a wide ability range than a narrow one (Burke & Sass, 2006). Structured cooperative or community-connected activities (Butin, 2005) can help realize the positive side of social goals while limiting the competitive downside.

Because mastery-approach goals reliably support the more sustained, thoughtful learning teachers usually want (Darnon, Butera, & Harackiewicz, 2006), several concrete tactics can shift the classroom balance toward it: offering students choice over specific tasks where feasible, since self-chosen work more often connects to genuine prior interest; explicitly pointing out connections between current material and students' existing interests when those connections aren't obvious on their own (e.g., linking a music-loving student's interest to acoustics in physics, hearing physiology in biology, or stylistic change over time in history); focusing feedback and structure on individual improvement rather than social comparison; and arranging collaboration rather than competition on shared tasks.

## Related Theories
- [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md) — task value and goal orientation both address why a learner engages, though EVT frames it as a value judgment while goal orientation frames it as the purpose behind pursuing a goal
- [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) — mastery goals are intrinsically motivated in SDT's sense, while performance goals are extrinsically motivated; both theories treat this distinction as consequential for depth of learning
- [Student Orientation Toward Achievement](student-orientation-toward-achievement.md) — Covington's self-worth-protection typology (success-oriented, overstriving, failure-avoiding, failure-accepting) describes a closely parallel set of learner profiles organized around approaching success versus avoiding failure
- [Attribution Theory](attribution-theory.md) — a mastery orientation's tolerance for errors as learning opportunities parallels attribution theory's preference for controllable, unstable failure attributions over stable, uncontrollable ones
