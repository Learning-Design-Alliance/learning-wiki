# Critical Thinking

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Critical thinking requires both a skill (analyzing the reliability and validity of information) and a disposition to actually apply that skill, in principle across any realm of knowledge, not only a single subject or topic (Halpern, 2003; Williams, Oliver, & Stockade, 2004). A critical thinker is not simply someone with a negative or fault-finding attitude; rather, the critical thinker asks key questions, evaluates evidence for ideas, reasons about problems logically and objectively, and expresses ideas and conclusions clearly and precisely — and can carry these habits of mind across more than one domain of life or knowledge.

What makes thinking "critical," rather than merely correct, is largely [metacognition](metacognition.md) — strategies for thinking about one's own thinking, and for monitoring the success and quality of that thinking. In fostering critical thinking, a teacher is fostering a student's ability to construct and control their own thinking, rather than being controlled by ideas unreflectively.

Two studies illustrate concretely different routes to critical thinking. In one, students were taught to **annotate** readings — writing questions and comments in the margins rather than simply underlining passages; students who actually used annotation more heavily produced essays that were later judged better-reasoned and more critically astute than essays from students who annotated less (Liu, 2006; see [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)). In another, students verbally described a recent, personal, disturbing incident, and classmates discussed it together to identify the precise reasons the incident was disturbing and the assumptions underlying the original student's account; one student's account of being snubbed by a store clerk led the group to surface an unstated assumption (suspected racial profiling), which the student then used as the basis for a research essay (Hawkins, 2006; see [Socratic Seminar](../patterns/socratic-seminar.md)). Both studies illustrate that critical thinking can be stimulated through quite different instructional means — written annotation versus oral group discussion — while both ultimately rely on, and further build, the learner's own prior critical-thinking skill.

### How to Teach It, and to Whom
How best to teach critical thinking remains debated along two axes. First, whether to **infuse** critical-thinking skills into existing subject courses, or teach them through **free-standing** dedicated units. Infusion has the potential advantage of integrating critical thinking across a student's whole education, but risks diluting understanding because critical thinking takes a different form in each learning context; the free-standing approach stands a better chance of being understood clearly and coherently, at the cost of obscuring how it relates to other courses and tasks — essentially the same dilemma posed by [transfer of learning](../principles/transfer-of-learning.md). Research comparing the two approaches does not settle the matter; either can work as long as it is implemented thoroughly and the teacher is genuinely committed to critical thinking's value (Halpern, 2003).

Second, who should be taught critical thinking. Surveys have found that teachers sometimes favor teaching critical thinking primarily to already high-achieving, higher-income, or university-track students (Warburton & Torff, 2005) — presumably on the assumption that such students can better benefit from or use it. There is little research evidence supporting this assumption, and it is ethically questionable regardless: Hawkins's (2006) discussion-based study above found critical thinking fostered even among students considered low-advantage.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Thorough, sustained implementation and genuine teacher commitment — either infusion or free-standing instruction can work, but only with real follow-through
#### Constraints
- Teaching critical thinking only to already-advantaged students is both poorly supported by evidence and ethically questionable, since research shows it can be fostered in low-advantage students as well

### Target Learners
- All students, not selectively the already high-achieving — evidence does not support restricting critical-thinking instruction to advantaged learners

### Target Learning Objectives
- Analyzing the reliability and validity of information; asking key questions; evaluating evidence; reasoning logically and objectively; expressing conclusions clearly; applying these habits across more than one domain
