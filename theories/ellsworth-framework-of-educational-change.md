# Ellsworth's Framework of Educational Change

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies, `q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Ellsworth surveys the research on educational change and groups it into a small set of perspectives, or "models", each represented by one author or group. Following Rogers's observation that change is a special case of communication, Ellsworth arranges them into one framework. A **change agent** wishes to communicate an **innovation** to an **intended adopter**. This is done through a **change process**, which opens a channel through the **change environment**. The environment also contains **resistance**, which can disrupt the process or distort how the innovation appears to the adopter. Each model addresses one part of that picture:

| Framework component | Model | What it helps with |
|---|---|---|
| Where to start (or stop an inappropriate change) | Fullan & Stiegelbauer, *The New Meaning of Educational Change* (1991) | Orientation for anyone trying to improve schools |
| The system being changed | Reigeluth & Garfinkle, *Systemic Change in Education* (1994) | Questioning the system's assumptions; its subsystems and stakeholders; the systems around it |
| The change process | Havelock & Zlotolow, *The Change Agent's Guide* (1995) | Planning, resourcing and trialling an innovation, and preparing the system to change again |
| The intended adopters | Hall & Hord's Concerns-Based Adoption Model (1987) | Tracking the concerns stakeholders have as change progresses, and timing interventions to them |
| Resistance | Zaltman & Duncan, *Strategies for Planned Change* (1977) | Diagnosing its cause: status threat, missing knowledge or skills, entrenched values, low confidence in the system |
| The innovation | Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1995) | The attributes of an innovation, or perceptions of them, that most influence adoption |
| The environment | Ely's conditions for change (1990) | Conditions that support implementation, such as evident leadership commitment and professional development |

The models are not a fixed sequence. A change agent can enter at whichever component the situation raises, and the models are meant to be used together. Ellsworth gives two examples. When adjusting an innovation's attributes (Rogers), make an innovation-component checklist (Hall & Hord) so that a critical part is not removed by accident. When checking Ely's conditions, check Reigeluth and Garfinkle's systemic conditions as well. The chapter also points beyond education, to human performance technology and human resource development.

Its concluding argument is systemic. A single innovation foreign to the rest of the system may be rejected, "like an incompatible organ transplant", so success depends on a coordinated bundle of innovations that leaves a coherent system after implementation.

## Implications

### Context
- Any design that has to be adopted by people other than its designer: a new course model in a department, a platform or practice introduced into schools, training rolled out across an organisation
- Most useful after the design exists and before it is implemented at scale, when the question shifts from "does it work" to "will it be taken up and kept"

### Target Learners
- The adopters of an innovation (teachers, administrators, trainers, learners), whose concerns and resistance the framework treats as things to understand rather than overcome by force
- Designers and change agents, for whom it is a map of the change literature

### Target Learning Objectives
- Lasting adoption of an innovation, rather than a trial that fades
- A diagnosis of the system an innovation is entering, including what it would have to change around it
