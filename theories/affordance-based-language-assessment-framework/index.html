Affordance-based assessment framework: linguistic ability as emergent property of organism-environment interaction¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The paper proposes an affordance-based framework for language assessment that shifts attention from measuring discrete outcomes toward documenting how learners engage with their affordance landscape, understood as the evolving array of opportunities for action. It argues that linguistic ability is not a pre-given trait but an emergent property of ongoing interactions, so the object of assessment is neither a latent trait nor a mediated scaffold but dynamic patterns of attunement and action. The framework is grounded in Gibson's ecological psychology and van Lier's applied-linguistic adaptation, and is operationalized through six foci and an Ecological Affordance Profile.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Assessment must afford authentic opportunities for action that reflect the complexity of language use

Constraints¶

  • Positioned as complementing, not replacing, existing outcome-based assessment practices

Target Learners¶

  • higher education language learners

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • communicative competence
  • learner agency
  • adaptability

Claims¶

  • Affordance resonance can be documented at scale through AI-generated logs identifying seed contributions and cascading uptake [+W]
  • Analysis of 52 higher-education assessment plans found outcome-based orientation dominant, with dynamic elements subsumed under summative scoring [+W]

Related Theories¶

  • Ecological dynamics as a theory of mathematics learning: mathematical concepts emerge from guided discovery of sensorimotor schemes oriented on attentional anchors

Examples¶

  • Ecological Affordance Profile: integrating micro-level observations, formative records, and summative accounts
  • Assess disruption and reclamation with rubric levels of adaptability and automated disruption-reclamation profiles
  • Feedback and feedforward typology for deliberate language practice
