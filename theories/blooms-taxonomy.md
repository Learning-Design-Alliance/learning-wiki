# Bloom's Taxonomy

## Description
Benjamin Bloom, with collaborators Engelhart, Furst, Hill, and Krathwohl, published *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I: The Cognitive Domain* in 1956, followed by *Handbook II: Affective Domain* (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1973). Bloom's group never published a psychomotor-domain handbook, citing a lack of experience teaching manual skills at the college level; that domain was filled in later by three independent researchers (Simpson, 1972; Dave, 1970; Harrow, 1972). Instructional designers commonly abbreviate the three domains as **KSA**: Knowledge (cognitive), Skills (psychomotor), Attitudes (affective). Within each domain, levels run from simplest/foundational to most complex, with lower levels commonly treated as prerequisite to higher ones — though this strict hierarchical assumption is itself contested (see Discussion below).

### Cognitive Domain
The cognitive domain has two influential versions. The **original (Bloom et al., 1956)** uses noun forms, six levels, simplest to most complex: **Knowledge** (recall data — *defines, lists, recalls, states*), **Comprehension** (understand meaning — *explains, summarizes, translates*), **Application** (use a concept in a new situation — *applies, computes, solves*), **Analysis** (separate material into parts to understand structure — *compares, differentiates, distinguishes*), **Synthesis** (build a new structure from diverse elements — *composes, designs, generates*), and **Evaluation** (judge the value of ideas or materials — *appraises, critiques, justifies*).

The **revised version** (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; also Anderson et al., 2000) renames each level as a verb, and — its single most citable structural change — **swaps the rank order of the top two levels**: **Remembering, Understanding, Applying, Analyzing, Evaluating, Creating**, with Creating now the most complex level and Evaluating fifth (versus the original's Synthesis-then-Evaluation order). The revision's three headline changes are: (1) noun-to-verb renaming, (2) this reordering, and (3) adding a **Cognitive Process × Knowledge Dimension matrix**, crossing the six process levels against four knowledge types — **Factual** (basic elements needed to know a discipline), **Conceptual** (interrelationships among elements within a larger structure), **Procedural** (how to do something — methods, algorithms, criteria for using skills), and **Metacognitive** (knowledge of cognition generally, and of one's own cognition specifically) — the last of which the original 1956 taxonomy barely addressed. An alternate five-part knowledge scheme (Clark & Chopeta, 2004; Clark & Mayer, 2007) splits "Conceptual" further into Concepts, Processes, and Principles, alongside Facts and Procedures; the resulting matrix (e.g., Facts × Remember = "list," Principles × Evaluate = "conclude") functions as a job aid for writing objectives and assessment items that specify both a cognitive process and a content type.

### Psychomotor Domain
Three competing models fill the domain Bloom's group left open. **Simpson (1972)**, the model most commonly treated as primary, proposes seven levels: **Perception** (using sensory cues to guide motor activity), **Set** (mental/physical/emotional readiness to act), **Guided Response** (early imitation and trial-and-error), **Mechanism** (habitual, moderately confident performance), **Complex Overt Response** (skillful, quick, accurate, largely automatic performance), **Adaptation** (modifying well-developed skills to fit new requirements), and **Origination** (creating new movement patterns for a novel problem). **Dave (1970)** proposes five levels — Imitation, Manipulation, Precision, Articulation, Naturalization. **Harrow (1972)** proposes six — Reflex Movements, Fundamental Movements, Perceptual Abilities, Physical Abilities, Skilled Movements, and Non-discursive Communication.

### Affective Domain
Krathwohl, Bloom, and Masia (1973) address feelings, values, and motivation in five levels: **Receiving Phenomena** (awareness, willingness to attend), **Responding to Phenomena** (active participation), **Valuing** (worth attached to an object or behavior, from simple acceptance to commitment), **Organizing** (building a value system by prioritizing and resolving conflicts among values), and **Internalizing Values/Characterization** (a value system that consistently and predictably governs behavior).

### Uses
The taxonomy is most often used to write lesson objectives, learning goals, and instructional activities pitched at a target level of complexity, and to design assessment questions that match that level. A common design heuristic pairs domain level with instructional-strategy intensity: lower cognitive/psychomotor/affective levels (Remembering/Perception/Receiving) pair well with more passive methods (lecture, reading, demonstration), while higher levels (Creating/Origination/Internalizing) require learner-driven methods (self-study, real-situation practice, mentoring).

## Implications

### Context
- Educators sometimes wrongly treat the taxonomy as strictly hierarchical and dismiss lower levels (e.g., Remembering) as unworthy of teaching; lower levels typically build skills higher levels depend on, and in some fields the "lower" level is itself the core professional skill
- Anderson and Krathwohl (2001) themselves suggest only the three lowest revised levels are clearly hierarchically ordered; the three higher levels may be closer to parallel than strictly sequential
- Sequencing challenge to strict hierarchy: Tomei (2010) argues it can be more effective to introduce application before underlying concepts — real-world context first, theory second — which connects this taxonomy to [Problem-based Learning](../principles/problem-based-learning.md)
- Distinctions between adjacent categories can be somewhat artificial, since a single cognitive task often involves multiple processes simultaneously rather than a single discrete level (Fadul, 2009) — a limitation of classifying mental processes into any taxonomy, not unique to Bloom's

### Target Learners
- Broadly applicable across K-12, higher education, and workplace/corporate training — the taxonomy's own worked examples span classroom tasks (writing an essay) and workplace tasks (hiring the most qualified candidate, calculating vacation time)

### Target Learning Objectives
- Objectives classifiable by cognitive complexity (recall through creation), by domain (knowledge, skill, or attitude), and, in the revised cognitive-domain matrix, by knowledge type (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive) — this is the taxonomy's most directly actionable use

### Discussion
Morshead (1965), reviewing the affective-domain handbook on publication, argued the classification "was not a properly constructed taxonomy" for lacking a systematic rationale — a critique the 2001 cognitive-domain revision is credited with partly addressing by re-establishing the framework on more systematic lines. Paul (1993) accepts that the six cognitive categories exist but questions whether they form a genuine sequential dependency rather than six related-but-parallel categories. The taxonomy's role in *systematizing* how educators talk about objectives is generally seen as more significant than any rigor gap in its original construction. Keene, Colvin, and Sissons (2010) connect the scaffolding logic behind ordering lower before higher levels directly to [Sociocultural Theory](sociocultural-theory.md) and Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development.
