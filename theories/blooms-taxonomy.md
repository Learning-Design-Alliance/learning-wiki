# Bloom's Taxonomy

## Description
Benjamin Bloom (1913-1999), working with his mentor Ralph W. Tyler at the University of Chicago, published *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives: Handbook I, The Cognitive Domain* in 1956, followed by a second handbook on the affective domain in 1964 (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1973). Bloom et al. (1956) identified three domains of educational goals — often summarized as **KSA**:

- **Cognitive Domain** — mental skills (Knowledge)
- **Psychomotor Domain** — manual or physical skills (Skills)
- **Affective Domain** — growth in feelings or emotional areas (Attitudes)

Each domain is organized as a hierarchy running from the simplest behavior to the most complex, on the premise that lower levels must generally be mastered before the next level can take place — though this strict hierarchical assumption has drawn criticism (see below). Bloom et al. (1956) produced a detailed compilation for the cognitive and affective domains but explicitly omitted the psychomotor domain, citing their own lack of experience teaching manual skills at the college level; other researchers later filled this gap with at least three competing psychomotor models.

### The Cognitive Domain
The original (1956) cognitive domain has six levels, from simplest to most complex: **Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation**.

In the mid-1990s, Lorin Anderson (a former student of Bloom's) and David Krathwohl revised the cognitive domain (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; Anderson et al., 2000), making three changes: renaming the levels from nouns to verbs, reordering the top two levels, and adding a Knowledge Dimension. The revised levels are **Remembering, Understanding, Applying, Analyzing, Evaluating, Creating** — swapping the original "Synthesis" and "Evaluation" so that Creating (synthesis-like) sits above Evaluating, on the reasoning that creating something new is a more demanding cognitive act than judging existing material.

| Original (1956) | Revised (2001) | Example (Goldilocks and the Three Bears) |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge: recall data or information | Remembering: recall or retrieve previously learned information | List three things Goldilocks did in the three bears' house |
| Comprehension: understand meaning, interpret, translate | Understanding: comprehend the meaning, translation, interpretation of instructions and problems | Explain why Goldilocks liked the little bear's chair best |
| Application: use a concept in a new situation | Applying: use a concept in a new situation or unprompted use of an abstraction | Predict what Goldilocks might have used if she had entered your house |
| Analysis: separate material into component parts | Analyzing: separate material or concepts into component parts so the organizational structure may be understood | Select the part of the story where Goldilocks seemed most comfortable |
| Synthesis: build a structure or pattern from diverse elements | (moved below Evaluating, renamed) Creating: build a structure or pattern from diverse elements | Tell how the story would have been different if it had been about three fish |
| Evaluation: judge the value of ideas or materials | Evaluating: make judgments about the value of ideas or materials | Decide whether Goldilocks was a bad girl, and justify your position |

Each level pairs with characteristic key words useful for writing objectives — e.g., Remembering: *defines, identifies, lists, recalls*; Understanding: *explains, summarizes, paraphrases*; Applying: *demonstrates, solves, uses*; Analyzing: *compares, differentiates, distinguishes*; Evaluating: *critiques, defends, justifies*; Creating: *designs, composes, formulates* (Clark, 2015a).

### Cognitive Process and Knowledge Dimension Matrix
The revised taxonomy also crosses the six cognitive processes with a **Knowledge Dimension**, adding a fourth knowledge type (metacognitive) to the three Bloom's original work had mentioned only in passing:

- **Factual** — the basic elements a learner must know to be acquainted with a discipline or solve problems
- **Conceptual** — the interrelationships among basic elements within a larger structure that let them function together
- **Procedural** — how to do something: methods of inquiry, and criteria for using skills, algorithms, techniques, and methods
- **Metacognitive** — knowledge of cognition in general, and awareness of one's own cognition (see [Metacognition](metacognition.md))

Crossing these four knowledge types against the six cognitive processes produces a 4×6 matrix (Krathwohl & Anderson, 2001) that functions as a performance-aid for writing precise objectives — e.g., a cell at Factual × Remembering might be "list," while Metacognitive × Create might be "actualize." A related five-part knowledge scheme (facts, concepts, processes, procedures, principles) is also used by some instructional designers (Clark & Chopeta, 2004; Clark & Mayer, 2007) as an alternative to the four-part matrix.

### The Psychomotor Domain
Because Bloom et al. (1956) did not produce a psychomotor model, at least three independent hierarchies have been proposed:

- **Simpson (1972)**, seven levels: Perception (using sensory cues to guide motor activity) → Set (readiness to act) → Guided Response (imitation, trial and error) → Mechanism (habitual, proficient responses) → Complex Overt Response (skillful, automatic performance) → Adaptation (modifying movement patterns for new requirements) → Origination (creating new movement patterns for a novel problem).
- **Dave (1970)**, five levels: Imitation → Manipulation → Precision → Articulation → Naturalization (performance becomes second-nature).
- **Harrow (1972)**, six levels: Reflex Movements → Fundamental Movements → Perceptual Abilities → Physical Abilities (fitness) → Skilled Movements → Non-discursive Communication (expressive use of gesture and body language).

### The Affective Domain
The affective domain (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1973) covers feelings, values, appreciation, enthusiasm, motivation, and attitude, in five levels from simplest to most complex: **Receiving Phenomena** (willingness to hear, selective attention) → **Responding to Phenomena** (active participation, e.g. participating in discussion) → **Valuing** (attaching worth to an object or behavior, from simple acceptance to commitment) → **Organizing** (building a value system by comparing, relating, and resolving conflicts among values) → **Internalizing Values** (a value system that consistently, predictably controls behavior — the value has become characteristic of the person).

### Matching Instructional Strategy to Taxonomy Level
An **Instructional Strategy Selection Chart** (Clark, 2015d) maps strategy type to the level being targeted across all three domains: passive methods (lecture, reading, demonstration) suit the lowest levels (Knowledge/Remembering, Perception/Set, Receiving Phenomena); active methods (practice with coaching, simulated settings) suit the middle levels; use in real situations and self-directed, informally-acquired learning (with mentoring/coaching to speed the process) suit the highest levels (Evaluation/Creating, Origination, Internalizing Values). The general pattern: lower levels of performance can normally be taught with more passive learning methods, while higher levels usually require some action or involvement by the learner.

### Application in the Classroom
Bloom's taxonomy helps a teacher target a student's [Zone of Proximal Development](sociocultural-theory.md) in the sense meant by Vygotsky: a student who knows few terms for a topic (stuck at Knowledge/Remembering and Comprehension/Understanding) may need support remembering and defining terms before they can make useful comparisons (Analysis/Analyzing). The teacher arranges the zone of proximal development that makes the higher-level task reachable, but the student still has to construct the comparison for themselves (Arduini-Van Hoose, 2020).

## Criticisms
As Morshead (1965) pointed out on publication of the affective-domain handbook, the classification lacked a systematic rationale of construction — a critique later acknowledged in the taxonomy's own 2001 revision, which reestablished it on more systematic lines. Some critics accept the six cognitive categories but question whether they form a genuine sequential hierarchy (Paul, 1993); educators sometimes over-read the hierarchy and mistakenly treat the lowest levels as unworthy of teaching, when in some fields (e.g., species identification in natural history) the lower levels are themselves the most important skill (Flannery, 2007; Lawler, 2016) — the lower levels enable building the higher ones, an application of Vygotskian [scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) (Keene, Colvin, & Sissons, 2010; Vygotsky, 1978). Anderson and Krathwohl (2001) themselves argue the three lowest levels are hierarchically ordered but the three higher levels are parallel rather than strictly sequential; others argue it can be pedagogically better to move to Application before formally introducing a concept (Tomei, 2010), aligning with problem-based approaches that put real-world context before theory. More broadly, the clean separation of cognitive processes into discrete categories can be seen as artificial, since any real cognitive task typically draws on several categories at once, and any attempt to cleanly classify cognition risks understating its holistic, interconnected nature (Fadul, 2009) — a criticism applicable to taxonomies of mental process generally.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Most useful when writing precise, assessable learning objectives, and when selecting instructional strategies matched to a target level of complexity
#### Constraints
- The apparent strict hierarchy can mislead teachers into treating lower levels as unimportant busywork, when in some domains the lower levels are the core skill
- The clean level-by-level classification is an analytical convenience; real tasks usually draw on multiple levels/domains simultaneously

### Target Learners
- Applicable across all ages and grade levels — the framework structures the objective, not the learner population

### Target Learning Objectives
- Writing objectives at a deliberately targeted level of cognitive, psychomotor, or affective complexity, and sequencing instruction from lower to higher levels within a domain
