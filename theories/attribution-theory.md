# Attribution Theory

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Attribution Theory (Weiner, 1986) holds that people try to explain the causes of their own successes and failures — attributing an outcome to ability, effort, luck, task difficulty, mood, fatigue, and so on — and that it is the *perceived* cause, whether or not it is the actual cause, that drives subsequent motivation and behavior (Park, 2018). Weiner (2010) classifies these attributions along three independent dimensions:

- **Locus** — whether the cause is internal to the learner (ability, effort) or external (luck, task difficulty).
- **Stability** — whether the cause is relatively constant over time (ability) or variable (effort, luck).
- **Controllability** — whether the learner can influence the cause (effort, choice of strategy) or not (luck, innate ability, task difficulty).

Each dimension drives a different motivational consequence. **Locus** affects self-esteem and self-directed emotion: success attributed internally produces pride, failure attributed internally produces shame or guilt. **Stability** affects expectancy for future outcomes: a stable attribution (ability) implies the same outcome will likely recur, so a stable failure attribution ("I'm just not good at this") is far more damaging to future motivation than an unstable one ("I didn't study enough this time," which implies next time could go differently). **Controllability** interacts with the other two to shape specific emotions: failure attributed to a controllable cause (effort) tends to produce guilt and a drive to change; failure attributed to an internal, uncontrollable cause (low ability) tends to produce shame; failure attributed to an external, uncontrollable cause tends to produce helplessness and disengagement, since the learner believes nothing they do will change the outcome. Attributing success to internal, *stable* factors like ability is itself a mixed blessing: it can build optimism about future success, but can also breed indifference to correcting mistakes, or produce pessimism the moment performance dips below the accustomed level ("Maybe I'm not as smart as I thought") (Dweck, 2000, 2006).

The clearest instructional implication is that internal, controllable, and — ideally — unstable attributions for failure (effort, strategy choice) sustain motivation better than internal-stable ones (fixed ability) or external ones (luck, task difficulty), because they leave the learner believing a different outcome is achievable through their own action. This is why "your effort really made a difference" is better feedback than "you're smart" after success, and "let's find a better strategy" is better than "this material is just too hard for you" after failure (Seifert & Sutton, 2018) — though attributions to effort are only credible when tasks are genuinely at an appropriate difficulty level and assessment is reliable enough that effort actually does predict outcomes.

Effort-based attributions are only convincing under three conditions: tasks pitched at a genuinely appropriate difficulty with reliable assessment; teachers ready to help individuals even when a task seems easy enough not to need it, since it is often hard to predict in advance how hard a task will actually prove for a particular student; and — often forgotten — recognizing that ability, though usually treated as a relatively fixed trait, actually changes incrementally over the long term through accumulated effort (a fluent second-grade reader was, at some point in the past, unable to read at all). Because effort's results are visible almost immediately while ability changes only gradually over weeks, months, or years, it is easy to treat a current ability gap as fixed rather than as the product of effort not yet accumulated — and recognizing that mismatch explicitly is one of the more effective ways to actually help students increase their abilities (Blackwell, Trzniewski, & Dweck, 2007; Schunk, Pintrich, & Meece, 2008).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tasks pitched at an appropriate difficulty level and assessed reliably — attribution-shaping feedback about effort or strategy is not credible if the task was actually too hard or the grading too inconsistent for effort to matter
- Willingness from instructors to frame their own explanations of student success/failure around internal, controllable causes rather than fixed ability or external luck
#### Constraints
- Praising ability directly ("you're smart") can be a blessing and a curse: it can build short-term optimism but also produce indifference to correcting mistakes, and can produce pessimism if the student later underperforms ("maybe I'm not as smart as I thought")
- Attributions to stable, uncontrollable, internal causes (fixed low ability) are the most damaging combination and the hardest to intervene on directly, since they require changing a belief about a stable trait, not just a single explanation

### Target Learners
- Learners who have recently experienced failure or underperformance, where the attribution they form will shape whether they re-engage or disengage
- Learners at risk of developing a fixed, stable, internal explanation for repeated struggle (closely related to learned helplessness, see [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md))

### Target Learning Objectives
- Sustained motivation and re-engagement after failure or setbacks
- Willingness to use and adjust learning strategies, which depends on believing that strategy/effort (not fixed ability or luck) drives outcomes

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Weiner's (1986) attribution framework draws on locus, stability, and controllability as the three dimensions organizing how causal beliefs translate into motivational and emotional consequences

## Related Theories
- [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md) — both concern learners' beliefs about the causes of their own performance; sustained internal-stable-uncontrollable failure attributions are a direct pathway to the learned helplessness that self-efficacy theory also describes
- [Self-Determination Theory](self-determination-theory.md) — controllable attributions support the sense of autonomy and competence SDT identifies as basic psychological needs
- [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md) — the stability dimension of an attribution directly shapes a learner's expectancy for future success, the central construct of expectancy-value theory
