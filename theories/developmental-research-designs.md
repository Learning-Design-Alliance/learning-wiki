# Developmental Research Designs — Cross-Sectional, Longitudinal, and Sequential

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Research design is the strategy or blueprint for deciding how to collect and analyze information, distinct from the specific methods used to gather it; when the goal is examining change over time, three designs are available, each with a different cost and a different vulnerability.

**Cross-sectional designs** examine participants of different ages tested at the same point in time, and are the most common developmental design because they are less time-consuming and less expensive than the alternatives. Their core limitation is that the results describe age *differences*, not change over time within the same people: if 80-year-olds score lower than 50-year-olds who score lower than 20-year-olds on some measure, the study cannot say whether any individual actually declines with age, since no one was followed as they aged — it's equally possible the 20-year-olds will keep scoring just as high decades from now. Cross-sectional comparisons are also vulnerable to **cohort effects**: since each age group belongs to a different generation, an observed difference may reflect that cohort's distinct life experience (education, economic conditions, technology, health and nutrition standards) rather than age itself.

**Longitudinal designs** begin with a single cohort — people of similar age and background — and measure them repeatedly over an extended period, so people can be compared with their own earlier selves and age-related change can actually be measured. This design carries its own two hazards. **Attrition** — participants who move, change contact information, die, or simply lose interest — erodes the sample over time, and is often *selective*: it tends to be the least healthy, least educated, and lowest-socioeconomic-status participants who drop out, which can bias what remains. **Practice effects** are the second hazard: repeated administration of the same or similar assessment can make participants appear to improve simply because they've practiced the task, not because they've genuinely developed.

**Sequential designs** combine both approaches: participants of different ages are enrolled at different points in time and then followed longitudinally, so the design supports longitudinal comparisons (within-person change), cross-sectional comparisons (age/cohort differences), and — uniquely — the ability to disentangle cohort effects from time-of-measurement effects from true age-related change. As Schaie and Baltes (1975) put it, cross-sectional and longitudinal designs can reveal a pattern of change, while sequential designs can help identify what's actually driving that pattern.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A large enough initial longitudinal sample to absorb expected attrition without leaving the remaining sample unrepresentative
- Enough resources (time, funding, participant retention effort) for a longitudinal or sequential design when the research question genuinely requires within-person change, not just an age comparison
#### Constraints
- A cross-sectional finding of age-group differences cannot, by itself, support a claim about individual developmental change — the design simply doesn't measure that
- Selective attrition in a longitudinal study can make its final sample systematically healthier, more educated, or more advantaged than its starting sample, biasing later-wave findings
- Improvement across repeated waves of a longitudinal study may reflect practice with the instrument rather than genuine development

### Target Learners
- Researchers and consumers of developmental research choosing or evaluating a design against what the research question actually requires

### Target Learning Objectives
- Recognizing which developmental claims a given study's design can and cannot support — age-difference versus age-change being the central distinction
