# Multiple Intelligences Theory

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)

## Description
Howard Gardner (1983) proposed that intelligence is not the single general factor ("g") that Charles Spearman and earlier theorists described, but a set of at least eight relatively independent intelligences — commonly listed as linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic. Gardner (1999) later proposed a ninth, **existential** intelligence, which he himself notes "has yet to experience full acceptance by educators in the classroom." A person typically excels in some of these and not others, which is a direct challenge to instructional designs that treat "ability" as a single scale a learner sits somewhere on.

Gardner justifies treating each intelligence as genuinely separate using eight identification criteria: isolation by brain damage, the existence of prodigies or exceptional individuals, a distinguishable set of core operations, developmental stages with an expert end state, evolutionary plausibility, susceptibility to symbolic encoding, and support from both experimental-psychology tasks and psychometric research.

Gardner's inter- and intrapersonal intelligences are often combined by other researchers into "emotional intelligence" — the ability to understand and regulate one's own and others' emotions and social relationships (Parker, Saklofske, & Stough, 2009). Daniel Goleman (1995) popularized the claim that emotional intelligence predicts life success better than traditional IQ, but this claim is contested: researchers have pointed to inconsistent definitions and measurement difficulty for the construct (Locke, 2005; Mayer, Salovey, & Caruso, 2004). Multiple Intelligences Theory itself faces a parallel and more sustained empirical challenge. Critics (Morgan, 1996) argue it largely relabels existing "cognitive styles" research as separate "intelligences." The psychometric community has pointed to the persistence of a general intelligence ("g") factor: Gottfredson (2004) reports that a test giving two items per intelligence found people scored similarly across all eight, showing no discriminant validity between them; Gardner's own response is that a g-factor exists but is itself simply a manifestation of logical-mathematical intelligence, not evidence against the broader theory. More strikingly, the theory has drawn a decades-long "no published validating studies" critique — repeated by Sternberg (1994), Allix (2000), Sternberg and Grigorenko (2004), and even by Gardner and Connell (2000, p. 292), who conceded "little hard evidence for MI theory," and by Gardner (2004, p. 214) himself, who wrote he would be "delighted were such evidence to accrue" and noted the theory "has few enthusiasts among psychometricians." Waterhouse (2006) adds a neuroscience critique: shared neural pathways and genetic factors across language, music, motor skill, and general intelligence undercut the claim that each intelligence operates via a wholly separate neural mechanism, and the theory lacks a clear evolutionary account of why nine independent intelligences would emerge. Developing valid, reliable psychometric measures for each proposed intelligence has proven difficult in general (Furnham, 2009; Gardner & Moran, 2006; Klein, 1997). The theory remains influential in justifying differentiated, multi-modal instructional design despite this unresolved empirical status.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Willingness to design multiple task/assessment formats (verbal, spatial, kinesthetic, interpersonal) rather than assuming a single format measures a learner's capability fairly
#### Constraints
- The theory's empirical validation remains contested — standard psychometric measures for the proposed intelligences are difficult to construct, and critics argue the eight categories may simply relabel existing "cognitive style" findings rather than identify genuinely separate abilities
- Should not be used to conclude a learner is incapable in a domain because it doesn't match their "strongest" intelligence — Gardner's own framing is about relative strengths, not fixed limits

### Target Learners
- Learners assessed or taught through only one modality (typically linguistic/logical), where the theory argues for recognizing strengths a narrower assessment format would miss

### Target Learning Objectives
- Objectives that can be demonstrated through varied task types (a project, a performance, a verbal explanation) rather than only a single test format

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Morgan (1996) argues the theory relabels existing cognitive-styles research rather than identifying genuinely independent abilities
- Standard measurement approaches struggle to validate the eight intelligences as empirically distinct (Furnham, 2009; Klein, 1997)
- No published studies have directly validated the theory as a whole, a gap acknowledged even by Gardner himself (Sternberg, 1994; Allix, 2000; Gardner & Connell, 2000; Gardner, 2004; Sternberg & Grigorenko, 2004)
- Waterhouse (2006) argues shared neural pathways and genetic factors across cognitive domains, and the absence of a clear evolutionary rationale, count against treating each intelligence as a separate neural mechanism
