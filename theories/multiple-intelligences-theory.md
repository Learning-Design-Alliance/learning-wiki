# Multiple Intelligences Theory

## Description
Howard Gardner (1983) proposed that intelligence is not the single general factor ("g") that Charles Spearman and earlier theorists described, but a set of at least eight relatively independent intelligences — commonly listed as linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic. A person typically excels in some of these and not others, which is a direct challenge to instructional designs that treat "ability" as a single scale a learner sits somewhere on.

Gardner's inter- and intrapersonal intelligences are often combined by other researchers into "emotional intelligence" — the ability to understand and regulate one's own and others' emotions and social relationships (Parker, Saklofske, & Stough, 2009). Daniel Goleman (1995) popularized the claim that emotional intelligence predicts life success better than traditional IQ, but this claim is contested: researchers have pointed to inconsistent definitions and measurement difficulty for the construct (Locke, 2005; Mayer, Salovey, & Caruso, 2004). Multiple Intelligences Theory itself faces a parallel empirical challenge — critics (Morgan, 1996) argue it largely relabels existing "cognitive styles" research as separate "intelligences," and developing valid, reliable measures for each proposed intelligence has proven difficult (Furnham, 2009; Gardner & Moran, 2006; Klein, 1997). The theory remains influential in justifying differentiated, multi-modal instructional design despite this unresolved empirical status.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Willingness to design multiple task/assessment formats (verbal, spatial, kinesthetic, interpersonal) rather than assuming a single format measures a learner's capability fairly
#### Constraints
- The theory's empirical validation remains contested — standard psychometric measures for the proposed intelligences are difficult to construct, and critics argue the eight categories may simply relabel existing "cognitive style" findings rather than identify genuinely separate abilities
- Should not be used to conclude a learner is incapable in a domain because it doesn't match their "strongest" intelligence — Gardner's own framing is about relative strengths, not fixed limits

### Target Learners
- Learners assessed or taught through only one modality (typically linguistic/logical), where the theory argues for recognizing strengths a narrower assessment format would miss

### Target Learning Objectives
- Objectives that can be demonstrated through varied task types (a project, a performance, a verbal explanation) rather than only a single test format

### Theory
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Morgan (1996) argues the theory relabels existing cognitive-styles research rather than identifying genuinely independent abilities
- Standard measurement approaches struggle to validate the eight intelligences as empirically distinct (Furnham, 2009; Klein, 1997)
