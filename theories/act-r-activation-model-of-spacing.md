# Activation-Based ACT-R Model of the Spacing Effect

> **Theory** · [All theories](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 2 mixed) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

## Description
Pavlik and Anderson (2005) extended the ACT-R declarative-memory equation to explain the spacing effect. An item's activation is the log of a sum of traces, one per practice, each decaying as a power of the time since that practice. The extension is that each trace's decay rate is set by the item's activation at the moment of practice: a trace laid down while the item is still highly active (shortly after the last practice) decays faster than one laid down after the item has faded. Massed practice therefore buys a quick gain that is lost quickly, and spaced practice a slower gain that lasts. In the article the model predicts correctness of recall from activation.

The model accounts for three patterns in the wiki: Pavlik and Anderson's own Japanese-English vocabulary experiment, Bahrick's crossover (narrow spacing better at a short retention interval, wide spacing better at a long one), and the finding that widening the spacing does not always help. Pavlik and Anderson report that it fits their restricted data set better than a modified version of Raaijmakers' SAM model. Mozer et al. (2009) granted that this model and SAM give reasonable post-hoc fits to spacing data but questioned their predictive value, which motivated their [multiscale context model](multiscale-context-model-of-spacing.md).

## Implications

### Context
- Paired-associate vocabulary learning with a fixed number of practices and a delayed test (1 or 7 days in Pavlik and Anderson's experiment, 30 days in Bahrick's data).
- Practice is costly in the model as well as in the data: wider spacing lowers accuracy during practice, which a design has to accept to gain at the delayed test.

### Target Learners
- University students learning foreign-language vocabulary.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Long-term recall of paired associates; choosing the spacing of practice for a given retention interval.
