# Funds of Knowledge

## Description
Funds of knowledge (Moll, Amanti, Neff & Gonzalez, 1992) refers to the historically accumulated and culturally developed bodies of knowledge and skill that households use for everyday functioning and well-being — practical know-how around work, caregiving, navigation, budgeting, and problem-solving. The framework treats this household and community knowledge as a genuine instructional asset, typically surfaced through family interviews or home visits, rather than as background irrelevant to formal disciplinary content. It is more operationally specific than a general commitment to valuing learners' backgrounds: it points instructors toward concretely identifying particular practices (e.g., how a family plans a bus route, or sequences steps in a caregiving routine) and building instructional tasks that connect those practices to disciplinary concepts.

**Expansion to embodied and cultural-practice resources.** Solomon et al. (2022) argue that the original framework, by focusing on verbal, written, and artifact-based channels, neglects the body itself as a site of funds of knowledge. They extend the construct to include embodied and cultural-practice resources — dance training, movement vocabularies, and expressive practices tied to cultural identity (e.g., ballet technique, krumping, stepping) — documented through sustained observation in the community spaces (dance centers, in their case) where those practices are valued, rather than through household interviews alone. Where the original formulation treats family practical know-how as an asset for disciplinary tasks, this expansion treats learners' trained bodies and movement repertoires as an asset for disciplinary sense-making.

## Implications

### Context
- Requires deliberately eliciting specific household/community knowledge (typically via family interviews), not just a general stance of cultural respect
- Most demonstrated with young children and families from minoritized or under-resourced communities, where formal assessments often under-recognize what children already know how to do

### Target Learners
- Young children and families whose everyday practical knowledge is not typically recognized as relevant to formal disciplinary content (e.g., computational thinking, mathematics, science)

### Target Learning Objectives
- Revealing competencies (e.g., informal computational thinking: sequencing, abstraction, data reasoning) that decontextualized assessments underestimate or miss entirely
