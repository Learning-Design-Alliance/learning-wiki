# Locus of Control

## Description
Julian Rotter (1966) proposed locus of control as a cognitive factor shaping behavior and personality, distinct from [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md): self-efficacy is a belief about one's own *ability* to perform a specific task, while locus of control is a broader belief about *who or what controls outcomes* in one's life generally. People with an **internal locus of control** ("internals") tend to believe their outcomes are the direct result of their own effort; people with an **external locus of control** ("externals") tend to believe their outcomes are controlled by other people, luck, or chance. Rotter framed the two orientations as ends of a continuum rather than a strict dichotomy.

A student who does poorly on a test after skipping study time illustrates the contrast: an internal is more likely to attribute the outcome to insufficient studying and resolve to study more next time; an external is more likely to attribute it to the test being unfairly hard and see little point in studying harder, since outcomes are seen as outside personal control. Researchers have found that people with an internal locus of control perform better academically, achieve more in their careers, are more independent, are healthier, cope better, and are less depressed than people with an external locus of control (Benassi, Sweeney, & Durfour, 1988; Lefcourt, 1982; Maltby, Day, & Macaskill, 2007; Whyte, 1977, 1978, 1980).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Best applied where a learner's persistence or disengagement after failure is the concern, not raw ability
#### Constraints
- Locus of control is a generalized belief across situations, not task-specific — it should not be conflated with self-efficacy, which can vary sharply from one domain to another for the same person

### Target Learners
- Learners who attribute poor performance to external, uncontrollable factors and consequently disengage from further effort
- Learners for whom attribution retraining (shifting explanations toward effort, a controllable and internal factor) may improve persistence

### Target Learning Objectives
- Building a sense that effort causally shapes outcomes, to sustain engagement after setbacks
- Not itself an instructional-objective framework — it describes a learner disposition relevant to how feedback and attributions for success/failure should be framed

## Related Theories
- [Self-Efficacy Theory](self-efficacy-theory.md) — self-efficacy is task-specific competence belief; locus of control is a broader, more stable belief about the general controllability of outcomes
- [Attribution Theory](attribution-theory.md) — locus (internal vs. external) is one of attribution theory's own core dimensions, applied there to explanations for specific past outcomes rather than a generalized trait
- [Expectancy-Value Theory](expectancy-value-theory.md) — internal locus of control has been linked to higher expectancy for success and task value in online learning contexts (Joo, Lim, & Kim, 2013)
- [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) — an internal locus of control is a plausible precondition for sustained self-regulatory effort, since self-regulation presumes outcomes respond to one's own strategic action
