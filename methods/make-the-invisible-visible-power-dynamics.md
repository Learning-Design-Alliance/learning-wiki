# Make the Invisible Visible (Power Dynamics)

> **Design Method** · [All design methods](index.md)

## Description
This strategy brings power dynamics to the forefront of learning design by naming them rather than leaving them tacit. It asks designers to make explicit who holds decision-making authority at each stage, and to choose deliberately among design postures along a continuum of shared power: user-centered design (designers consult users), co-design (stakeholders join the design team as partners), and user-created design (learners hold genuine authority over decisions). The strategy also employs structured observation tools to assess how a learning environment affirms or minimizes student identities. The goal is equitable design: challenging unexamined assumptions about who belongs, who decides, and whose expertise counts.

## Design Implications

Surfacing power dynamics converts an implicit political process into an inspectable design variable. Research on student voice and partnership indicates that when students participate meaningfully in pedagogical decision-making, engagement and trust improve, but effects depend on whether authority is genuinely shared rather than symbolically delegated [~M]. Design decisions made without surfacing power asymmetries tend to reproduce the defaults of the most powerful stakeholders [-W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A willingness on the part of leadership to expose and potentially redistribute decision-making authority
- Structured observation tools (e.g., shadowing protocols, identity-affirmation walkthroughs) and time to use them
- Skilled facilitation for discussions about power, so that naming dynamics does not collapse into blame or defensiveness
- A mechanism for closing the loop: stakeholders must see how their input changed decisions, or participation reads as tokenism [-M]

#### Constraints
- Tokenistic participation — consulting students without ceding any authority — can depress trust and reduce future willingness to engage [~M]
- Discussions of power can trigger defensiveness among staff who experience them as accusations; without careful facilitation the conversation stalls or hardens positions [-W]
- In high-stakes accountability environments, teachers and leaders may perceive shared design authority as a risk, quietly reverting to top-down decisions [-W]
- Observation tools focused on identity can misfire if observers lack training, producing deficit-framed judgments of students rather than of the environment [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Consultation mode:** designers gather stakeholder input but retain authority — lowest risk, lowest redistribution
- **Co-design mode:** mixed stakeholder teams share authorship of designs, with explicit decision rules for disagreements
- **Student-created design:** students hold authority over defined domains (e.g., advisory structures, elective offerings), with adults as advisors
- Observation-only entry points (shadowing, identity walkthroughs) allow teams to build shared evidence before tackling authority questions directly

### Target Learners
- Educators, school design teams, and leaders seeking more equitable learning environments
- Students, who benefit when their identities are affirmed and they hold authentic decision rights; autonomy-supportive environments predict stronger intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Historically marginalized students in particular, for whom identity affirmation in the environment is most consequential for belonging and persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Dispositional: designers learn to notice and name power dynamics rather than defaulting to their own assumptions
- Equitable design practice: selecting the appropriate point on the consultation–co-design–student-created continuum for a given decision
- Environmental diagnosis: identifying where the school affirms or alienates student identities
- Belonging and identity development as outcomes for students

### Instructions
1. **Observe first.** Use structured observation or shadowing to gather evidence on how the environment affirms or minimizes student identity; ground later discussion in shared observations rather than impressions.
2. **Name the dynamics.** Facilitate a discussion in which the team explicitly maps who currently holds decision rights over curriculum, environment, and policy — making the invisible visible.
3. **Develop shared criteria.** Build [Criteria Development](../elements/criteria-development.md) as a team: agree on what an identity-affirming, power-aware learning environment should look like.
4. **Reach consensus on changes.** Use [Consensus Discussion](../elements/consensus-discussion.md) to decide which decisions will move along the continuum toward co-design or student-created design.
5. **Close the loop.** Report back to students and stakeholders on what changed and why; repeat observation cycles to check whether the environment actually shifted.

## Examples
- **CityBridge School Design Fellowship** — fellows use observation tools focused on student identity development and affirmation, paired with Transcend's *Designing for Learning Primer* and research-summary cards connecting identity to learning science, to diagnose and redesign power arrangements in school models.
- **Transcend Education's "Designing for Learning" resources** ([transcendeducation.org](https://www.transcendeducation.org)) — provide observation instruments and design cards that operationalize identity-affirming, power-aware design.
- **Student voice partnerships in higher education** — programs in which students co-research and co-redesign courses with faculty (the Students as Partners movement) redistribute pedagogical authority and report improved course design and trust.
