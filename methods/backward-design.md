# Backward Design

> **Design Method** · [All design methods](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Backward Design is a curriculum planning strategy in which the designer first identifies the desired learning results, then determines what evidence would demonstrate achievement of those results, and only then plans instructional activities. Developed by Wiggins and McTighe as "Understanding by Design," it reverses the common activity-first or content-first planning habit, ensuring that every lesson and assessment serves an explicit outcome.

## Design Implications

Backward Design operationalizes [constructive alignment](../principles/clear-structure.md) [+M]: when objectives, assessments, and activities are mutually reinforcing, learners can direct effort more efficiently and instructors can detect drift between what is taught and what is tested. Its central discipline is the second stage — designing [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) *before* instruction — which forces designers to define observable evidence rather than vague coverage goals.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit, prioritized learning outcomes (Wiggins & McTighe's "enduring understandings" vs. mere familiarity)
- Authentic performance tasks or criteria that would genuinely evidence the outcome, not just recall proxies
- Willingness to discard or redesign existing activities that no longer serve the stated outcomes
- Goals must be defined before the activity is designed

#### Constraints
- Outcomes written as vague verbs ("understand," "appreciate") defeat the evidence stage; without observable criteria, assessment design reverts to habit [-M]
- Overly rigid adherence can narrow instruction to what is easily measured, marginalizing hard-to-assess goals like creativity or disposition [-W]
- Requires institutional time and collaboration; individual instructors working in isolation often skip the evidence stage under time pressure [-W]
- In domains with strong canonical sequencing (e.g., foundational mathematics), content-logic may legitimately drive some ordering decisions [~M]
- The relative priority of process versus content goals depends on the venue and audience

#### Implementation Variability
- **Course-level:** single course redesign around 3–5 transfer goals
- **Program-level:** mapping outcomes across a degree, with each course contributing assessed evidence
- **Standards-based K-12:** unpacking standards into essential questions and performance tasks
- **Competency-based variants:** outcomes become demonstrable competencies with mastery thresholds, as in [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md)

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit indirectly, but the clearest gains accrue to learners who rely on transparent expectations to allocate study effort — making goals and criteria visible supports [self-efficacy and persistence](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Novices especially, who cannot yet infer what matters from exposure to content alone and benefit from [clear structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) [+M]
- advanced high-school students
- college students

### Target Learning Goals
- Transfer-oriented understanding rather than rote coverage
- Integration of knowledge across lessons toward demonstrable performance
- Any goal where valid evidence of achievement can be specified in advance

### Instructions
1. **Identify desired results.** Prioritize a small set of enduring understandings and essential questions; state outcomes in observable terms.
2. **Determine acceptable evidence.** Design the [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) tasks and rubrics that would demonstrate the outcomes before planning any teaching; align formative checks with the same criteria, since [feedback is most effective at the task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
3. **Plan learning experiences.** Sequence instruction (e.g., via [advance organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [application](../elements/application.md) tasks, [coaching](../elements/coaching.md)) only where it produces the evidence defined in step 2.
4. **Review alignment.** Audit every activity and assessment against the outcomes; revise or cut misaligned elements.

## Description
The article recommends designing inquiry activities by specifying goals first and then building the activity to convey them. The authors state: "We approached the design of this inquiry using backward design (Wiggins & McTighe 2005) in which scientific process, attitudinal, and content goals are defined first." For their venues, process and attitudinal goals were prioritized over content goals, and the same materials could be reused for different content goals.
