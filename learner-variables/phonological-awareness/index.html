Phonological Awareness¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 92 claims (56 show a difference, 15 mixed, 21 no difference) · 57 studies (25 associational, 18 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 design, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 17 of 57 report an effect size · 90 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Phonological awareness is the ability to hear and manipulate the sound structure of spoken words: recognising rhymes, splitting words into syllables and individual sounds (phonemes), and blending sounds into words. The broader term phonological processing, which this page also covers, includes detecting and handling speech sounds rapidly in both spoken and written words and holding sound information briefly in memory. These skills are measured with tasks such as rhyming, blending (combining separate sounds into a word), elision (saying what remains when a sound is removed) and phoneme manipulation.

Phonological awareness underpins learning to read in an alphabetic system: it lets children map sounds to letters, which is what phonics instruction teaches, and so to decode whole words. In young children it is described as a strong predictor of how well they will learn to read, and sound-letter knowledge also supports early spelling. In the upper elementary grades, phonological processing and literacy develop reciprocally, each feeding the other.

It sits within the wiki's broader reading and language page and leads into decoding. Phonological difficulties are a common feature of dyslexia, covered under learning disabilities.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Matters most for early reading and spelling instruction, and for older struggling readers whose decoding has not become automatic.
  • A rich home literacy environment can aid its development.
  • Learners with hearing loss may develop it differently depending on their early language exposure.

Target Learners¶

  • Preschool and early-elementary children learning to read (PK-3), and upper-elementary learners (grades 4-6) still building phonological processing.
  • Learners with dyslexia or reading difficulties.
  • Simultaneous bilinguals, who may show an advantage in phonological awareness.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Decoding and word reading.
  • Spelling and early writing.
  • Vocabulary growth.

Measures¶

  • The Phonological Awareness Test 2 (PAT 2) (Robertson & Salter, 2007): covers rhyming and detecting and manipulating phonemes, ages 5 to 9.
  • Test of Preschool Emergent Literacy (TOPEL), Phonological Awareness subtest (Lonigan, Wagner, Torgesen & Rashotte, 2007): blending and elision tasks for preschool children.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Auditory Processing: perceiving speech sounds depends on auditory processing.
  • Writing Skills: linking sounds to letters supports spelling and the mechanics of writing.
  • Access: hearing loss can affect phonological skills, depending on early language exposure.
  • Home Learning Environment: a strong home literacy environment can aid phonological awareness.
  • Morphological Awareness: knowing word parts includes knowing how sound and stress shift between related words (sign, signature).
  • Decoding: decoding applies knowledge of letter-sound relationships that rests on phonological awareness.
  • Working Memory: briefly holding sound information (phonological short-term memory) is part of phonological processing.
  • Vocabulary: phonological skills and vocabulary grow together.
  • Alphabet Knowledge and Print Awareness: letter knowledge predicts rhyming and blending, and learners with stronger phonological awareness show better print awareness.
  • Processing Speed: faster processing makes phonological processing more efficient.
  • English-Learner Status: simultaneous bilinguals may show an advantage in phonological awareness.
  • Motivation: children with weak phonological skills tend to read less for pleasure, which slows further growth.
