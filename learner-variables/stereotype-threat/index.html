Stereotype Threat¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 20 claims (15 show a difference, 3 mixed, 2 no difference) · 14 studies (6 review, 4 causal, 3 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 1 of 14 report an effect size · 20 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Stereotype threat is the situation in which a negative cultural stereotype about a group a learner identifies with, for example by race, gender, disability, age, income or language, leads to lower performance than the learner is capable of. Learners who know the stereotype can come to fear confirming it, especially when the relevant part of their identity is made salient, such as by asking for it before a test or describing a test as diagnostic of ability. The lower score then reflects the situation, not the learner's capability, and the group difference it produces is not evidence of a difference in ability. Proposed mechanisms in the LVN summaries include anxiety and preoccupation that take up working memory, weaker belonging, and, over time, disengagement from and devaluing of the domain. To be affected, learners must already know the stereotype, which develops through childhood. It is studied experimentally, by comparing performance under threatening and non-threatening testing conditions, and through interviews about stereotype awareness; it is a property of situations rather than a trait a learner carries. The LVN covers it from pre-kindergarten to adulthood, including adults learning technology skills. It bears on the wiki's pages on gender, race and ethnicity and socioeconomic status, where gaps are read as reflecting circumstances and treatment rather than as caused by the group.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • How a test or task is framed, and whether group identity is made salient, can change performance.
  • Discussing stereotypes openly and why they are baseless, and framing ability as able to grow, are described as protective.
  • Parents' and teachers' stereotyped beliefs, for example about girls and mathematics, can shape what learners expect of themselves.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners from groups targeted by academic stereotypes, once they are aware of those stereotypes.
  • Adolescents and adults in high-stakes evaluations; older adults facing stereotypes about memory or technology.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Performance on tests and evaluative tasks, especially in mathematics, reading and STEM.
  • Belonging, self-efficacy and persistence in a domain, and later course and career choices.

Measures¶

  • Diagnostic versus non-diagnostic test comparisons: compares learners vulnerable to the stereotype who are told a reading, writing or mathematics test is diagnostic of ability with those told it is not; lower scores under the diagnostic framing indicate threat.
  • Testing conditions (e.g., Ambady et al., 2001): varies testing conditions, such as making group identity salient, and compares performance.
  • Stereotype-Consciousness Interview (McKown & Strambler, 2009): measures children's knowledge of cultural stereotypes, a precondition for susceptibility.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Affect Regulation: threat produces anxiety and stress that can depress performance.
  • Belonging: threat can weaken belonging in a field and steer learners away from it.
  • Working Memory: worry about confirming a stereotype takes up working memory.
  • Motivation: threat can spur effort to disprove a stereotype or lead to disengagement.
  • Self-Efficacy: threat can lower self-efficacy, and a fixed view of ability can amplify it.
  • Home Learning Environment: caregivers' stereotyped beliefs, for example about girls and mathematics, can increase vulnerability.
  • Socioeconomic Status: stereotypes about low-income learners can produce threat on tests framed as diagnostic.
  • Academic and Critical Literacy: over time threat can lead learners to disengage from and devalue a domain.
  • Identity: learners from stigmatised groups may be more aware of stereotypes and so more exposed to threat.
  • Prior Knowledge: a learner must already know a stereotype to be affected by it.
  • Visual Processing: reported gender gaps on spatial tasks may reflect stereotypes about girls and STEM rather than ability.
  • English-Learner Status: stereotypes about non-native accents can affect how adult learners are judged.

Examples¶

  • Frame reading assessment as a game to reduce stereotype threat for boys
