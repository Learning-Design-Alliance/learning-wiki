Sleep¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 37 claims (22 show a difference, 10 mixed, 5 no difference) · 16 studies (7 causal, 4 review, 4 associational, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q3 · 6 of 16 report an effect size · 37 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Sleep is the period in which consciousness is suspended and the body and brain rest and recuperate. During sleep the brain consolidates memories, the body heals and recovers energy, and waste products of brain activity may be cleared. Its quantity, regularity and quality matter for nearly every aspect of cognition, as well as for physical and emotional well-being. When learners do not get enough good sleep, mood and cognitive skills suffer and focus is lost more easily. Sleep is measured with self-report and parent-report questionnaires on sleep habits, timing, quality and daytime sleepiness. The LVN treats it as relevant from pre-kindergarten to adulthood, and suggests extra cognitive supports, such as redirecting attention when a learner's mind wanders, for students who are short of sleep. It overlaps with physical well-being, of which adequate sleep is a part, and with long-term memory through consolidation.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Tired learners benefit from supports that reduce load on attention and memory, such as prompts that bring a wandering mind back.
  • Schedules and homework load bear on how much learners sleep.
  • Housing and neighbourhood conditions outside the classroom affect sleep quality.

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents, children and adults with short or irregular sleep.
  • Learners with sensory integration difficulties, trauma histories or unstable housing, who are more prone to sleep disturbance.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Attention, memory and self-regulation during learning.
  • Retention of new knowledge, including vocabulary.
  • Mood and emotional well-being.

Measures¶

  • Sleep Timing Questionnaire (Monk et al., 2003): self-report of sleep-wake patterns on weekdays and weekends.
  • Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (Buysse et al., 1989): self-report of seven aspects of sleep quality, including duration, disturbance, medication and daytime dysfunction.
  • Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (Owens et al., 2000): parent questionnaire on sleep behaviour for children aged 4 to 12.
  • Cleveland Adolescent Sleepiness Questionnaire (Spilsbury et al., 2007): measures daytime sleepiness for ages 11 to 17.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Adverse Experiences: trauma raises the risk of sleep disturbances.
  • Affect Regulation: poor sleep goes with anxiety and low mood.
  • Attention: sleep loss weakens sustained attention.
  • Long-Term Memory: sleep consolidates newly learned material into long-term memory.
  • Physical Well-Being: enough good sleep is part of physical health.
  • Socioeconomic Status: poor housing and low-income circumstances go with poorer sleep quality.
  • Working Memory: short-term sleep deprivation reduces short-term and working memory.
  • Executive Function: poor sleep reduces inhibitory control.
  • Self-Regulation: behavioural self-regulation declines when sleep is short or poor.
  • Sensory Integration: sensory integration difficulties, especially touch sensitivity, often come with sleep disturbance.
  • Safety: unsafe living conditions disturb sleep.
  • Communication Skills: sleep supports vocabulary learning, and deprivation affects verbal fluency.

Examples¶

  • Optimize consolidation through sufficient sleep, spaced and interleaved study, and rest periods between learning
  • Prioritize improving sleep quality for lower-performing students rather than increasing academic pressure
