Learning-Style Preferences¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 37 claims (20 show a difference, 9 mixed, 8 no difference) · 19 studies (7 causal, 5 review, 4 associational, 2 theoretical, 1 design), q1–q3 · 9 of 19 report an effect size · 36 claims rest on one study

Bottom line¶

Matching instruction to a learner's preferred "learning style" does not improve learning. The meshing hypothesis (teach visual learners visually, auditory learners aurally, and so on) has been tested directly many times, and the studies that cross learners' styles with matched and mismatched instruction do not find the interaction it predicts (Pashler et al., 2008; Rogowsky et al., 2020): Learning styles matching does not improve learning [+S]. Learners do report preferences, and those preferences can matter for comfort and choice, but they are not a basis for designing instruction.

The single studies listed below report differences between learners who describe their style differently (in beliefs, or in gains in one condition). None of them is a test of matching instruction to style, and none overturns the finding above. What does work is matching the format to the content: a diagram for spatial relations, narration with animation, worked examples for novices.

Description¶

Learning-style preferences are learners' reported preferences for one mode or format of instruction, often classified with instruments such as VARK. The preferences are real in the sense that learners report them, but the meshing hypothesis, that teaching in a learner's preferred style improves learning, has repeatedly failed when tested properly: studies that cross style with matched and mismatched instruction find no interaction. The page records the evidence so a design can account for what learners prefer without building on the matching claim. Matching the format to the content (a diagram for spatial relations) is supported; matching it to the learner's style is not.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Preferences can inform choice and comfort, but not the expectation of better learning from matching.
  • Time spent assessing styles is time not spent on practices with evidence.

Target Learners¶

  • All learners: the evidence does not support sorting learners by style.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Achievement, which matching to style has not been shown to improve.
