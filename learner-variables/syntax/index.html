Syntax¶

Description¶

Syntax is knowledge of how sentences work: how the order of words, the arrangement of clauses and the use of punctuation change what a sentence means. Readers draw on it to work out who did what to whom in a long sentence, and writers draw on it to turn an idea into a sentence that says what they intend. It is assessed through samples of a learner's speech or writing, coded for features such as words per clause and clauses per sentence, and through standardised language batteries with syntax subtests.

Syntactic knowledge starts to develop in early childhood and keeps growing through middle and high school, when texts in each subject use longer and more complex sentences and students are expected to write them. The Learner Variability Navigator treats it as a foundation for reading fluency and comprehension in the upper grades and for more sophisticated writing across genres. It is closely tied to morphological awareness, since many word endings carry grammatical meaning, and it draws on working memory to keep track of clauses while a sentence is read or written. It is a narrower part of what Reading and Language covers, and the spoken side of it overlaps with oral language.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Texts in the later grades, and discipline-specific texts in particular, carry denser sentence structures than early readers meet.
  • Sentence-combining, sentence-level modelling and discussion of how a sentence's structure changes its meaning are the usual supports.
  • Learners who are deaf or hard of hearing, and those still learning the language of instruction, may need syntax targeted directly.

Target Learners¶

  • Readers from preschool through high school who decode well but lose meaning in long or complex sentences.
  • Writers whose sentences stay short or tangled when the task calls for complex ideas.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Reading comprehension of increasingly complex texts.
  • Writing quality, including sentence variety and complexity across genres.

Measures¶

  • Measures of syntactic complexity (Beers & Nagy, 2007): Student writing samples are coded to identify how many words per clause and clauses per T-units (sentences) students use.
  • Wiig Criterion Referenced Inventory of Language (Wiig, 1990): Students' oral language is assessed through specific probes designed to assess their syntactic complexity and other metalinguistic skills.
  • Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals (CELF) (Semel, Wiig, & Secord, 2013): Measures language skills, including Syntax, in children aged six to 21.
  • Language Sample (Miller & Iglesias, 2012): Examines syntactic structures students produce relative to peers. Specific structures are expected to appear at certain points in development (see Brown's Stages of Language Development).

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Access: hearing loss can limit access to spoken grammar, and syntax is often a target of support for learners with cochlear implants.
  • Working Memory: holding clauses in mind while a sentence is read or written depends on working memory.
  • Writing Skills: control of complex sentence structure is part of what makes writing more sophisticated in any genre.
  • Metacognition: learners with stronger academic language, including complex syntax, tend to monitor their own understanding more while reading.
  • Morphological Awareness: word endings that mark tense, number and agreement sit where word structure and sentence structure meet.
  • English-Learner Status: learners using a language other than their home language may show distinctive grammatical patterns, and balanced bilinguals can show heightened syntactic awareness.
  • Socioeconomic Status: average grammatical skill differs by family income, a gap that reflects differences in resources and language exposure, not a cause.
  • General Cognitive Ability: syntax helps learners use context to work out figurative language, which LVN files under verbal reasoning.
  • Vocabulary: a larger vocabulary goes with longer, more complex sentences in both understanding and writing.
  • Executive Function: inhibiting competing sentence structures may help writers produce more complex sentences.
  • Inferencing: difficulty with sentence structure can make it harder to infer the meaning of idioms and metaphors.
  • Reading Fluency: a working knowledge of basic syntax is one of the skills that fluent reading rests on.
