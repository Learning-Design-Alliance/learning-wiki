Operations and Place Value¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 61 claims (44 show a difference, 10 mixed, 7 no difference) · 32 studies (12 causal, 12 associational, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 design, 1 review, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 6 of 32 report an effect size · 59 claims rest on one study

Description¶

This page brings together two factors from the Learner Variability Navigator. Operations covers addition, subtraction, multiplication and division: at first learners treat an operation as an instruction about which calculation to do, and LVN stresses that they also need a conceptual understanding of what each operation means, which lets them estimate an approximate answer as well as calculate an exact one quickly and accurately. Place value is understanding that in the base-ten system the same digit stands for different values (ones, tens, hundreds) depending on its position, which children work out as they explore the size of numbers and how numbers relate.

Operations are assessed with standardised numerical operations subtests that ask learners to write numbers, count and solve arithmetic problems up to fractions and negative integers, and with computational estimation tasks. Place value is a focus from preschool to grade 2; operations are followed from preschool through grade 10. Place value overlaps with number sense, which in the wiki covers numerical magnitude and the relations among numbers. Operations draw on arithmetic fact retrieval, working memory and executive function, and are affected by academic anxiety about mathematics.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Teaching procedures together with what an operation means supports estimation as well as exact calculation.
  • Place value develops through work with number size and relations in the early grades, often with physical or visual models.
  • Mathematics anxiety and stereotype cues can lower performance on calculation tasks.

Target Learners¶

  • Young children building base-ten understanding.
  • Primary and lower-secondary learners extending operations to multi-digit numbers, fractions and negative numbers.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Accurate, fluent calculation and computational estimation.
  • Conceptual understanding of operations and of the base-ten system, as a base for fractions and algebra.

Measures¶

  • Numerical Operations Subtest of the Wechsler Individual Achievement Test (WIAT-III) (Wechsler, 2009): Standardized test of procedural knowledge of Operations: writing numbers, counting and solving arithmetic problems, up to fractions and negative integers.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Executive Function: inhibition helps learners apply newly learned calculation strategies rather than habitual ones.
  • Long-Term Memory: calculation procedures are stored in long-term memory, and retrieval from it relates to performance.
  • Working Memory: verbal short-term and working memory relate to fluency with operations.
  • Spatial Ability: mental rotation skill predicts performance on calculation tasks.
  • Visual Processing: visual processing skills relate to procedural calculation.
  • Mathematical Flexibility: flexible use of estimation strategies goes with stronger conceptual knowledge of estimation.
  • Affect Regulation: mathematics anxiety can lower performance on operations problems.
  • Physical Well-being: physical fitness has been associated with better performance on arithmetic tasks.
  • Attention: attentive classroom behaviour goes with better computational estimation.
  • Metacognition: learners who judge their own performance accurately on division tend to perform better.
  • General Cognitive Ability: reasoning skill relates to computational estimation and to later performance on operations.
  • Arithmetic Fact Retrieval: fluent calculators retrieve facts for simple problems and use procedures for complex ones.

Examples¶

  • Use intensive practice of counting procedures in primary schools to strengthen and preserve future adults' arithmetic abilities
