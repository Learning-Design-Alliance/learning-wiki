English-Learner Status¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 90 claims (54 show a difference, 21 mixed, 15 no difference) · 65 studies (27 associational, 14 causal, 10 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 qualitative, 3 design, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 10 of 65 report an effect size · 89 claims rest on one study

Description¶

English-learner (EL) status marks a learner who is acquiring the language of instruction while learning content in it. Schools classify learners on a proficiency test and reclassify them once they reach a threshold, so the EL group changes composition over time: the learners who succeed leave it. In the research the status appears as a predictor of outcomes, as a moderator of instructional effects (language supports that help ELs more), and as the target of programmes such as bilingual and dual-language models. It is narrower than reading and language, which covers reading and language skill in every learner.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Because reclassified learners leave the group, the current EL group's results understate how former ELs do.
  • Content instruction in a language a learner is still acquiring needs language supports built in, not added afterwards.

Target Learners¶

  • English learners and multilingual learners in schools and colleges that teach in English, and second-language learners generally.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Language proficiency, reclassification, and content achievement in mathematics, science and reading.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do learners classified as English learners reach lower achievement and attainment than their peers, and does the gap hold for those who reach proficiency? ELs lag behind peers in achievement and attainment partly due to limited exposure to academic content [+M]
  • Does when a learner exits English-learner status (reclassification) change their later achievement and attainment? Timing of English learner reclassification influences later outcomes such as high school graduation [+M]
  • Does teaching through the learner's home language (dual language or mother-tongue instruction), rather than early transfer to the school language, improve school-language and subject learning? Teaching students in a language they understand benefits both language learning and broader learning [+M]
  • Do instructional supports and programmes help English learners more, less or as much as their fluent peers? Spoken–written advantages are larger for second-language and nonfluent first-language readers and for low prior knowledge learners [+M]
