Safety¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables Evidence · 74 claims (51 show a difference, 17 mixed, 6 no difference) · 44 studies (16 associational, 10 review, 6 qualitative, 5 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 design), q1–q4 · 2 of 44 report an effect size · 74 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Safety here means both being and feeling physically and psychologically secure in the places a learner spends time: home, school or work, the neighbourhood and the wider community. It covers violence and crime, bullying and cyberbullying, and, from adolescence on, a learner's own risk-taking behaviour such as substance use. It matters for learning because a learner who feels safe can focus on the task, while one who feels unsafe tends to miss school and take part less in class, losing learning opportunities. For adults, the LVN adds that learners who had bad experiences of school need to feel safe enough to learn again. It is measured with self-report bullying and victimisation scales, neighbourhood questionnaires and screening tools for risky behaviour. It overlaps with adverse experiences, since feeling unsafe can be traumatic, and with belonging.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • School climate, anti-bullying practice and predictable routines bear directly on whether learners can attend and focus.
  • Adult programmes should allow for learners whose participation in education changes relationships at home.
  • Attendance and participation are the visible signs of a safety problem before achievement is.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners in unsafe neighbourhoods or homes, and learners who are bullied or cyberbullied.
  • Adolescents, whose own risk-taking becomes part of the picture.
  • Adult learners re-entering education after negative school experiences.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Attendance, participation and engagement.
  • Focus on learning, and through it achievement in reading, writing and mathematics.

Measures¶

  • Electronic Bullying Questionnaire (Kowalski & Limber, 2007): self-report of how often a person has been cyberbullied or has cyberbullied others.
  • Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (World Health Organization): self-report screening of drinking behaviour and alcohol-related problems.
  • Peer Victimization Scale (Austin & Joseph, 1996): assesses being bullied at school for children aged 8 to 11.
  • Gatehouse Bullying Scale (Bond et al., 2007): assesses bullying victimisation at school for ages 10 to 15.
  • Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale (NEWS) (Saelens et al., 2003): residents' perceptions of neighbourhood features including safety, traffic hazards and crime.
  • Neighborhood Questionnaire (Greenberg & Lengua, 1995): a parent's view of neighbourhood safety, public services and violent crime.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Adverse Experiences: feeling unsafe can be traumatic, and trauma lowers felt safety.
  • Affect Regulation: bullying and victimisation bring more negative emotion and mental health strain.
  • Belonging: a stronger sense of belonging goes with less risk-taking.
  • Socioeconomic Status: low-income households face higher rates of violence.
  • Access: learners with hearing or vision loss can meet safety risks in getting around.
  • Physical Well-Being: unsafe neighbourhoods limit outdoor play and exercise, and bullying goes with poorer health.
  • Self-Regulation: impulsivity underlies some adolescent risk-taking.
  • Social Supports: support networks protect against bullying, though for some adults study can strain them.
  • Sleep: unsafe living conditions go with more disturbed sleep.
  • Attention: intoxication, one form of risk-taking, narrows what is attended to.
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills: peers, including online, can draw young people into risk-taking.
  • Executive Function: heavy alcohol use is associated with weaker inhibition.
