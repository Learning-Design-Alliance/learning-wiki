Critical Thinking¶

Description¶

Critical thinking is purposeful, reasoned evaluation: asking who is presenting information, in what context and on what evidence, considering several perspectives and kinds of evidence before drawing an inference, and using that to make decisions and solve problems. It combines a set of reasoning skills with a disposition to use them. It is often measured by questionnaires about attitudes toward critical thinking, as well as by reasoning tasks.

It is described as developing from early childhood, when children begin to ask questions and weigh evidence, through adolescence, when growing capacity for abstract, logical and hypothetical thinking changes what learners can do with it. It is treated as preparation for learners' roles as participants in society and as a basis for lifelong learning. What counts as critical thinking, and how it is expressed, can differ across cultures and communities.

It draws on metacognition (monitoring one's own reasoning), executive function and working memory, and on prior knowledge: learners need some knowledge of a topic to think critically about it. Related wiki pages include epistemic beliefs, which bear on how learners judge knowledge claims, and problem solving.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Needs content to work on: critical thinking about a topic presupposes some knowledge of it.
  • Discussion, debate and peer collaboration are described as occasions to practise it.
  • Drawing on learners' own identities and communities may support its development.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners from the early grades through high school, with abstract and hypothetical reasoning maturing in adolescence.
  • Learners who accept information without asking about its source or evidence.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Evaluating sources, evidence and arguments.
  • Reasoned decisions and solutions to problems.
  • Curiosity and further inquiry prompted by noticing gaps in knowledge.

Measures¶

  • California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (Facione et al., 2001): learner questionnaire on attitudes and dispositions toward critical thinking.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Prior Knowledge: learners may need some background knowledge of a topic before they can think critically about it.
  • Executive Function: inhibition lets learners set aside irrelevant beliefs while reasoning, and flexibility helps them consider alternatives.
  • Collaboration: working with peers may give learners a lower-stakes place to question and test ideas.
  • Communication Skills: discussion and debate give practice in evaluating claims and justifying positions.
  • Academic and Critical Literacy: critical thinking is described as part of acquiring academic literacies.
  • Interest: noticing gaps in one's own knowledge can spark curiosity and information seeking.
  • Identity: critical thinking can take different forms across cultures and communities.
  • Metacognition: monitoring one's own reasoning is described as underlying critical thinking.
  • Motivation: willingness to think a question through is described as a precondition for critical thinking.
  • Sleep: good sleep may support the abstract reasoning and problem solving critical thinking draws on.
  • Processing Speed: age-related gains in processing speed are associated with improvements in reasoning.
  • Visual Processing: visuospatial skills help learners represent ideas and the relations among them.
