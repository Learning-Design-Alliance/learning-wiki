## Description
How much uninterrupted time a learner actually gets, and whether progress survives the gap to the next session. It sets page length, whether a task finishes in one sitting, and whether a multi-week deliverable survives a bad month. The awkward part is that the schedule which produces the best retention is the one learners reliably judge worst: spacing feels harder and less effective while producing more durable learning [~S], so this dimension cannot be designed by learner preference.

## Implications

### Context
- Sets whether a task must complete in one session, and whether progress is recoverable after an interruption.
- Distributing practice beats massing it for retention, at a cost in perceived fluency [+S].
- Learners misjudge this specifically, so self-scheduled study drifts toward massing [-M].

### Target Learners
- Learners with fragmented time — carers, shift workers, anyone studying between obligations.
- Learners whose attendance is irregular, for whom continuity rather than duration is the binding constraint.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Dominates long-term retention, which is what most instruction is nominally for.
- Barely affects immediate post-test performance, which is exactly why massing looks good and is not.

## Related Learner Variables
- Self-regulation — a learner who cannot pace themselves needs the schedule imposed.
- Attention — decides how much can be done inside a session; this decides how sessions relate.
- Access — a device shared with a household changes when study is possible at all.

## Examples
- [Spaced Practice](../strategies/spaced-practice-scheduling.md) — the scheduling response
- [Interleaving](../strategies/interleaving.md) — mixes types within a session rather than blocking them

- [Adaptive Learning](../elements/adaptive-learning.md) — adjusts what is scheduled next from performance
- [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md) — decouples progress from elapsed time entirely
