Learner Variables¶

Canonical learner characteristics claims report findings about — one page per variable, so the same concept does not fragment across differently-worded tags.

75 entries · 0 stable · 75 in review · 0 drafts

In Review¶

Jump to: A · B · C · D · E · G · H · I · L · M · N · O · P · R · S · T · V · W

A¶

  • Academic and Critical Literacy - A learner's command of the ways of reading, writing and reasoning used in academic disciplines and real-world domains, including questioning a text's purpose and bias.
  • Academic Anxiety - Anxiety tied to learning and assessment in a subject, such as mathematics anxiety, test anxiety and foreign-language anxiety.
  • Academic Self-Concept - A learner's perception of their own ability in an academic domain, such as how good they think they are at mathematics or at reading.
  • Access - Whether a learner can perceive and operate the material at all — device, bandwidth, sensory and motor.
  • Achievement Emotions - Emotions tied to learning activities and their outcomes, such as enjoyment, boredom, frustration, pride, shame and hope.
  • Achievement Goal Orientation - The purposes a learner pursues in achievement settings: to master the material (mastery goals) or to demonstrate competence relative to others (performance goals), each in an approach or avoidance form.
  • Adverse Experiences - Events that can cause trauma, such as abuse, neglect, witnessing violence or instability at home, and the chronic stress they leave behind.
  • Affect Regulation - What a learner does with frustration, anxiety and failure while the work is still in front of them.
  • Age and Developmental Stage - A learner's age, grade level or stage of development, from early childhood to adulthood, as it bears on what instruction suits them.
  • Algebraic Thinking - A learner's ability to generalise, represent, justify and reason about mathematical structures and relationships, from patterns in arithmetic to equations and functions.
  • Alphabet Knowledge and Print Awareness - A young learner's knowledge of letter names, shapes and sounds, and their understanding that print carries meaning and works in particular ways on the page.
  • Arithmetic Fact Retrieval - A learner's ability to recall basic number combinations, such as single-digit sums and products, quickly and accurately from memory.
  • Attention - How long a learner can sustain focus on one thing, and how easily something else takes it away.
  • Auditory Processing - How the brain makes sense of the sounds that reach it through hearing: locating, distinguishing and interpreting sounds, especially speech.
  • Autism Spectrum Disorder - Autism, a lifelong developmental difference in social communication and in patterns of behaviour, interests and sensory processing, recorded in research by clinical diagnosis or school category; it varies widely between people and is distinct from intellectual ability.

B¶

  • Belonging - Whether a learner expects to be treated as a full participant in this setting, and how much of their attention that question consumes.

C¶

  • Civic Mindedness - A learner's sense of themselves as a contributor to their community: attending to others' needs, understanding how civic systems work and affect people, and acting with others toward shared goals.
  • Collaboration - A learner's ability to work effectively and respectfully with others toward a shared goal: expressing and listening to ideas, compromising and sharing responsibility for the work.
  • Communication Skills - A learner's ability to express thoughts, ideas and feelings and to understand others, through speech, sign, writing, gesture, drawing or technology, including talking about subject matter such as mathematics.
  • Creativity - A learner's ability to generate novel and useful ideas, usually measured as divergent thinking: the fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration of ideas produced for an open-ended problem.
  • Critical Thinking - A learner's ability and disposition to evaluate information and arguments, weigh evidence and perspectives, and reason to a judgement or decision.

D¶

  • Decoding - A learner's ability to turn written words into spoken words and meanings, by applying letter-sound knowledge to unfamiliar words and by recognising familiar words at sight.
  • Digital Literacy - Whether a learner can operate the interface the instruction is delivered through, independently of whether they can reach it.
  • Domain Context - The sector, role or setting a scenario has to be placed in before a learner recognises it as their own.

E¶

  • Emotional Intelligence - A learner's ability to perceive, understand, use and manage emotions in themselves and others.
  • English-Learner Status - Whether a learner is still acquiring the language of instruction (an English learner, multilingual learner or second-language learner), as classified by a school system or measured by proficiency tests.
  • Epistemic Beliefs - A learner's beliefs about the nature of knowledge and knowing: whether knowledge is certain or tentative, simple or complex, handed down by authority or built from evidence.
  • Executive Function - The set of control processes, inhibition, working-memory updating and cognitive flexibility, that let a learner direct attention and behaviour toward a goal.

G¶

  • Gender - A learner's gender as recorded in research data, used to describe differences in participation, attitudes and outcomes and to test whether an instructional effect differs between groups.
  • General Cognitive Ability - A learner's general reasoning and problem-solving capacity, measured by intelligence or general ability tests, as it bears on learning and on how instruction works.
  • Geometric Reasoning - A learner's ability to define, analyse and argue about shapes and spatial relationships, up to writing formal geometric proofs.

H¶

  • Home Learning Environment - The learning opportunities, materials, practices and attitudes a learner's home, family and community provide outside formal instruction.

I¶

  • Identity - A learner's developing sense of who they are, including the social identities, such as race, ethnicity, gender, culture, language and disability, that shape how they meet the world.
  • Inferencing - A reader's ability to fill in what a text leaves unsaid by connecting it with background knowledge, experience and other parts of the text.
  • Interest - A learner's engagement with and liking for a topic, whether a lasting personal interest or interest sparked in the moment by the situation.

L¶

  • Learning Disabilities and Developmental Differences - Identified learning, developmental and intellectual disabilities (dyslexia and other specific learning disabilities, autism, intellectual disability), and the special educational needs that follow from them.
  • Learning-Style Preferences - A learner's stated preference for receiving information in one form (such as visual, auditory or kinaesthetic), and the claim that matching instruction to it improves learning.
  • Long-Term Memory - The memory system that stores knowledge, skills and experiences over hours to decades so they can be retrieved and used later.

M¶

  • Mathematical Flexibility - A learner's ability to hold and move between several representations of numbers and several strategies, choosing the one that suits the problem.
  • Measurement - A learner's understanding of measuring: picking out an attribute such as length, area or time and quantifying it by comparison with a unit of the same kind.
  • Metacognition - A learner's awareness of their own thinking and their ability to monitor and steer it: planning an approach, checking understanding and changing strategy when something is not working.
  • Mindset - A learner's belief about whether ability or intelligence is fixed or can grow with effort and good strategies (implicit theories of intelligence).
  • Morphological Awareness - A learner's awareness of the meaningful parts of words (roots, prefixes and suffixes) and ability to use them to read, understand, spell and build longer words.
  • Motivation - Whether a learner is willing to spend effort here, and what makes the spending feel worth it.

N¶

  • Need for Cognition - A learner's tendency to engage in and enjoy effortful thinking, such as complex problem solving and abstract reasoning.
  • Number Sense - A learner's understanding of numerical magnitudes and the relations among numbers, shown in tasks such as comparing two quantities or placing a number on a number line.

O¶

  • Operations and Place Value - A learner's procedural and conceptual understanding of arithmetic operations and of the base-ten place-value system that written numbers rely on.
  • Oral Language - A learner's spoken language skills, including vocabulary, grammar, listening comprehension and the ability to tell an organised story.

P¶

  • Personality Traits - A learner's characteristic patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving, usually described by the Big Five: conscientiousness, openness, agreeableness, extraversion and neuroticism.
  • Phonological Awareness - A learner's ability to notice and work with the sounds of spoken language (rhymes, syllables and individual phonemes) and to process those sounds quickly when reading and spelling.
  • Physical Well-Being - A learner's physical health and fitness, including nutrition, regular exercise and adequate sleep, as it bears on cognition, mood and learning.
  • Prior Knowledge - A learner's existing domain knowledge in the subject area before instruction begins.
  • Problem Solving - A learner's ability to work through unfamiliar challenges by setting goals and bringing knowledge, resources and creative thinking to bear on a solution.
  • Processing Speed - The rate at which a learner takes in information, makes sense of it and produces an appropriate response.
  • Proportional Reasoning - A learner's understanding of ratios and the relationships between them, as expressed in fractions, percentages and rates.

R¶

  • Race and Ethnicity - A learner's racial or ethnic group as recorded in research and administrative data, used to describe gaps in opportunity and outcomes and to test whether an instructional effect differs across groups.
  • Reading and Language - What a learner can read fluently, and whether the language of instruction is the one they think in.
  • Reading Fluency - A learner's ability to read connected text accurately, at a good pace and smoothly, silently or aloud, so that attention is free for comprehension.

S¶

  • Safety - Being, and feeling, physically and psychologically secure at home, at school or work, and in the neighbourhood and wider community.
  • Self-Efficacy - A learner's belief in their own ability to carry out the actions needed to succeed at a particular task or in a particular subject.
  • Self-Regulation - Whether a learner can plan, monitor and adjust their own work without the structure being supplied.
  • Sensory Integration - The brain's ability to take in, organise and combine information from several senses and turn it into fitting physical and emotional responses.
  • Sleep - How much, how regularly and how well a learner sleeps, the period in which the body and brain rest, recover and consolidate memories.
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills - A learner's ability to understand others' perspectives and feelings and to build and keep positive relationships with peers, teachers and colleagues.
  • Social Supports - The network of people, such as family, friends, teachers and community, that a learner has and believes they could turn to for help.
  • Socioeconomic Status - A learner's family income, wealth, parental education and occupation, and the resources and constraints that come with them.
  • Spatial Ability - The ability to generate, retain, retrieve and transform visual and spatial images, such as mentally rotating an object or relating shapes to one another.
  • Statistical Reasoning - A learner's ability to reason about data and uncertainty: posing questions, collecting and organising data, and describing and drawing inferences from it.
  • Stereotype Threat - The risk that a learner performs below their actual capability when a negative stereotype about a group they belong to is made salient in an evaluative setting.
  • Syntax - A learner's grasp of how sentences are built: how word order, sentence structure and punctuation carry meaning, in reading, speech and writing.

T¶

  • Time and Continuity - How much uninterrupted time a learner actually gets, and whether progress survives the gap until the next session.

V¶

  • Visual Processing - The cognitive work of making sense of what the eyes take in: recognising, ordering and interpreting visual information such as letters, numbers, shapes and diagrams.
  • Vocabulary - The words a learner knows and can use, receptively and expressively, including the academic and subject-specific words that school texts depend on.

W¶

  • Working Memory - How much a learner can hold and manipulate at once before the material itself becomes the obstacle.
  • Writing Skills - A learner's ability to write, from the foundational skills of handwriting, spelling and conventions to composing texts in a genre and arguing from evidence.
