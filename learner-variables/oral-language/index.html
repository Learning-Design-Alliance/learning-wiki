Oral Language¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 104 claims (61 show a difference, 20 mixed, 23 no difference) · 74 studies (26 associational, 16 review, 15 quant-synthesis, 14 causal, 2 theoretical, 1 design), q1–q3 · 25 of 74 report an effect size · 101 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Oral language covers the spoken side of language: understanding what is said, using vocabulary and grammar to express ideas, and organising sentences into a coherent account or story. The Learner Variability Navigator includes two factors here: narrative skills, the ability to tell and understand stories, and the broader language skills that support early mathematics as well as reading. Narrative skill is often assessed by asking a child to tell a story from a wordless picture book or sequencing cards and scoring its overall structure and its sentence-level detail; early literacy batteries assess related skills such as vocabulary and phonological awareness.

It matters most in preschool and the early grades. Telling stories helps children understand the stories they later read, and spoken language skill predicts the quality of early written composition. LVN also notes that the language skills that matter for reading matter for early mathematics: learning number words, counting and talking about operations all run through language, and children with dyslexia, which often involves difficulties with phonological processing and verbal working memory, may also find mathematics harder. It overlaps with Reading and Language, vocabulary, syntax and phonological awareness, and with the language side of learning disabilities.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Shared reading, conversation and storytelling at home and in preschool are where these skills mostly grow.
  • Mathematics instruction in the early grades carries language demands of its own, such as number words and explanation.
  • Learners with hearing loss may have stronger language skills in a signed language than in spoken language.

Target Learners¶

  • Preschool and early-primary children, especially those who arrive with less developed spoken language.
  • Children with dyslexia or developmental language difficulties, in both literacy and early mathematics.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Reading comprehension and early written composition.
  • Early mathematics, including counting and operations.

Measures¶

  • Story Generation Tasks: Macrostructure and microstructure of narrative elements can be assessed by having students tell a story using a wordless picture book or sequencing cards.
  • The Phonological Awareness Test 2 (PAT 2) (Robertson & Salter, 2007): Measures all aspects of Phonological Awareness, including rhyming and the ability to detect and manipulate phonemes.
  • Test of Preschool Early Literacy Skills (TOPEL) (Lonigan, Wagner, Torgesen, & Rashotte, 2007): Measures emergent literacy, including print knowledge, vocabulary, and phonological awareness.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Access: hearing loss can affect spoken language and narrative skills, though early support and a strong language foundation help.
  • Home Learning Environment: language-rich homes give children spoken language and storytelling skills before school starts.
  • Socioeconomic Status: average narrative and vocabulary skills differ by family income, a gap reflecting differences in resources and language exposure, not a cause.
  • Syntax: telling a coherent story depends on command of sentence structure.
  • Vocabulary: a strong vocabulary is part of telling a good story and of following spoken mathematics.
  • Writing Skills: spoken language skill predicts the quality of early written composition.
  • Phonological Awareness: oral language and awareness of speech sounds develop together in the preschool years.
  • Arithmetic Fact Retrieval: phonological skills help children store and retrieve number facts, so language difficulties can show up in fact recall.
  • Number Sense: vocabulary and early language skills support learning number words, counting and cardinal meanings.
  • Long-Term Memory: storing and retrieving number facts verbally links phonological skills to arithmetic.
  • Operations and Place Value: children with stronger language skills tend to do better on arithmetic operations.
  • Safety: exposure to neighbourhood violence has been linked to lower vocabulary scores.
