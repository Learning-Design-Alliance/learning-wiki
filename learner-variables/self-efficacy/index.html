Self-Efficacy¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 160 claims (118 show a difference, 25 mixed, 17 no difference) · 132 studies (52 associational, 27 causal, 20 review, 14 qualitative, 8 design, 6 theoretical, 5 quant-synthesis), q1–q4 · 28 of 132 report an effect size · 156 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Self-efficacy is Bandura's term for task-specific confidence: how sure a learner is that they can do what a task requires. It is measured with self-report scales tied to a domain (mathematics self-efficacy, writing self-efficacy, computer self-efficacy). It predicts effort, persistence and achievement, it moderates some instructional effects, and it is itself an outcome that instruction can raise, chiefly through experiences of success, seeing similar others succeed, and specific feedback. It is narrower than motivation and distinct from general self-esteem.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Raised by mastery experiences, so tasks sequenced for early success and feedback that names what the learner did well matter.
  • Self-report scales are sensitive to wording and timing; a gain in confidence is not a gain in skill.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with low confidence in a subject, such as students who have struggled with mathematics or writing before.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Persistence, choice of further study in the subject, and achievement.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does a learner's belief that they can succeed at a task predict whether they start and persist, and which goals and feedback raise it? Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]
  • Which instruction raises learners' self-efficacy: close goals, feedback on progress, guided practice? Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [+M]
  • Does a gain in reported confidence mean a gain in skill? Self-efficacy judgments correspond less well to behavior in learning settings than in performance settings [+M]
