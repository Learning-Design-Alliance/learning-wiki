## How to read this page

**Autism is a characteristic of a learner, not a cause of poor outcomes and not a measure of ability.** Autistic learners differ from one another as much as any other group does: in language (from no speech to fluent speech), in intellectual ability (anywhere across the range, assessed separately from the diagnosis), in sensory sensitivities and in interests. A group average below says nothing about the learner in front of you.

The claims on this page are of two kinds, listed separately. **Differences found** compare autistic with non-autistic people on some measure (processing speed, multisensory integration, a hiring assessment, preparation for life after school); a marker there says whether a difference was found (+), only under conditions (~) or not found (−). Some of these sources use clinical language ("deficit", "impairment"); the summaries keep what was measured and how, not that framing. A gap in outcomes such as transition preparation also reflects services, expectations and access, not autism alone. **Instruction studied with autistic learners** reports what a support or teaching approach did for autistic learners; a marker there rates that approach's effect, and none of those studies compares autistic with non-autistic learners. For design, the second list is usually the useful one.

## Description
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is defined in current diagnostic manuals (APA, 2013) by differences in social communication and interaction, together with restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, including sensory sensitivities, present from early development. The term "spectrum" records that presentation varies widely: some autistic people use no speech and need substantial support, others are fluent speakers who live independently and experience mainly social and sensory differences. Many autistic people and advocates prefer identity-first language ("autistic learner"); research sources mostly use "students with ASD". Both appear on this page as the sources use them.

In the research autism appears as a population a design targets (communication supports, self-monitoring, parent-mediated intervention), as a predictor (autistic and non-autistic groups compared on cognitive, sensory or later-life measures), and occasionally as a moderator. It is recorded by clinical diagnosis or by a school's special education category, which do not identify the same learners. It is distinct from [learning disabilities and developmental differences](learning-disabilities.md) in general (that page covers the wider group, autism included), and from intellectual disability, which can co-occur but is not implied by the diagnosis. Reported prevalence has risen since the 1980s, which reflects a broader diagnostic definition and greater awareness at least as much as any change in underlying prevalence; the belief that vaccines cause autism has no scientific support.

## Implications

### Context
- Calibrate expectations to the learner's own profile, assessed separately for language, intellectual ability, sensory needs and interests, not to the diagnosis.
- Predictable structure, advance notice of changes and more than one way to communicate (spoken, written, pictorial, a device) are low-cost supports that serve many learners, not only autistic ones.
- What a learner is entitled to (accommodations, a communication device, an IEP or EHC plan) is set by law and by the learner's plan, not only by the designer.

### Target Learners
- Autistic learners at any age, from early childhood to adults in work, with or without intellectual disability and with or without speech.
- Learners with a documented autism diagnosis, whose support should follow their individual profile rather than a generic "autism" template
- Learners whose intellectual ability may be anywhere across the range independently of the diagnosis, which needs separate assessment

### Target Learning Objectives
- Functional communication (requesting, commenting, conversation), where aided augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is the best-studied support recorded here.
- On-task and self-managed behaviour in school settings, through self-monitoring.
- Social participation and preparation for life after school.
- Less disruption from changes in routine, where predictable structure is itself a support

## Description
**Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** involves early-appearing, functionally impairing disturbances in three domains (APA, 2013): **social interaction** (not initiating conversation, avoiding eye contact, apparent preference for being alone); **communication** (ranging from absent speech to one-word responses, echoed speech, or difficulty reciprocating conversation and reading nonverbal cues like facial expression and gesture); and **repetitive behavior or interests** (stereotyped movements like rocking, marked distress at small changes in routine, or unusually intense, narrowly fixated interests). The "spectrum" qualifier reflects genuinely wide variation in severity — the DSM-5 dropped the earlier separate Asperger's disorder diagnosis (previously described as a milder form, with average-or-higher intelligence and strong vocabulary but social/communication impairment) once research failed to show it differed qualitatively from the broader autism spectrum. ASD is not the same condition as intellectual disability, though the two can co-occur, and the DSM-5 specifies ASD symptoms are not caused or explained by intellectual disability.

ASD affects an estimated 1 in 88 U.S. children, about 5 times more common in boys (1 in 54) than girls (1 in 252) (CDC, 2012). Reported prevalence has risen sharply since the 1980s, but the rise is difficult to interpret causally — a broadened diagnostic definition, increased community identification effort, and greater clinical awareness of subtler presentations are all plausible contributors, separate from any true change in underlying prevalence. The disorder is strongly genetically influenced (identical-twin concordance 60-90% vs. 5-10% for fraternal twins/siblings; Autism Genome Project Consortium, 2007), implicating genes involved in synaptic circuit formation, alongside environmental risk factors (pollutant exposure, urban/rural residence, vitamin D deficiency) that may interact with genetic susceptibility. **There is no scientific evidence linking ASD to vaccination** — a large controlled study comparing vaccination histories of 256 children with ASD against 752 control children found no relationship between the number of vaccine immunogens received in the first two years of life and later ASD diagnosis (DeStefano, Price, & Weintraub, 2013).

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Awareness that ASD's three defining domains (social, communicative, behavioral) can present very differently across the spectrum, from significant impairment to independent adult functioning with subtler social/communication difficulty
- Distinguishing ASD from intellectual disability, since instructional expectations and supports should be calibrated to actual cognitive ability, not assumed from the ASD diagnosis alone
#### Constraints
- The "vaccines cause autism" belief has no scientific support and should not inform any instructional or family-communication decision
- Rising reported prevalence reflects diagnostic and awareness changes at least as much as any true prevalence change, and should not itself be treated as evidence of a single new causal factor

### Target Learners
- Students with a documented ASD diagnosis, whose specific support needs should be based on their individual profile across the three domains rather than a single generic "autism" template
- Students whose intellectual ability may be average, above average, or impaired independently of their ASD diagnosis, requiring separate assessment

### Target Learning Objectives
- Functional communication and social participation appropriate to the student's specific profile
- Reduced disruption from changes in routine, where predictable structure is itself a support
