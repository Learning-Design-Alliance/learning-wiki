# Academic and Critical Literacy

## Description
This page brings together two related factors from the Learner Variability Navigator; its third, Core Academic Literacies (general fluency across the core subjects), is on [prior knowledge](prior-knowledge.md). Disciplinary literacy is the ability to read, write, speak and present in the ways each discipline uses (proofs, lab reports, historical arguments) and, for adults, in real-world domains such as health and finance. Critical literacy is the ability to identify a text's purpose, motives and possible biases, which supports comprehension and critical thinking and prepares learners for civic participation.

They are assessed through performance tasks rather than a single test: argumentative essays judged on discipline-appropriate evidence, scenario-based reading-to-write assessments, Socratic seminars and media inquiry projects scored with rubrics, and, for adults, functional health and everyday literacy tests. LVN describes them across the life course: young children gain knowledge through play and experience; in middle childhood and adolescence learners build fluency in each subject and engage more when they can connect content with life beyond school; reading and writing in secondary school become organised by discipline; and adults need the conventions of their own field and of domains like health and finance. LVN reports that students explicitly taught to question texts show higher reading comprehension. These skills build on [prior knowledge](prior-knowledge.md), [inferencing](inferencing.md) and [critical thinking](critical-thinking.md); the adult health and financial strand overlaps with [digital literacy](digital-literacy.md) and [number sense](number-sense.md).

## Implications

### Context
- Secondary schools organise reading and writing by subject, so each discipline's conventions need to be taught where they are used.
- Adult education often frames literacy around real tasks such as health information and personal finance.
- Texts that connect to learners' identities and lives, and explicit instruction in questioning a text, are common supports.

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students meeting discipline-specific texts and writing tasks.
- Adults building expertise in a field, or managing health and financial information; English learners acquiring academic language.

### Target Learning Objectives
- Comprehension and production of discipline-specific texts.
- Critical reading for purpose and bias, perspective taking and civic participation.
- Functional health and financial literacy in adults.

## Measures
- **Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults (TOFHLA)** (Parker et al., 1995): This test measures the reading comprehension and numeracy skill components of health literacy in adults using real-life health situations.
- **Revised Observed Tasks of Daily Living (OTDL-R)** (Diehl et al., 2005): This measure includes task-based questions relating to health, financial, and everyday situational literacy.
- **Socratic Seminars** (Polite & Adams, 1997): Students prepare questions and reflections on a text and participate in discussion on the text following a set protocol which can be assessed by teacher-created rubrics.
- **Media Inquiry Projects** (Goodman, 2005): Students create different forms of media (e.g., documentaries, podcasts) based on varied texts and are assessed on their presentations using teacher-created rubrics which include questioning skills and ability to see multiple perspectives.
- **Global Integrated Scenario Based Assessments** (GISA; Shore et al., 2017): Students are presented with a variety of computer-based sources on a particular topic and are asked to complete scaffolded writing summaries to address a specific task.
- **Disciplinary writing task** (Monte-Sano et al., 2014): Students complete argumentative essays within the chosen content area to assess their use of content-specific evidence and discipline conventions in writing.

## Related Learner Variables
- [Prior Knowledge](prior-knowledge.md): background knowledge of a field, such as finance, goes with stronger literacy in that field.
- [English-Learner Status](english-learner-status.md): learners acquiring the language of instruction build academic language in it in different ways depending on their schooling in their first language.
- [Communication Skills](communication-skills.md): requesting, understanding and discussing information, such as health information, goes with stronger domain literacy.
- [Processing Speed](processing-speed.md): declines in processing speed among older adults go with lower health literacy.
- [Self-Efficacy](self-efficacy.md): adults with higher health literacy report more confidence in managing their own health (LVN files self-efficacy under learner mindset).
- [Access](access.md): hearing loss can block access to health information and so to health literacy.
- [Physical Well-being](physical-wellbeing.md): lower health literacy is associated with poorer health outcomes in adults.
- [Socioeconomic Status](socioeconomic-status.md): health literacy may soften the link between low income and poor health outcomes; income marks resources, not a cause of literacy.
- [Metacognition](metacognition.md): monitoring one's reading goals and strategies supports critical analysis of a text.
- [Affect Regulation](affect-regulation.md): emotional engagement during instruction can enrich critical discussion of content.
- [Social Awareness and Relationship Skills](social-awareness-and-relationship-skills.md): critical reading involves taking other perspectives, which can build empathy.
- [Home Learning Environment](home-learning-environment.md): students value curricula and literacy environments that let them explore identity, relationships and society critically.
