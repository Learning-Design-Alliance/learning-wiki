Adverse Experiences¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 31 claims (26 show a difference, 2 mixed, 3 no difference) · 18 studies (7 review, 6 associational, 2 theoretical, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 design), q1–q3 · 2 of 18 report an effect size · 31 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Adverse experiences are events that can be traumatic: physical, sexual or emotional abuse and neglect, witnessing violence at home or in the community, family instability, and discrimination experienced as racial trauma. The concern for learning is the stress response they set off. Repeated or prolonged stress releases stress hormones that can change how the body and brain develop, and childhood is a sensitive period for this, so effects on cognition, health, emotional well-being and achievement can carry into adulthood. Exposure is usually measured with self-report or parent-report trauma checklists. The LVN treats it as relevant at every age it covers, from pre-kindergarten to adult education: for children it bears on learning to read, write and do mathematics, and for adults it includes earlier negative experiences of school that make returning to learning harder. The summaries are equally clear that harm is not fixed: children's brains are plastic enough to recover, evidence-based interventions exist, and protective factors such as social supports, a sense of belonging and a positive identity can buffer the effects. It overlaps with affect regulation, which covers the emotional side, and with safety.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Classrooms and programmes that feel physically and emotionally safe, predictable and supportive are the condition for learning after trauma, not an extra.
  • Learners may need support with attention, memory and self-regulation that is not a sign of low ability.
  • Adults returning to education may carry negative memories of school; low-stakes entry points help.

Target Learners¶

  • Children and adolescents exposed to abuse, neglect, violence or family instability.
  • Refugee and displaced learners, and learners facing discrimination.
  • Adult learners whose earlier schooling was a negative experience.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Reading, writing and mathematics achievement, through effects on attention, memory and self-regulation.
  • Emotional well-being, engagement and persistence.

Measures¶

  • Brief Trauma Questionnaire (Schnurr et al., 2002): self-report of lifetime exposure to traumatic events such as accidents, combat, illness, abuse and disasters.
  • Traumatic Events Screening Inventory for Children (TESI-C) (Ippen et al., 2002): parent-report and child-report versions screening a child's exposure to injury, disaster, domestic and community violence, and abuse.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Affect Regulation: trauma raises the risk of mental health difficulties and of weaker emotion regulation.
  • Belonging: a strong sense of belonging can buffer the academic effects of trauma.
  • Executive Function: maltreatment is linked with weaker inhibition and cognitive flexibility, while flexible coping goes with better outcomes.
  • Long-Term Memory: childhood maltreatment is linked with changes in brain structures that form long-term memories.
  • Physical Well-Being: trauma has long-term costs for physical health.
  • Safety: feeling unsafe can itself be traumatic, and trauma lowers a learner's sense of safety.
  • Sleep: trauma raises the risk of sleep disturbances.
  • Social Supports: supportive relationships buffer adverse events, and abuse by trusted people can erode them.
  • Socioeconomic Status: low-income circumstances bring more exposure to chronic stress and adversity, and less cushioning against it.
  • Working Memory: abuse in childhood is associated with short-term and working memory deficits.
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills: neglect can hinder social skills, though trauma can also deepen empathy.
  • Attention: chronic stress can make sustained attention to study harder.
