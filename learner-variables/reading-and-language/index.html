Reading and Language¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 194 claims (124 show a difference, 29 mixed, 41 no difference) · 139 studies (36 causal, 34 associational, 29 quant-synthesis, 25 review, 8 theoretical, 4 design, 3 qualitative), q1–q4 · 32 of 139 report an effect size · 185 claims rest on one study

Description¶

What a learner can read without effort, and whether the language of instruction is the one they think in. LVN's largest factor family — decoding, vocabulary, fluency, composition, disciplinary literacy and language of instruction all sit here. The load-bearing fact is that decoding which is not automatic spends the same budget comprehension needs, so a reading problem presents as a thinking problem [+S].

How strongly it relates to achievement¶

Mathematics. These are correlations across meta-analyses (Breit et al. 2025, a systematic review of 30 meta-analyses; overview [+W]): how strongly learners who differ on this characteristic differ in mathematics achievement, not how much changing it would change achievement. Many of these characteristics are correlated with one another, and the review is about mathematics only.

  • Verbal achievement r = .67, reading .52, comprehensive language .51, mathematics vocabulary .49, language .42: the strongest group of correlates of mathematics achievement. Claim [+W]

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Sets text density, glossing, the plain-language floor, and whether the language at home differs from the language of instruction.
  • Vocabulary is incremental and context-dependent: exposure alone is unreliable, and explicit instruction alone is narrow — the pairing beats either [+M].
  • A page that is hard to read is indistinguishable, in its results, from a page that is hard to understand.

Target Learners¶

  • Multilingual learners, for whom the gap between conversational and academic language is the usual issue rather than ability.
  • Any learner in a discipline with its own register — mathematics and science included.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Gates comprehension of every text-delivered outcome, so it is a validity threat wherever reading is not the construct.
  • Directly targeted where literacy itself is the goal.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does a learner's oral language and vocabulary predict how well they come to read and understand texts? A 1997 conference paper reports, citing Sticht, a positive correlation between preschool oral language skills and later reading achievement [+W]
  • Does systematic phonics teaching improve reading more than unsystematic, no or other phonics-light teaching, and for which readers? Systematic phonics outperforms unsystematic or no-phonics instruction on children's reading overall, but its advantage over whole-word teaching on novel words is untested in children and contested [+S]
  • How much do repeated encounters add, and do words met in input last without review? Vocabulary Knowledge Grows Incrementally With Repeated Encounters In Varied Contexts [+S]
  • Does teaching through the learner's home language (dual language or mother-tongue instruction), rather than early transfer to the school language, improve school-language and subject learning? Teaching students in a language they understand benefits both language learning and broader learning [+M]

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Working memory — effortful decoding consumes it before comprehension begins.
  • Prior knowledge — vocabulary is partly a knowledge measure, which is why the two correlate so strongly.
  • Access — audio and text-to-speech move the same content past a decoding barrier.

Examples¶

  • Accessible Syntax — lowers sentence-level demand without lowering content
  • Explicit Vocabulary Instruction — direct teaching of the words the text assumes
  • Pre-Teaching Vocabulary — front-loads the terms so the reading is not the first encounter
  • Clarify Vocabulary, Symbols, and Language Structures — the disciplinary-register case
  • Audiobooks — decouples the content from the decoding demand
