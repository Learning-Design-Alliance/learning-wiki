Social Supports¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 94 claims (73 show a difference, 15 mixed, 6 no difference) · 84 studies (23 associational, 16 review, 15 qualitative, 11 causal, 8 theoretical, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 design), q1–q3 · 8 of 84 report an effect size · 94 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Social supports are the people available to help a learner if needed, and the learner's perception that they are there: parents and family, friends and classmates, teachers and other school staff, and the wider community. The support they give can be emotional (care and compassion), appraisal (useful feedback), instrumental (time, money, resources) and informational (advice). The LVN stresses perception: feeling that support would be there, even if it is never called on, contributes to well-being and academic success, and help that is actually received can strengthen that perception and a learner's sense of efficacy. Effective support looks different across families and cultures and can be provided across income levels; what matters is what the people around a learner do. Supports can be eroded by family separation, bereavement, parental imprisonment, substance misuse or illness, by bullying and discrimination, and by moving schools, and a relationship with another trusted adult can partly make up for that. It is measured with perceived-support scales for children, adolescents and adults. It overlaps with belonging and with the home learning environment.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Schools and programmes can build supports into their structure, so that every learner has trusted adults and peers to turn to.
  • Adult learners' persistence depends partly on support from family and friends, which study can also strain.
  • Transitions, such as moving school or starting college, put existing networks at risk.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners whose networks have been disrupted by moves, bereavement, family change or bullying.
  • Adult learners balancing study with family and work.
  • Learners still acquiring the language of instruction, who may lose ground in the family language.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Academic engagement and achievement.
  • Emotional well-being, persistence and self-efficacy.

Measures¶

  • Social Support Questionnaire, Short Form (Sarason et al., 1987): self-report of the number of supportive relationships an adult has and satisfaction with them.
  • Adult Learner Social Supports (Lundberg et al., 2008): self-report of support adult learners receive from friends and family.
  • Child and Adolescent Social Support Scale (CASSS) (Malecki, Demaray, & Elliott, 2000): perceived support from parents, teachers, classmates and school for grades 3 to 12.
  • Social Support Scale for Children (SSSC) (Harter, 1985): perceived support from parents, teachers, classmates and friends.
  • Penn Interactive Peer Play Scale (PIPPS) (Fantuzzo & Hampton, 2000): whether a young child has positive, successful peer relationships.
  • Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) (Goodman, Meltzer, & Bailey, 1998): peer relationships and prosocial behaviour alongside conduct, attention and emotional difficulties.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Access: hearing or vision loss can bring isolation and less engagement with a support network.
  • Adverse Experiences: support buffers adversity, and abuse by trusted people erodes support.
  • Affect Regulation: stronger perceived support goes with fewer emotional difficulties.
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills: learners with stronger relationship skills report stronger support.
  • Socioeconomic Status: housing instability can break up networks, and support can soften the cognitive effects of low income.
  • Belonging: caring relationships with teachers and peers build belonging, and belonging encourages use of support.
  • Safety: support protects against bullying, though study can make some adults less safe at home.
  • Motivation: close family relationships and peers' values shape motivation to succeed in school.
  • Physical Well-Being: support goes with better health, possibly by easing stress.
  • English-Learner Status: losing the family language can cut learners off from family and community support.
  • Self-Efficacy: support goes with persistence, self-concept and self-efficacy.
  • Academic and Critical Literacy: perceived support from parents and teachers goes with engagement and achievement.

Examples¶

  • Build relational school resources (teacher–student relationships, peer support, encouragement, extracurriculars) to scaffold self-regulation for low-efficacy marginalized students
  • Build a communal network of adults as an alternative developmental support system for male children
