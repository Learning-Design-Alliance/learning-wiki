Autism Spectrum Disorder¶

How to read this page¶

Autism is a characteristic of a learner, not a cause of poor outcomes and not a measure of ability. Autistic learners differ from one another as much as any other group does: in language (from no speech to fluent speech), in intellectual ability (anywhere across the range, assessed separately from the diagnosis), in sensory sensitivities and in interests. A group average below says nothing about the learner in front of you.

The claims on this page are of two kinds, listed separately. Differences found compare autistic with non-autistic people on some measure (processing speed, multisensory integration, a hiring assessment, preparation for life after school); a marker there says whether a difference was found (+), only under conditions (~) or not found (−). Some of these sources use clinical language ("deficit", "impairment"); the summaries keep what was measured and how, not that framing. A gap in outcomes such as transition preparation also reflects services, expectations and access, not autism alone. Instruction studied with autistic learners reports what a support or teaching approach did for autistic learners; a marker there rates that approach's effect, and none of those studies compares autistic with non-autistic learners. For design, the second list is usually the useful one.

Description¶

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is defined in current diagnostic manuals (APA, 2013) by differences in social communication and interaction, together with restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, including sensory sensitivities, present from early development. The term "spectrum" records that presentation varies widely: some autistic people use no speech and need substantial support, others are fluent speakers who live independently and experience mainly social and sensory differences. Many autistic people and advocates prefer identity-first language ("autistic learner"); research sources mostly use "students with ASD". Both appear on this page as the sources use them.

In the research autism appears as a population a design targets (communication supports, self-monitoring, parent-mediated intervention), as a predictor (autistic and non-autistic groups compared on cognitive, sensory or later-life measures), and occasionally as a moderator. It is recorded by clinical diagnosis or by a school's special education category, which do not identify the same learners. It is distinct from learning disabilities and developmental differences in general (that page covers the wider group, autism included), and from intellectual disability, which can co-occur but is not implied by the diagnosis. Reported prevalence has risen since the 1980s, which reflects a broader diagnostic definition and greater awareness at least as much as any change in underlying prevalence; the belief that vaccines cause autism has no scientific support.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Calibrate expectations to the learner's own profile, assessed separately for language, intellectual ability, sensory needs and interests, not to the diagnosis.
  • Predictable structure, advance notice of changes and more than one way to communicate (spoken, written, pictorial, a device) are low-cost supports that serve many learners, not only autistic ones.
  • What a learner is entitled to (accommodations, a communication device, an IEP or EHC plan) is set by law and by the learner's plan, not only by the designer.

Target Learners¶

  • Autistic learners at any age, from early childhood to adults in work, with or without intellectual disability and with or without speech.
  • Learners with a documented autism diagnosis, whose support should follow their individual profile rather than a generic "autism" template
  • Learners whose intellectual ability may be anywhere across the range independently of the diagnosis, which needs separate assessment

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Functional communication (requesting, commenting, conversation), where aided augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is the best-studied support recorded here.
  • On-task and self-managed behaviour in school settings, through self-monitoring.
  • Social participation and preparation for life after school.
  • Less disruption from changes in routine, where predictable structure is itself a support

Claims¶

  • Processing speed in adults with ASD shows no significant association with SAS-II social adjustment, and only a near-significant positive association with GAF functioning [~W]
  • The article attributes the discrepancy with inspection-time studies to methodology, arguing standardized measures are sensitive enough to detect processing speed deficits in ASD [~W]

Differences found between autistic and non-autistic people¶

  • Adults with ASD without intellectual disability show a large deficit in composite processing speed versus IQ-matched controls on standardized measures [+M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • The processing speed deficit in adults with ASD appears on each of the three standardized subtests: Trail Making A, Symbol Coding, and Category Fluency [+M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Slower processing speed in adults with ASD is associated with greater impairment on ADOS Communication and Reciprocal Social Interaction scores [~M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Psychosis, ASD, and ADHD-Inattentive Type independently elevate risk for slow processing speed relative to referred youth without diagnoses [+M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • ASD shows a distinct executive dysfunction profile of impaired cognitive flexibility and planning with intact inhibitory control [+M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Autistic individuals show reduced multisensory integration, most consistently for speech-related stimuli [~M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Multisensory temporal precision in autism is linked to speech integration, language abilities, and clinical symptomatology [~M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Autistic and general-population job seekers do not differ significantly on the Disconumbers and Shapedance game-based assessment package [-M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Score distributions on both game packages appear roughly equal across autistic and general-population candidates [-M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • A small significant difference favors general-population candidates on the Digitspan and Shapedance package [~M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Among 2012 disability groups, youth with intellectual disability, autism, deaf-blindness, multiple disabilities, and orthopedic impairments were most at-risk in preparation for life after high school [+M] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Adolescents with ASD are described as frequent targets of cyberbullying because they may miss social cues and lack self-advocacy skills [+W] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes
  • Teens with ASD are described as vulnerable to compulsive internet use that limits in-person social interaction [+W] — learners who differ on it differ in outcomes

Notes for reading these: the processing-speed findings come from two samples (verbal adults matched on IQ, Haigh et al. 2018; youth referred to a psychiatric clinic, Braaten et al. 2020), not from school learners in general. The multisensory findings are one review's reading of mixed studies (half of four illusion studies found a difference; children more than adults). The hiring-assessment study (Willis et al. 2021) found no difference on one game package and a small one (d = 0.22) on another, which bears on whether a selection or placement assessment disadvantages autistic candidates. The transition finding is a comparison among disability groups, reported without per-group figures, and reflects services and opportunities as well as the learners. The two internet-safety statements are a practitioner brief's assertions with no data behind them.

Instruction studied with autistic learners¶

  • Aided AAC interventions had large effects on targeted behavioral outcomes in individuals with ASD, per a meta-analysis of 24 single-case studies [+M] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • AAC interventions showed positive effects on all targeted behavioral outcomes, with greater effects for communication skills than other skill categories [+M] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • PECS and speech-generating devices produced larger effects than other picture-based AAC systems, which nonetheless showed small effects [~M] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Aided AAC systems show advantages over manual signs for acquiring requests, and motor imitation and matching skills predict manual sign acquisition [+M] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Individuals with developmental disabilities tend to prefer speech-generating devices over picture exchange and manual signs, with substantial individual differences [~M] — population: developmental disabilities including autism
  • Parents of children with autism using AAC commonly report perceived benefits, most frequently increased communication opportunities [+W] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Barriers to integrating AAC into daily routines and accessing AAC programs are frequently reported despite high perceived benefits [~W] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Parents more often report initial difficulty accepting AAC than continued difficulty after time has passed [~W] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Self-monitoring interventions produce strong effects for students with ASD across behavioral targets and school settings (overall Tau-U = .83) [+M] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Parent Implemented Intervention Improves Outcomes [+S] — population: instruction studied with autistic learners
  • Twenty participants aged 3 to 14 with varying disabilities all demonstrated literacy gains through shared story reading [+W] — population: extensive support needs, autism among them
  • In a pilot randomized trial, children whose parents received Parental Friendship Coaching were reported by parents and by blind teachers to show better social behaviors and greater peer acceptance than control children [~W] — carried over from the folded principle page; the trial was with children with ADHD, not autistic children, so it is not evidence about autism

Notes for reading these: the AAC effects rest mainly on one meta-analysis of single-case studies (Ganz et al. 2012) and one overview of reviews (Nam et al. 2018); single-case designs show change within a few individuals, and no numeric effect is recorded here. The parent survey (75 families in Nablus) reports perceived benefits and barriers, not measured communication. Self-monitoring rests on 16 single-case studies with 28 students. Parent-mediated intervention rests on two meta-analyses of randomised trials with small pooled effects (g about 0.23 for young children). The shared-story-reading review's 20 participants include learners with intellectual, physical and visual disabilities as well as autism.

  • Learning Disabilities and Developmental Differences — the wider group, which includes autism
  • Processing Speed — where the processing-speed comparisons sit
  • Sensory Integration — sensory and multisensory processing
  • Communication Skills — the outcome most AAC studies measure
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills — social participation outcomes
  • Executive Function — cognitive flexibility and planning

Examples¶

  • Supporting Students with Intellectual Disabilities — a distinct condition that can co-occur with autism but is not implied by it
  • Universal Design for Learning — predictable structure and several means of communication, which serve autistic learners as one case of a broader design principle
  • Self-Monitoring — the self-monitoring evidence for students with ASD
  • Use multisensory exposure and training as targets for remediation of sensory-based developmental and cognitive impairments
  • Calming Proprioceptive Input
