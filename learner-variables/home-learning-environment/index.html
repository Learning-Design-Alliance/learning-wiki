Home Learning Environment¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables
Evidence · 61 claims (47 show a difference, 8 mixed, 6 no difference) · 51 studies (20 associational, 9 review, 8 quant-synthesis, 5 qualitative, 5 design, 3 causal, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 11 of 51 report an effect size · 57 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The home learning environment is what families, caregivers and communities provide outside formal instruction that helps a learner develop: books and other materials, shared reading and writing, talk, mathematics games and everyday number activity, and the values, expectations and attitudes the people around a learner hold about learning. The LVN describes it under several names by content area: the home literacy environment for early reading, the literacy environment of home, school, work and community for older students and adults, and the mathematics learning environment, where parents' and teachers' attitudes and anxiety about mathematics shape learners' own mindsets. Children who read, write and do mathematics with caregivers before school often progress more easily, and the quality of the home environment is associated with achievement and with socio-emotional well-being into adolescence. It is also where learners learn about their identity, culture and community, and the LVN encourages educators to learn about it and build on the knowledge learners bring from home. It is measured with parent checklists and surveys, home observation instruments, student self-reports of reading materials and practices, and print-exposure tests. Differences in the home environment that track socioeconomic status reflect resources and circumstances, and the LVN recommends classroom supports and resources for families so that every learner has a comparable foundation. It overlaps with social supports.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Resources and guidance for families, such as books, games and shared reading, can strengthen the home environment.
  • Classroom supports can make up for unequal access to materials and to internet at home.
  • Building on learners' home knowledge, languages and culture connects instruction to what they bring.

Target Learners¶

  • Young children before and during the start of school.
  • Learners with limited access to books, materials or internet at home, and multilingual families.
  • Adults, whose literacy environment includes work and community.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Early literacy and numeracy, reading motivation and print exposure.
  • Mathematics attitudes and mindset, and later achievement.
  • Identity development and socio-emotional well-being.

Measures¶

  • Reading Pattern Survey (Rodrigo et al., 2014): self-report of reading attitude, habits and print exposure, including books read and library visits.
  • Classroom Literacy Environmental Profile (CLEP) (Wolfersberger et al., 2004): rates the richness of the literacy environment in grade 1 to 6 classrooms.
  • Author Recognition Test (ART) (Stanovich & West, 1989): print exposure measured by recognising real authors among foils.
  • Home Literary Environment Survey (Kubis, 1996): student self-report of access to reading materials and literacy practices at home.
  • Home Literacy Checklist: a parent questionnaire on the home literacy environment.
  • Inventory of Parental Influence (e.g., Campbell & Verna, 2007): parents' views of family processes such as academic pressure, support and help with schoolwork.
  • Mathematics Anxiety Rating Scale (Alexander & Martray, 1989): self-reported mathematics anxiety in parents and teachers, which can shape children's attitudes.
  • Early Childhood Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment (HOME): measures the quality of a young child's home environment.

Related Learner Variables¶

  • Access: hearing loss in a child or parent changes how language and literacy are supported at home.
  • Motivation: a supportive literacy environment goes with more motivation to read, and motivated readers read more.
  • Socioeconomic Status: economic pressures can limit books, internet and materials at home.
  • Affect Regulation: parents' and teachers' mathematics anxiety can pass to children.
  • Prior Knowledge: more print exposure goes with more background knowledge.
  • Belonging: culturally relevant and voluntary reading supports identity and connection.
  • English-Learner Status: multilingual households may have fewer English reading materials or less internet access.
  • Self-Efficacy: adults' attitudes to mathematics shape learners' mathematics self-concept.
  • Social Awareness and Relationship Skills: talk about feelings and reading fiction support understanding others.
  • Stereotype Threat: caregivers' stereotyped beliefs can increase vulnerability to threat.
  • Academic and Critical Literacy: the quality of the home environment contributes to academic performance.
  • Communication Skills: print exposure goes with oral language skill.

Examples¶

  • Start early: foster a positive math climate at home and school to prevent the distal avoidance pathway
