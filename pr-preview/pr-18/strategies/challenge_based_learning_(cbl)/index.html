    Challenge Based Learning (CBL)¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Challenge Based Learning (CBL) is a collaborative, inquiry-driven framework in which learners identify a real-world challenge, investigate it through guided research, and act by developing and implementing evidence-based solutions. The framework moves through three interrelated phases — Engage (moving from a big idea to a concrete, learner-owned challenge), Investigate (guided and student-directed inquiry), and Act (designing, implementing, and evaluating solutions) — with documentation and reflection throughout. CBL was developed and piloted by Apple with educators and is documented in the Challenge Based Learning White Paper (Nichols & Cator, 2009; Apple Inc., 2011).

    Design Implications¶

    CBL situates learning in authentic problems, which raises perceived relevance and task value — a key driver of engagement and persistence [~M]. Because learners work on whole, complex problems rather than isolated subskills, CBL supports transfer of knowledge to novel situations [~M]. Its effectiveness, however, depends on scaffolding: unguided inquiry alone tends to underperform explicit guidance for novices, so CBL implementations must pair learner autonomy with coaching, structure, and just-in-time instruction [~S]. The productive-struggle character of the Engage and Investigate phases can also deepen conceptual learning when followed by structured consolidation Productive failure improves conceptual learning relative to direct instruction followed by practice. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • An authentic challenge connected to learners' world, framed during Challenge Identification
    • Collaborative team structures and norms with defined roles and individual accountability
    • Access to information sources, experts, and tools for investigation
    • Time — CBL typically spans weeks, not single sessions
    • Ongoing instructor Coaching and formative checkpoints rather than front-loaded lecturing

    Constraints¶

    • Novices without adequate scaffolding experience high extraneous load and flounder in open-ended problem spaces; guided inquiry outperforms pure discovery, especially for learners with low prior knowledge [~S]
    • The time cost is substantial; coverage-driven curricula and high-stakes standardized assessment schedules frequently crowd out the Act phase, reducing CBL to a simulation of action [-W]
    • Group work without structured roles and accountability can produce social loafing and uneven learning across team members [-M]
    • Assessment systems built for individual content mastery are a poor fit; misaligned grading undermines the collaborative, iterative character of the work [-W]
    • When the challenge is too far from learners' prior knowledge, initial engagement collapses; the big idea must connect to something learners can activate [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Scale: from a single unit within a course to a whole-school program
    • Locus of the challenge: teacher-provided big ideas with learner-derived challenges, or fully learner-identified challenges
    • Act phase depth: awareness campaigns, prototypes, policy proposals, or implemented community interventions
    • Subject integration: single-subject CBL or interdisciplinary implementations

    Target Learners¶

    • Middle school through adult learners; the framework was originally piloted in secondary and higher education
    • Learners with enough prior knowledge to conduct guided inquiry productively; for complete novices, front-load direct instruction before opening the investigation [~M]
    • Learners who benefit from autonomy and authentic purpose; autonomy-supportive designs strengthen intrinsic motivation Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation. [+S]
    • Learners whose confidence needs building — successful challenge completion and public action can strengthen persistence beliefs Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Deep subject-matter knowledge applied to authentic contexts
    • 21st-century competencies: collaboration, communication, critical thinking, and self-direction
    • Problem identification and solution design — defining a problem well, not just solving a given one
    • Civic and community engagement through implemented action

    Instructions¶

    1. Engage — Present a big idea (e.g., water, health, sustainability); through essential questioning, learners narrow it to a specific, actionable challenge (Challenge Identification)
    2. Commit — Teams adopt the challenge and develop a guiding question
    3. Investigate — Conduct guided then self-directed research: background reading, data collection, interviews, and fieldwork; instructors provide Coaching and just-in-time content instruction
    4. Analyze — Teams synthesize findings and identify evidence-based solution directions
    5. Act — Design, implement, and document a solution; connect to Authentic Audiences & Purposes where possible
    6. Reflect and Assess — Document the process, evaluate impact, and debrief learning against both content and competency goals

    Related Strategies¶

    • Project-Based Learning — CBL is a variant of PBL distinguished by learner-identified challenges and a mandatory action phase
    • Case-Based Learning — shares authentic-problem grounding but analyzes existing cases rather than acting on live challenges; case-based approaches have their own evidence base for exam performance Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]
    • Service-Learning — overlaps in the Act phase when solutions serve a community partner

    Related Elements¶

    • Challenge Identification — the Engage-phase move that converts a big idea into a learner-owned challenge
    • Coaching — the instructor role during inquiry, replacing front-loaded instruction

    Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

    • Anchored Instruction — like CBL, anchors learning in a realistic problem context that sustains inquiry
    • Collaborative Inquiry — the investigation phase enacts this pattern's cycle of questioning, evidence gathering, and synthesis
    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the Act phase provides authentic practice with coaching and articulation in a real setting

    Tools¶

    • Apple's Challenge Based Learning resources and the CBL Classroom app (Apple Education)
    • Collaborative documentation platforms (shared docs, wikis) for the Investigate phase
    • Digital storytelling and prototyping tools for the Act phase

    Examples¶

    • Apple's original pilot (2011) — Six schools across three countries implemented CBL challenges such as reducing energy consumption and improving community health; students designed and ran awareness campaigns and measurable interventions (Apple Inc., 2011)
    • University engineering courses — Teams take on municipal sustainability challenges (e.g., stormwater management) with city partners, moving from stakeholder interviews to implemented pilot designs
    • K-12 environmental science — A "food waste" big idea narrows to a school-cafeteria challenge; students audit waste, propose menu and logistics changes, and present results to the district board
