    Retrieval Practice Warm Ups¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Retrieval practice warm ups are brief activities at the start of a lesson in which learners recall previously learned material from memory — answering questions, solving a few problems, or summarizing prior content — before new instruction begins. They function simultaneously as retrieval practice, spaced review, and a formative check that surfaces gaps before new material builds on old.

    Design Implications¶

    Warm ups exploit the testing effect: recalling information strengthens its memory trace more than rereading or restudying it Retrieval practice produces durable, transferable learning. [+S]. Because warm ups recur at the start of each lesson, they naturally implement spaced retrieval, which amplifies the retention benefit over single-session practice Spaced retrieval outperforms massed retrieval for long-term retention. [+S]. They also serve an activation function, bringing prior knowledge into working memory so new instruction can connect to it Activation of prior knowledge improves learning from new instruction. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Questions or problems targeting material from previous lessons, not the current one
    • Low stakes: ungraded or lightly graded, so errors are safe and informative
    • A brief feedback or review step so learners correct errors immediately Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process. [+S]
    • Short duration (3–8 minutes) so it does not crowd out new instruction

    Constraints¶

    • Retrieving material learned only minutes ago yields little benefit; the warm up must target earlier content for spacing to operate Spaced retrieval outperforms massed retrieval for long-term retention. [-M]
    • If warm ups become graded high-stakes quizzes, they shift from retrieval to assessment and can raise anxiety without adding learning benefit [~M]
    • Purely factual recall questions build retention of facts but transfer little to application; at least some questions should require inference or application [~M]
    • Errors retrieved with high confidence but without corrective feedback can entrench misconceptions High-confidence errors improve retention only when corrected. [~S]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Brain dump: learners write everything they remember about a topic; works when content is broad and questions would cue too narrowly
    • Two-question quiz: 2–4 short answer or multiple-choice items, often delivered via clickers or tools like Kahoot! or Quizizz
    • Cumulative problem warm up: one or two problems mixing this week's and last month's material, common in mathematics (e.g., "Do Now" routines in Illustrative Mathematics curricula)
    • Retrieval + elaboration: after recall, learners explain why an answer is correct, adding a generative step

    Target Learners¶

    • All learners benefit from retrieval, but warm ups are especially valuable for learners who do not spontaneously self-test while studying Retrieval practice produces durable, transferable learning. [+S]
    • Struggling learners benefit from the low-stakes error surfacing, provided feedback follows promptly Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process. [+S]
    • Learners with high prior knowledge still gain from retrieval, though they may need harder, application-level questions to avoid trivial recall [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Long-term retention of declarative and procedural knowledge
    • Fluency building: fast, accurate recall of foundational facts and procedures
    • Cumulative course coherence: keeping earlier units accessible while later ones build on them

    Instructions¶

    1. Select 2–5 questions covering material from at least one week prior, mixing fact recall with one application item.
    2. Present the questions before any review of the material — retrieval must come from memory, not notes.
    3. Set a short timer (3–5 minutes) and keep stakes low; frame errors as useful information.
    4. Reveal answers and give brief corrective feedback, addressing common errors aloud Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process. [+S].
    5. Transition into new instruction by linking the warm up content to today's topic (Activation).
    6. Rotate question sources across the term so all major content is revisited on a spaced schedule.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Exit Tickets — the end-of-lesson counterpart; exit ticket responses supply the material for the next day's warm up
    • Spaced Practice — warm ups are the most reliable classroom mechanism for implementing spacing
    • Interleaving — mixing question types across topics in the warm up enacts interleaving at small scale
    • Formative Assessment — warm ups double as daily formative checks

    Examples¶

    • "Do Now" routines in US mathematics classrooms: 2–3 cumulative problems on the board as students enter, reviewed together before the lesson
    • Retrieval Practice (Agarwal & Bain) — teacher-facing resources documenting starter quizzes and "brain dump" warm ups across K–12 subjects
    • Language classrooms: opening each session with 5 vocabulary items from previous weeks recalled orally before new vocabulary is introduced
