    Anticipation Guide¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    An anticipation guide is a short list of declarative statements — often plausible-sounding misconceptions — that learners agree or disagree with before encountering new material. After instruction, learners return to their initial responses, compare them against what they now know, and discuss or revise them. The strategy activates prior knowledge, surfaces misconceptions, and creates purpose for reading or instruction.

    Design Implications¶

    Anticipation guides work by activating relevant prior knowledge before new content arrives, which improves integration and recall Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]. Their distinctive power comes from statements that learners answer confidently but incorrectly: encountering evidence that contradicts a committed answer produces cognitive disequilibrium, which motivates conceptual change Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]. High-confidence errors are especially valuable targets, because they are more memorable and more likely to be corrected than low-confidence guesses High-confidence errors improve retention when corrected. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • 5–10 statements that are debatable, tied to the key ideas of the text or lesson (not trivia), and — ideally — include common misconceptions stated plausibly
    • A mechanism for committing to answers (written, clicker, poll) so initial positions are recorded and revisable
    • A structured revisit after instruction: learners re-answer, compare, and explain what changed and why (Class Discussion works well here)

    Constraints¶

    • Statements that can be answered from surface features or guessed correctly without understanding produce no disequilibrium and little benefit [~M]
    • If the post-instruction revisit is skipped, the guide degenerates into a generic warm-up with no conceptual-change mechanism [-M]
    • Learners with strong accurate prior knowledge gain little; activation of what they already know is redundant [~M]
    • Poorly worded ambiguous statements can install the misconception in learners who would not otherwise have held it [~W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Pre/post only — silent individual completion, minimal discussion; fastest format
    • Discussion-based — initial responses debated in pairs or whole class before instruction, surfacing disagreements that the text then resolves
    • Digital/polling — tools like Pear Deck or clickers aggregate responses anonymously, making the class's misconception profile visible to the instructor in real time
    • Extended guides — learners add a "evidence from the text" column, converting the guide into an Annotating scaffold during reading

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners who hold common misconceptions about the topic — the strategy is most powerful when initial wrong answers are confident High-confidence errors improve retention when corrected. [+M]
    • Reluctant readers, who gain a personal stake and a purpose for reading [~M]
    • Less valuable for learners with strong, accurate prior knowledge, for whom activation is redundant [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect models with disciplinary ones
    • Prior knowledge activation: connecting new content to what learners already know Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]
    • Reading comprehension: setting a purpose and monitoring for confirmation or refutation
    • Metacognition: noticing and evaluating one's own beliefs against evidence

    Instructions¶

    1. Identify the 3–5 key ideas and the most common misconceptions about the upcoming topic; write one plausible statement per idea, mixing true and false statements.
    2. Before instruction, have learners individually agree/disagree with each statement and briefly justify one or two choices (Activation).
    3. Optionally, run a brief Class Discussion on the most divisive statements — do not reveal correct answers yet.
    4. Deliver instruction or reading (Advance Organizers can frame it).
    5. After instruction, learners revisit their responses, mark changes, and cite the evidence that changed their minds; close with a whole-class debrief on the statements most people revised.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Activating Prior Knowledge — the broader family; anticipation guides are a structured, commitment-based variant
    • KWL Chart — a related pre/during/post scaffold with open-ended prompts instead of agree/disagree statements
    • Erroneous Examples — the worked-example analogue: presenting flawed material for learners to diagnose

    Examples¶

    • Science misconceptions: Before a unit on photosynthesis, students respond to "Plants get most of their mass from soil" — the classic misconception documented by Driver et al.; post-instruction revisiting forces confrontation with the mass-from-carbon-dioxide account.
    • Reading Rockets — published templates and guidance for using anticipation guides in K–12 literacy instruction.
    • History: Before a unit on the causes of WWI, students agree/disagree with "One assassination caused the war," then revisit after examining primary sources.
