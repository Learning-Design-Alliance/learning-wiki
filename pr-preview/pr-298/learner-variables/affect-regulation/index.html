Affect Regulation¶

Learner Variable · All learner variables Evidence · 142 claims (99 show a difference, 29 mixed, 14 no difference) · 95 studies (35 associational, 20 causal, 14 review, 9 qualitative, 7 quant-synthesis, 6 theoretical, 4 design), q1–q4 · 25 of 95 report an effect size · 136 claims rest on one study

Description¶

What a learner does with frustration, anxiety and failure while the work is still in front of them. It decides whether a hard check needs a recoverable framing and how failure is surfaced. Anxiety is not only unpleasant — it consumes the same working memory the task needs, so it degrades performance through a mechanism entirely separate from motivation [-M]. Naming an emotion measurably reduces its grip, which is why the strategies here look unexpectedly linguistic.

Implications¶

Context¶

  • Decides whether a difficult check reads as recoverable, and how a wrong answer is surfaced.
  • Failure can be productive when it is designed and framed as generative rather than as a verdict [+M].
  • Vocabulary is the lever: a learner who cannot name the state cannot regulate it.

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with domain-specific anxiety — mathematics being the best-documented case.
  • Younger learners still building the vocabulary, and any learner in a high-stakes setting.

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Affects performance on timed and high-stakes assessment more than on untimed work, which is a validity problem.
  • Regulation itself is a legitimate objective in social-emotional curricula.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do learners with higher anxiety about a subject or a test achieve less in it? Math anxiety has a small-to-moderate negative correlation with math achievement (r = −.28) in a meta-analysis of correlations from 223 studies [+M]
  • Does the format and setting of an assessment change how anxious learners are about it? Students taking online and proctored online exams report higher test anxiety than students taking on-campus exams and non-proctored online exams [+M]
  • Do school and youth mindfulness programmes improve students' regulation, wellbeing and academic outcomes? The evidence base for school mindfulness interventions is promising but too variable for definitive practice recommendations [~M]
  • Do learners who regulate their emotions better show more motivation and stronger executive control? Emotion regulation is positively associated with academic motivation among university students [+M]
