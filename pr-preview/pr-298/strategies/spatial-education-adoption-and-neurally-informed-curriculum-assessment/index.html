Adopt in-school spatial education curricula and assess their transferability using neural change alongside traditional assessments¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends wider in-school adoption of spatial education, noting that classroom-based evidence provides new empirical support where enthusiasm has not yet permeated education policy and practice. It further proposes neurally informed curriculum assessment: leveraging neural changes that predict transferability to evaluate curricula on relatively small scales — identifying existing curricula likely to achieve transfer, identifying changes that can improve transfer, and informing development of novel transferable curricula — while assessing curricula rather than individual students to avoid the practical and ethical limitations of large-scale neuroimaging.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Longitudinal fMRI measurement at more than one time point paired with the curriculum
  • propensity scoring–based matching or comparable designs to minimize selection bias in real-world classrooms

Constraints¶

  • Neural change should bolster, not replace, traditional assessments; how broad or specific neural-change-based prediction is remains to be determined by testing wider arrays of transfer tasks

Target Learners¶

  • high school students in STEM courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • transferable reasoning abilities
  • spatial thinking

Affordances¶

  • Mental Model Theory Spatial Basis Verbal Reasoning

Claims¶

  • Curriculum-related neural changes during spatial scanning predict and mediate transfer to verbal reasoning [+M]
  • Neural change during spatial scanning predicts verbal reasoning transfer better than traditional performance-based assessments, and best prediction combines both [+M]
  • The spatial curriculum produced near transfer to improved spatial scanning and increased self-reported spatial habits of mind, but not to mental rotation [+M]
  • The spatial curriculum produced longitudinal neural changes during reasoning: increased aIPS activity, increased SpatialMap-to-DLPFC connectivity, and increased nonspatial-to-spatial representational similarity [+M]
  • A spatial STEM curriculum yields far transfer to improved verbal deductive reasoning in high school students, including on nonspatial relation trials [+M]
  • Improvement in spatial scanning predicts and mediates the curriculum's transfer effect to verbal reasoning [+M]
