Limit the number of instructions and tasks in dialogic activities, since too many impair dialogue¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

From the ANT analysis of the vaccination lesson, the authors draw a design lesson for dialogic education: designers envisioned that card classification would enrich dialogue, but "Too many instructions, too many tasks to do, impair dialogue". The classification task black-boxed the network into production of a material product, dissolving the dialogic network. Designers of dialogic activities should therefore keep task demands light enough that argumentation can persist.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Activities should still provide artifacts and arguments that can seed discussion, but without overloading students with simultaneous task demands

Constraints¶

  • The recommendation arises from one analyzed lesson; the authors confess that in their program students prioritized the teachers' demands, which contributed to the board subordinating them

Target Learners¶

  • Grade 7 students in interdisciplinary dialogic programs

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Sustained collaborative dialogic argumentation around societal issues

Claims¶

  • SEDA identifies co-construction and dialogic shifts but does not explain their mechanisms, whereas ANT explains shifts via changing power relations among actors [+W]
  • Non-human actants such as a Bristol board and task instructions can subordinate students and render classroom talk non-dialogic [+W]
  • Dialogic networks in classroom talk can dissolve into non-dialogic ones when task instructions and artifacts regain power over students [+W]
