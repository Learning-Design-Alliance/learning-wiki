Physical Activity¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 27 claims (16 for, 9 mixed, 2 against) · 24 studies (10 causal, 7 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 4 of 24 report an effect size · 21 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns movement placed in or around learning time to change the state learners bring to the next task: a short bout of physical activity between seated segments, a physically active lesson, organised active play at recess, and exercise scheduled after a learning episode. For learners whose on-task behavior falls during long seated stretches (restless, drifting, slow to start the next segment), a brief bout of movement placed between segments, followed at once by a named task, is expected to increase on-task behavior in the segment that follows (persistence, measured as observed time on task). It is a readiness support, not instruction: it teaches no content by itself, and any learning benefit runs through better use of the time that follows.

Two systematic reviews in the wiki test this for children in classrooms, and they bound it. Classroom-based activity (movement breaks and physically active lessons) in primary-school children increased observed on-task behavior with a medium pooled effect, and in a registered review of single bouts with children aged 4 to 17, time on task improved after the bout in 9 of 11 interventions, depending on its length and intensity. Neither review found consistent gains on cognitive tests (executive function, attention tests) or academic performance, and one notes that the few studies with longer follow-up suggest the on-task gain may fade (Classroom physical activity increases children's on-task behaviour, with no consistent gain on attention or other cognitive tests [+S] for on-task behavior, [~S] for attention and achievement). A school study of a programme of 10-minute classroom activity breaks (Energizers) in third and fourth grade agrees and adds the condition on initial state: on-task behavior during the academic instruction that followed improved by 8% overall (ES = 0.60) and by 20% among the students who were least on task beforehand (ES = 2.20) (Systematic implementation of Energizers improves on-task behavior… [+M], The least on-task students show the largest improvement after Energizers activities… [+M]; one study, a subgroup result). So the tested relationship is movement → on-task behavior shortly afterwards, in school-age children, most for those who drift most. That movement improves attention as measured by tests, achievement, or anything in adults during a lesson is not shown by any claim in this wiki.

A second, separate relationship concerns memory: when exercise happens relative to learning. In adult laboratory studies a bout of exercise during learning slightly hurt later memory, a bout before had a small non-significant effect, and a bout after learning helped, most clearly when it came some hours later (The timing of acute exercise relative to learning modulates memory consolidation [~S]). That model is owned by Memory Consolidation; this page uses it only to decide where a bout goes, and does not repeat it.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's observed state before the bout (an already-engaged group has little on-task behavior to gain, and in the one study that looked, the least on-task students gained most), on the bout's length and intensity, on what follows it (a named task, not open time), on whether the movement competes with encoding of hard new material, and on the horizon (the evidence is about the minutes after a bout, not weeks).

How the neighbouring converted pages sit beside this one: Mindfulness Activities owns the quiet settling routine at a transition; a movement break is the active alternative for the same moment, and the two can be offered as a choice. Check-ins owns the short report of a learner's state and what the teacher does with it; a movement check-in ("show me your energy level") is a check-in, not this page. Memory Consolidation owns what happens between learning and the moment it is needed, including exercise timing. Self-Monitoring owns checking one's own work against a criterion; noticing one's own restlessness and choosing to move is where the two meet.

Default design¶

The two reviews test movement against no movement for on-task behavior in school-age children; they do not set a schedule, and nothing in the wiki tests the design for adults. The steps below are a default a designer can act on and revise; each says whether a claim tests it or whether it is an untested proposal.

  1. Find the seated stretch where on-task behavior drops, and put the bout there. Watch or ask where learners start drifting (commonly after 20 to 30 minutes of continuous seated work for primary children, longer for older learners; an untested proposal). Place the bout between two segments, not in the middle of an explanation. Basis: the reviews test bouts placed between or within lessons (Classroom physical activity… [+S]); the drift point is a local observation.
  2. Choose one of two doses. About 5 minutes of vigorous movement (learners breathe hard: jumping, fast stepping in place, a short active game), or more than 10 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity (an active lesson segment, a brisk walk). Time on task improved after either kind in 9 of 11 interventions, in children aged 4 to 17 (Classroom physical activity… [+S]); a programme of 10-minute classroom breaks in grades 3 and 4 also raised on-task behavior in the lesson that followed and raised children's in-school step counts against classes without it (Energizers improved on-task behavior… [+M], Energizers increased in-school steps [+M]; the step count is an activity outcome, not a learning one). Where the next task needs sustained attention, prefer the 5-minute vigorous bout: the same review reports attention-test gains after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after 10 to 12 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. Shorter bouts (1 to 3 minutes of stretching) are common in practice and are untested here.
  3. End the bout with a fixed transition and a named next task. Use the same closing cue every time (a count-down, a breathing cycle, "feet still, eyes on the board"), and state the task that starts now. Untested as a routine; teacher reports from a randomized recess programme found transitions from recess into class less difficult in programme schools (Playworks made transitions from recess to classroom learning less difficult, per teacher reports [+M]), carried here from organised recess to classroom breaks.
  4. Do not put strenuous movement in the middle of learning hard new material. In adult laboratory studies, exercise during encoding slightly hurt later memory (Acute exercise timing… [~S], adults, lab tasks; carried to classrooms untested). Place bouts after a segment of new material, not during it. Physically active lessons that make learners move to answer (stand for true, walk to the correct corner) suit review and practice of material already met better than a first explanation; that split is an untested proposal. On on-task behavior itself, a review reports that the 10-minute breaks raised time on task when done between lessons, while activity built into a classroom task in another study did not raise it (it did not lower it either) (Classroom energizers improved on-task behaviour, but PA during a classroom task showed no improvement [~M]; two observational school studies as one review reports them), which is a further reason to treat the stand-alone break, not the active lesson, as the default for on-task behavior.
  5. Put longer activity after demanding learning, not before it, when the goal is retention. If the timetable allows a choice, schedule recess, PE or a walk after the lesson that introduced hard material rather than before it; the adult evidence favours activity after learning, most clearly some hours after (Acute exercise timing… [~S]). See Memory Consolidation for the model.
  6. Where the movement can carry meaning, map it onto the concept, then onto the symbol. A gesture or whole-body enactment of a quantity or process (spreading fingers for wavelength, tilting arms for a slope, walking a timeline) gives a physical experience to link to the formal representation afterwards. A brief physical experience of angular momentum improved college physics quiz scores, but physical and virtual manipulation were equally effective in another study (Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning [~M]); in a pilot, physics students reported that a pinch-and-spread gesture helped them "feel" wavelength and amplitude, a perception survey only (AR pinch gesture pilot [+W]). Always follow the movement with an explicit link to the diagram or formula.
  7. Offer options and a no-questions opt-out. Give a seated or standing version of every move, a low-intensity option, and the choice to stretch at the desk instead. Untested proposal, from the earlier page (comfort, mobility, health and cultural expectations differ).
  8. Plan the arc across sessions, then keep only what shows a gain. A default, all untested proposals: in the first two weeks the teacher leads one or two scripted 5-minute bouts per session at fixed points, and records for each the on-task behavior in the first 10 minutes after (a quick scan count every 2 minutes is enough), noting separately the few learners who are least on task before the bout, since they are where the one school study found the largest gain (The least on-task students show the largest improvement… [+M]). In weeks three and four, learners take turns leading from a menu of four to six moves, and the teacher adds a bout when the class shows the drift signs from step 1, not only at the fixed points. From week five, a learner or group that stays on task through the long segments can move to a self-chosen bout (stand, stretch, walk to the back) taken when they notice restlessness, rather than a whole-class one. Every four to six weeks compare on-task counts after bouts with those in segments without one, and drop or change bouts that show no difference, since one review reports the gain may fade with longer follow-up (Classroom physical activity… [~S]).

When a condition is not met: if the group is already on task through long segments, a scheduled whole-class bout has little to gain; keep it optional. If the room or the learners' health rules out vigorous movement, use the longer moderate dose or desk-based movement and record whether on-task behavior changes. If the outcome that matters is test performance rather than behavior, do not expect the bout to deliver it.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learners do in the seated stretch now. Movement is expected to help a group that drifts off task; for a group already on task the reviews give little to gain, and in one school study the least on-task students gained most. If unstated, ask: during a long seated segment, when do learners start to drift, and how many are off task by then?
  • What the outcome is. On-task behavior soon after a bout is what the reviews support; test-measured attention, achievement and retention are not. If unstated, ask: what should be different after the break, how would you see it, and when?
  • Who can move, where, and how. Age, mobility, health, space, dress and mode (in the room, remote, on a phone) decide which moves are possible and safe. If unstated, ask: how much space is there, are all learners able to stand and move, and is anyone joining remotely?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is behavior under stated conditions: how many learners are on task in the minutes before and after a bout, how long the class takes to settle into the next task, who takes part and who opts out, and what learners say about how they feel. "Restored attention", "regulated" and "fatigued" are inferred states. A class that is quieter after a bout has shown that it is quieter, not that it is learning more.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Classroom physical activity increases children's on-task behaviour, with no consistent gain on attention or other cognitive tests [+S] for on-task behavior, [~S] for attention and achievement: two systematic reviews (classroom, child and adolescent). Watson et al. (2017) pooled four studies of classroom-based activity in primary-school children: a medium positive effect on observed on-task and off-task behavior (SMD = 0.60, 95% CI 0.20–1.00), and no significant pooled effect on cognitive functions such as executive function (SMD = 0.33, CI crossing zero); most behavior studies reported an acute gain, the few with longer follow-up suggested it may fade, and the authors warn heterogeneity rules out firm conclusions. Daly-Smith et al. (2018), a registered review of 18 single-bout studies with children aged 4 to 17: time on task improved after the bout in 9 of 11 interventions, after longer moderate-to-vigorous bouts (more than 10 minutes) or shorter more intense 5-minute bouts; no consistent evidence of better cognition or academic performance; attention improved after a 5-minute vigorous break but not after 10–12 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. This is the only claim here that tests the page's relationship, and it supports behavior, not learning. The claim's discussion speaks of effects on attention after 5–20 minutes of moderate activity and of larger benefits for younger learners and those with attention difficulties, which its two evidence entries do not report; the page takes its doses from the entries only.
  • Systematic implementation of Energizers improves on-task behavior during academic instruction in third- and fourth-grade students [+M], with The least on-task students show the largest improvement after Energizers activities, improving on-task behavior by 20% [+M] and A classroom-based physical activity program (Energizers) increases children's daily in-school physical activity compared with no program [+M]: one study, Mahar et al. (2006), classroom, child. In a multiple-baseline design across classrooms (62 third- and fourth-grade students), on-task behavior during academic instruction after the 10-minute classroom activity breaks was 8% higher than before them (ES = 0.60), and 20% higher among the least on-task students (ES = 2.20; a subgroup selected on low baseline, so regression to the mean is a rival explanation). In a quasi-experimental comparison (243 students), programme classes took more in-school steps than classes without it (ES = 0.49). It confirms the reviews' behavioral finding in one programme, adds the initial-state condition, and measures no learning outcome.
  • Classroom energizers improved on-task behaviour, but PA during a classroom task showed no improvement [~M]: a systematic review of acute activity and children's attention (Janssen et al., 2014) reporting Mahar et al. (2006) beside Grieco et al. (2009), in which activity built into classroom tasks did not raise time on task (and did not lower it, against an inactive lesson). Two observational school studies as the review reports them; it qualifies the active lesson, not the break.
  • The timing of acute exercise relative to learning modulates memory consolidation [~S]: a meta-analysis of 25 experiments with healthy adults aged 18 to 84 found one bout during encoding slightly hurt episodic memory (d = −0.12), before encoding had a small non-significant effect (d = 0.11, helping young adults and hurting older ones), within 4 hours after learning helped (d = 0.47), and 4 or more hours after helped most (d = 1.05, two studies, wide interval); in one randomized three-group experiment, exercise 4 hours after learning improved 48-hour recall of picture-location pairs and exercise immediately after did not. Laboratory tasks with adults; it sets where a bout goes for retention, not whether a classroom break helps behavior.
  • Teachers in Playworks schools reported significantly better student behavior at recess and readiness for class [+M], with the narrower Playworks made transitions from recess to classroom learning less difficult, per teacher reports [+M] and Students in Playworks schools reported better behavior and attention in class after sports, games, and play [+M]: one randomized evaluation of a programme placing full-time coaches in schools to organise active recess play; teacher and student reports, no effect sizes printed. It bears on organised activity outside lessons and the transition back, not on classroom breaks, and the outcomes are reports of behavior and readiness, not learning. See Playworks.
  • Physical Experience Enhances Science Learning [~M]: a brief physical experience of the forces in angular momentum improved college physics quiz scores (lab experiments and a randomized classroom experiment; abstract only, no effect size); in another study of undergraduate heat-and-temperature concepts, physical, virtual and combined manipulation were equally effective and all beat traditional instruction. It supports enacting a concept, and qualifies it: what helped was manipulation, not physicality.
  • Exercise Increases Hippocampal Volume [~S]: a year-long randomized trial in older adults found aerobic walking increased anterior hippocampal volume by about 2% against a declining stretching control; a meta-analysis of 14 controlled trials (737 participants) found no significant effect on total hippocampal volume and a positive effect only on the left hippocampus, largely by preventing decline. It limits the page: it is a slow structural pathway in mostly older adults, not a learning gain, and does not justify classroom breaks on brain-growth grounds.

Learners, outcomes and settings differ across these claims (primary children's time on task, one programme's step counts, adults' lab recall, teachers' reports of recess, college physics quizzes, older adults' brain volume), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a learning gain from classroom movement breaks at a delay.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective for a movement break is usually more of the next segment spent on task. Elicit separately what learners want from it: to stop feeling stiff or sleepy, to talk to a friend, to have fun, to avoid being watched while moving, or nothing at all. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values the social part may come back louder, not calmer; one who dislikes moving in front of others may sit out the very routine meant for them.

For example, adults on a full-day compliance training may want the day to end on time and to stay comfortable, while the designer's objective is attention during the afternoon cases. A five-minute walking pair discussion of the next case serves both: it moves them, it does not lengthen the day, and it starts the next task. Record which one learners actually take up.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of segments with and without a bout, in comparable groups, show no difference in observed time on task, or if the gain disappears within a few weeks of routine use, as one review's longer follow-ups hint. If it holds for children but not for adolescents or adults in the same kind of comparison, restrict the page to children. If the larger gain for the least on-task learners does not appear when they are chosen in advance on several counts and compared with segments without a bout, drop the drifting-learners row's targeting and treat that result as regression to the mean. If classroom studies show movement during new-material segments does not hurt later recall, the advice to keep encoding still (taken from adult lab studies) should be dropped. If studies of active lessons show achievement gains that breaks do not, the page should separate the two. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "the wrong kind of movement" after the fact.

On-task behavior in the minutes after a bout, test-measured attention, achievement, delayed retention and well-being are separate claims. The present evidence supports the first for school-age children, and establishes no schedule, no number of bouts per day and no effect on learning.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model, and others found while rewriting it. Each says what it is and how far it bears on the page. All were re-read on 2026-10-19 against their evidence entries; the three Energizers claims attached on 2026-10-15 moved into the model above, and the rest stay here because they do not change it.

  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] — cited by the earlier page for movement as a way of noticing and regulating one's state; it is a review and a theoretical synthesis of self-monitoring in self-regulated learning and does not mention physical activity, so it bears on the page only through step 8's self-chosen bouts. Owned by Self-Monitoring.
  • Physical well-being — exercise, sleep, and nutrition — impacts learning [+W] — cited by the earlier page; one practitioner synthesis (q2, rigour r1) reporting neuroscience findings that aerobic exercise can transiently improve plasticity and increase hippocampal volume, and that sleep and nutrition matter. A general well-being claim, not a test of movement in learning time; its exercise part is qualified by the hippocampal-volume meta-analysis above.
  • In a pilot with 29 introductory radiation physics students, students reported that the pinch-and-spread gesture helped them feel wavelength and amplitude, with amplitude receiving the highest mean rating of 4.59 [+W] — cited by the earlier page; one pilot perception survey (29 medical imaging majors, 1-to-5 scale) of a gesture-controlled AR simulation; it measured perceptions, not understanding. Used in step 6 as an illustration only.
  • Listening to vocabulary lessons while physically active improves End-of-Unit exam performance over traditional intentional learning alone [+W] — one small experiment (51 first-year Ukrainian ESL students, three classes) adding audio vocabulary listening during physical activity to ordinary teaching; the group scored higher on the end-of-unit exam (mean 81.9 against 63.2). The comparison adds listening time as well as activity, so it does not separate the two; rigour r1.
  • Amount of listening time while active was not significantly related to End-of-Unit exam performance [~W] — within the same study's 14 active-listening students, more listening time did not significantly predict exam score (r = .346, p > .05); a small-sample dose check, not a test of activity.
  • After a researcher presentation on brain breaks, an estimated 80-90 percent of one school's teachers implemented the breaks in their classrooms [+W] — a principal's estimate in one school's case report; bears on teacher adoption of breaks, not on their effect.
  • In a randomized controlled trial, children in Playworks schools spent more time in vigorous physical activity at recess than students at non-Playworks schools, and more teachers in Playworks schools reported students engaged in intense recess activity [+M] — the same programme evaluation, measuring how active recess was (accelerometers, teacher surveys); it shows organised recess raises activity, not learning or behavior in class. Also listed under the evidence by question.
  • Nature exposure relieves stress in learners, including experimentally demonstrated reductions in heart rate and self-reported stress [~W] — a review reporting lower heart rate and stress with green window views, and healthier cortisol rhythms in forest schooling that the review says could not be attributed to physical activity; bears on outdoor active breaks only as a reminder that setting and movement are separate causes.

Claims¶

  • In two reviewed studies, CB-PA improvements in attention measures were moderated by other variables such as cognitive engagement and timing [~W]
  • Classroom-based physical activity interventions improve attention and/or on-task behavior in most reviewed studies of schoolchildren aged 4–18 [+W]
  • No significant differences in attention or working memory were found between intervention and control groups at either follow-up [-W]
  • In a randomized three-arm trial, medication but not physical exercise or neurofeedback was related to improved response inhibition and reduced theta power in children with ADHD [-W]
  • In the highest-quality reviewed study, classroom MVPA was negatively associated with students being off task or inattentive (β = −0.17; p = 0.042) [+W]
  • Physical activity is positively correlated with self-regulation in Chilean children aged 8–12 [+W]
  • School-setting studies show no overall significant difference between active and classroom lessons, but subgroup effects emerge [~W]
  • Over a three-year CB-PA intervention, the percent of time spent in MVPA was significantly associated with the percent of time on task (p < 0.01) [+W]

Related Principles¶

  • Mindfulness Activities — both can function as readiness and regulation supports before or during learning.
  • Check-ins — movement-based check-ins can help surface energy, readiness, and attention.
  • Quiet Learning Spaces — environment design affects whether movement and rest can coexist productively.
  • Skills Sprint — brief movement breaks can support focus across intensive practice sessions.
  • Memory Consolidation — owns the timing of exercise relative to learning for later retention.
  • Self-Monitoring — noticing one's own restlessness and choosing a self-directed movement break.
  • Clear Structure — a predictable place for the break between lesson segments.

Examples¶

  • Short movement breaks: Learners stand, stretch, walk, or reset between dense instructional segments.
  • Walk-and-talk discussion: Pairs discuss a prompt while moving, then return to record insights.
  • Optional movement stations: Learners choose a physically active way to reset before resuming work.
  • Embodied concept routines: Learners use gesture or movement to represent a process, sequence, or relation.
  • Strategies applying this principle: Physical Activity Breaks, Classroom Physical Activity Breaks, Physical Activity & Recess, GoNoodle: Physical Activity & Movement Breaks.
  • A programme: Playworks (organised active recess with full-time coaches).
