Spaced Learning¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 58 claims (32 for, 24 mixed, 1 against, 1 unmarked) · 40 studies (22 causal, 9 quant-synthesis, 8 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 12 of 40 report an effect size · 51 claims rest on one study

Description¶

A conditional model relating learner state, activity and subsequent observation; the linked pattern specifies a reusable design policy.

Conditional relationship¶

When later retrieval is the target, distributing comparable learning opportunities can improve delayed performance relative to massing; gap, prior learning, retrieval conditions and assessment horizon constrain the relationship. This is a conditional explanatory model, not a universal individual forecast. The spaced learning pattern supplies a reusable observation and design policy. Specific local examples below illustrate it rather than define its scope.

The relationship is tested, at the grain of spaced against massed practice of the same amount. A synthesis of the verbal-recall literature found spaced presentation of the same study time raised final-test recall from 36.7% to 47.3% correct across 271 comparisons, with only 12 showing no benefit or a reversal; a meta-analysis across verbal, cognitive and motor tasks put the effect at d = 0.46, moderated by task type and larger in less rigorous studies; and a randomised experiment with 1,354 adults found the best gap beat a same-session review by d = 1.1 on recall tested up to a year later: Spaced practice improves retention [+S]. Spacing out retrieval practice beat massing it in a meta-analysis of 29 studies (g = 0.74), a comparison of spaced with massed retrieval, not of retrieval with rereading: Spacing out retrieval practice beats massing it [+M]. In a field experiment after a credit workshop, spaced quizzes beat massed quizzes (d = .66) and no quizzes (d = .39) about five months later, while massed quizzes did not differ detectably from none: Spaced beat massed retrieval and no practice [+M]. Weaker designs point the same way but do not isolate spacing: a dissertation experiment compared structured computer-assisted instruction with spaced practice and spaced review against unstructured instruction, so structure and spacing changed together (Structured CAI with spaced practice and review beat unstructured CAI [+W]); and a 51-student ESL quasi-experiment with monthly audio repetition of target vocabulary scored 84.1 against 80.6 (Spaced listening outperformed comparison students [+W]). In a one-group revision-podcast study, the centre whose learners kept their gains at 30 days and the centre whose scores decayed differed in how they used the podcasts; reading that as distributed against massed listening is the authors' interpretation of usage nobody assigned, with no control group: Retention diverged by centre [~W]. A review of classroom-oriented spacing research states the same relationship for final tests generally, without a printed effect size: Spaced sessions improve final-test performance [+M]; two further narrative reviews say it holds across kinds of learning, domains and ages, with demonstrations in educational settings, again with no study or effect printed in the text the wiki holds (Spacing benefits diverse learning in educational settings [+W]; Spacing enhances learning across domains and the lifespan [+W]). A re-analysis of the 242 studies behind Dunlosky et al.'s (2013) review of ten study techniques put distributed practice highest (d = 0.85, against an overall 0.56), with heterogeneity so high (I² about 85%) that conditions matter more than the average: Distributed practice ranked highest of ten techniques [+M]. In a randomised classroom trial (17 precalculus sections, 128 students), weekly recall quizzes cycling through earlier fundamental concepts beat quizzing on current material only on a retention index six months later (81.6% against 77.3%, no effect size printed): Weekly spaced recall quizzes improved six-month retention [+M].

The relationship has a developmental boundary in the wiki's evidence. In two small within-subject experiments, nursery-school children (mean age about 4) recalled massed and spaced repetitions of pictures equally well (46.7% each in one, 34.7% against 32.0% in the other), although repeating a picture did help them: Preschoolers showed no spacing effect in free recall [~M]. First-graders given the same procedure recalled spaced repetitions better (54.2% against 37.5%): First-graders showed the spacing effect [+M], and the difference between the two ages was itself significant (a grade-by-spacing interaction, F(1,34) = 4.65): Grade and spacing interacted in children's recall [~M]. The spacing there was lag within one list, tested at once, not gaps of days; the wiki holds no claim testing day-scale spacing with children under six.

Learner state, objective and valued goal¶

This is a reusable design model. A study result supplies a bounded observation; it does not reveal the state or future outcome of a new learner.

Before selecting a configuration, record the target task and representation, task-specific prior experience, access and language constraints, time available, observed responses and the exact help provided. Preserve an unknown rather than replacing it with a generic label such as novice. Ask the learner what they want to accomplish and why it matters. Record that valued goal separately from the designer's learning objective; agreement is an observation to elicit, not an assumption. If they diverge, negotiate the task or purpose and retain the unresolved difference.

Define the intended capability, a representative assessment task and scoring instrument, the criterion chosen for this design, and the assessment horizon. Agree what response would warrant changing support and how to check later independent performance. Criteria are local decisions, not universal mastery thresholds. If the brief only asks for better learning, elicit these fields before selecting an exact dose or forecasting a gain.

Keep the record in this form: response under stated conditions → uncertain state hypotheses → discriminating observation → next activity → response at the stated horizon. Difficulty, accuracy, speed, confidence and engagement are different observations. None alone proves learning. Record a learner's changed purpose or constraints as well as a changed performance.

Observation and adaptation¶

This diagnostic policy is a design proposal, not a validated scheduling algorithm. Start with an unassisted sample of the target items, record item-level errors and the time since last exposure, then separate cued recognition, recall and meaningful use. Record restudy, corrective feedback, retrieval opportunities and actual gaps; changing them together changes the intervention.

Observation Rival interpretations and discriminating observation Proposed next action
An item is not recalled Weak initial encoding and loss of access across the gap are different possibilities. Check comprehension/recognition with a cue and whether the learner can explain the association; retest unassisted after relearning and an interval. If the association is not understood, clarify and re-encode it. If understood but inaccessible, use corrective feedback and another separated retrieval. Do not optimize the gap from a single failure.
Immediate recall is fluent Recent repetition and durable retrieval both predict this. Use an unassisted check after the target delay. Maintain the delayed check rather than count fluency as retained learning; revise the schedule only after collecting later evidence.
The learner rates items they just went over several times in a row as well known The recent run of exposures makes items feel familiar whether or not they will be recalled later; in one free-recall experiment learners rated massed items as more likely to be recalled than spaced ones while recalling them worse (Learners judged massed items more memorable though recall was worse [~M]); in the same experiment, asking for the ratings did not change the spacing benefit (Likelihood ratings left the spacing effect unchanged [+W]). Compare the rating with an unassisted check after a gap. Do not shorten or drop spaced reviews on the learner's sense of knowing; use the delayed check to decide.
Words are recalled but not used appropriately Item retrieval and contextual language use are different capabilities; ask for meaning and use in a new conversational context. Add contextual practice if recall succeeds but use fails. Do not claim that a word-pair recall study validates conversation transfer.

If six of ten words are recalled today, that is a sample response, not a calibrated probability of recall next week. For a learner valuing conversation, record the designer's recall criterion (for example eight of ten is a local choice), the sampled prompts and the one-week horizon; also agree how appropriate use will be judged in a representative conversation. Do not silently substitute recall for that valued goal. If these fields are missing, elicit them before choosing a calendar schedule.

Evidence-bounded expectation¶

A word-pair experiment separated total retrieval spacing from its relative arrangement: after first correct recall, repeated retrievals with intervening trials improved one-week recall relative to massed retrieval. At matched total nominal spacing, expanding, equal and contracting arrangements did not differ detectably: absolute versus relative spacing observation. The full source was checked. This does not establish equivalence or an advantage for expanding gaps, a universal calendar gap, or conversational use. Retain total practice, corrective feedback, initial learning criterion, actual gaps and final-test horizon when comparing schedules.

The same pattern holds at synthesis grain: across 54 effect sizes, expanding and uniform schedules of retrieval practice did not differ reliably (g = 0.034), with the number of retrieval exposures per item moderating that null (Spacing out retrieval practice beats massing it [~M]). An equal schedule may be selected for practical reasons while this exact comparison remains unresolved; do not predict a gain from changing only equal to expanding gaps on this evidence. Calendar timing and the dose needed for a particular learner remain local questions. The gap that helps depends on the delay to the test, and not monotonically: at a 1-day retention interval a 1-day gap beat a 1–15 minute gap, while one study suggested performance drops once the gap grows beyond a day (ISI effects are nonmonotonic and depend on retention interval [~M]); in the 1,354-person experiment the best gap grew with the test delay but shrank as a proportion of it, from about 20–40% of a one-week delay to about 5–10% of a one-year delay (Spaced practice improves retention [+S]); and in a word-list experiment, repetitions a week apart lost to same-day repetitions on an immediate test (.24 against .42) and won on a test a week later (.14 against .06) (Short spacing won at a short test, long spacing at a long one [~M]). When comparing schedules locally, give a fixed number of review trials rather than relearning to a criterion, which hands longer gaps more practice (Relearning-to-criterion designs confound spacing with practice amount [~W]; a methodological argument, not a tested result). Lower practice fluency can coexist with better delayed performance, but difficulty alone does not identify the cause or predict the outcome.

The evidence by question¶

Each question below links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it into one table, one row per distinct study, with each study's design and effect as recorded and no averaged verdict (eval/synthesis/spaced-learning.json, scripts/synthesize_claim.py). The count is of distinct studies across the claim and the narrower claims that bear on it.

  • Does spreading study or practice over sessions improve later retention more than massing the same amount of it? Spaced Practice Improves Retention [+S]: 12 distinct studies.
  • How long should the gap between sessions be, given when the knowledge is needed? The optimal interstudy interval increases as the retention interval increases [+M]: 5 distinct studies.
  • Should the gaps expand, stay equal, or contract? Total retrieval spacing improved one-week vocabulary recall while relative schedules were not distinguished [~M]: 4 distinct studies.
  • In a flashcard or review system, how many new items should enter and how should reviews be shared out? Under the Leitner Queue Network model, raising the rate of new-item introduction beyond a threshold causes a phase transition in learning rate, which a Mechanical Turk vocabulary experiment reproduced. [~M]: 1 distinct studies.
  • Will learners space their study on their own? Learners Misjudge Spacing Benefits [~S]: 4 distinct studies. A review adds that learners underuse spacing and retrieval practice generally, without frequency data: Learners underuse effective learning strategies [+W].

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: how long until the knowledge is needed (the test, the exam, the job), how many sessions the learners will have, and whether each return to the material can be a recall attempt rather than a reread.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model, or that were attached later. Those that bear on the model were worked into it on 2026-10-15, and the eight attached since were read and worked in on 2026-10-19 (the developmental boundary, the sense-of-knowing observation row, and the young-children and flashcard-queue rows); the ones left here were read and do not test spacing, and each label says how far it bears. Eighteen more were linked by the orphan-claim linker later and read against their Evidence entries the same day (wave 2, 2026-10-19): ten now sit in the model (the review and re-analysis sentences and the precalculus trial in the conditional relationship; the grade-by-spacing interaction in the developmental boundary and the young-children row; the rating result in the sense-of-knowing row; the proportionality result in the gap paragraph and the weeks-away and same-day rows; the relearning confound in the first row and the gap paragraph; the two expanding-against-fixed claims in the expanding row; the trial's instructor and class-hour claims in the new taught-course row; the underuse review in the self-paced row and the evidence by question), one (the very-short-retention claim) was already in the model, and the seven below stay here.

  • The dropout schedule was particularly effective for initially incorrect items, an advantage not attributable to initial ISI length or item difficulty in Experiment 2 [~W] — read 2026-10-19: bears on dropping recalled items from a session, an item-level scheduling policy; it sits in the pattern's short-session row and first-spaced-test observation row rather than here
  • The interleaving benefit is not caused by spacing, and the benefit extends to delayed tests [-W] — read 2026-10-19: a systematic review finding the interleaving benefit survives when spacing is held constant; it answers the interleaving bullet in this list (interleaving is a separate effect, not this page's relationship) and changes no spacing choice
  • With incidental orienting tasks that eliminate structural encoding, distributed presentations produce better free recall than massed presentations [+W] — read 2026-10-19: a review's account of two lab word-list experiments on why spacing works; mechanism, no design choice
  • Recall of between-lists repeated words matched two-independent-representations predictions at short retention intervals but exceeded independence after long retention intervals [~W] — read 2026-10-19: a model test inside the Glenberg and Lehmann (1980) experiments; mechanism, no design choice
  • When the orienting task prevented interitem associations, the within-list lag effect was attenuated after long retention intervals and this attenuation was not a floor effect [~W] — read 2026-10-19: which encoding processes carry the within-list lag effect, lab word lists; mechanism, no design choice
  • The effect of retention interval on reading comprehension after retrieval practice does not differ significantly between lower-order and higher-order thinking questions [~W] — read 2026-10-19 (not about this page): retrieval practice tested immediately or three weeks later; no spacing contrast

  • The training-by-content interaction did not vary with retention interval duration in the human experiment [~W] — read 2026-10-19 (not about this page): example-to-problem ratios for facts against skills, with an uncontrolled retention interval; no spacing contrast

  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [~W] — read 2026-10-15: about working-memory capacity and grouping, not the timing of practice; does not bear on the spacing relationship

  • High-confidence errors lead to better retention after correction than low-confidence errors. [~W] — read 2026-10-15: bears only on corrective feedback after a failed recall (the first observation row), not on spacing; no spacing contrast
  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~W] — read 2026-10-15: self-regulated learning reviews; at most background to the self-paced row, with no spacing contrast
  • Interleaving category examples improves inductive category learning for visual and mathematical materials, but not for expository texts or word categories [+M] — read 2026-10-15: interleaved against blocked presentation for category induction (g = 0.42, reversed for word categories); interleaving also spaces exposures, but the claim does not separate the two, so it does not test this page's relationship
  • Podcast Dose Response Learning Gain [+M] — read 2026-10-15: episode completion, not the timing of listening, predicted gain; does not bear on spacing
  • AI-generated revision podcasts produce statistically significant immediate learning gains in medical students at both study centres [+W] — read 2026-10-15: immediate pre-post gains from podcasts, no spacing contrast
  • Learners valued AI revision podcasts for portability and 'dead time' use but identified absent pauses and AI voice monotony as design barriers [+W] — read 2026-10-15: learners' views of podcast design (pauses, voice); does not bear on spacing
  • Podcast gains extend beyond recall to higher-order application and analysis items at both centres [+W] — read 2026-10-15: immediate gains on higher-order items, no spacing contrast
  • Topic-wise gains were heterogeneous, with some topics showing no meaningful change [~W] — read 2026-10-15: gains varied by topic; no spacing contrast

Source verification and open tests¶

Karpicke & Bauernschmidt (2011), author-hosted full text. Method and results checked for the bounded contrast above.

The examples are local design instances, not additional observations from those studies. Test the proposed interpretation and activity branches with new learner responses; compare competing designs under matched conditions before claiming a causal or predictive advantage. Reassess the model when the observations disagree with it.
