Math anxiety has a small-to-moderate negative correlation with math achievement (r = −.28) in a meta-analysis of correlations from 223 studies¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · quant-synthesis r2 · q3 · i2 medium · n=332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) from 223 studies

Math anxiety — feelings of tension, apprehension, or dread when manipulating numbers or solving mathematical problems — goes with lower mathematical achievement. The one synthesis recorded here pools correlations, so it shows the association and its moderators, not that anxiety causes the lower scores: the relation may run both ways. The usual causal account, that anxiety consumes working memory needed for computation and reasoning and so impairs performance most under evaluative pressure or high working memory demand, is discussed below as a mechanism no entry here tests.

Evidence¶

Barroso et al. 2021¶

Barroso, C., Ganley, C. M., McGraw, A. L., Geer, E. A., Hart, S. A., & Daucourt, M. C. (2021). A meta-analysis of the relation between math anxiety and math achievement. Psychological Bulletin, 147(2), 134–168. doi:10.1037/bul0000307

q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies · i2 · medium effect, r=-.28 · n=332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) from 223 studies · quant-synthesis · r2

Multilevel meta-analysis of 223 studies published 1992–2018, modeling 747 correlation coefficients from 332 independent samples (~385,441 participants) to account for non-independence among effect sizes from the same sample. The pooled correlation between math anxiety and math achievement was r = −.28 (small-to-moderate, negative, statistically significant), and the relation held for nearly every moderator subgroup tested (grade level, math-ability level, adolescent/adult anxiety scales, math topic of the anxiety scale, and assessment type), with the sole exception of anxiety and approximate-number-system tasks. Grade level, ability level, and scale/assessment type were significant moderators of the size of the relation, and effect sizes were somewhat weaker in grades 3–5 and postsecondary samples than at other ages.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The dominant account is processing-efficiency: anxious thoughts (worry, rumination) act as a secondary task that occupies the same working memory resources mathematics demands, so performance drops most on problems with high working memory demands. This places math anxiety in the same family as other load-inducing impairments — see Cognitive overload degrades learning and Chunking reduces working memory load. Within Cognitive load theory, anxiety functions as an additional source of extraneous load rather than a separate affective phenomenon.

Boundary conditions. The degradation is typically strongest under timed, high-stakes, or evaluative conditions, and weakest when pressure is removed or when learners can offload computation (e.g., scratch work, calculators for routine steps). Highly anxious individuals are often not low-ability: performance gaps between high- and low-anxious learners widen with pressure, consistent with anxiety suppressing rather than reflecting competence. This makes the effect context-dependent rather than uniform — a [~]-direction pattern in which the impairment emerges only when situational demands collide with anxiety-generated load.

Directionality. The relationship is likely reciprocal — poor performance breeds anxiety, and anxiety degrades subsequent performance — so this claim should be read as one loop in a cycle, not a simple one-way cause. This also means interventions can target either side of the loop: reducing evaluative pressure or building fluency to remove sources of failure.

Design implications. If the working-memory account holds (no entry here tests it), the impairment is load-driven rather than ability-driven, so instructional designs that reduce working memory demands — worked examples, externalized computation, untimed practice — should disproportionately benefit highly anxious learners. Timed testing and public performance of mathematics are the conditions most likely to widen anxiety-based gaps; designers using Test-enhanced learning should weigh retrieval-practice benefits against the evaluative pressure that can trigger impairment in anxious learners. Designers working within Cognitive load management should treat anxiety as an additional source of extraneous load, not merely an affective side issue, and appraisal-focused approaches such as Belonging interventions improve outcomes address the evaluative concerns that drive the anxiety in the first place.

Open questions. Effect sizes vary widely across age groups and task types; whether anxiety specifically harms arithmetic retrieval, procedural execution, or novel problem-solving to different degrees remains contested. The field also lacks strong causal identification for the working-memory account in classroom settings, where most evidence is correlational.
