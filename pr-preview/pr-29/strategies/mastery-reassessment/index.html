    Mastery Reassessment¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Mastery reassessment is a grading and instructional strategy in which an initial assessment is treated as formative: learners who fall short of a proficiency standard receive targeted reteaching or corrective instruction, then attempt a parallel reassessment. The reassessment score typically replaces the original rather than being averaged with it, signaling that the goal is eventual proficiency rather than performance on a single occasion. This operationalizes the core commitment of mastery learning — that time and support vary while the achievement standard stays constant.

    Design Implications¶

    Reassessment aligns grading with the mastery learning finding that most learners can reach high standards when given the time and corrective instruction they need Kulik et al.'s meta-analysis found mastery procedures raise achievement, with the effect strongest when mastery is paired with formative testing and corrective feedback [+S]. The replacement (rather than averaged) score is critical: averaging penalizes the late learner for the pace of their learning and undermines the incentive to remediate. Reassessment works best when the original assessment yields diagnostic information that drives the reteaching, not merely a score Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and process levels rather than the self. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Assessments organized around explicit, communicated proficiency criteria (Assessment with rubrics or learning targets)
    • A parallel or equivalent reassessment form — not the identical item set, which invites memorization of answers
    • Targeted corrective activities between attempts (Practice, Coaching, or Fading-structured reteaching)
    • A grading policy that defines eligibility (e.g., completion of remediation) and score replacement rules

    Constraints¶

    • Without required remediation between attempts, reassessment becomes retaking: learners re-attempt without changing their preparation, and gains are minimal [-M]
    • Unlimited low-cost retakes can encourage minimal first effort among learners with weak achievement goals [~M] — eligibility conditions (completed practice, correction of errors) mitigate this
    • Reassessment is costly in instructor time; scaling requires item banks, peer-supported remediation, or Adaptive Mastery Learning systems that automate the reteach-retest cycle
    • Poorly matched reassessment forms (easier items, different constructs) corrupt the proficiency inference [-M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Full replacement: the new score fully replaces the old — strongest mastery signal, highest stakes on item equivalence
    • Capped replacement: reassessment can raise a score to a ceiling (e.g., 90%), preserving some incentive for first-attempt effort
    • Unit-level reassessment: learners reassess at the end of a unit after all corrective work, rather than per quiz
    • Continuous mastery tracking: platforms such as Khan Academy use mastery levels that decay over time, requiring periodic re-demonstration rather than one-time retakes

    Target Learners¶

    • Struggling learners who need more time than a fixed schedule allows — the group for whom mastery learning shows its largest effects [+S]
    • Learners whose early failure would otherwise depress self-efficacy and persistence [+M]; a replaceable score reframes early failure as information rather than verdict
    • High achievers benefit less and may find mandatory reassessment structures redundant [~W]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Foundational knowledge and skills where later learning depends on prior proficiency (prerequisite structures in mathematics, language, science)
    • Procedural fluency that requires multiple spaced attempts to consolidate
    • Competencies assessed against standards rather than ranked against peers

    Instructions¶

    1. Define proficiency criteria for each learning target and share them before instruction (Clear Structure).
    2. Administer the initial assessment and diagnose errors by target, not just total score (Assessment).
    3. Assign targeted corrective work matched to diagnosed gaps — Practice with Feedback, Coaching, or Analogies to rebuild underlying concepts.
    4. Require completion of corrective work as the eligibility condition for reassessment.
    5. Administer a parallel reassessment form; replace the original score upon demonstrated proficiency.
    6. Track mastery across targets over time (Check-in or mastery dashboard) so reassessment informs pacing decisions.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Formative Assessment — reassessment is the grading-policy extension of formative assessment cycles
    • Competency-Based Learning — reassessment operationalizes the competency principle that advancement depends on demonstrated proficiency, not seat time
    • Adaptive Learning — adaptive systems automate the diagnose-remediate-reassess loop

    Examples¶

    Khan Academy — Mastery system in which skill levels can decay and learners re-demonstrate proficiency through new exercise sets, with hints and videos serving as the corrective layer.

    Modern Classroom Project — Self-paced blended units in which learners must pass a mastery check to move on; retakes after teacher-guided review are built into the model.

    Standards-based grading in K-12 districts (e.g., Ken O'Connor's and Rick Wormeli's frameworks) — District grading policies that mandate reassessment opportunities with score replacement for standards-based report cards.
