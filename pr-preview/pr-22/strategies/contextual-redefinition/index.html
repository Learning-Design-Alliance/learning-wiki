    Contextual Redefinition¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Contextual redefinition is a vocabulary strategy in which learners first meet a target word embedded in a sentence or passage whose surrounding language constrains the word's meaning. Learners generate a tentative definition from the context, after which the teacher or material confirms, refines, or corrects it with an explicit definition. The sequence — encounter, hypothesize, verify — converts passive word exposure into an active inference-and-feedback cycle.

    Design Implications¶

    The strategy leverages inference from context while protecting against the well-documented weakness of context-only learning: incidental acquisition from reading is slow and often imprecise [~M]. The hypothesis step activates prior knowledge and creates a "need to know," and the explicit confirmation step supplies the accurate meaning that context alone frequently fails to deliver [~M]. Because learners commit to a guess before receiving the definition, feedback becomes more informative and memorable — a mechanism consistent with pretesting and errorful-guessing effects [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Sentences rich enough to genuinely constrain the word's meaning — not neutral frames where any meaning fits
    • A deliberate hypothesis phase: learners commit to a tentative definition before hearing the correct one
    • Immediate, explicit confirmation with a clear definition, example, or Non-Examples
    • Multiple encounters with the word across varied contexts to support retention Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]

    Constraints¶

    • Natural text contexts are frequently unreliable cues; readers often infer wrong or overly narrow meanings from context alone [~M] — the verification step is not optional
    • Weak or ambiguous contexts produce confident wrong hypotheses, which can entrench errors if not corrected High-confidence errors improve retention when corrected. [~M]
    • Inefficient for teaching large volumes of words; best reserved for high-utility, conceptually rich vocabulary
    • Less effective for learners with very limited background knowledge, who lack the semantic resources to constrain a hypothesis

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Whole-class oral format: teacher presents sentence on board, students propose meanings, teacher confirms — suitable for introducing words before reading
    • Written worksheet format: several sentences per word, students write definitions, then check against a key
    • Student-generated contexts: after explicit teaching, students write their own constraining sentences for peers to interpret, reversing the roles
    • Digital adaptation: platforms present a word in context, collect a prediction, then reveal a definition and schedule review

    Target Learners¶

    • Upper elementary through adult learners who can generate and evaluate plausible word meanings
    • Struggling readers benefit from the explicit confirmation step, which compensates for weak incidental word-learning from context [~M]
    • English language learners, when contexts are carefully constructed and paired with accessible syntax Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Word meaning acquisition: building accurate definitional knowledge of high-utility vocabulary
    • Inference skill: practicing meaning derivation from linguistic evidence
    • Reading comprehension support: pre-teaching vocabulary that would otherwise block passage understanding

    Instructions¶

    1. Select 3–5 high-utility words that are essential to understanding an upcoming text.
    2. Write a sentence for each word in which the surrounding language strongly constrains the meaning (clauses, appositives, contrasts, cause-effect signals).
    3. Present the sentence and ask learners to propose a tentative meaning — require a commitment, not a shrug (Activation).
    4. Confirm or correct each hypothesis with an explicit, learner-friendly definition and a second example (Assessment as quick formative check).
    5. Contrast the correct meaning with a near-miss to sharpen boundaries (Non-Examples).
    6. Re-encounter the words in the target text and schedule brief spaced reviews over subsequent sessions Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

    Related Strategies¶

    • Frayer Model — extends a confirmed definition into a four-quadrant concept analysis
    • Pretesting — shares the guess-then-feedback mechanism
    • Morphological Analysis — complements contextual inference with word-part analysis

    Examples¶

    • Before-reading vocabulary routine: a middle-school science teacher presents "evaporate" in "The puddle evaporated, leaving only a faint ring of dust," collects student guesses, then confirms the definition before students read the water-cycle chapter.
    • ISIP/READ 180-style digital vocabulary: adaptive reading platforms present target words in sentence contexts, log learner predictions, and schedule spaced re-encounters across sessions.
    • Beck, McKeown & Kucan's robust vocabulary instruction: Tier Two words (e.g., reluctant, coincidence) introduced through teacher-crafted contexts followed by interactive verification and multiple-context follow-up.
