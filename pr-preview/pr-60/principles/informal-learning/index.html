    Informal Learning¶

    Principle · All principles

    Description¶

    Boileau (2019), drawing on Jay Cross, frames formal and informal learning as opposite ends of a continuum rather than a strict dichotomy. Formal learning is "pushed" to learners according to a predetermined curriculum decided by someone else — "like riding a bus: the driver decides where the bus is going; the passengers are along for the ride" (Cross, 2007, p. 236). Informal learning is "pulled" by the learner's own perceived need — "like riding a bike; the rider chooses the destination, the speed, and the route" (Cross, 2007, p. 236) — and is unofficial, self-directed, and situated in meaningful, performance-linked experience rather than separated in time and place from its application.

    Merriam and Bierema (2014) identify four sub-types: self-directed learning (learner-initiated goal-setting, resource identification, and self-evaluation), incidental learning (an unplanned byproduct of doing something else), tacit learning (subconscious, intuition- or emotion-based), and integrative learning (non-conscious tacit knowledge combined with conscious learning to produce creative insight).

    The scale of informal learning is large and persistently under-resourced. The widely cited (if empirically loose) "70:20:10 rule" holds that roughly 70% of workplace learning comes from on-the-job experience, 20% from mentoring, and only 10% from formal coursework — yet Cross's spending/outcome paradox observes that organizations spend roughly 80% of training budgets on the formal 10%, for a correspondingly small share of the actual performance-relevant learning that occurs. Falk and Dierking similarly estimate that up to 95% of science learning occurs outside formal schooling, through free-choice engagement with museums, hobbies, and self-directed inquiry. Practical mechanisms that operationalize informal learning include microtraining (short, ~15-minute segments combining prior-knowledge activation, demonstration/practice, feedback, and a transfer strategy, embedded near the work setting) and Performance Support Tools — job aids Rossett and Schafer (2007) classify as sidekicks (real-time, in-the-moment guidance, like GPS turn-by-turn navigation) or planners (used in advance to access community-curated knowledge, like planning a route before a trip), evaluated by four factors: convergence (proximity to the task), simplicity, relevance to performance, and personalization.

    Boileau also notes a cultural dimension: informal, experiential learning is undervalued in Western culture relative to "book learning," even though non-Western cultural traditions more often treat learning as communal, lifelong, and holistic rather than individualistic and confined to a schooling period (Merriam & Kim, 2011).

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Willingness to invest in the performance-support and mentoring structures (job aids, coaching relationships, communities of practice) that actually carry most workplace learning, rather than defaulting to formal coursework as the primary lever
    • For microtraining specifically: proximity to the work setting and a structure that activates prior knowledge, demonstrates/practices the skill, and gives feedback, all within a short (~15-minute) segment

    Constraints¶

    • Microtraining is best suited to performance remediation, knowledge refreshing, and mastery-building for content already familiar to learners — it is poorly suited to novice learners unless combined with additional scaffolding to build prerequisite knowledge
    • The spending/outcome paradox is a persistent organizational tendency, not a one-time fix: formal training remains easier to budget, schedule, and credential than informal learning, so the imbalance tends to recur even when leaders are aware of it
    • Digital open badges issued for MOOC completion complicate the "unofficial" character of informal learning once attendance is tracked and paid certification is introduced — Law (2015) notes learners are willing to pay for recognition of otherwise-informal learning, which shifts its nature

    Target Learners¶

    • Adult and workplace learners, where the large majority of actual learning happens outside formal training programs
    • Self-directed learners pursuing personal or professional development through hobbies, informal science engagement, or on-the-job problem-solving

    Target Learning Objectives¶

    • Job performance and task completion, where the learner is "pulled" by an identified skill or knowledge gap rather than working through a predetermined curriculum
    • Lifelong, cumulative learning that persists across a learner's whole life rather than being bounded by a schooling period

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Self-Regulated Learning [+M] — self-directed learning, one of the four informal-learning sub-types, depends on the same goal-setting, strategy-selection, and self-evaluation processes SRL describes

    Claims¶

    Related Principles¶

    • Performance Technology — Performance Support Tools (sidekicks and planners) are one of the concrete mechanisms through which informal learning is supported and mediated
    • Problem-based Learning — both emphasize learner-directed engagement driven by an authentic need rather than a predetermined sequence

    Examples¶

    • A GPS navigation app used turn-by-turn while driving (a performance-support "sidekick") versus used in advance to plan a route (a "planner")
    • A 15-minute microtraining module delivered near the work setting to refresh a skill an employee already has some familiarity with
    • Informal science learning through museum visits, hobbies (amateur astronomy, beekeeping), or self-directed internet research prompted by a personal event (e.g., a medical diagnosis)
