    Luminous Science Transdisciplinary Curriculum¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    Most cross-disciplinary STEAM projects ask students to apply one discipline's methods to illustrate another's content (e.g., "make art about science"), which tends to keep disciplines multidisciplinary — combined in parallel — rather than transdisciplinary — critically integrated. This pattern instead anchors a unit in a single shared, changing phenomenon (a hydroponic garden) and a single flexible representational medium (programmable, hand-crafted sculptural lanterns whose light color, brightness, and animation can simultaneously encode scientific data, artistic narrative, and computational logic) that does not belong cleanly to any one discipline's normal toolkit. Because no single discipline's standard evaluation criteria apply cleanly to the artifact, teachers and students are forced to negotiate what makes a "good" representation across art, science, and computing simultaneously — the productive struggle of that negotiation is where transdisciplinary thinking is documented to emerge.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A shared, observable, changing phenomenon that supports multiple disciplinary questions at once (the source case used a classroom hydroponic garden with measurable moisture, temperature, light, and CO2)
    • A representational medium flexible enough to carry data (science), narrative and form (art), and interactive logic (computing) at once, and unfamiliar enough that no discipline's standard criteria apply automatically (the source case used wire-and-paper sculptural lanterns with embedded programmable LEDs)
    • A mixed-disciplinary teacher design team (art, science, computing) working through both making and planning phases together, so real-time negotiation of criteria happens among adults before it needs to happen with students

    Constraints¶

    • Teachers need to see enough of their own discipline's required content represented in the project before they can commit classroom time to it — a project that reads as "mostly art" or "mostly computing" to a science teacher risks being abandoned for a lower-integration alternative
    • Institutional structures that presuppose a single discipline's definition of "problem" (e.g., a school-wide Problem-Based Learning mandate built around scientific problem-solving) can actively conflict with transdisciplinary framing, since an artistic "problem" (how to manifest an idea) does not fit a PBL rubric built for a scientific one
    • Mixed-discipline design groups sacrifice some depth for breadth: pairs of same-discipline teachers pursued deeper disciplinary investigation than mixed-discipline pairs, who had to spend time establishing shared vocabulary first

    Grain Size¶

    Unit (a multi-week classroom project; the source professional-development workshop that produced Year 1 units ran 5 days)

    Target Goals¶

    • Developing transdisciplinary thinking — not just combining disciplines, but critically reflecting on and expanding the epistemic criteria each discipline brings
    • Mixed-disciplinary teacher making supports transdisciplinary epistemic liberation [+M]

    Target Learners¶

    • K-12 teachers (as co-designers and makers) and their students, across art, science, and computing classrooms

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Disciplinary integration levels (Boix Mansilla) — multidisciplinary (parallel), interdisciplinary (actively merged), and transdisciplinary (critically reflecting on and expanding disciplinary boundaries)
    • Metarepresentational competence (diSessa) — extended here from students to teachers: judgment about what makes a representation "good" across disciplines

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    1. Identify a shared, observable, changing phenomenon that supports questions from multiple disciplines at once (e.g., a classroom garden with measurable moisture, temperature, light, and CO2).
    2. Select or design a representational medium flexible enough to carry data, narrative, and interactive logic simultaneously, and unfamiliar enough that no single discipline's standard criteria apply automatically (e.g., programmable sculptural lanterns).
    3. Convene a mixed-disciplinary teacher team for a hands-on making workshop, building the artifact themselves before planning classroom implementation, so criteria negotiation happens among the co-designers first.
    4. During making, surface and discuss disciplinary tensions explicitly as they arise, rather than letting one discipline's criteria silently dominate; document both "compromise" and "critical surrender" resolutions.
    5. Plan classroom implementation ensuring each participating teacher can identify sufficient coverage of their own required standards within the project, adjusting timeline or classroom placement (e.g., after-school) if not.

    Personalization¶

    • The specific phenomenon and representational medium are interchangeable; what matters structurally is that no discipline's criteria fit the artifact by default, forcing genuine cross-disciplinary negotiation.

    Examples¶

    • Two teachers built a narrative of "mountain gnomes" responding to garden sensor data into their lantern design while separately insisting the data display stay simple — an active compromise (interdisciplinary integration) between narrative and clarity criteria.
    • A group modeling the lantern as a humanoid body with plant processes (blood = water transport) hit a science-vs-expression impasse ("the scientist in me hates combining those data types") and resolved it by adding a supplementary written "plaque," rather than forcing one criterion to fully dominate — an instance of critical, transdisciplinary negotiation rather than either discipline simply winning.
