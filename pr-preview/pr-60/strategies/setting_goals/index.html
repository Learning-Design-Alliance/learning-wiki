    Setting Goals¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Lessons have clear learning intentions with goals that clarify what success looks like. Lesson goals always explain what students need to understand and what they must be able to do. Clear goals help the teacher plan learning activities and help students understand what is required, providing a reference point against which progress can be monitored and Assessment interpreted.

    Design Implications¶

    Goal-setting theory shows that specific, challenging goals reliably produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S]. For novices, however, the type of goal matters: goals framed around learning processes and strategies outperform outcome-framed goals, which can depress performance and increase anxiety when learners lack the skills to reach the outcome Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]. Goals also serve a self-regulatory function — learners who know the target can self-monitor and adjust, which is a core mechanism of Self-Regulated Learning.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Learning intentions defined before the lesson, expressed in terms of observable understanding or performance, not coverage of content
    • Success criteria that make "good" concrete — exemplars, rubrics, or worked models students can compare against
    • Goals communicated in learner-accessible language, not curriculum-code language
    • Alignment between goals, activities, and Assessment so students are not evaluated on something the goal never stated

    Constraints¶

    • Goals that are stated but never revisited have little effect; the benefit comes from students using them to monitor progress, not from their mere presence on a slide [-M]
    • Specific performance goals assigned to novices on complex tasks can impair learning — learners fixate on the outcome and skip the strategy-building that produces it Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [-M]
    • Overly narrow goals can narrow learning: when learners pursue a specific target, they often neglect adjacent material and reduce incidental learning [-M]
    • Goals perceived as externally imposed can undermine motivation when they crowd out autonomy; supporting learner choice over goals preserves engagement Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Teacher-set goals vs. co-constructed goals: co-construction increases ownership but requires learners to have enough knowledge to articulate a target
    • Proximal (lesson-level) vs. distal (unit- or term-level) goals: proximal goals support self-monitoring; distal goals provide purpose — effective practice layers both
    • Mastery vs. performance framing: mastery-oriented goals ("understand X") sustain persistence better than normative goals ("beat the class average") [~M]

    Target Learners¶

    • All learner levels, but goal framing must match expertise: novices benefit from process and mastery goals, while advanced learners can handle specific outcome goals Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]
    • Learners with weak self-regulation benefit most from explicit goals paired with Check-Ins, since the goal gives them a standard to self-monitor against Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Clarifying learning intentions and success criteria for students
    • Supporting metacognition: goals are the standard against which self-assessment and Self-Explanation operate
    • Motivation and persistence: specific goals sustain effort on difficult tasks Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S]

    Instructions¶

    1. Define the learning intention in terms of what students will understand or be able to do — not what activity they will complete
    2. Derive success criteria: what evidence would show the goal is met? Prepare an exemplar or model where possible
    3. Share the goal at the start of the lesson in learner-friendly language, possibly via Advance Organizers that situate it in the unit's bigger picture
    4. Design activities that visibly connect to the goal; use Cognitive Load Management so the goal is achievable within the lesson's working-memory budget
    5. Revisit the goal during and at the end of the lesson — have students judge their own work against the success criteria (Assessment for Learning)
    6. Adjust subsequent goals based on evidence of understanding rather than proceeding on the original plan

    Related Strategies¶

    • Activating Prior Knowledge — goals land better when connected to what learners already know
    • Achievable Micro-Goals — decomposing a lesson goal into proximal steps learners can monitor

    Related Elements¶

    • Advance Organizers — frame where the lesson goal sits in the larger structure
    • Check-Ins — recurring opportunities to compare progress against the goal
    • Assessment — goals only function when assessment is visibly aligned to them

    Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

    • Gagné's 9 Events — "inform learners of objectives" is the second event, positioned before content presentation
    • Direct Instruction — goals and success criteria are stated explicitly before modeling begins
    • 4C ID — whole-task learning goals drive the design of task classes and support

    Examples¶

    • WALT/WILF (We Are Learning To / What I'm Looking For) — a widely used primary-school routine in UK and Australian classrooms: the learning intention (WALT) and success criteria (WILF) are posted and referenced throughout the lesson
    • IRIS Connect lesson design — professional development resources on sharing objectives and success criteria with students
    • Standards-based grading systems (e.g., Khan Academy mastery tracking) — each exercise is tied to an explicit skill goal, and learners see their progress toward mastery of that specific goal
