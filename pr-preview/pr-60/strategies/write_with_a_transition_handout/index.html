    Write with a Transition Handout¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Before learners write, the instructor provides a handout organizing common transitions by rhetorical function (e.g., contrast, cause, sequence, concession). The instructor models where transitions fit in a text and explains how each one signals a relationship to the reader, converting an implicit stylistic convention into an explicit, usable tool.

    Design Implications¶

    Transition handouts work because they externalize a structural convention that proficient writers apply automatically, freeing working memory for content during composition Chunking reduces the working-memory load of complex tasks. [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on modeling and explanation: a bare list of transition words produces mechanical insertion, while demonstrating transitions in context — showing how "however" sets up an expectation and "therefore" closes one — builds procedural knowledge learners can transfer. Handouts function as a temporary scaffold and should be faded as learners internalize the moves Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A handout organized by rhetorical function, not an alphabetical word list — learners need to know what job a transition does, not just that it exists
    • Instructor modeling on authentic text: annotate a paragraph showing where transitions fit and why (Provide Guidance)
    • Immediate application in a writing task (Practice), ideally with revision of a draft rather than only first-draft use
    • Discipline-appropriate examples; transitions in a lab report differ from those in an argumentative essay

    Constraints¶

    • Learners may overuse transitions or deploy them to connect ideas that are not actually related, producing a veneer of coherence over disorganized reasoning [-M]
    • Word-level fixes cannot repair paragraph-level structural problems; if the underlying logic is unclear, transitions mask rather than fix it [-M]
    • Handouts used as reference-only artifacts (distributed, never modeled) show little effect on writing quality [-W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Tiered handouts: a basic list (first, next, however) for novice writers; a discipline-specific set (consequently, notwithstanding, these findings suggest) for advanced or disciplinary writing
    • Sentence-frame variants that embed transitions in full stems ("One counterargument is…; however,…") for learners who need more structure
    • Digital versions as writing-tool checklists integrated into drafting platforms or revision checklists

    Target Learners¶

    • Novice writers, especially adolescents and undergraduates, who have not internalized cohesion conventions [+M]
    • English language learners, who benefit from explicit signaling of English rhetorical conventions [+M]
    • Advanced writers gain little; explicit transition instruction becomes redundant once cohesion is automatized [~W]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Writing coherence: signaling logical relationships between sentences and paragraphs
    • Disciplinary literacy: mastering the connective conventions of academic and professional genres
    • Revision skill: using transitions as diagnostic markers for gaps in reasoning during self-editing

    Instructions¶

    1. Prepare a handout of transitions grouped by rhetorical function, with a short example sentence for each (Provide Guidance).
    2. Model: project a short paragraph, think aloud about the relationship between ideas, and select and insert transitions, explaining what each signals to the reader.
    3. Have learners apply the handout in a short writing or revision task (Practice).
    4. Debrief: learners identify one transition they used and justify why it fits the relationship; peers check whether the signaled relationship actually holds.
    5. Fade the handout over successive assignments, shifting it to a revision checklist.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Sentence combining and sentence-frame instruction — adjacent explicit-writing strategies that, like transition handouts, make syntactic conventions visible before independent writing
    • Mentor-text analysis — studying published transitions in authentic texts deepens the function-based understanding the handout introduces

    Examples¶

    • An undergraduate composition course distributes a function-sorted transition handout, annotates a model argumentative paragraph together, then requires students to revise their own drafts by underlining each transition and verifying the relationship it claims.
    • A secondary science department maintains a shared "lab report transitions" handout (therefore, in contrast, these data indicate) used across courses, aligning with disciplinary literacy goals.
    • The Purdue OWL provides a widely used function-organized transition reference that instructors adapt into classroom handouts.
