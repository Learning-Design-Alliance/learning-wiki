Programmed Instruction¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 17 claims (5 for, 10 mixed, 2 against) · 16 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 associational, 2 theoretical, 1 review), q1–q4 · 5 of 16 report an effect size · 13 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable sequence for teaching well-defined content one small step at a time: a frame gives a little information → the learner makes an active response (writes or selects an answer) before the answer is shown → the answer is confirmed or corrected at once → the next frame follows, at the learner's own pace (linear programming) or chosen by the response (branching: a remedial frame after an error, a skip ahead after strong performance) → a unit check decides whether the learner moves on. The intended change is in recalling facts, applying rules and carrying out procedures (factual, procedure), measured on items the learner has not just seen in a frame.

It sits inside three converted neighbours and does not repeat their models. The Mastery Learning pattern owns the unit criterion, the corrective loop and the recheck; this page uses that gate at the end of each run of frames. The Immediate Feedback principle owns when feedback should come and what form it takes; this page applies it at the grain of one frame. The Adaptive Learning principle owns when adjusting the path to the learner helps; branching programming is its oldest form. Game-Based Mastery Learning is a modern descendant that adds a game layer to the same small-step core, and Direct Instruction is the teacher-led, group-paced relative. Self-pacing as such belongs to the Self-Directed Learning pattern and principle.

No claim in this wiki tests programmed instruction itself: no recorded study compares frames with a response and immediate confirmation against another form of the same content, and none compares linear with branching programs or small steps with large ones. The nearest evidence is of four kinds, each carried here by extrapolation: Keller's Personalized System of Instruction (PSI), a descendant that keeps self-paced units and a mastery test, from one large 1990 meta-analysis; feedback after a retrieval attempt; intelligent tutoring systems, which branch on each response; and one experiment and one meta-analysis on learner control of pacing. The old page's account (Molenda, 2008) says that most of PI's specific hypotheses (small steps, linear order, confirmation as a universal reinforcer) did not hold up under testing, while PI as a format still did better than conventional group instruction in comparison studies; that history is the old page's, from its Key Sources, not from a claim page. The sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, with each step's evidence status stated.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Before the first frame, a short set of unprompted items from the end of the unit and one or two from its prerequisites. Record each response, whether it was right, and how long it took. Use expertise values where known (novice, intermediate, advanced); record unknowns as unknown rather than assuming novice.
The content Whether it breaks into steps that each have a checkable right answer (factual, procedure, a rule), or whether answers are open, contested or judged as a whole (complex-skill, design, argument). Which steps depend on which. Which errors learners commonly make.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-self-paced, online-instructor-led, workplace-clinical), device (paper, computer, phone only), single-session or days-weeks, whether a teacher, tutor or proctor is present and for how long, whether learners must finish by a date.
Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants to be able to do and by when (pass a certification, do a task at work, keep up with a class). Record disagreement with the designer objective, and how much extra time the learner will accept for repeating frames.
Designer objective The capability each unit stands for, its knowledge type, the unit criterion (the share correct on the unit check) and its justification, and which later units depend on it. If the brief names its own check, rubric or criterion, record it as given.
Outcome The unit check and the course examination, scored on items not shown in the frames; whether the examination is written locally or is an independent standardized test; completion (persistence) and time to criterion (learning-time); and any delayed horizon.

A request to "turn this module into self-paced bite-sized steps" does not specify these. Ask what the right answer to each step is, what the learner should be able to do at the end, and whether the learner must finish by a date, before choosing step size, branching rules or a criterion.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default sequence is the earlier page's five steps, with a placement step and a unit gate added from the neighbouring patterns. Steps 3 and 6 have neighbouring claims, carried by extrapolation; the others are untested here.

  1. Place the learner. Give the starting items (inputs, above). A learner who meets the unit criterion on them skips the unit's frames and takes the unit check; a learner who misses a prerequisite starts with its frames. Evidence status: untested proposal. More proficient learners respond faster at the step level in tutoring data (higher prior proficiency goes with faster step-level responses [~W], associational, eight algebra-tutor deployments), which is a reason to expect placement to save their time, not evidence that it does.
  2. Break the content into frames. Each frame presents one piece of information or one step, then asks for a response that uses it. For a new rule, state the rule, give one or two examples, then give incomplete examples for the learner to complete. Evidence status: the rule-example sequence [~W], a 1966 conceptual paper reporting that this order "appeared to be an excellent method" in early programmed-instruction work; an argument, not a comparison. Step size is untested here: start with steps a novice gets right on about four frames in five (a proposal), and enlarge them when errors are rare.
  3. Require a response before showing the answer, then confirm or correct at once. Constructed responses (typed or written) for recall; selected responses only where the final task is recognition. After an error, show the correct answer and, for a rule, the reason; after a correct answer, a short confirmation is enough. Evidence status: feedback after retrieval [+M]: in a laboratory word-pair study, giving the correct answer after a wrong retrieval greatly raised retention a week later while feedback after correct answers made little difference; in classroom studies, quizzes with corrective feedback gave a larger testing effect than quizzes without, a between-study comparison that an earlier meta-analysis did not find [~M]. These are quizzes and word pairs, not programmed frames.
  4. Branch on the response. After two errors on frames of the same kind (a proposal), route the learner to remedial frames that teach the step a different way, then return; after a run of correct, fast responses (a proposal: five in a row), offer a skip to the next section's first check. Evidence status: untested for frames. Intelligent tutoring systems [~S], which adjust to each response, outperformed teacher-led large-group instruction, other computer-based instruction and textbooks in two meta-analyses, but not individual human tutoring or small groups, and their gains were smaller on standardized than on locally written tests.
  5. Let the learner go at their own pace inside a dated schedule. Each learner works through the frames on their own time, but each unit has a target date, and a learner who falls behind is contacted. Evidence status: self-paced mastery programs (PSI) reduced college course completion [-M] (23 of 32 studies found higher completion in the conventional class), and group-based mastery programs had larger effects than self-paced ones [~M]. The dated schedule is the claim page's own suggestion (deadlines or pacing support), not a tested remedy.
  6. Gate the unit on a check without prompts. End each unit with items the learner has not seen in a frame, with no cues; advance at or above the criterion; below it, give corrective frames and a parallel recheck (run as the Mastery Learning pattern sets out). If the brief names its own check, use it as the gate and add no other test. Evidence status: mastery programs, PSI among them, raised examination performance [+M] over conventional instruction (mean 0.52 SD across 103 studies; PSI 0.48, not significantly different from group-based programs), and higher unit standards went with larger effects [~M]. Those programs had units, tests and proctors, not necessarily frames.

Dose and progression across sessions (labelled proposals; no claim here gives a number):

Stage Session length Frames and support Moves on when
Early sessions (first unit or two) 15–20 minutes of frames, one sitting Small steps; each frame carries a cue (a highlighted word, a partly completed answer); rule given before examples; remedial branch after two errors of one kind The unit check, without cues, is at or above the criterion
Middle sessions 20–25 minutes Cues removed from about half the frames; steps combine two earlier steps; two or three review frames from earlier units at the start Unit check at criterion, and the review frames from earlier units are answered without cues
Late sessions 25–30 minutes, including a mixed review Frames without cues; larger steps or whole short problems; one in four frames draws on earlier units; the learner explains one answer in their own words Course check at criterion on items from all units, with no cues

A session ends early when the learner has made three errors on remedial frames for the same step (a proposal): stop, record the step, and have a person look at it before the next session.

Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Frames answered correctly, unit check failed Give two unit-check items with the frame's cue restored and two without. If the cued items are right and the uncued wrong, the cues were carrying the answer: remove cues sooner and add frames without them. If both are wrong, the step was learned in the frame's wording only: add frames that vary the surface. Untested.
Repeated errors on one kind of frame Ask the learner to say what the frame is asking; give one prerequisite item. If they misread the frame, rewrite it; if the prerequisite fails, branch back to it; if both are fine, the step is too large: split it. One error pattern does not settle which.
Very fast responses Check accuracy on the fast items and give one constructed-response item where the frames used selected responses. Fast and accurate on constructed items suggests the learner already knows the step (faster step responses go with higher proficiency [~W], associational): offer the skip. Fast and inaccurate suggests guessing or clicking through: require typed responses.
Very slow responses Ask what the learner is doing on a slow frame; compare with their accuracy. Slow and accurate may be careful work; slow and inaccurate may mean stuck. In tutoring data, slower response tendencies went with faster learning for higher-proficiency students and slightly slower learning for lower-proficiency ones (response time and prior proficiency [~W], associational). Branch the second case to remedial frames; do not hurry the first.
Learner looks at the answer before responding Compare first-attempt accuracy on frames where the answer was hidden with those where it could be seen. Hide the answer until a response is entered; on paper, use a mask or a separate answer page.
Learner stops working or falls behind the dated schedule Ask what the learner wants from the course and what got in the way. Contact at the missed date, agree a new date, offer a short session with a person; see the completion row in the table below.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether each step has a checkable right answer. Frames with immediate confirmation suit facts, rules and procedures; where the task is judged as a whole or has no single answer, bare right/wrong confirmation does little and may mislead. If unstated, ask: for each step, can you write the correct answer in advance?
  • Whether learners must finish by a date and who will notice if they stop. Self-pacing raised non-completion in PSI. If unstated, ask: what happens to a learner who has not finished by the end of the course, and is there a person who checks?
  • What the learner already knows. Placement and step size depend on it, and a learner who already knows the unit gains nothing from its frames. If unstated, ask: can you give five unprompted items from the end of the unit before the first frame?

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Self-paced units with a mastery gate (PSI). Mastery Learning Improves Outcomes [+M]: one meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations (67 of PSI, almost all self-paced and in college; 36 of group-based Learning for Mastery); across 103 studies of examination performance the mean effect was 0.52 SD, with PSI at 0.48 and LFM at 0.59, not significantly different. Its synthesis table gathers 7 distinct studies. The moderator analysis [~M] found larger effects for group-based than self-paced programs, for higher unit standards, on locally written than on standardized tests (standardized effects near zero, -0.05 to 0.09, in four studies with both), and where the comparison group got less quiz feedback; its table gathers 6 distinct studies. The cost: PSI reduced completion [-M], h = -0.14 across 32 studies, not enough to explain the examination advantage (0.46 SD where PSI completion was lower, 0.55 where higher). Carry to frames only this far: a self-paced program with a unit gate can raise course-test scores, and self-pacing without dates costs completion.
  • Confirmation and correction after each response. Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+M]: in classroom studies, quizzes with corrective feedback gave g = 0.537 against g = 0.374 without, a between-study comparison; in one laboratory experiment (258 participants, Luganda–English word pairs), the correct answer after a wrong retrieval raised retention a week later by 494%, and feedback after correct responses made little difference. Its table gathers 6 distinct studies. Qualified by Adesope et al.'s meta-analysis [~M], where testing effects did not differ with and without feedback (g = 0.63 and 0.60). So the correction after an error is the part to keep; a confirmation after every correct answer may add time and little else.
  • Branching on each response. Intelligent tutoring systems improve achievement over non-individualised instruction [~S]: tutoring systems against teacher-led large-group instruction g = 0.42, against other computer-based instruction 0.57, textbooks 0.35, human tutoring -0.11, small groups 0.05 (Ma et al. 2014, from the abstract); median 0.66 SD in 50 evaluations, smaller on standardized tests (Kulik & Fletcher 2016). Its table gathers 10 distinct studies, one of which found adaptive task difficulty did not help second-graders. Tutoring systems model the learner far more finely than a branching program; carry it to "routing on the response can beat a fixed group sequence", no further.
  • Who sets the pace. Learner pacing on complex material [~M]: learner-paced animation beat system-paced animation on difficult questions only, for primary-school students in one experiment; learner control in general had an effect near zero (g = 0.05) across 18 studies. Its table gathers 8 distinct studies. PI's self-pacing is not by itself where its benefit lies.
  • Limits on bare confirmation. Outcome feedback impedes complex-task learning [-W] (Kluger & DeNisi's report of laboratory cue-learning work, no effect size) and immediate knowledge of results during computerised testing made essentially no difference to achievement estimates [~W] (Weiss 1980, a testing study, not instruction): right/wrong alone is weak where the task has many cues or no single answer.

Do not rank programmed instruction against teacher-led instruction, worked examples or tutoring from these claims: none tests frames. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (the same content as frames with response and immediate correction, against the same content as text with end-of-section questions, on new items at a stated delay, with completion recorded) and the local observation that would stand in for it: unit-check accuracy on unseen items, time to criterion and completion, for learners placed into the program against those placed out.

The evidence by question¶

  • Self-paced or group-paced progression through small units, at what criterion, measured how? Mastery learning effects vary with the mastery standard, pacing, test type and the control group's feedback [~M]
  • Does confirming or correcting each response add to learning from making the response? Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims read for this page that bear on it less directly; each says how far.

  • Mastery learning effects are larger for lower-aptitude students [+M] — from the same 1990 synthesis (13 studies; 0.61 SD against 0.40, not significant): bears on who gains from a gated program, not on frames.
  • Mastery learning gains persist at follow-up [+M] — the same synthesis (11 studies, about eight weeks): bears on the delayed horizon for gated programs, PSI included.
  • A review reports that pretesting helps paired-associate learning only when correct-answer feedback is immediate, but helps trivia-fact learning even when feedback is delayed [~M] — bears on whether the confirmation must come at once; for simple paired items it must, for richer facts it may wait. Owned by the Immediate Feedback principle.
  • Intelligent tutoring systems can improve learning outcomes, particularly with immediate actionable feedback [+W] — a framework paper's second-hand report of tutoring meta-analyses; adds no study.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested program. New pharmacy assistants, adults on rotating shifts, must learn to read prescription abbreviations and calculate quantities before working alone in four weeks; they study on their phones, and one pharmacist has about 15 minutes a week for the group. The table changed the default: placement with ten unprompted abbreviation and calculation items skips two learners past the abbreviation unit; frames show one abbreviation or one calculation step per screen, require a typed answer, and show the correct answer and its reason after an error; the pharmacist's 15 minutes go to anyone stopped by the three-error rule; each unit has a target date, with a message at a missed date. The brief names the pharmacy's own dispensing checklist, so the unit check is that checklist applied to five new prescriptions, and no other test is added. A learner says she wants to stop asking colleagues what abbreviations mean; the designer's objective is error-free quantities. They agree to record both. The claims cover college PSI courses, classroom quizzes and tutoring systems, not this population, device or horizon; these are local choices to observe.

Record: content steps and their right answers → placement items and responses, with time → frames shown, response to each, first-attempt accuracy, cue present or not → branches taken and why → time per session and dates met or missed → unit-check items (unseen), score, criterion → candidate interpretations (cue-dependence, prerequisite gap, step too large, guessing, already known) and their basis → recheck and delayed check at the stated horizon. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of mastery without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Self-paced learning, immediate feedback, practice, feedback, hints, fading, retrieval practice, adaptive mastery learning and assessment. Principles the pattern draws on: Mastery Learning, Immediate Feedback, Adaptive Learning, Active Learning, Scaffolding and Fading and Chunking. Theory: Behaviorism and the six-element programmed-instruction framework, whose source also reports that the generality of its principles came into question.

This pattern is scoped to well-defined content whose steps have checkable answers. Open, ill-structured and whole-task goals need other configurations (case-based learning, worked examples, direct instruction with feedback on reasoning). Whether frames with a response and immediate correction teach more than the same content in other forms, for which learners, at what cost in completion, and at what horizon, remains to be tested.

Related Patterns¶

  • Mastery Learning — owns the unit criterion, correction and recheck this pattern uses at the end of each run of frames; PSI, its self-paced variant, is programmed instruction's direct descendant
  • Direct Instruction — the teacher-led, group-paced descendant with scripted small steps and unison responding
  • Game-Based Mastery Learning — a modern descendant that adds game mechanics (streaks, levels, adaptive difficulty) to the same small-step, immediate-feedback, self-paced core PI established
  • Tutor-led strategy set for effective adult language lessons: structure, small steps, early success, continuous practice and constant re-use

Examples¶

  • Skinner's original teaching-machine frames and linear programmed textbooks (e.g., English 2600)
  • Crowder's AutoTutor and TutorText branching programmed books
  • The Personalized System of Instruction ("Keller Plan"): self-paced units with a mastery test and proctor feedback before advancing
