Evidence¶

Allensworth 2008¶

Allensworth, E., Nomi, T., Montgomery, N., & Lee, V. E. (2008). College Preparatory Curriculum for All: Consequences of Ninth-Grade Course Taking in Algebra and English on Academic Outcomes in Chicago. Consortium on Chicago School Research. https://consortium.uchicago.edu/publications/college-preparatory-curriculum-all-consequences-ninth-grade-coursetaking-algebra-and

q3 · i? · causal · r2

From the discussion of the cohort evaluation: graduation rates did not decline when students began high school in college-preparatory rather than remedial courses, and very low ability students were only slightly more likely to graduate.

"Requiring all students to take college-prep courses rather than accumulating credits through remedial coursework did not aggravate an already high dropout rate."

Elaine Allensworth 2008¶

Elaine Allensworth, Takako Nomi, Nicholas Montgomery, & Valerie E. Lee. (2008). College Preparatory Curriculum for All: Consequences of Ninth-Grade Course Taking in Algebra and English on Academic Outcomes in Chicago. Consortium on Chicago School Research at the University of Chicago. https://ccsr.uchicago.edu

q2 · i? · associational · r?

Authors' interpretation in the Discussion of the null graduation finding from the RQ2/RQ3 models; they argue this no-difference finding matters because educators feared higher demands would drive students out of school.

"Requiring all students to take college-prep courses rather than accumulating credits through remedial coursework did not aggravate an already high dropout rate."

  • Mandated rigorous-curriculum policies in CPS sometimes had adverse consequences: the IDS initiative saw test scores decline and the college-prep-for-all policy increased course failure without achievement gains — related
  • Overall policy effects were concentrated among the lowest-ability students: larger credit gains but more failures and lower math GPAs, with ninth-grade test scores unaffected — related
  • Long-term outcomes showed few benefits: slight increases in upper-level math credits for low-ability students but slightly lower final GPAs and reduced 4-year college enrollment for others — related
  • Overall policy effects: lowest-ability students earned more ninth-grade credits but also failed more, and long-term outcomes were small to non-existent, with no increase in college entrance — related
