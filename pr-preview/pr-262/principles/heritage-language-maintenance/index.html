Preserve Heritage Language While Acquiring an Additional Language¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 21 claims (16 for, 5 mixed) · 19 studies (5 review, 5 theoretical, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 qualitative, 2 causal, 1 associational), q1–q3 · 1 of 19 report an effect size · 19 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the decision about what happens to a learner's heritage (home) language while they learn the language of school or work. For a learner who arrives speaking a language other than the school or majority language, and who is acquiring that additional language, continuing to use and develop the heritage language (through instruction or support in it, literacy in it, and its continued use at home) is expected not to slow acquisition of the additional language, and, where the heritage language is developed to an academic level (reading, writing, school vocabulary), to give the learner something to transfer: concepts, literacy skills and word knowledge already held need not be learned again from nothing. The contrary policy, replacing the heritage language with the school language as quickly as possible, is expected to cost the heritage language without a matching gain in the school language. Outcomes are the learner's school-language achievement (general-achievement, usually standardised reading or literacy tests in pre-k-12), heritage-language proficiency, and the accuracy with which a bilingual learner's needs are identified (assessment-accuracy).

What tests this, and how far. No claim in the wiki tests heritage-language maintenance against its absence for comparable individual learners. The "no cost" half rests on associational findings reported second-hand in reviews: in a Manitoba study the share of instruction in French was unrelated to English achievement and strongly related to French achievement, and Spanish use at home raised Spanish skills with no English cost (Promoting minority children's L1 raises L1 achievement at no cost to L2 achievement [+M]); young Spanish–English bilinguals who knew more Spanish at school entry were more likely to have higher English skills at the end of the year (Spanish use at home does not limit English learning [+M]). The "transfer" half rests on Cummins's interdependence account and the studies it cites (L1 and L2 CALP are interdependent [+M]; older immigrant students with established L1 literacy developed L2 academic proficiency faster [+M]). The one randomised test in the wiki is programme-level: in the strongest study the What Works Clearinghouse reviewed, students assigned to dual language programmes in Portland scored higher on grade 5 literacy (effect size 0.14) (Dual language programs improved English literacy achievement [+M]), but not the English learners in that sample, whose literacy, science and mathematics differences were not significant (Literacy benefits of dual language programs did not extend to the English learner subsample [~M]). A non-significant difference is consistent with "no cost" but does not establish it. Several of these claims trace to the same source: the Finnish-migrant findings (Skutnabb-Kangas and Toukomaa, 1976) stand behind three of them (mother-tongue preservation, semilingualism and academic interdependence) through Cummins's reviews, so they are not independent confirmations.

The relationship is conditional on how far the heritage language is developed (the transfer argument needs academic, not only conversational, proficiency, and limited proficiency in both languages is reported as harmful: semilingualism [~M]), on whether the school language also gets strong, continuing support (minimal school-language literacy followed by an unsupported transfer is argued to be inappropriate: early-exit transitional programmes [+W]), on whether the planned allocation between languages actually happens (in one dual language programme English was given priority in practice despite guidelines: English priority over Spanish [~M]), and on the learner's age and literacy on arrival.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Support Early Language Development owns responsive adult–child talk for building a first language in the early years; its routines apply in whichever language the family uses, and this page decides which languages they run in. Cultural and Life Experiences Connections owns using the learner's own experience as material for tasks; the heritage language is one of the things that page draws on. Accessible Vocabulary and Syntax owns keeping the school language of materials within reach while the learner acquires it.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher, programme lead or specialist can act on and revise. Each step says what its basis is; the minutes and frequencies are untested proposals.

  1. Find out the languages and how they are used, before planning. In the first week, ask the family (in their language, through an interpreter or bilingual staff where needed) which languages the learner hears and uses, with whom, and for what (talk, reading, religion, school work), and whether the learner reads or writes the heritage language. Record it; it sets every later step. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement of "meaningful, ongoing opportunities to actually use the heritage language".
  2. Assess in both languages before deciding on support, placement or referral. Give a content assessment (mathematics, a story retell) in the heritage language as well as the school language, and do not read conversational fluency in the school language as academic proficiency. Basis: Spanish–English bilinguals performed better on kindergarten mathematics assessments when tested in Spanish [+M]; assuming conversational fluency indicates proficiency has led to misdiagnosis and premature exit from support [+W] (an author's argument, no data).
  3. Tell families to keep using the heritage language at home; never advise switching to the school language. Explain that home use of the heritage language has not been found to cost school-language learning, and that a shared language keeps parents able to help with school work. Basis: Spanish use at home does not limit English learning [+M]; Promoting minority children's L1 at no cost to L2 [+M] (home English use deteriorated Spanish with no English gain); the parent-involvement subclaim of the heritage-language hub [+M] (a practitioner account).
  4. Choose the strongest programme model the setting can staff. Where enough learners share a language and bilingual teachers exist, a dual language programme (both languages used for content, enrolment balanced between speakers of each) is the default. Basis: Dual language programs improved English literacy achievement [+M], qualified by the null for English learners (~M) and by how few studies meet standards (only two of 122 [~M]). Otherwise, school-language instruction with planned home-language support (steps 5–7). Untested as a comparison of the two.
  5. Give the heritage language a planned, daily role in school-language lessons. A working default: let learners plan, discuss or note-take in the heritage language for 5 to 10 minutes before each school-language writing or discussion task; provide bilingual texts or a bilingual glossary for each new topic; pair learners who share a language for the first talk about a hard text. Untested proposal for the doses; the strategies are Primary Language Support with Bilingual Texts and Bilingual Books and Cognate Attention.
  6. Develop heritage-language literacy, not only talk. Where the heritage language has a written form the learner can be taught, plan two or three sessions a week of 20 to 30 minutes of reading and writing in it (a heritage-language class, a community school, a bilingual aide, family reading with books sent home). Basis for literacy over talk: L1 and L2 CALP are interdependent [+M]; L1 academic proficiency speeds L2 academic proficiency [+M]. The doses are untested proposals.
  7. Teach the connections between the languages explicitly. In each unit, point out cognates, compare how the two languages express one idea (word order, gender, tense), and ask learners to say the same thing two ways. Basis: Fluent bilingualism is linked to metalinguistic awareness of how the two languages relate [+M] (case studies and a synthesis); the vocabulary-savings subclaim of the heritage-language hub [+M]. Untested as an instructional routine.
  8. Deliver specialist language intervention in both languages. For a learner with a language impairment that shows in both languages, have the speech-language specialist use the home language alongside the school language, with a bilingual assistant or the family where the specialist does not speak it. Basis: the Kohnert subclaim of the heritage-language hub [+M] (bilingual preschoolers, clinical study, no effect size).
  9. Do not withdraw school-language support at the moment of transition. When a learner moves from a bilingual or support programme into mainstream classes, keep school-language academic support (and heritage-language literacy) running through the next year at least. Basis: early-exit transitional programmes that transfer students with no support are inappropriate [+W] (an author's argument); teaching in a language students understand benefits language and broader learning [+M] (a World Bank narrative review). The one-year figure is an untested proposal.
  10. Check the allocation you planned is the one delivered. Once a term, observe or log the minutes actually spent in each language and which language carries the hard content; a programme can drift towards the school language without anyone deciding it. Basis: English priority over Spanish in practice [~M] (one qualitative observation).

Progression across years (untested arc). Early years (pre-kindergarten to about age 7): oral heritage-language development and early literacy in it where possible, school-language talk through play and stories, assessment in both. Middle primary: literacy in both languages, with weekly explicit cross-language comparison (step 7) and content previewed in the heritage language. Upper primary and secondary: content learned mainly in the school language, the heritage language kept as a resource for planning and reading and, where offered, as a subject taken for credit. A learner moves on from language support on academic school-language measures (reading and writing grade-level content), never on conversational fluency alone, and keeps a lighter form of support for a year after (step 9). For a newcomer aged about 10 or older who already reads in the heritage language, start content reading in it from the first week and expect faster progress than with a younger beginner (L1 academic proficiency speeds L2 academic proficiency [+M]).

When a condition is not met: if no adult in the setting speaks the learner's language, use bilingual texts, translation tools for glossaries, peers who share the language and the family as the heritage-language partners, and keep steps 2, 3 and 9, which need no bilingual staff; if the heritage language has no written form or no materials, develop it orally (retelling, discussion, family stories) and make the cross-language comparisons orally.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How far the learner's heritage language is developed, and in which skills. A newcomer who reads and writes the heritage language at grade level can transfer that literacy; a child who understands but no longer speaks it, or who has had interrupted schooling, needs it developed before it can carry much. If unstated, ask: does the learner speak, read and write the heritage language, at what level, and when did schooling in it stop?
  • What the setting can staff and allocate. A dual language programme needs bilingual teachers and enough learners sharing one language; a class with one teacher and ten home languages can still assess in both, advise families, use bilingual texts and keep support after transition. If unstated, ask: who in the setting (teachers, aides, peers, families) speaks the learner's language, and how much time could go to it each week?
  • What the decision is for. Placement, referral for a suspected disorder, exit from support and day-to-day teaching each need a different move, and the evidence differs for each. If unstated, ask: is this about how to teach, how to assess, whom to refer, or when to end support?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is the learner's performance on a stated task in a stated language: what they say, read or write in each language, to whom, with what help, and how it compares with the same task in the other language. "Is losing the heritage language", "has academic proficiency" and "has a language disorder" are inferred states. Performance in the school language alone shows what the learner can do in that language, not what they know.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares learners who kept and developed a heritage language with comparable learners who did not, on later school-language outcomes. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

  • Preserving a bilingual student's heritage language supports rather than hinders English acquisition [+M]: three entries with different mechanisms. A vocabulary study reports that prior knowledge saves time relearning vocabulary (q2); bilingual preschoolers with impairments in both languages did better when intervention used the home language alongside English (q2, clinical); a practitioner account says a shared family language keeps parents able to help with school work (q1). None prints an effect size, and no source text was available to check them. The Hansen entry's citation differs from this page's earlier Key Sources (see below), and its relevance to first-language transfer should be checked against the paper.
  • Promoting minority children's L1 raises L1 achievement at no cost to L2 achievement [+M]: two 1970s studies reported in Cummins's 1979 review: in Manitoba the share of French instruction was unrelated to English achievement and strongly related to French; Spanish home use raised Spanish with no English cost, and English home use lowered Spanish without raising English. Associational, second-hand, no effect sizes.
  • Spanish use at home does not limit English learning [+M]: a WestEd brief citing Hammer et al. (2020): across three aggregated studies, young Spanish–English bilinguals who knew more Spanish at school entry were more likely to have higher English skills at year's end. Associational; no effect sizes printed.
  • Preservation of the mother tongue predicts second-language achievement among migrant children [+M]: the UNESCO study of Finnish migrant children in Sweden, reported in Cummins's review: those who best preserved Finnish were best in Swedish. Associational; the same source stands behind the semilingualism and interdependence claims, so it is one finding, not three.
  • Dual language programs improved English literacy achievement [+M] and the null for English learners [~M]: one randomised study (Steele et al., 2017; 844 grade 5 students, Portland) rated by the WWC: effect size 0.14 on literacy for the full sample, not significant for English learners on literacy, science or mathematics. This is the only causal test of a heritage-language-developing programme in the wiki, and for the learners this page is about it shows neither a gain nor a loss.
  • The evidence base on dual language programs is thin: only two of 122 identified studies met WWC standards [~M]: effects in most settings are unknown. It limits every programme-level recommendation here.
  • Semilingualism is associated with detrimental cognitive and academic consequences [~M]: the same 1976 source, cited by Cummins, who stresses the term is not a strictly linguistic concept. It qualifies the model: maintaining a heritage language at a low level while the school language is also weak is not what the expectation covers. The term itself is contested; use the observation (limited academic proficiency in both), not the label.
  • L1 and L2 CALP are interdependent [+M] and L1 and L2 academic proficiencies are developmentally interdependent [+M]: Cummins's statement of the common underlying proficiency, citing his own earlier reviews, and Rivera's 1984 collection reporting that 10–12-year-old immigrants with established L1 literacy developed L2 academic proficiency faster than younger ones. Neither prints new data; the age finding is confounded with age itself.
  • Teaching students in a language they understand benefits both language learning and broader learning [+M]: a World Bank brief on language of instruction in low- and middle-income systems; warns that early-exit policies may hamper subject learning. A narrative review, no pooled estimate.
  • English acquisition was given priority over Spanish acquisition in practice [~M]: one observation and interview in a dual language science class. It qualifies any programme plan: the allocation on paper may not be the one delivered.

Learners, languages, countries and decades differ across these claims (Finnish children in 1970s Sweden, Franco-Manitobans, Spanish–English kindergarteners in the United States, a Portland lottery), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures heritage-language proficiency and school-language achievement in the same learners over time with a comparison group.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does maintaining and developing a learner's heritage language cost their acquisition of the school language, or support it? Preserving a bilingual student's heritage language supports rather than hinders English acquisition [+M]
  • Does teaching through the learner's home language (dual language or mother-tongue instruction), rather than early transfer to the school language, improve school-language and subject learning? Teaching students in a language they understand benefits both language learning and broader learning [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before, or found while rewriting it, that bear on it less directly.

  • Most LEAs planned English-only TK with home-language support or dual language programs, but 17% planned no language supports [~W]: a 2022 survey of California districts' plans for transitional kindergarten (N = 1,108). Context on what programmes offer, not evidence about the relationship; cited by the earlier page.
  • Early-exit transitional bilingual programs that provide minimal early-grade English literacy and then transition students to all-English classrooms with no support are inappropriate [+W]: an author's program-design argument with no comparative data; used above for the transition step.
  • Dual language programs are associated with higher achievement and with EB/non-EB achievement gap closure by seventh grade [+W]: Collier and Thomas's longitudinal work reported second-hand; associational, no effect sizes; it does not separate programme effects from who enrols.
  • Rizal Experiment showed literacy achieved more quickly in local and national languages [+W]: a historical account of a Philippine programme (1946–1972); no sample or effect size.
  • Dual language learners especially benefit from high-quality early care and dual language immersion programs [+W]: an advocacy report citing research without detail.
  • Fluent bilingualism is linked to metalinguistic awareness of how the two languages relate [+M]: case studies and a synthesis; used above for the cross-language comparison step. Its benefit is for fluent, balanced bilinguals.
  • Mead argued that treating the mother tongue as inferior impedes movement between home and school language structures [+W]: an anthropological argument, no data; used above for the family-attitudes row.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually the school language: grade-level reading, writing and subject achievement in it, by a stated year. Elicit separately what the learner and the family want from the heritage language: to talk with grandparents, to keep a religious or community practice, to belong, to have a qualification in it, or, for some adolescents, to be seen as a speaker of the school language among peers and to leave the heritage language behind. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants to fit in may refuse heritage-language work in front of classmates; a family that values the heritage language may withdraw from a school that seems to discourage it.

For example, a secondary school's objective for a 14-year-old newcomer may be passing an English-medium science course within two years, while the learner wants to keep reading and writing Arabic well enough to finish schooling abroad later and the parents want the family's language kept. Science notes and glossaries in Arabic and English serve both; record the learner's heritage-language reading as well as the English science result, so that progress in English is not read as the whole of what the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies following comparable learners over years, with heritage-language use or instruction recorded and the same total instructional time, found that more heritage-language use or instruction predicted lower school-language achievement; or if the English-learner null in the Portland lottery study were replicated as a loss rather than no difference. If independent replications of the Finnish-migrant findings did not show the association, the "transfer" half rests on Cummins's account alone and should be stated as a hypothesis. If dual language programmes help native speakers of the school language but not heritage-language learners, programme choice should be justified on maintenance and identity grounds, not on school-language gains. Do not protect the model by calling every null result "too little heritage-language development" after the fact; record how much heritage-language use and instruction there was before the outcomes are known.

Heritage-language proficiency, school-language conversational fluency, school-language academic achievement, subject learning, accurate identification of disorders and the learner's own goals are separate outcomes. The present evidence sets no dose of heritage-language instruction, no age at which to add the school language and no exit criterion.
