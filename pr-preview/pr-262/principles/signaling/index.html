Signaling Principle¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 26 claims (10 for, 14 mixed, 1 against, 1 unmarked) · 18 studies (8 causal, 5 review, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 6 of 18 report an effect size · 22 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision inside Multimedia Learning: whether, and how heavily, to cue the essential material and its organisation. When learners study a lesson whose essential elements and structure they cannot yet pick out for themselves (a novice on this material), adding cues that point to those elements and to how they connect is expected to improve learning over the same lesson without the cues. Cues include headings, an overview sentence or outline, highlighting or bold, arrows, colour that links a word to the part of the picture it names, and spoken emphasis. Signals add no new content; they change where attention goes.

The claims in this wiki test the relationship directly, in two meta-analyses, an overview of meta-analyses and a series of experiments. They also report where it bends:

  • The size. Across 29 experiments (2,726 participants) signaling was associated with better learning, d = 0.38 (Alpizar et al. 2020). The largest signaling meta-analysis, as reported in Noetel et al.'s (2022) overview, gives g = 0.43 (k = 209) on learning and g = 0.25 on reduced cognitive load. Mayer's (2008) programme found signaled lessons better on transfer in 6 of 6 experiments, median d = 0.52.
  • Prior knowledge. In an eighth-grade field study, signals linking text to pictures helped learners with low prior knowledge and harmed those with high prior knowledge, with higher extraneous load (Richter et al. 2018). In an eye-tracking study with middle-school students, revised visuals (labels, colour coding, call-out boxes) reduced processing for low-prior-knowledge students and increased it for middle-prior-knowledge students, with no difference on the post-lesson test (Clinton et al. 2017). Noetel et al. found no consistent prior-knowledge moderation across multimedia principles as a whole, so the reversal is recorded for signaling in two studies, not as a general rule.
  • Pacing and complexity. Good multimedia design, signaling among it, mattered more for complex material and for system-paced lessons (Noetel et al. 2022). Alpizar et al. report that pacing and other presentation features moderated the effect.
  • What is cued. Cues move attention to what is marked, whether or not it matters: underlining low-relevance text raised scores on low-relevance questions (Nist & Hogrebe 1985), and learners shown a cued object noticed less elsewhere on the screen (Mutlu-Bayraktar & Bayram 2018). A signal helps the outcome it points at.
  • Outcome. In expository text, organizational signals often changed what college readers recalled more than how much (Lorch & Lorch 1996). Most of the effects were measured soon after a short lesson.
  • Representation and age. In a middle-school electricity study, signaling the written text helped with an abstract animation (d = .35) but not significantly with a concrete one (Kutbay & Akpınar 2020).

How this sits beside its neighbours: Multimedia Learning holds the umbrella model (words with relevant pictures, and the principles that qualify it) and its pattern the reusable design; this page goes deeper on the cueing decision only and does not repeat that model. Clear Structure covers making a lesson's or course's organisation explicit more broadly (organizers, graphic organizers, course-level structure); signaling is its cue-level instance within a presentation, and the two share the meta-analysis behind Clear structure improves learning. Annotating is about marks the learner makes; signals are made by the designer. Cognitive Load Management gives the shared explanation: a cue saves a novice the search for what matters.

Default design, while the dose is untested¶

The claims establish the direction, the size on average and the prior-knowledge boundary. None in this wiki sets a dose (how many cues per screen, which kind of cue, when to fade them). This default is a labelled proposal: the kinds of cue and the fade follow the claims named, the numbers are untested choices.

  1. Check the condition. Is the material complex enough that a newcomer could miss what matters (a busy diagram, an animation, a long expository text, a system-paced video)? Are the learners new to this content? If both are no, signal lightly or not at all (Signaling improves learning [~M], the Richter et al. reversal).
  2. List the essential elements first, from the learning goal and the assessment the brief uses: the three to five ideas, parts or steps the test or task will ask about. Cue only these. Cues move attention to whatever is marked (Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention [+M]). Three to five is an untested proposal.
  3. Signal the organisation. Open with a one- or two-sentence overview of the steps or parts, give each section a heading that names its idea, and close with a summary (Signaling principle, Mayer 2008 [+M]: overview sentences, headings and vocal emphasis).
  4. Signal within the display. Mark the one element each screen, slide or paragraph is about (bold, highlight, an arrow), and use the same colour for a term and the part of the picture it names. In narration, stress the key words. On a busy screen, pair a highlight with a pointing cue such as an arrow (The signalled and cued change was most detected [+W]). One cued element per screen is an untested proposal.
  5. Early sessions (first one or two on a topic): full signals as above, including the colour or label links between text and picture.
  6. Later sessions on the same topic, once a short probe shows learners can name the parts and steps unaided: drop the text–picture link cues first, keep headings and the overview. Added link cues harmed learners with high prior knowledge and raised processing for middle prior knowledge (Signaling improves learning [~M]; Revised visuals reduce processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle [~M]). The probe (two or three "name the parts" items) and the order of fading are untested proposals.
  7. Move on when the learner explains the material without the cues. If performance drops with cues removed, restore them for one more session (untested proposal).
  8. Judge it on the brief's own test, and check that the test asks about the cued elements; a cue that points at what the test does not ask about cannot show a gain.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Three facts change the decision most. Ask for them when the brief omits them: how much do the learners already know about this material? (a newcomer or someone who could explain it already); how complex is the display and who sets the pace? (a busy system-paced animation, or a short learner-paced text); and what will the learners be tested or asked to do? (so the cues point at it). Most of the evidence is from lab lessons with adult (postsecondary) learners on immediate tests; rows below that carry it to children, other media or longer horizons say so.

Observation, state and explanation¶

Record what the learner did with the cued material under stated conditions: which cues were present, who set the pace, and what they were asked to do afterwards. Where a learner looked, how long they took and what they rated as effort are observations; an organised representation of the material is an inferred state.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Recalls the cued items but not how they connect Cues marked elements but not their organisation; the test asked only for the items Add an overview and connecting cues; ask for an explanation of how the parts act on each other.
Learner who knows the topic slows down or does worse with cues Cues are redundant with what they already know and add material to process Give the uncued version and compare on the same test; probe prior knowledge first.
Scores well on cued content and poorly on the rest Attention went to the cues, as intended or too much; the cues missed something the test asks Check the cue list against the assessment; ask about an uncued but essential item.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A single contrast shifts confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Signaling improves learning [+S]: Noetel et al. (2022), an overview of 29 reviews, ranks signaling among the multimedia principles with the largest benefits and gives g = 0.43 (k = 209) from the largest signaling meta-analysis; Alpizar et al. (2020), a meta-analysis of 29 studies (44 effect sizes, 2,726 participants), d = 0.38, with study, participant, presentation and method features moderating it and clearer benefits in higher-quality studies. The third entry qualifies it: Richter et al. (2018), a quasi-experimental field study with eighth graders, found text–picture integration signals helped low-prior-knowledge learners and harmed high-prior-knowledge ones. All three were read from abstracts or a preprint.
  • Signaling principle: highlighting essential words improves transfer from multimedia lessons [+M]: 6 of 6 experiments (computer lessons on airplanes, paper lessons on lightning and biology), median d = 0.52 on transfer. One research programme, reported in a review; read its median as that programme's result.
  • Signaling (cueing) increases learning and reduces cognitive load in multimedia [+M]: the same Noetel et al. overview, adding that signaling reduced cognitive load (g = 0.25, k = 27), increased learning-relevant fixations (g = 0.39, k = 14) and slightly raised motivation (g = 0.13, k = 13).
  • Clear structure improves learning [~S]: Alpizar et al. again, and Lorch & Lorch (1996), two experiments with 139 college readers in which organizational signals often did not raise the amount recalled but shifted what was recalled.
  • Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance [+M]: 67 college freshmen; underlining high- or low-relevance text raised scores on questions about whichever was underlined. One experiment, immediate test.
  • The signaling effect holds only for abstract animation representations [~M]: middle-school students learning electricity; signaled written text beat unsignaled written text with abstract animation (d = .35) but not significantly with concrete animation (p = .074). The entry calls the design a quasi-experiment.
  • Revised visuals reduce cognitive processing of lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle prior knowledge students [~M] and Revisions to visuals had no effect on post-lesson test scores [~M]: Clinton et al. (2017), 62 middle-school students reading an algebra lesson with original or revised visuals (labels, colour coding, call-outs, applying signaling, contiguity and coherence together). The eye-tracking measures moved in opposite directions by prior knowledge; the test score did not move. A non-significant difference is not equivalence.
  • The signalled and cued change was the most detected change [+W] and Signalling focuses attention and reduces change detection elsewhere [~W]: Mutlu-Bayraktar & Bayram (2018), an eye-tracking case study with a small group of undergraduates (12 in the detection counts); 10 of 12 detected the change on the signalled and cued object. Descriptive counts, no comparison group.
  • Narration with finger-tracking animation directs bilingual preschoolers' attention to the target-language print [+M], Attention drops after the animation ends [~M] and Comprehension benefit only marginal [~M]: Yow & Priyashri (2019), eye-tracking with preschoolers; the cue moved looking to the print while it ran (partial η² = .74), less after, and comprehension rose only marginally (p = .060) in dual-language books and not at all in single-language ones.
  • Spoken text modality does not significantly differ from signaled written text modality [~M]: Kutbay & Akpınar again; a null result, not a test of equivalence.
  • Signaling in multimedia (labeled text with illustrations) is associated with increased learning outcomes [+W]: a narrative review for dual-language instruction, attributing the result to three cited studies; no effect sizes.
  • Highlighting shows low utility for improving learning outcomes [-M]: Dunlosky et al. (2013); learner-made highlighting, a different act from designer signals, gave little benefit over reading.

Taken together: designer-made cues that point at the essential material improve learning from complex multimedia lessons by roughly a third to a half of a standard deviation on average, mostly on immediate tests with university students. Cues help what they point at; for learners who already know the material they can add load, and in two studies they did. No claim in this wiki tests the number of cues, a fading schedule, or delayed outcomes.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do cues that highlight the essential material improve learning, and by how much? Signaling Improves Learning [+S]
  • Do the cues still help learners who already know the material? Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [~S]
  • Does it matter what is cued, and what happens to attention elsewhere? Relevancy of emphasized text directs attention and influences test performance [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Change in the most-focused central object was detected at a low rate unless it was signalled and cued [+W]: the same case study as the change-detection claims above.
  • Among two objects with the same signalling, participants focused more on the arrow-cued object [+W]: same study; a pointing cue beat a highlight alone for attention.
  • Changes were detected more easily on screens presenting fewer objects [+W]: same study.
  • Revised visuals reduce cognitive load while reading lesson text for low prior knowledge students but increase it for middle [~M], Revisions to math-relevant visuals affect cognitive load differently by prior knowledge group [~M] and Revised visuals reduce processing of math-relevant visuals for low prior knowledge students [~M]: further measures from Clinton et al. (2017).
  • Multimedia enhancing features increase attention to print in single-language e-books [+M]: Yow & Priyashri, Study 2.
  • Multimedia design principles produce a moderate average improvement in learning, with large differences between principles [+M]: Noetel et al.; signaling is among the larger ones.
  • Multimedia principles benefit novices [~S] and Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant as expertise grows [~S]: Kalyuga et al.'s expertise-reversal account, from other cognitive-load paradigms than signaling.
  • Advance organizers produce a small average learning benefit, larger for high-ability learners [~S]: the organizer, a neighbour of the overview signal, owned by Clear Structure.
  • In one experiment, students who generated their own underlining did not score significantly higher than students given experimenter underlining [~M].

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is that learners select the essential material and see how it fits together. Ask separately what the learner wants from it: a learner revising for a test values cues that match the test; a learner using the material on a job values cues on what the job needs, which may differ. Cues that serve the designer's list and not the learner's purpose will move attention, and the learner may still miss what they came for. Record both aims and point the cues at the outcome that will be checked.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with comparable novice learners, holding content and time equal, find no learning advantage for cued over uncued material on aligned tests, or if the advantage seen on an immediate test is absent at a delay. It should be narrowed if more studies like Richter et al. and Clinton et al. find cues harming learners with moderate prior knowledge, which would make a prior-knowledge probe a requirement rather than a refinement. If the gains turn out to come from the cues matching the test items rather than from organising the material, the explanation should shift from organisation to attention alone. Do not protect the model by calling every null result a case of too many cues after the fact.
