Employ flexible error treatment strategies matched to objectives, competence, affect, and correction effectiveness¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 30 claims (11 for, 18 mixed, 1 against) · 21 studies (9 causal, 6 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 qualitative, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 4 of 21 report an effect size · 26 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the choice of how to treat a learner's error: whether to correct it at all, which errors to correct, in what form (a recast, a prompt to self-correct, a clue about the rule, an explicit correction with or without a rule explanation), and how to tell whether the treatment is working. Its source is second-language teaching (an EFL classroom), and every study behind it is about second-language errors, oral or written; carrying it to errors in other subjects is untested.

The relationship it proposes is conditional. For a second-language learner producing errors during either form-focused work (practicing a target structure) or meaning-focused work (talking or writing to get something across), a treatment chosen for the activity's objective, the learner's current competence with the form, the learner's affective state and whether earlier corrections have taken hold is expected to improve accuracy on the treated forms, at immediate-performance and at delayed-retention, while keeping the learner willing to speak or write, better than one correction policy applied to every error. That matched-against-uniform comparison is the page's own relationship, and no claim in the wiki tests it: no study recorded here assigns treatments by learner, objective or affect and compares that with a fixed policy.

What the wiki's evidence does establish is narrower. Corrective feedback on second-language errors, taken as a whole, improves development and the effect holds at delayed tests (two meta-analyses [+S]). The forms differ in ways a flexible policy could use: in classrooms prompts and explicit correction showed larger effects than recasts (one meta-analysis and a review [~M]); in one EFL quasi-experiment metalinguistic feedback beat recasts straight after treatment, but the difference was gone ten days later (immediate, not delayed [~M]); and the same meta-analyses report moderators (learner age, laboratory against classroom, treatment length) that the default design below uses as rows. Which errors to treat (those that block meaning first) and the risk that untreated errors become fixed come from one theoretical article and are untested proposals here.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Immediate Feedback owns when a correction arrives and treats timing as a local choice; this page uses that reading and adds the second-language reasons for delaying (fluency activities, a learner's face). Error Analysis owns using an error as the object of study (comparing an incorrect with a correct example); this page is about the teacher's or tool's response to a live error, and it treats the teacher's analysis of learner errors as a diagnostic step, not the treatment. Feedback Loops holds the broader model of acting on a response and checking the next one. The unconverted neighbour Integrate explicit error correction with meaningful activities states one of this page's rows (correct within meaning-focused tasks when both accuracy and fluency are goals) as a principle of its own.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (and its source's rules for which errors to treat), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Name the activity's objective before it starts, and set the correction policy from it. In a form-focused activity (practicing a target structure), correct errors in the target form as they happen. In a meaning-focused activity (a discussion, a role-play, free writing), interrupt only for errors that stop the message getting through, and note the rest for later. Untested as a policy; the split between errors that block meaning (global) and errors in one element of a sentence (local) is from a theoretical article (global versus local errors [+W]).
  2. Decide which errors to treat, not all of them. Treat first the errors that block communication and the errors in the forms the lesson targets; tolerate other local errors. Separate an error (the learner does not yet know the form) from a mistake (a slip in a form the learner knows): a mistake needs only a signal, an error needs the correct form. Of the errors, one the learner has not yet been taught can simply be corrected; one that follows a wrong rule the learner applies consistently merits a reminder so the learner corrects it. All from the source's theoretical argument (global versus local errors [+W]; errors versus mistakes [+W]); untested.
  3. Choose the form of correction from the learner's competence with the form. Where the learner can likely fix the error with a nudge (intermediate or above, the form has been taught), use a prompt: repeat the error with rising intonation, give a clue about the rule, or ask the learner to try again. Where the learner lacks the form (novice for it, low proficiency, little practice reading feedback), give the correct form and a one-line rule explanation. Basis: in classroom studies prompts and explicit correction had larger effects than recasts (recasts supported, effectiveness varies [~M]); in one written-feedback experiment with low-intermediate learners, a bare error code without explanation did not beat no feedback straight after treatment, while feedback with a rule explanation or the correct form did (immediate posttest [+M]); a review reports that learners with low linguistic knowledge and feedback literacy engage less with written feedback (student factors [~M]). The matching itself is untested.
  4. Protect the learner's willingness to speak. For a learner who is anxious, new to the group or easily embarrassed, give the chance to self-correct first (a pause, a raised eyebrow), correct privately or in writing rather than in front of the group, and keep public correction for the whole class's common errors, unattributed. Basis: learners in one online-interaction study repaired their own errors far more often than they corrected each other, which the authors read as avoiding face threat (self-correction dominates [~M]); six EFL teachers interviewed avoided immediate correction because they believed it intimidates (teachers favour delayed correction [~M], a belief, not a learning outcome); a systematic review reports that private practice with AI tools reduces fear of mistakes (AI and anxiety [~M]). Untested as a matching rule.
  5. Run a short feedback slot after meaning-focused work. Collect errors while learners talk or write (a notebook, a shared document), then spend about five minutes at the end on the two or three errors that were commonest or blocked meaning: write them up without names, ask the class to correct them, give the rule. The slot length and the count are untested proposals; that a delay does not cancel the effect of correction is consistent with the meta-analyses' delayed results (corrective feedback durable [+S]), which did not compare slot timings.
  6. Check whether the treatment is working, and change it if not. After correcting a form, watch the learner's next attempts and, a week or two later, their unprompted use of it in free speech or writing. Do not judge a form of correction by the next attempt alone: in one classroom study of generated feedback on programming submissions, the next submission improved on average by almost nothing under any feedback condition (near-zero immediate gains [-M], carried from programming to language); and in the EFL study, metalinguistic feedback's lead over recasts at the immediate test had gone at ten days (gains decline over a delay [~M]). If a form is still wrong in free use after three or four corrections, move it up the support ladder (prompt, then clue, then correct form with rule), then to a planned mini-lesson (step 8). The thresholds are untested proposals.
  7. Plan the arc across sessions, not only one lesson. A default for a term of six to ten weekly sessions: keep a short list of two or three target forms per learner or class. In early sessions on a form, correct it explicitly with the rule; in middle sessions, prompt and let the learner correct; in late sessions, correct it only in the end-of-activity slot, and only when it recurs. Take a form off the list when the learner produces it correctly, unprompted, in free speech or writing in two separate sessions; add the next commonest error in its place. The arc and the threshold are untested proposals. Longer classroom treatments showed larger effects than short-to-medium ones in one meta-analysis, while the other found the reverse across all settings (both on this claim [~S]), so treatment length is not settled.
  8. Give a persistent error its own lesson. An error that survives repeated correction, or that many learners share, gets a planned short focus-on-form lesson (the form introduced before the task, then practiced within it) rather than more on-the-spot correction. Basis: the source warns that some errors left untreated become fixed (errors as feedback, fossilization risk [+W]); one weak study reports learners given grammar points before a task repeated each other's errors less (proactive focus on form [+W]). Untested as a rule.

When a condition is not met: if no one hears the learner's attempts as they happen (self-paced online work, homework), use written correction with the correct form and a rule line for novice learners, a code or underline for those who can correct themselves, and ask for a revised version; if the activity's objective is not known, ask before the activity, because it decides whether to interrupt at all.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • The activity's objective: accuracy on a form, or getting meaning across. It decides whether to interrupt at all, and which errors count. If unstated, ask: in this activity, is the learner practicing a particular structure, or using the language to do something?
  • The learner's competence with the form and with feedback. A learner who knows the form can fix it from a prompt; one who does not needs the form given, with the rule. If unstated, ask: has this form been taught, and when prompted, can the learner correct it?
  • How the learner takes correction, and in front of whom. A learner who stops talking after being corrected in public has lost more than the form gained. If unstated, ask: how confident is the learner, who else is present, and does the learner want to be corrected (all errors, some, privately)?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a learner's utterance or text under stated conditions, the treatment given (record its form: recast, prompt, clue, explicit correction, rule explanation, or none), whether the learner responded to it (repeated the correct form, corrected themselves, carried on), and later unprompted use of the form. "Knows the form", "has fossilized this error" and "is discouraged by correction" are inferred states. Repeating a recast shows the learner heard it; it does not show the form is acquired.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Repeats the correct form after a recast, then makes the same error in the next activity The recast was heard as agreement, not correction; the form is not yet known; a slip under communicative pressure Prompt instead of recasting next time and see whether the learner can correct it; give a short untimed item on the form.
Corrects at once when prompted, but not in free speech A mistake in a known form (attention, not knowledge); the form is known but not yet automatic Compare accuracy in a slow written task with accuracy in timed speech; if the gap is large, more practice, not more correction.
Stops speaking, or gives shorter answers, after being corrected in front of others Face threat or anxiety; not understanding what was corrected; disagreement with the correction Switch that learner to private or written correction and compare their participation over the next sessions; ask privately what they took from the correction.
Correct at the end of the lesson, wrong a week later Little consolidation; the correction's effect was short-lived; interference from new forms Check the form in unprompted use at the delay and record all practice and correction in between.
Never makes an error in a form at all The form is known; the learner avoids it (uses a simpler structure) Set a task that requires the form; avoidance shows no error, so absence of error is not evidence of competence (limitations of error analysis [~W]).
The same error across many learners A shared first-language transfer; a gap in the teaching; a misleading example in the materials Look at where the error comes from (first-language transfer is commoner early, overgeneralization of a target-language rule later, per a theoretical account (error sources [~W])), and teach the form to the group.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A correction is itself an intervention and changes what it is meant to measure, so record each one, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares an error-treatment policy matched to the objective, the learner's competence and affect with a uniform policy. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

  • Corrective feedback on second-language errors has a significant effect on L2 development that is maintained on delayed tests [+S]: two meta-analyses. Across 15 classroom quasi-experiments (N = 827), oral corrective feedback against control groups had d = 0.74, with between-group means of 0.63 at immediate and 0.84 at delayed posttests; prompts showed larger effects than recasts; younger learners benefited more; long treatments beat short-to-medium ones. The second (33 studies, abstract only) reports a medium effect maintained over time, larger in laboratory than classroom and in foreign- than second-language contexts, and larger for shorter treatments. Its studies table counts 4 distinct studies behind it and the narrower claims bearing on it. It supports treating errors; it says nothing about matching treatment to learners one by one.
  • The literature is supportive of recasts, but recast effectiveness varies with the language area and learner knowledge, focus, and aptitudes [~M]: a narrative review and the classroom meta-analysis (recasts d = 0.53 against controls; prompts 0.83; explicit correction 0.84; prompts significantly better than recasts in within-group contrasts, with wide intervals for all three). The review's statement that recasts work differently by language area and learner is the nearest the wiki comes to the page's matching idea; it is a review's summary, not a test.
  • Metalinguistic feedback and recasts, but not clarification requests, significantly improve Iranian EFL learners' wh-question performance on an immediate post-test [+M], Metalinguistic feedback outperforms recasts on the immediate post-test, but the two do not differ significantly on the delayed post-test [~M], Metalinguistic feedback's gains on wh-question performance decline over a ten-day delay while recast gains hold, leaving the two no different [~M] and Corrective feedback treatment and time interact significantly in shaping wh-question learning gains across the three testing periods [~M]: one quasi-experiment with 134 Iranian EFL learners in four intact classes, feedback given during a spot-the-difference task, grammaticality-judgement outcomes at pre-, immediate and ten-day delayed tests. Metalinguistic feedback led straight after; at ten days it and recasts no longer differed, and both still beat clarification requests and control. The third claim adds a meta-analysis (abstract only) finding implicit feedback's effect better maintained than explicit feedback's, without saying how recasts and metalinguistic feedback were classed. Together they warn against choosing a form on its immediate effect. No effect size is printed.
  • On the immediate posttest of explicit L2 knowledge, direct CF and metalinguistic CF with rule explanations outperformed the control group while metalinguistic code CF did not. [+M] and All four written CF types, including metalinguistic code CF, outperformed the control group on the delayed posttest of explicit L2 knowledge two weeks later. [~M]: one experiment with 93 low-intermediate Korean EFL learners randomly assigned to four written-feedback types and a control over four sessions. A bare error code was behind at the immediate test and caught up by two weeks; all four beat control with large effects then. It supports giving low-proficiency learners the form or a rule line when quick change matters, and qualifies it at a delay.
  • Learners in all three corrective-feedback groups improve irregular past tense performance on both GJT and EIT from pretest to posttests, a within-group gain that does not separate the feedback types [~M]: 92 learners in three feedback groups all improved on irregular past tense on two measures (partial η² = .26 and .21) across pretest, immediate and delayed tests. The comparison is over time within the feedback groups, so it supports feedback in general and does not separate the types; it bears on this page only as one more study where several forms worked.
  • Immediate post-feedback improvement is near zero on average and does not differ significantly across feedback conditions [-M]: in a classroom study of generated feedback on introductory programming submissions, the change in pass rate from one submission to the next was near zero on average under every condition, with fewer than 35% of transitions improving. It is from programming, not language, but it is the limit on the page's "correction effectiveness" check: one next attempt is a noisy sign of whether a treatment works.
  • Global errors hinder communication while local errors affect only a single sentence element, informing which errors to correct [+W] and Errors serve as valuable feedback for teachers and learners, but unhandled errors risk fossilization [+W]: the page's source article, theoretical, no data. They give the rules for which errors to treat; they are proposals, not findings. (The previous page marked both [+M], above what a single theoretical source allows.)
  • Instructors favored delayed over immediate oral corrective feedback, believing immediate correction intimidates students [~M] and Unprompted self-correction dominates negative feedback in NNS-NNS CMC interaction, interpreted as a face-saving preference [~M]: an interview study of six EFL teachers and a count of repairs in learner-to-learner online interaction. They bear on the affective half of the page; neither measures learning or anxiety.

Learners, forms, feedback channels (oral, written, computer-mediated) and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them by effect label. None compares a matched policy with a uniform one, and none measures whether correction lowers participation.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

  • Treatment version significantly affects student attitudes, with the lean program most negative and no-practice next [~M] — cited by the previous page; undergraduates' attitudes toward six versions of a computer-based lesson. It bears on this page only through the online self-paced row (learners dislike a lesson stripped of practice); it is not about error treatment.
  • Prompts raised learners' pragmatic awareness of refusals more than explicit feedback or control after a 10-week treatment [~M] — one study reported in a review: prompts beat explicit feedback for pragmatic awareness (refusals), a different target from grammar. A candidate for the prompts row.
  • Explicit teaching is more effective for EFL copular be learning but its effect is not durable [~M] — explicit instruction (not feedback) with Japanese seventh graders, reported in a review; bears on the delayed-horizon row by analogy.
  • Language proficiency, beliefs, feedback literacy and learning objectives moderate students' engagement with and uptake of WCF [~M] — used in the default design; a bibliometric review's summary of written-feedback studies, not a test.
  • Child EFL learners receiving recasts in task-based lessons improve their production of third person singular -s from pretest to posttest, in a study with no no-recast comparison group [+M] — used in the child row; pretest–posttest without a comparison group.
  • All four written CF types, including metalinguistic code CF, outperformed the control group on the delayed posttest of implicit L2 knowledge. [~M] — the same experiment as the code-only rows, on a writing measure; consistent with them.
  • Corrective feedback facilitates L2 grammatical development, with explicit feedback stronger immediately and implicit feedback better maintained over time [~M] — a review's second-hand report of the same 33-study meta-analysis; adds nothing independent to the horizon row.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually accuracy on named forms in unprompted use at a stated horizon, alongside the fluency the course is for. Elicit separately what the learner wants from correction: some learners ask for every error to be corrected, some want only the errors that would embarrass them at work, some want to be left to talk. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants all errors corrected and a designer who wants fluency can agree on an end-of-activity list; a learner who does not want public correction can still get it privately.

For example, an adult preparing for job interviews in English may value being understood and sounding competent, while the designer's objective for the unit is past-tense accuracy. Correcting every past-tense slip during a mock interview serves the designer's objective and may cost the learner's confidence; noting the slips and treating the two that change meaning ("I work there" for "I worked there") after the interview serves both. Record both goals, so that a clean past-tense test is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies that assign correction by objective, proficiency or anxiety find no advantage over a single sensible policy (for example, prompts for everyone), or find that the advantage at the immediate test is gone at a delay, as the gap between metalinguistic feedback and recasts was. If correction during meaning-focused activities turns out not to reduce learners' talk, the case for deferring local errors rests on the global/local argument alone. If the classroom advantage of prompts over recasts holds for low-proficiency learners too, the competence row should drop its "give the form" branch. Do not protect the model by calling every failure a mismatch of treatment to learner after the fact.

Accuracy on the treated form straight after correction, accuracy at a delay, accuracy in unprompted use, and the learner's willingness to keep speaking are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no rule for which form suits which learner, no correction-slot length, and no criterion for when an error is "fossilized".
