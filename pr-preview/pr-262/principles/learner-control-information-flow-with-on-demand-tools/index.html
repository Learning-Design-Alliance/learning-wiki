Keep the learner in control of the information flow with on-demand tools and supports¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 20 claims (9 for, 10 mixed, 1 against) · 20 studies (8 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 2 associational, 2 design, 2 theoretical, 1 qualitative), q1–q3 · 3 of 20 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one decision: whether the helps a learner may need while working through rich material (a glossary or dictionary for the words in a scene, an explanation in the first language, replaying a scene, a model answer or feedback after an attempt, a hint) are put under the learner's control, available at once on request, rather than withheld, pushed by the system on a fixed schedule, or handed out by an instructor who drives the session. For a learner meeting material a little above their level (a second-language scene or text with unknown words, a complex-skill performance), keeping the material whole and the helps one step away is expected to support comprehension of the whole and verbal-association learning of the words looked up (immediate-performance, delayed-retention), better than the same material with no helps, and at least as well as the same helps given on someone else's schedule. The earlier page's source, a 1985 account of interactive videodisc work for language learning, put it as exercises that "provide a holistic experience, while at the same time providing specific tools when needed".

No claim in the wiki compares on-demand, learner-controlled helps with the same helps pushed by the system or by an instructor, in language learning or in video. The claims test neighbouring parts:

  • That the helps themselves can help. Glosses beside a second-language text improved immediate vocabulary recall over no glosses in one randomised study with 135 Taiwanese undergraduates, but did not improve reading comprehension; the general glossing claim gathers 2 distinct studies. Captions on second-language video show large effects on comprehension and vocabulary in one synthesis. These glosses and captions were present, not requested.
  • That control over the help can matter, under a condition. In one laboratory motor-learning experiment, learners who could decide after a trial whether to see their result were more accurate at a 24-hour retention test than learners given the same feedback schedule without choosing; deciding before the trial gave no benefit. Who decides is one variable; when they decide is another.
  • That control in general often does nothing. Across 18 studies of learner control in educational technology the average effect was near zero (g = 0.05); the general learner-control claim gathers 11 distinct studies, and its narrower findings repeatedly report controls that learners rarely use (a stop/play button on an animation; a hint budget spent early or hoarded). Students who never asked for a hint on at least one problem were more likely to wheel-spin.

So the relationship is conditional on whether the learner uses the help, when the decision to use it is taken (after an attempt, at the point of difficulty, appears to matter more than before), what the help gives away (a hint or a gloss, against a full answer), the learner's level relative to the material (in the gloss study the benefit appeared only at the two middle proficiency levels), and the outcome measured (words looked up, against comprehension of the whole).

How it sits beside its neighbours: Segmenting owns whether a continuous stream is cut into pieces and who sets the pauses, and concludes that designed segments, not a pause button, carry that effect; this page takes segmenting as given and is about the content helps offered inside and between segments. Multimedia Learning holds the words-with-pictures model and pacing as one of its moderators. Scaffolding and Fading owns help that a tutor or system raises and lowers contingently; this page is the case where the learner, not the tutor, decides when help comes, and the two combine when the on-demand help is graduated. Self-Regulated Learning owns teaching learners to monitor and to seek help; the prompts and the help-use plan below borrow from it. Autonomy owns choice as a motivational condition; the choice here is a cognitive one, about information, and the evidence for it is different.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it. Durations are untested proposals for a 40–50 minute session built around a 2–4 minute video scene or a 300–600 word text.

  1. Meet the whole first (about 5 minutes). Play the scene through, or have the text read through once, without stopping and with the helps closed, so the learner has the context and its clues before looking anything up. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's "holistic experience". If the scene runs longer than about four minutes or explains interacting steps, cut it into segments at natural breaks first, as Segmenting describes.
  2. Open the helps for a second pass (15–20 minutes). The learner replays, backs up and looks up freely: a glossary of the words that occur in this scene with their meanings, captions in the language of the scene, a short explanation of cultural or grammatical points in the learner's first language, and a way to repeat any line. Each help is one keystroke or tap away and does not take the learner out of the scene. Evidence that glosses and captions help vocabulary when present: Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses [+M], All three L1 gloss conditions significantly improve immediate vocabulary recall over a no-gloss control in EFL reading [+M], Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+M]. That putting them on request beats always-on or absent helps is untested here.
  3. Let the learner attempt, then decide whether to see the model (10 minutes). At each point where the learner must respond (answer a character, choose a branch, say the line), the learner responds first and then chooses whether to see or hear the model response or feedback. Carried from motor learning, untested for language: Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results (Self-After, Self-Both) were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test than learners who decided before the trial (Self-Before) and than their yoked counterparts. [+M]; a choice made before the attempt gave no benefit (Having control over the knowledge-of-results decision only before a motor trial (Self-Before) produced no statistically significant retention or transfer benefit over a yoked group receiving the same KR schedule. [~M]).
  4. Make a help a hint before it is an answer. Where a help can solve the task for the learner (a translation of the whole line, a full model answer, an AI assistant), offer a smaller step first (the key word, the first half of the reply) and the full answer only on a second request. Evidence about unrestricted answer-giving help, second-hand: Unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice performance but harmed later exam performance, and a guardrailed hint-based version mitigated the harm, as reported by the whitepaper citing Bastani et al. [~W]; learners asked for this themselves in one design study (K-12 learners preferred incremental hints and multiple attempts over immediately revealed solutions [+W]).
  5. Watch for learners who use no help at all, and prompt them. If a learner has opened no help in a scene and then answers wrongly, prompt once ("the glossary has this word; replay line 4"). Do not force the help. Basis: Not requesting any hints in at least one problem is related to greater wheel-spinning, consistent with help avoidance [~M] (correlational, maths practice software), and controls are often left unused (Learner flow control over animations is rarely used, and the authors report no correlation between its use and knowledge-test results although the printed Spearman r is 0.464 [-W]).
  6. Give the learner a plan for help, not a quota. Tell learners when help is most worth taking (after a first try, on the line they could not follow, on a word that recurs), rather than rationing it. Basis: The Coach's hint budget was managed poorly: students split into early-spenders and savers, and within the Coach condition only those with a deliberate hint strategy scored higher [~W]; the plan is an untested proposal.
  7. Do not let the instructor drive. In class, the teacher does not stop the scene to explain every word or decide when learners may look things up; the teacher circulates, answers what the helps cannot, and runs the debrief. Untested; from the earlier page's warning that instructors who drive without tools "misuse the medium and reduce lessons to drill".
  8. End with an unaided pass (5–10 minutes). Replay the scene, or a new scene with the same words, with helps closed; the learner answers comprehension questions in the brief's form or takes their part in the exchange. This is the observation that says what the learner can do without the help; assisted success during step 2 does not. Untested proposal.
  9. Across sessions, move the helps from first language to the target language, and from first pass to second pass. A default arc for a unit of six to ten sessions: in the first two or three, glossary and explanations in the first language, captions on during the second pass; in the middle sessions, glossary definitions in the target language, captions on request only; in the last sessions, the first pass and the response points with all helps closed and the helps open only on the second pass. Move a learner to the next scene when they follow it on the unaided pass and respond correctly at most response points (set the number locally, for example four of five) with no more than a handful of look-ups on the second pass; return to the previous step of the arc if they cannot follow the unaided pass. The arc and its thresholds are untested proposals.

When a condition is not met: if learners have no device to control (a single projected screen), give each learner a printed glossary of the scene and a turn at calling for a replay; if the material is well within the learners' level, drop the helps and spend the time on the response points, since the gloss study found no benefit at its highest proficiency level (Proficiency moderates gloss effects: vocabulary benefits appear at Levels 2 and 3 but not Levels 1 and 4 [~M]).

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How far above the learner the material sits. Helps carry most when the learner can follow the gist but not every word; a learner who follows nothing needs easier material, and one who follows everything needs no helps. If unstated, ask: on one play-through without help, how much of the scene can the learner retell, and how many words in a sample paragraph are unknown to them?
  • Whether learners will use the help, and when. On-demand help works only if it is taken, at the point of difficulty, and not as a way round the task. If unstated, ask: in a trial scene, which helps did learners open, how often, and before or after trying?
  • What counts as success: the words, the whole, or the exchange. Glosses help the words looked up more reliably than comprehension of the whole text. If unstated, ask: will the learner be checked on vocabulary, on following the scene, or on taking part in a conversation like it, and with or without helps?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: which helps were opened, when (before or after an attempt), how often, on which lines or words; the response at each response point; and the result of the unaided pass. "Understands the scene", "is avoiding help" and "is leaning on the help" are inferred states. Success with the helps open shows what the learner can do with them, not without them.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Opens no help and answers wrongly Does not know the help is there; avoids help (pride, being seen); misjudges own understanding Ask the learner to point to the help; offer it privately; ask for a confidence rating before the answer is shown.
Opens every gloss, answers well with helps, fails the unaided pass The help carried the task; words looked up were not retained; the unaided pass is harder than intended Re-test the looked-up words alone; give an unaided pass on the same scene the next day.
Rewinds the same line many times without progress The line is above the learner's level; a sound or word they cannot segment; distraction Show the line's caption once; ask what they hear; offer an easier version of the line.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares learner-controlled, on-demand helps with the same helps given by the system or an instructor. The claims below test parts of the model.

  • Students using an annotated interactive videodisc of a film outperformed students who saw the same film on videotape with a lecture, in retention and recall of information [+W]: the earlier page's source. An intermediate Spanish class worked through an annotated videodisc of a film in the lab, with no lecture, against a class that had a lecture and viewed the film on videotape; the videodisc group scored higher on retention and recall, reported as significant beyond the 0.01 level and as "almost two full standard deviations" above the videotape group. No standardised effect size is printed (i?), rigour is coded r1, and the comparison changes the medium, the annotations, the lecture and the control at once, so it cannot show that learner control was the active ingredient. Marked below its M cap for that reason.
  • Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]: primary-school pupils learning the causes of day and night did better with learner-paced animations (segmented, or with stop/play buttons that were rarely used) than with a system-paced one, on difficult questions only; a meta-analysis of learner control in educational technology (18 studies, 29 effects) found g = 0.05 overall, with moderate effects in social-studies courses and comprehensive technology programmes. Its table gathers 11 distinct studies. It qualifies the page: control is not a benefit in itself.
  • Learner flow control over animations is rarely used, and the authors report no correlation between its use and knowledge-test results although the printed Spearman r is 0.464 [-W]: a log analysis of stop, start and spooling use in an animation-with-voice condition; learners rarely used the control. The printed r of 0.464 sits oddly with the authors' "no correlation", and no significance test is printed. It bears on the assumption that learners will use control they are given.
  • Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses [+M], with All three L1 gloss conditions significantly improve immediate vocabulary recall over a no-gloss control in EFL reading [+M] and L1 glosses do not significantly improve EFL reading comprehension compared with no glosses [~M]: the general claim is a second-hand report of gloss meta-analyses inside a review of language games, with no effect size printed. The primary study (Cheng and Good 2009, 135 Taiwanese undergraduates randomised within intact classes) found all three first-language gloss formats raised immediate vocabulary recall over no glosses, and none raised reading comprehension on a five-item test. The glosses were on the page, not requested.
  • Proficiency moderates gloss effects: vocabulary benefits appear at Levels 2 and 3 but not Levels 1 and 4 [~M] and Learners hold positive attitudes toward glosses, but their perceptions of which gloss type works best do not match measured performance [~M]: the same study; benefits only at the middle proficiency levels, and learners' sense of which gloss helped did not match their scores. Learners liking a help does not show it works for them.
  • Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+M]: a meta-meta-analysis reporting one meta-analysis (Montero Perez et al. 2013), g = 0.99 for comprehension (k = 15) and g = 0.87 for vocabulary (k = 10). Captions in those studies were provided, not requested; it supports having them available, not putting them under learner control.
  • Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results (Self-After, Self-Both) were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test than learners who decided before the trial (Self-Before) and than their yoked counterparts. [+M] and Having control over the knowledge-of-results decision only before a motor trial (Self-Before) produced no statistically significant retention or transfer benefit over a yoked group receiving the same KR schedule. [~M]: the one test in the wiki of learner control over the same help against yoked controls receiving the identical schedule. University volunteers, a slider-positioning task, retention at 24 hours, ηp² = 0.25 for the interaction, rigour r1. It is motor learning, not language or video, so it is carried here as a proposal for when the choice is offered.
  • Not requesting any hints in at least one problem is related to greater wheel-spinning, consistent with help avoidance [~M], The Coach's hint budget was managed poorly: students split into early-spenders and savers, and within the Coach condition only those with a deliberate hint strategy scored higher [~W] and Unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice performance but harmed later exam performance, and a guardrailed hint-based version mitigated the harm, as reported by the whitepaper citing Bastani et al. [~W]: help under learner control is used unevenly. A decision-tree analysis of maths practice logs links never asking for a hint to wheel-spinning; in a programming course with a 25-hint budget, only students with a deliberate plan scored higher; and a whitepaper reports, second-hand, a randomised trial in which unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice performance and lowered exam performance once removed, with a hint-only version reducing the harm. These are the reason for steps 4 to 6 and for the unaided pass.

Learners, materials and outcomes differ across these claims (undergraduates reading EFL texts, primary pupils watching an animation, volunteers moving a slider, secondary and college students in maths and programming software), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures delayed comprehension of second-language video under on-demand against pushed helps.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does handing learners control over pace, path, help or feedback improve learning, and when? Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]
  • Do glosses and look-up helps beside second-language material help learners learn its words and understand it, and for whom? Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Every claim the earlier page cited is used in the model above (the annotated videodisc study, learner flow control, and learner pacing). These further claims were read for this rewrite and bear on the page only at its edge:

  • Forced pacing eliminates positive transfer from computer aiding and leads subjects to use strategies requiring many more tests than necessary [+W]: twelve subjects on a computer-aided fault-diagnosis task; under forced pacing, training with an aid gave no transfer, which the authors read as needing time to reflect on what the aid does. Supports leaving the learner time with a help; not language learning.
  • Students pause the video after a few sequences to consult a segment menu and judge workload by segment count [~W]: a classroom report from a video sequencer; learners used control to check how much work remained. About what learners do with control, not what they learn.
  • Video-watching sequences involving pausing and rewinding are negatively correlated with math task performance in a flipped precalculus lesson [~W]: used in the situation table as a signal of difficulty; correlational, 49 students, not significant in the full regression.
  • K-12 learners preferred incremental hints and multiple attempts over immediately revealed solutions [+W]: a participatory design study of a programming tutor; a preference, not an outcome.
  • Students and teachers respond favorably to embedded aids: approximately 90% of students reported no difficulty understanding or using the aids, and over 75% wanted similar aids throughout the book. [+W]: acceptability of aids built into a textbook; says nothing about whether they were taken on demand or what was learned.
  • Marginal glosses, dictionary use, and repeated occurrence of unknown words each positively affect incidental vocabulary learning [+M]: a review's report of one randomised study with advanced university learners of French; dictionary use is the closest the wiki comes to a gloss the learner chooses to look up, but it is not compared with marginal glosses here.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually comprehension of material like the scene, the words it carries, or taking part in an exchange like it, unaided, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to get through an immigration desk and a hotel check-in on a business trip, to follow a television series, to pass a course test, to stop feeling lost in meetings. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values finishing the scene quickly may open every help and retain little; one who values not looking slow may refuse help they need; one who wants to talk may skip the glossary and go straight to the response points. Help use reflects the goal as well as the difficulty.

For example, a business traveller (the earlier page's product example) may want to handle the questions at a customs desk, while the designer's objective is the scene's vocabulary. Glossary work serves the objective; an unaided run of the customs exchange tests the learner's goal. Record both, so success on a vocabulary check is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of the same helps offered on request against the same helps pushed by the system or the teacher, with comparable learners and an unaided outcome, show no advantage for on request, or show that learners who choose their help retain less at a delay. If such comparisons repeat the motor-learning result that only a choice made after an attempt helps, step 3 becomes the core of the model. If learners at the target level rarely open the helps, availability alone is not the design and the prompts in steps 5 and 6 carry it. If glosses help vocabulary but not comprehension in video as they did in reading, the model should claim the words, not the whole. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the learner did not use the tools properly" after the fact.

Use of a help, assisted performance, unaided immediate performance, delayed retention and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no number of helps, no order of helps, and no threshold for moving on.
