Fostering Creative Thinking¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 28 claims (18 for, 8 mixed, 2 against) · 27 studies (9 causal, 6 review, 4 theoretical, 3 design, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 8 of 27 report an effect size · 24 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page is about producing ideas and products that are new and useful in a domain the learner already partly knows: many uses for an object, alternative solutions to an open problem, a poem or game made from this week's vocabulary, a model of a phenomenon, a maker project. For a learner whose output on such a task is few ideas, ideas close to the first one, or the answer they expect the teacher to want, a design that (a) poses prompts with many acceptable answers, (b) keeps generating separate in time from judging, grading and reward, and (c) has the learner produce their own ideas before turning to a tool, model answer or AI, is expected to yield more and more varied ideas during the activity (skill-performance, measured as fluency and originality), to keep the learner's interest in the activity (motivation-affect, persistence), and to leave the learner more able to generate ideas unaided on a later task (near-transfer). Judging, convergent practice and content mastery are not the enemy: they come before generation (knowledge of the objects is what ideas are made from) and after it (choosing, combining, refining). The claim is that they should not occupy the same minutes as generation.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's knowledge of the content (generation from nothing produces little; the earlier page notes creative answers depend partly on knowing the objects involved), on whether evaluation is expected during the activity, on what any reward is contingent on, and on whether the outcome is measured with or without the help that was available while practising.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship as a whole. None compares protected, unevaluated generation time with the same activity under evaluation and measures creative output. The claims below test parts of it, mostly from outside creative tasks:

  • Expected tangible rewards for an already-interesting task lower later free-choice engagement with it, while verbal praise does not (two competing meta-analyses and laboratory work, q3–q4). This is the strongest evidence near the page, and it measures motivation, not creativity.
  • Paying children a penny per idea raised the number of ideas they produced in the session (one randomized experiment with fifth graders). Reward contingent on producing ideas is not, on this evidence, a brake on idea output while the reward is in force; the undermining finding concerns what learners do once the reward is gone.
  • University students who used ChatGPT freely on a creative task were more original on that task, but not on a later task without it; a "think first, ChatGPT later" group was more original on the later unaided task (one randomized experiment).
  • A short structured creativity program was followed by higher creativity scores (one-group pretest–posttest studies, no control group).
  • A single 50-minute lesson teaching secondary students strategies for generating ideas, and for watching their own generating, raised fluency, flexibility and originality more than a regular brainstorm lesson, in two cohorts (one quasi-experiment with classes randomly assigned). Practice with open prompts alone is not, on this evidence, the best first move.
  • University students who did an unrelated distracting task before listing and choosing their ideas picked their most creative idea (as trained raters judged it) more often than students who thought about the task consciously or answered at once (one paper, two experiments). Choosing benefits from a gap after the task, which fits keeping convergence apart from generation.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Autonomy owns the general relationship between autonomy support, rewards and intrinsic motivation, and this page uses it for creative activity without repeating it. Motivation owns diagnosing which motivational component is low. Gamification owns points, badges and streaks added to practice. Cognitive Flexibility owns representing one idea several ways for transfer, and Epistemic Cognition owns how learners judge what counts as knowing, which bears on the multiple-model design below. Transfer of Learning treats divergent, abstraction-oriented thinking as one mechanism of transfer.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, made concrete. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Teach the content first, then play with it. Run creative work on content learners have already met: the vocabulary they have been taught, the materials they have handled, the model they have built. Untested proposal; from the earlier page (creative answers depend partly on knowledge of the objects; convergent content mastery supports creativity "up to a point").
  2. Pose an open prompt with many acceptable answers. "How many uses can you think of for a cup?", "Write a poem or invent a word game with these ten words", "Which ways could this circuit fail?". Give 5–8 minutes of individual, silent generation with a goal of quantity ("as many as you can, including odd ones") before anyone shares. Untested proposal; the prompts are the earlier page's; the minutes are a proposal. Before the first such prompt in a unit, teach the generating strategies explicitly rather than relying on brainstorm practice alone: one lesson (about 50 minutes in the one study) that names strategies for producing more, more varied and less obvious ideas, has learners try them, and has them check their own lists against them (how many categories? how far from the first idea?). Basis: A 50-minute metacognitive strategy instruction produced positive effects on secondary students' fluency, flexibility and originality [+M] (Grade 11, two cohorts at one school, compared with a regular brainstorm lesson; the claim page does not list the strategies taught, so the wording here is a proposal, and the outcome was measured soon after, not months later).
  3. Keep judging out of the generating minutes. During generation the teacher and peers do not comment on whether ideas are good, right or wrong, positively or negatively; every idea is recorded. The earlier page's source notes that instructors find this hard: say aloud at the start that ideas will not be judged until the next step, and hold yourself to it. Where learners fear embarrassment, collect ideas anonymously (cards, a shared board or a poll that hides names from peers but not from the teacher). Basis: the authors' account in one undergraduate physics course (Anonymous real-time sharing mitigates evaluation apprehension [+W], no test reported); otherwise untested.
  4. When learners stall, ask what they are assuming. If ideas dry up or all share one function, ask "what have you assumed this object, word or rule is for?" or "what does the task not actually say?" before offering more strategies. Basis: Functional fixedness blocks solutions that require reinterpreting an object's function [+M] (one experiment cited second-hand and a classroom anecdote; it shows the obstacle, not that the question removes it).
  5. Then converge, as a separate step. After generation, spend about as long again choosing, combining and improving: learners group ideas, apply criteria (stated in advance, or theirs, see the table), pick one or two and develop them. This is where judgement belongs. Where learners must pick their own best idea, put a short unrelated task (another activity, a break) between generating and choosing rather than asking them to choose at once or to deliberate on the spot. Basis for the order: untested proposal from the earlier page (learning content and playing with content "are not mutually exclusive"). Basis for the gap: Unconscious thinkers outperform conscious thinkers and immediate responders in selecting their most creative idea (Experiment 1) [+M] and its replication on a second task, Unconscious thinkers better select both their most and least creative idea (Experiment 2) [+M] (one paper, university students in a laboratory, selection judged against trained raters; the length of the distraction is not given on the claim pages, and no classroom test exists in the wiki).
  6. Own ideas before AI. If generative AI is available, learners write their own ideas first and use the tool afterwards to extend or critique them, not to start. Basis: the regulated "think first, ChatGPT later" group was more original on the later unassisted task [+M] and free ChatGPT use raised originality on the assisted task, an advantage that disappeared without it [+M] (one randomized experiment, university students).
  7. Separate assessment time from creative time. Assess the content (the vocabulary test, the energy-flow quiz) at its own time; do not grade the ideas produced in a generation session. Where creative work must be assessed, assess the process over time (a portfolio of drafts, sketches, failures and reflections; a rubric that scores process, understanding and product separately) rather than the final artifact alone. Do not promise tangible rewards for taking part in creative activities learners already enjoy; feedback that names what worked is safe. Basis: Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [+S] (measured on interest and free-choice persistence, not creativity, mostly in laboratory tasks); Grading practices can diminish interest, encourage easier paths and reduce quality of thinking in maker education [+W] (an argument citing Kohn). The portfolio and rubric come from the earlier page and the maker-assessment framework; untested.
  8. Plan the arc across sessions. A default for a unit of several weeks: the first session opens with the strategy lesson in step 2; early sessions then give one 15–20 minute creative segment a week on familiar objects or just-taught content, teacher-posed prompts, ungraded, ideas shared anonymously; middle sessions move prompts onto the unit's own content and let learners help set the convergence criteria; late sessions have learners pose the problem themselves and develop one idea into a product, with critique rounds only after each generation round and a portfolio entry each session. Move a learner from teacher-posed to self-posed problems once they produce ideas in more than one category (not only variants of one use) on two prompts running. All quantities are untested proposals; the three-week duration of the CREACT studies (CREACT improves 5th-grade students' creative thinking performance in drawing arts [+W], no control group) shows only that a program of this length was followed by higher scores.
  9. For modeling units in science, keep several model types and do not force them into one. Build at least two non-redundant model types of the same phenomenon (a physical model, a computational one, a diagram), ask "what can you learn from each model?" rather than "which is right?", and follow learner questions that arise even when they leave the unit's objective. Basis: Heterogeneity-seeking modeling curricula surface epistemic commitments in most students [+W] (sixth graders, one design study, no comparison curriculum and no learning outcome measured); the topic must support several models (an ecosystem does), and without a whole-class comparison the models risk becoming parallel busywork (the earlier pattern's constraint, untested).

When a condition is not met: if learners know little of the content yet, teach it first and keep the creative segment short; if every activity in the course is graded, mark the creative segment explicitly as ungraded and say when the graded part comes.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the creative work is for, and how it will be judged. Generating ideas as practice, a product for a real audience, and a graded creative assignment need different handling of evaluation. If unstated, ask: will anyone grade, rank or publish what learners produce, and when?
  • What the learner already knows of the content and of open tasks. Novices in the domain have little to recombine; learners used only to single-answer tasks may freeze or wait for the "right" answer. If unstated, ask: can they name several features or uses of the objects involved, and have they done an open-ended task before?
  • What help is available while generating. Generative AI, model answers and a teacher's suggestions all raise output during the task; the question is what the learner can do without them later. If unstated, ask: will learners have AI or examples during the task, and will they have them when it counts?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is what a learner produces under stated conditions: the prompt, the time, who could see the ideas, whether evaluation, reward or a tool was present, the number of ideas, how many distinct categories they fall into, and how far each is from the commonest answers in the group. "Is creative", "is afraid of being judged" and "is fixated" are inferred states. A short list from one session says little about capacity.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares creative work done with and without evaluation in the same minutes, or a generate-then-judge sequence with an unseparated one. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

  • Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [+S]: a 128-experiment meta-analysis found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement (d = −0.28 to −0.40), while positive verbal feedback enhanced it; a competing 96-experiment meta-analysis found no overall undermining and a minimal negative effect only for expected tangible rewards given for doing a task. Its ## Studies bearing on this claim table counts 8 distinct studies. It measures interest and free-choice persistence on mostly laboratory tasks, not creativity, and the two meta-analyses disagree on size.
  • Self-determination theory holds that autonomy support raises intrinsic motivation, and a meta-analysis finds expected tangible rewards undermine it [+S]: a review and the same 128-experiment meta-analysis (d = −0.4 for rewards on already-interesting tasks), with a conceptual framework proposing that "cognitive" autonomy support (evaluating work against one's own standard) fosters the most enduring investment in deep thinking. Its table counts 8 distinct studies, one with an effect size coded. It supports letting learners set convergence criteria; it is not a creativity measure.
  • Free ChatGPT use boosts originality on the assisted creative task but the advantage disappears when assistance is removed [+M] and The regulated 'think first, ChatGPT later' group outperformed both other groups in independent originality on the unassisted task [+M]: one study, 196 university students randomly assigned to human-only, free-AI and regulated-AI groups. Free AI raised originality on the assisted product-improvement task (d = 0.79 over human-only) but not on the later unassisted invention task (d = 0.04); the think-first group did not differ on the assisted task (d = 0.18) and was more original on the unassisted one (d = 0.52 over human-only, 0.48 over free AI). The delay between tasks is not stated on the claim pages. A second-hand account of the same study was merged into the free-use claim page; it is not a second study. Three further AI claims point the same way without testing the sequence: a second-hand report that LLM access raised individual writers' story ratings while making stories across writers more alike (LLM access homogenises creative output [~W]), which matters where the variety of a class's ideas is the goal; a survey of design students in which using AI to generate prototypes, but not to brainstorm, went with a felt loss of creativity or ownership (Prototype generation linked to ownership loss [~W], correlational self-report); and a review theme that overreliance on automated writing evaluation may lower creativity (Overreliance on AWE [-W], no statistic). For children, a scoping review of 22 small studies reports only possible benefits of generative AI for divergent thinking and creative self-efficacy, without confirmed efficacy (GenAI and children's creative thinking [~W]).
  • A 50-minute metacognitive strategy instruction produced positive effects on secondary students' fluency, flexibility and originality of ideas in both cohorts [+M]: Grade 11 students at one Dutch school (102 and 117 in two cohorts), classes randomly assigned to a strategy lesson or a regular brainstorm lesson, pretest and posttest. Positive effects on all three outcomes in both cohorts; no effect size is printed on the claim page, and the strategies taught are not described there. This changes the default: step 2 now opens a unit with explicit strategy instruction rather than open-prompt practice alone. It does not test separating generation from judgement.

  • Unconscious thinkers outperform conscious thinkers and immediate responders in selecting their most creative idea (Experiment 1) [+M] and Unconscious thinkers better select both their most and least creative idea (Experiment 2 replication) [+M]: Ritter et al. (2012), 112 and then 68 university students randomly assigned to choose at once, to think consciously, or to do a distracting task first; the distracted group's choice of their most creative idea agreed with trained raters more often (χ² main effects, p < .01 and p < .05; no effect size printed), and in Experiment 2 also their least creative. One paper, two tasks, laboratory, the same authors. It supports a gap before choosing (step 5); it measures selection, not the number or originality of ideas produced.

  • Concrete reward increases children's mean ideational fluency on creativity tasks relative to control conditions [~M]: 191 fifth graders randomly assigned to control, immediate or delayed penny-per-idea reward produced more ideas under both reward conditions; per task, immediate reward raised ideas on all four tasks and delayed reward on three (Reward effects hold across individual creativity tasks [~M]); pennies for "good" ideas only showed no incentive effect, in a condition the authors set aside as confounded (Rewarding only 'good' ideas produced no incentive effect [~M]). One study, 1970, no effect size printed. This limits the model: a reward contingent on producing ideas raised output while it was in force; the page's caution about rewards rests on later free-choice interest, which this study did not measure.

  • Stepwise constructive-conflict guidance reduced new idea generation, while the antagonistic agent played a more balanced role [-M]: novice interaction designers given step-by-step critique guidance added fewer new ideas in their second iteration than those who reflected on their own (Hedges' g = −1.03); an interactive AI-agent condition fell between, not significantly different from either. One study. It qualifies the convergence step: heavy structure in critique can crowd out new ideas.
  • Functional fixedness — treating an object's or idea's function as fixed — blocks solutions that require reinterpreting it. [+M]: an experiment (cited second-hand, sample not reported) found functional fixedness in a technologically sparse culture, and a classroom anecdote shows a teacher's repeated question about assumptions unblocking a student. It supports the assumption question as a move; it does not test it.
  • Twenty-three 5th graders' creativity scores rise after nine CREACT drawing-arts lessons in a one-group pretest-posttest study without a control group (effect size .71) [+W]: 23 fifth graders, nine lessons over three weeks, total creativity rose from 52.32 to 71.95 (an effect size of .71 the article calls large; coded i2 medium). One group, no control, so practice on the test and maturation are not ruled out. Marked W here for the missing comparison. The same article reports, second-hand, an earlier one-group high-school study in which CREACT raised performance on poem and story tasks but had little effect on a paradox task (Prior research found CREACT improved poem and story tasks but had a low effect on a paradox task [~W]): gains from a short program may depend on the kind of task, so report which tasks improved rather than a gain in "creativity".

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these (laboratory interest measures, children's idea counts, university originality ratings, laboratory idea selection, secondary students' divergent-thinking scores, a drawing test), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures creative output months later.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do rewards and praise undermine voluntary engagement, and what do rewards do to idea output? Expected tangible rewards reduce later free-choice engagement with a rewarded task, while verbal praise does not [+S]: its table gathers this claim's studies with the children's reward-per-idea experiment and the maker-grading argument.
  • Does using generative AI while producing creative work leave learners more original when they later work without it? Free ChatGPT use boosts originality on the assisted creative task but the advantage disappears when assistance is removed [+M]
  • Can a short creativity program raise creativity scores? Short-term creativity interventions can be effective in changing creativity positively [+W]
  • Does teaching generating strategies explicitly do better than brainstorm practice? A 50-minute metacognitive strategy instruction produced positive effects on secondary students' fluency, flexibility and originality of ideas in both cohorts [+M]
  • Does a gap between generating and choosing improve which idea learners pick? Unconscious thinkers outperform conscious thinkers and immediate responders in selecting their most creative idea (Experiment 1) [+M]

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually stated as a capability ("generates several useful alternatives to an open problem in this domain, unaided") or a product. Elicit separately what the learner wants from creative work: to make something they are proud of, to have fun with the content, to get a good mark, to avoid looking foolish, to make something for someone. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants a good mark will steer towards the safe idea the teacher seems to want; one who wants to make something for a friend may care more about usefulness than novelty; one who wants not to look foolish will produce little in a named round.

For example, a teacher's objective may be fluent use of ten new vocabulary words, while a learner wants to write a funny poem to read to a younger sibling. The poem session serves both if the words are required and the poem is not graded; a vocabulary test at another time checks the teacher's objective. Record both, so that a good poem is not read as mastery of the words, nor a weak test score as a failure of the creative session.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing creative activities with and without evaluation in the same session, on matched learners and tasks, find no difference in idea count, originality or later unaided output; or if reward per idea raises output without lowering later voluntary engagement, in which case the caution about rewards in step 7 is too broad for idea generation. If think-first sequencing with AI does not replicate beyond one university sample, or the unaided advantage disappears at a longer delay, step 6 becomes a preference. If one-group gains from short creativity programs vanish against a control group that takes the same tests, the arc's dose has no support at all. If explicit strategy instruction does not beat brainstorm practice outside one secondary school, or its advantage is gone a few weeks later, the opening strategy lesson in step 2 becomes optional; if a gap before choosing does not improve learners' choices in classrooms, step 5's gap goes. Do not protect the model by blaming every null result on hidden evaluation after the fact.

Output during an activity, originality judged by raters, interest afterwards, unaided performance on a new task, and creative work the learner values are separate outcomes, and the present evidence ties none of them to the separation of generating from judging.

Claims¶

  • Open-ended tasks are associated with higher subsequent disciplinary engagement [~W]
  • CREATE improves students' originality immediately after the intervention across all three originality scoring methods [+W]
  • Grades themselves, regardless of formula, detrimentally affect creative thinking, long-term retention, interest in learning, and preference for challenging tasks [+W]
  • PGR students rejected GenAI generation of research ideas, original arguments, or whole papers as violating authorship norms, while accepting surface-level support [+W]
  • Immediate responders generate fewer ideas than conscious and unconscious thinkers (Experiment 1) [+W]
  • People expecting a reward for a task perform worse than those expecting nothing, especially on tasks requiring cognitive sophistication and open-ended thinking [+W]

Related Principles¶

  • Transfer of Learning — divergent, abstraction-oriented thinking is also one of the general mechanisms that supports transfer
  • Autonomy — owns the relationship between autonomy support, rewards and intrinsic motivation that the evaluation and reward steps here rely on
  • Motivation — diagnosing which motivational component is low before choosing a creative activity
  • Gamification — points, badges and streaks around practice; the reward caution here applies to them
  • Cognitive Flexibility — representing one idea several ways, close to the multiple-model step
  • Epistemic Cognition — how learners judge what counts as knowing, which the heterogeneity-seeking modeling design surfaces

Examples¶

  • A teacher who must test students' vocabulary understanding can still set aside separate time for students to write poems or invent word games with the same vocabulary, supporting both mastery and creative exploration.
  • Use student-owned portfolios with digital and non-digital documentation to assess maker growth over time
  • Use a three-part process-understanding-product rubric for formative and summative maker assessment
  • Discourage mental offloading to AI by balancing guidance depth and frequency with application opportunities
  • A student, running a computational model with 31 guppies to match her planned physical biosphere, paused mid-simulation and said "that's how it's gonna look—it's gonna be so lonely," then revised the model to 1 guppy — a modeling decision driven by a caring commitment the curriculum never assigned as a goal.
  • Two students presenting food-web diagrams of the same ecosystem in different formats (one an "energy pyramid," one organized around habitats) were asked by the teacher what each model showed, rather than which was more correct — both were retained as valid, complementary representations.
  • Think first, AI later: university students list their own ideas for improving a product before opening ChatGPT, then use it to extend the list; their originality is judged on a later task done without it.
  • Design AI feedback and task scope to leave room for open-ended creative exploration
  • Writing Lab six-lesson lesson-series pattern alternating divergent and convergent thinking with a fixed compare-contrast, generate, select, apply, evaluate activity cycle
