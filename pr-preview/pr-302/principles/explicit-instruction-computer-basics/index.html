Explicit Instruction: Computer Basics¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 18 claims (12 for, 6 mixed) · 26 studies (10 causal, 6 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 theoretical, 2 qualitative, 1 associational, 1 design), q1–q4 · 1 of 26 report an effect size · 12 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one capability: carrying out the everyday routines of a computer, phone or tablet that a learner needs for their own purposes (turning on and unlocking the device, using a trackpad, mouse or touch screen, opening and switching between applications, logging in, setting up and using an email account, saving, finding and attaching a file, filling in an online form, recovering from a simple error). For an adult learner who is a novice on these routines, often an older adult or someone returning to learning with little prior access to computers, and whose observed starting response is to hesitate, ask for help at each screen, click at random, or stop when something unexpected appears, explicit instruction is expected to produce more accurate and more confident unaided performance of the taught routines on the learner's own device (skill-performance, near-transfer) than leaving the learner to explore or giving a general explanation alone. Explicit instruction here means: demonstrate one routine at a time on the device the learner will use, name the interface conventions and words as they appear, have the learner carry out the steps at once with help that responds to what they do, and withdraw that help as they succeed. The target is a procedure; its use in the learner's own tasks (a health portal, a job application, messages to family) is near-transfer, and coping with an unfamiliar screen is further transfer that this model does not promise.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship directly. No claim compares explicit instruction in computer basics with exploration or with other teaching, for adults or for older adults, and none measures performance weeks later on the learner's own tasks. The nearest evidence is one experiment with undergraduates in a computer-literacy course, in which versions of a computer-based lesson that included practice produced higher posttest scores than versions without practice (In one experiment with 256 undergraduates, computer-based lesson versions that included practice produced significantly higher posttest scores than versions without practice [+M]). The other claims used below come from other tasks and learners (school science, long division with a tutor, writing revision, memory research, cognitive-load studies) and say so where they are used. The page's four key sources (Chaffin & Harlow 2005; Dickinson et al. 2011; Seo et al. 2019; Tsai et al. 2017) are about older adults and technology but have no claim page in the wiki, so nothing here is drawn from them.

The relationship is conditional on the learner's starting response with this device and routine (a learner who already performs a routine unaided needs a harder task, not a demonstration; Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]); on whether someone can see each attempt and respond to it (the evidence for responsive help is one-to-one tutoring); on whether practice uses the learner's own device and accounts; on the learner's confidence and willingness to ask for help; and on whether the routine serves a goal the learner holds.

How this sits beside the converted siblings. Direct Instruction holds the general explain–model–guide–hand-over relationship and its comparison with discovery; this page is one application of it to device and interface routines. Guided Practice holds the practice phase between a demonstration and independent work, and Scaffolding and Fading how support is lowered and withdrawn; the default design below uses both and does not repeat them. Procedural Learning holds how procedures are built and sequenced generally. Chunking holds how much is presented at once. Explicit Instruction: Internet Search takes over once device and browser routines are stable, and Digital Learning holds the separate question of learning through a digital medium.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a tutor or programme designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Start from a task the learner wants to do. In the first session, ask what the learner wants to use the device for (a message to family, a health portal, a job search, coursework) and choose the first routines from that task. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's caution that generic "computer basics" lessons are weak when not tied to real learner goals.
  2. Find the starting point by watching, not asking. Hand the learner their own device and ask them to do the first step of the chosen task. Note where they stop or what they do instead. Teach from the first step they cannot do; skip what they already do unaided. Untested proposal; the reason to skip what is already secure is Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M] (cognitive-load studies, not computer basics).
  3. Teach one short routine at a time. A routine is one sequence of three to seven steps with one result (open the browser and go to a saved page; attach a file to an email). Introduce no more than two new routines in a session, and name each new word or screen convention as it appears ("this is the address bar"). Evidence for keeping units small and meaningful: Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S] (memory research; the claim's studies are not about computer instruction). The step counts are untested proposals.
  4. Demonstrate the routine on the learner's device, slowly, saying each step. Take two to five minutes: show it once at normal speed so the learner sees the result, then once step by step, saying what you look for on the screen and why you press what you press. Make the goal of the first attempts the steps, not the finished result ("find the paperclip, then choose the file"). Evidence for explicit guidance over exploration for novices: Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [+S] (a meta-analysis of discovery against explicit instruction across domains, and a primary-school science experiment; carried here to adults and devices). For aiming first attempts at the steps: Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] (high school students learning a writing revision skill; carried here). For older adults, demonstration by a coach was reported to raise confidence in one digital programme: Qualitative findings: ease of use supports engagement and confidence, but challenge must be tailored to ability and content must be culturally relatable [+W] (interviews with older adults in a falls-prevention programme, not a computer-skills course).
  5. Have the learner do it at once, with hands on the device. The learner, not the tutor, performs the steps two or three times in a row, while the tutor watches and helps only where the attempt shows it is needed: a pointing cue first, then naming the step, then showing it again, and less help after a success. Do not take the mouse or the phone. Evidence that practice belongs in the lesson: In one experiment with 256 undergraduates, computer-based lesson versions that included practice produced significantly higher posttest scores than versions without practice [+M] (undergraduates in a computer-literacy course; horizon not reported). Evidence for help that responds to each attempt: Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] (fourth and fifth graders, one-to-one tutoring on long division). The number of run-throughs is an untested proposal.
  6. Leave a step card the learner made. After the guided run-throughs, the learner writes or dictates the steps in their own words, with a screenshot or sketch of the screen if that helps, and keeps it beside the device. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement for visible step structure and plain-language explanation of interface terms.
  7. Practise the same routine again in the next session, unaided first. Open each later session by asking the learner to do last session's routines without help, card allowed, on the real task. Count a routine as learned when the learner does it unaided, without the card, at the start of two sessions in a row; then stop practising it and move on. Untested proposal for the criterion; withdrawing help as success appears follows Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+S] (worked-example fading with ninth graders and college students, and a review of scaffolding research; carried here).
  8. Teach recovery as a routine of its own. Once two or three routines are secure, demonstrate what to do when something unexpected appears (a pop-up, a password prompt, a page that will not load): stop, read the screen aloud, go back one step, close and reopen. Practise it on staged errors, and use any error the learner actually made earlier as the first example. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's inclusion of troubleshooting simple errors.
  9. Move to the learner's real task, then to a new one. In late sessions, the learner does the whole task they named in step 1 from start to finish, with help only on request, and then one task they have not been taught that uses the same routines (a different website's form; a different file). Untested proposal; from the earlier page's caution that permanent over-direction can block transfer.

Dose and progression across sessions (untested proposals). A default course is six to eight sessions of 60 to 90 minutes, one or two a week, for one learner with one tutor or a group of up to six with one helper for every two or three learners. In each session: about 10 minutes of unaided warm-up on earlier routines; about 15 minutes for each new routine (demonstration two to five minutes, guided run-throughs about ten); about 15 minutes on the learner's own task; a short close where the learner says what they will try at home. Early sessions (1–2): the device itself, the pointer or touch gestures, opening and closing, one routine from the learner's task, with full demonstration and help beside them. Middle sessions (3–5): two new routines a session, the learner's step card replacing most of the tutor's prompts, and recovery from errors. Late sessions (6–8): the learner's whole task and one untaught task, help on request only, step cards put away. A learner moves on from a routine after two consecutive sessions of unaided success, and leaves the course when they can complete their own task alone; one who still cannot start a routine after two sessions on it goes back to a smaller routine, not to more of the same demonstration.

When a condition is not met: if no one can watch each attempt (a large group with one tutor, a self-paced video course), give every learner the same short routine with a printed step card, ask all learners to show their screens at a set point so the tutor can scan them, and go first to those who are stuck; if the learner already does the routine unaided on the first try, drop the demonstration and go to the real task.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learner does now, on their own device. A learner who cannot yet use the pointer or unlock the phone needs a different first session from one who can browse but has never attached a file. Self-report is unreliable in both directions, especially when confidence is low. If unstated, ask: when they pick up their own device and try the first step of the task they want to do, where do they stop?
  • Who will be beside each learner during practice, and for how long. Responsive help depends on someone seeing each attempt; a volunteer with ten learners, a librarian on a busy shift and a video course are different designs. If unstated, ask: during practice, who will look at a learner's screen before their next attempt, and how often?
  • The task and device the learner will use afterwards. A health portal on a phone, job applications on a library computer and coursework on a laptop need different routines; practice on a device unlike the learner's own may not carry over. If unstated, ask: on what device, with what accounts, for what task will they use this next week?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is an attempt under stated conditions: the device, the routine, whether the step card was used, the help given (record its level: pointing, naming the step, showing again), the steps done, where the learner stopped, the time taken, and what they did with help on the next attempt. "Has the routine", "is anxious", "lacks the vocabulary" and "is dependent on the tutor" are inferred states. Success with the tutor beside them shows what the learner does with that help; it does not show what they will do at home alone.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself practice and changes what it measures, so record each one, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki tests explicit instruction in computer basics against another way of teaching it, for any group of learners. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

  • In one experiment with 256 undergraduates, computer-based lesson versions that included practice produced significantly higher posttest scores than versions without practice [+M]: 256 undergraduate computer-literacy students, randomly assigned within pretest blocks to six versions of one computer-based lesson; the four versions with practice scored significantly higher on the posttest than the two without (means above 17 against below 15). It is the closest study to this page in its content (a computer-literacy course), but its learners were undergraduates, not novice adults or older adults; the lesson was delivered by computer, not by a tutor; it does not describe the practice or any feedback; it prints no effect size; and the horizon is not reported. Its synthesis table gathers 12 distinct studies on the wider question of practice after a model.
  • Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [+S]: three studies. A meta-analysis of 580 comparisons found explicit instruction ahead of unassisted discovery (a small effect, d = 0.38), while discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples or scaffolding came out ahead of other instruction; an experiment with 112 third- and fourth-grade children found many more learned a science procedure from direct instruction than from discovery; and a narrative review argues guidance helps until learners have the knowledge to guide themselves. It supports demonstrating routines rather than leaving novices to explore. None of the studies is about adults or devices, and the meta-analysis's abstract does not break results down by prior knowledge.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders tutored one-to-one on long division under fully contingent support (8 per condition) solved more problems than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test; a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching. Its synthesis table gathers 25 distinct studies. It supports helping only where each attempt shows it is needed; it is one-to-one evidence with children, and the claim's Discussion notes that contingency is scarce and hard to achieve in whole-class teaching.
  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S]: two reviews and an experiment on immediate memory (4 distinct studies in its synthesis table); the limit for novel material is put nearer four chunks than seven, and a chunk helps only when it is meaningful to the learner. It supports short routines and one new item at a time; it is memory research, not a test of how computer lessons are segmented.
  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: 84 high school girls learning a writing revision skill did better when assigned goals about the strategy than goals about the final score, with a suggestion that shifting to outcome goals helps once the skill is mastered; a theoretical chapter synthesises related studies. Its synthesis table gathers 3 distinct studies. It supports aiming a novice's first attempts at following the steps; it is not about technology.
  • Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+S]: three experiments (ninth graders and college students) in which fading worked examples into problems favoured near transfer over static example–problem pairs, and a review naming fading as a defining feature of scaffolding; its synthesis table gathers 10 distinct studies. It supports withdrawing help as success appears; the claim says it does not establish one fading rate or that fading prevents long-term dependence.
  • Limit: Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies and three experiments with electrical trainees, in which guidance that helped novices lost its advantage, or hurt, as trainees gained experience; its synthesis table gathers 16 distinct studies. It is the reason not to demonstrate what a learner already does, and it gives no point at which an individual crosses over.
  • Limit: Individually removing objectives, examples, or review from a well-designed computer-based lesson did not significantly reduce achievement [~M]: in the same computer-literacy experiment, lessons without examples, without objectives or without review scored close to the full lesson and not significantly differently (equivalence was not tested). With the practice finding above, it suggests that in that lesson the hands-on practice mattered more than any one other element; it does not show demonstrations are unnecessary.
  • Students notice the absence of practice and examples: attitude items about those events are rated lower when the events are missing [+M]: in the same experiment, the groups without practice rated the practice items lower than groups with it. It bears on how learners experience the lesson, not on what they learned.
  • Qualitative findings: ease of use supports engagement and confidence, but challenge must be tailored to ability and content must be culturally relatable [+W]: interviews with 13 older adults and 10 social care staff in a digital falls-prevention programme reported that older adults, including those with lower digital literacy, found it easy to use, that a coach's demonstrations raised some participants' confidence and motivation, and that technical glitches reduced motivation. It is the only claim here about older adults using digital tools; it is about a health programme, not computer-skills instruction, and reports perceptions.
  • Learning ability does not decline with age in healthy older adults [+M], Common language teaching methods such as oral drills, memorization, and fast-paced competitive activities disadvantage older learners [~M] and The most cited barriers to adult learning are situational, but adults also face internal barriers such as low confidence in their ability to learn [~M]: secondary summaries (a 1987 digest on older language learners and a 2015 report on adult education technology) with no data of their own. They inform the pacing and confidence rows above as context, not as tested effects.

Learners, tasks, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (undergraduates on a computer-based lesson, children with a tutor, high school writers, trainees reading circuit diagrams, older adults in a health programme), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures whether an adult novice still performs a taught routine, unaided, weeks later on their own device.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually unaided performance of named routines on the learner's own device, with stated aids (a step card or none), at a stated horizon (the end of the course; a month later at home). Elicit separately what the learner wants: to video-call grandchildren, to stop depending on a family member for the bank, to apply for jobs, to manage a health condition through a portal, or just not to feel left out. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values never asking their family again may refuse help they still need; one who values getting a form submitted today may be content to have the tutor do it, and learn nothing they can repeat.

For example, a retired learner may want to see photos their daughter sends, while a library programme's objective is a checklist of skills (email account, attachments, search, file saving). Teaching attachments through the daughter's photo emails serves both; teaching them through a sample file serves only the checklist. Record both outcomes, so that a ticked checklist is not read as the capability the learner came for.

The evidence by question¶

  • For a learner new to a device or program, does demonstrating and explaining each routine beat leaving them to explore it with little support? Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [+S]
  • In a computer-skills lesson, does hands-on practice after the demonstration raise performance over the same lesson without it, and do the other lesson events matter as much? In one experiment with 256 undergraduates, computer-based lesson versions that included practice produced significantly higher posttest scores than versions without practice [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims about digital skills read for this page and not used in the model, with how far each bears on it.

  • The effect of autonomy on digital skills is conditional on teacher support, acting as a compensatory mechanism when teacher support is low [~W] — school-level autonomy and teacher support in PISA 2022 data for school students; an association with no printed statistic. It bears on school systems, not on teaching adults device routines.
  • Learning Studio participation increased students' comfort with, and skills around, the Studio technologies [+W] — school students' self-reported comfort with specific equipment over a pilot year, from surveys; no comparison group. It bears weakly on whether hands-on use raises comfort, in a different population.
  • Training improved accuracy across all digital-forensics experience and generative-AI familiarity groups, with the largest estimated gains among analysts with no forensics experience and frequent generative-AI users [~W] — intelligence analysts trained to tell real from AI-generated images, in exploratory subgroups of about ten; gains were largest for those with no forensics experience. It is a judgement skill with professionals, not device routines, and bears only on the general point that novices gain most from training.
  • Segmenting Improves Multimedia Learning [+M] — a meta-analysis of 56 studies finding that presenting multimedia instruction in segments rather than continuously improves retention and transfer, with small to medium effects. It bears on the self-paced video row (one routine per segment) but has not been read against device-skills videos.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of explicit instruction with guided exploration or with self-paced tutorials, on matched adult novices and their own devices, show no difference in unaided performance at a delay, or show that a gain at the end of the course is gone a month later at home. If learners taught by demonstration and practice cope worse with unfamiliar screens than learners who explored with support, the late sessions should open earlier to untaught tasks. If small-group or peer-supported practice matches one-to-one help, the one-to-one preference is a cost, not a requirement. If older adults do as well with the same pace as younger learners, drop the pacing changes for them. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too fast" or "not enough practice" after the fact.

Doing a routine with the tutor beside them, doing it unaided at the end of a session, doing it at home weeks later, doing the learner's own task, and coping with a new screen are separate claims. The present evidence establishes none of them for computer basics; it establishes no number of sessions, no routine size and no hand-over criterion.

Examples¶

  • Address Digital Literacy — introduces basic digital tools and terminology explicitly
  • Browser and email onboarding with modeled steps, guided repetition, and immediate troubleshooting
  • File-management mini-lessons tied to real tasks such as uploading assignments or attaching documents
  • Small-group digital-skills labs where learners practice the same routine repeatedly with support nearby
  • Support digital inclusion through onboarding processes, device lending and technical support when deploying digital learning programmes with older adults
