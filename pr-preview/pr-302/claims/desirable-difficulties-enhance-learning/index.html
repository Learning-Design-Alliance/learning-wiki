Retrieval practice and spaced retrieval improve achievement and retention by medium amounts, while interleaving helps category learning only for some materials and blocking beats it for words¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 3 studies · 3 quant-synthesis r? · q4 · i2 medium

Introducing certain effortful conditions during learning — such as retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving — can slow visible performance during instruction while improving long-term retention and transfer. The claim is about desirable difficulties specifically: conditions that are effortful but surmountable, not obstacles that exceed the learner's capacity.

Evidence¶

Yang et al. 2021¶

Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 147(4), 399–435. doi:10.1037/bul0000309

q4 · systematic review and meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect, g=0.499 · n=222 studies (48,478 students) · quant-synthesis · r?

A systematic review and meta-analysis of the testing effect in real classrooms rather than the laboratory, pooling 222 independent studies with 48,478 students. Taking quizzes or practice tests on studied material (retrieval practice) raised academic achievement by a medium amount compared with restudying and other learning strategies. The size of the benefit varied with what the control group did, whether the practice and final tests had the same format, whether corrective feedback was given, how many times learners were tested, and the study design.

Latimier et al. 2020¶

Latimier, A., Peyre, H., & Ramus, F. (2021). A Meta-Analytic Review of the Benefit of Spacing out Retrieval Practice Episodes on Retention. Educational Psychology Review, 33(3), 959–987. doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8

q4 · meta-analysis (robust variance estimation) · i2 · medium-to-large effect, g=0.74 · n=29 studies (39 + 54 effect sizes) · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of 29 studies on spaced retrieval practice, which the authors describe as combining two "desirable difficulties": retrieval practice and spacing. Spreading repeated retrieval of the same content over time gave a strong retention advantage over massing it (g = 0.74, 39 effect sizes). Expanding the gaps between retrievals was no better than keeping them even (g = 0.034, 54 effect sizes), although expanding schedules helped more when items were tested more often.

Brunmair & Richter 2019¶

Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. Psychological Bulletin, 145(11), 1029–1052. doi:10.1037/bul0000209

q4 · multilevel meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect, g=0.42 · n=59 studies (158 samples, 238 effect sizes) · quant-synthesis · r?

A multilevel meta-analysis comparing interleaved with blocked presentation of items for inductive (category) learning, across 59 studies. Interleaving helped by a moderate amount overall, most for paintings and other visual materials (g = 0.67) and less for mathematical tasks (g = 0.34). It qualifies the claim: for word materials blocking beat interleaving (g = -0.39), and effects for expository texts and tastes were not significant. Interleaving helped more when categories were similar to each other and items within a category were less similar, so the difficulty is desirable only for some materials.

Discussion¶

The difficulty must be surmountable. Desirable difficulties work because effortful retrieval and discrimination trigger deeper processing and strengthen memory traces — but only when learners can actually overcome the difficulty. If the challenge exceeds available working-memory resources, it becomes an undesirable difficulty and performance degrades, consistent with Cognitive overload degrades learning. and Chunking reduces working memory load. The designer's task is to keep difficulty within the learner's zone of capability, which is the same constraint that governs Cognitive load management and underpins Cognitive load reduction improves learning. — reducing extraneous load is precisely what frees capacity so that the intrinsic difficulty of retrieval and discrimination can be productive rather than overwhelming.

Expertise reversal. The same difficulty that helps novices can hinder advanced learners. As expertise grows, conditions that force extra effort on already-mastered material become redundant or counterproductive — see Expertise reversal effect and Cognitive load theory. Difficulty should be faded or re-targeted as competence develops, not held constant.

Transfer appropriateness. A difficulty is most desirable when it mirrors the conditions of the eventual test or use: practicing under varied, delayed, or interleaved conditions prepares learners for retrieval situations that blocked, immediate practice does not. Feedback must still be available so that errors made under difficulty are corrected rather than reinforced.

Fluency misjudgment. Learners judge their learning by momentary fluency, so they often prefer easier strategies that produce worse long-term outcomes — and may resist conditions that feel harder. Designers should expect short-term performance dips, communicate the rationale for effortful practice, and use assessments that reward delayed retention rather than immediate performance; productive struggle connects to Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.

Evidence status. No specific studies are catalogued in the Evidence section yet; the framework originates with Robert Bjork's work on desirable difficulties, and key experimental and meta-analytic support (particularly for retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving) still needs to be added before this claim can be rated.

Open questions. How much difficulty remains desirable as expertise grows, and how difficulty should be calibrated dynamically over the course of instruction, remain open design questions. Adaptive approaches that adjust challenge to current learner state — see Adaptive learning improves outcomes. — are one candidate mechanism, but their fit with deliberately under-scaffolding practice has not been established here.

Merged from “Desirable Difficulties Improve Retention” (desirable-difficulties-improve-retention): The core trade-off. The claim hinges on a dissociation between performance during learning and retention after it. A condition that produces fluent, error-free practice often feels effective and can look effective on an immediate test, yet yields poorer delayed retention than a condition that slows learners down [+S]. This is why "desirable" must be defined relative to a delayed retention or transfer measure, not to learner satisfaction or immediate performance. The distinction maps onto the broader contrast between conditions of learning and conditions of performance described in cognitive load theory.

What makes a difficulty desirable. The difficulties in question are those that force additional encoding operations — retrieval from memory, generation of an answer, comparison across contrasting cases, or re-engagement with material after partial forgetting. Difficulty that merely consumes working memory without triggering those operations (e.g., unnecessarily awkward formatting, split attention, or search that yields no answer) is not covered by this claim and is better treated through cognitive load reduction [-S]. In this sense the claim is the productive counterpart of active learning: generation and production during instruction are common sources of desirable difficulty.

Boundary conditions. Three moderators are likely to matter. First, the difficulty must be surmountable: if learners fail to reconstruct or generate the target content, the extra effort produces incomplete encoding rather than stronger memory [~M]. Second, learner expertise moderates the effect — for novices, high intrinsic load plus an added difficulty can push total load past working-memory capacity, degrading learning (see cognitive overload degrades learning and the expertise reversal effect) [~S]; for more advanced learners, the same difficulty may be beneficial or even necessary. Third, learners' preferences are an unreliable guide: conditions that feel harder are often the ones that produce more retention, so perceived difficulty and actual learning can diverge in either direction [~M] — a direct challenge to designs that optimize for learner satisfaction or fluency alone.

Open questions. The dose–response relationship (how much difficulty is optimal), the interaction between different difficulty types (e.g., spacing combined with interleaving), and the durability of effects across domains and age groups all remain to be established with evidence on this page. Until evidence entries are added, this page should be treated as a framing claim whose subclaims (e.g., for retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving individually) will each carry their own evidence.
