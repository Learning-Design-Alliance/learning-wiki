    Digital Open Badges¶

    Element · All elements

    Description¶

    Digital badges are small digital images representing a learner's achievement within a specific domain, embedded with metadata (issuer, date, criteria/rubric, and evidence of learning) that makes what was actually learned far more transparent than a traditional grade or degree (Farmer & West, 2016). Open badges add a specific technical affordance on top of this: they can be exported to portable "backpacks" and displayed across institutions and platforms, rather than being locked into one issuing system — this portability is what separates them from closed badge systems like Khan Academy's or Duolingo's.

    Farmer and West (2016) identify open badges as solving two persistent credentialing problems: they recognize competencies developed in informal settings (after-school programs, workplace training, self-directed online learning) that traditional grading structures cannot capture at all, and they make formal credentials more transparent by breaking a single opaque grade into discrete, evidenced micro-competencies. Badges have been used to serve several distinct functions beyond simple certification: as a map of learning pathways (badges sequenced to guide a learner through a domain), as a motivational and self-regulation tool (a visible, choosable goal that increases learner autonomy), and as a competency-based assessment mechanism that lets learners redo and rework a task until they meet the badge's criteria — a direct alternative to time-based, norm-referenced grading.

    Design Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A well-specified, evidenced set of criteria for each badge (rubric, required evidence) — this is what makes badges more transparent than a grade, and what critics argue too many "lightweight" badges lack
    • A backpack or portfolio system that supports import/export if portability across institutions is a design goal (this is what distinguishes an open badge from a closed one)

    Constraints¶

    • Badge proliferation ("lightweight" badges issued without rigor) can clutter the badging landscape and undermine end users' (employers', other institutions') ability to judge a badge's value — the badging community's central open problem is establishing norms for rigor (West & Randall, 2016)
    • Ecosystem recognition is difficult: a badge earns real value only when institutions or organizations outside the issuer recognize and accept it, which requires alignment of standards, requirements, and objectives that often do not naturally align across institutions
    • General awareness of badges among decision-makers (employers, admissions, HR) remains low, limiting badges' practical signaling value regardless of design quality

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners in informal or blended settings whose competencies would otherwise go uncredentialed (after-school programs, vocational/work-based training, self-directed online learners)
    • Learners who benefit from increased autonomy and choice in what to pursue and demonstrate, since badge systems often let learners select which badges to work toward

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Recognition and credentialing of discrete, evidenced competencies rather than an aggregate grade
    • Increased self-direction, self-regulation, and intrinsic motivation through choice and visible incremental progress
    • Support for competency-based (mastery) assessment, where learners rework a task until badge criteria are met, rather than time-based grading

    Affordances¶

    • Mastery Learning
    • Self-Determination Theory

    • Immediate Feedback

    Examples¶

    • Purdue's Passport system — lets faculty create and issue their own badges (e.g., for intercultural learning courses, educational technology courses)
    • Teacher Learning Journeys (Penn State, NASA, and NSTA partnership) — 63 professional-development activities offered as badges and stamps, letting teachers self-regulate their own PD pathway
    • The MOUSE Squad program — badges recognizing technology skills developed by disadvantaged students in after-school settings, used as evidence on college applications
