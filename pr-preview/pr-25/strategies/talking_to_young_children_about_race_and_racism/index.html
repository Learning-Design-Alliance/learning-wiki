    Talking to Young Children About Race and Racism¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    This strategy involves parents and caregivers proactively initiating conversations with young children (roughly ages 3–8) about race, racism, and anti-racism, rather than waiting for children to raise the topic or adopting a "colorblind" silence. Conversations are honest, specific, and developmentally calibrated, and are typically supported by curated media, picture books, play guides, and discussion prompts. The adult's role is to name unfairness when children observe it, answer questions directly, and model anti-racist responses.

    Design Implications¶

    Children notice racial cues from infancy and form in-group preferences by the preschool years; silence about race does not prevent bias — it leaves children to construct explanations from ambient social cues Developmental intergroup theory: adults' implicit and explicit cues shape children's intergroup attitudes. [+M]. Explicit, other-focused conversation about the experiences of people from other racial groups reduces children's intergroup bias more than generic diversity exposure or colorblind messaging Brief conversations about other-focused experiences reduce children's racial bias. [+S]. Structured contact and dialogue-based interventions reduce prejudice in children and adolescents, with effects strongest when programs are sustained and discussion-based Prejudice-reduction interventions in schools improve intergroup attitudes. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A safe, supportive conversational environment where children's questions are treated as legitimate
    • Age-appropriate media, books, and play/discussion guides (e.g., Case Study prompts drawn from stories, Class Discussion-style questioning adapted to the home)
    • Adult comfort and preparation: caregivers benefit from rehearsing responses to common questions ("Why does her skin look different?") before conversations arise
    • Ongoing commitment — single conversations have limited durability; the strategy works as repeated, integrated dialogue

    Constraints¶

    • Caregivers who are uncomfortable or inexperienced discussing race often avoid or defer the topic, which functions as colorblind socialization and can leave bias unaddressed [-M]
    • Vague "everyone is equal" messaging without specific discussion of unfairness does not reduce children's bias and can mask structural inequities [~M]
    • One-off conversations decay; effects on intergroup attitudes require reinforcement across home and school settings [~M]
    • Media exposure alone, without adult mediation and dialogue, produces weaker attitude change than guided conversation [-W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Story-based: reading picture books featuring protagonists of color and pausing to discuss characters' experiences and unfair treatment
    • Media-mediated: using programs such as PBS KIDS or Sesame Workshop segments with accompanying watch-and-play guides
    • Event-triggered: responding to a specific incident (a slur, an exclusion, a news event) with a calm, concrete explanation
    • Family-commitment: brainstorming and rehearsing concrete anti-racist actions the family can take (what to say, whom to tell)

    Target Learners¶

    • Young children (approximately 3 months to 8 years), whose intergroup attitudes are actively forming and highly responsive to adult messaging [+M]
    • Parents, caregivers, and early-childhood educators, who need both content knowledge and conversational confidence
    • Children from both marginalized groups (who benefit from positive racial identity and preparation for bias) and majority groups (who benefit from reduced in-group bias) [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Awareness of human diversity and positive feelings about one's own and others' racial identities
    • Recognition of unfairness and development of empathy for those treated unfairly Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
    • Behavioral readiness: knowing specific actions to take when witnessing or experiencing racism
    • Sense of belonging and self-worth for children from racialized groups

    Instructions¶

    1. Prepare yourself. Caregivers clarify their own understanding and rehearse age-appropriate language before initiating conversations; discomfort is normal and should not delay starting.
    2. Open with observation, not lecture. Use a book, show, or real event as a Case Study and invite the child's questions and comments in a Class Discussion style adapted to one-on-one conversation.
    3. Name race and unfairness specifically. Use concrete language ("That was unfair because of the color of his skin") rather than abstract or colorblind statements.
    4. Connect to feelings. Use a Check-In to help the child name their own emotions and imagine the feelings of the person treated unfairly.
    5. Rehearse action. Through Act It Out or role-play, practice what the child could say or do if they see or experience racism.
    6. Return and extend. Revisit the topic across weeks and months with new books and media, treating it as an ongoing family practice rather than a single talk.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Building Empathy — empathy for out-group members is a key mechanism through which these conversations reduce bias
    • Acting-Role-Play — rehearsal of anti-racist responses converts discussion into behavioral readiness
    • Active-Listening — the caregiver stance that keeps children's questions and misconceptions surfacing

    Examples¶

    • PBS KIDS Talk About Race & Racism — video content plus caregiver guides for watch-and-play conversations with young children.
    • Sesame Workshop — "Coming Together" resources, including the ABCs of Racial Literacy, with discussion guides and family activities.
    • EmbraceRace (embracerace.org) — webinars, book lists, and action guides for caregivers raising children in racially diverse or unjust contexts.
    • Families reading picture books with strong Black protagonists (e.g., from the Coretta Scott King Book Awards list) and pausing to discuss characters' experiences of fairness and unfairness.
