    SIFT Method¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    The SIFT method, developed by Mike Caulfield, is a streamlined approach to evaluating online information: Stop and check your emotional reaction, Investigate the source, Find better coverage of the claim, and Trace claims, quotes, and media to their original context. Unlike traditional checklist-based source evaluation (e.g., CRAAP), SIFT treats the open web itself as the fact-checking tool — learners "read laterally," leaving an unfamiliar page to see what independent sources say about it and about the claim.

    Design Implications¶

    SIFT operationalizes the professional fact-checking practices documented by Wineburg and McGrew, whose studies found that professional fact-checkers outperformed historians and Stanford students precisely because they left the page immediately to read laterally Lateral reading outperforms vertical reading for evaluating online sources. [+S]. Checklist approaches that keep learners on the page ("vertical reading") are exploited by sophisticated misinformation, which often looks professional and cites plausible-sounding evidence Checklist-based source evaluation is less effective than lateral reading. [-M]. SIFT's four moves are deliberately cheap — each should take seconds to a few minutes — so that evaluation becomes a habitual reflex rather than a burdensome task Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Internet access during instruction so learners can practice leaving the page and searching for independent coverage
    • Authentic web content for practice, including realistic but flawed sources — sanitized examples do not transfer
    • Brief direct instruction in each move, followed by immediate Practice on live pages
    • A Think-Aloud demonstration of expert lateral reading so learners see the moves in action

    Constraints¶

    • Fails when learners lack basic search skills; "find better coverage" presumes fluency with query formulation and result scanning
    • Less effective if taught as a memorized sequence rather than applied to real pages — transfer requires varied practice with authentic misinformation Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources. [~M]
    • The "Stop" move depends on metacognitive awareness that novices often lack; without explicit modeling, learners skip it and react to content emotionally
    • Time pressure and strong prior beliefs can override the strategy — motivated reasoning reduces willingness to investigate sources that confirm one's views [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Taught as a full sequence or as individual micro-moves ("just do the S and the I")
    • Adapted for younger learners as simplified heuristics ("leave the page and see what others say")
    • Embedded in discipline-specific contexts: evaluating journal articles in science, primary sources in history, datasets in statistics
    • Delivered via Caulfield's open course materials, or integrated into library information-literacy instruction

    Target Learners¶

    • Secondary and postsecondary students who get most information through social media and search Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources. [+S]
    • Adults in professional contexts (journalism, health, finance) who must triage sources quickly
    • Less necessary for learners already fluent in lateral reading; instruction should fade as evaluation becomes automatic Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Source evaluation: judging the credibility of an unfamiliar source in seconds, not minutes
    • Media literacy: recognizing when content is stripped of its original context
    • Metacognitive regulation: pausing before sharing or trusting emotionally charged content

    Instructions¶

    1. Stop. Pause when encountering an unfamiliar or emotionally provocative claim; notice the reaction before engaging. (Metacognitive Check-In)
    2. Investigate the source. Open a new tab and search for what known outlets say about the source or author — read laterally, not vertically.
    3. Find better coverage. Search for the claim itself on fact-checking sites and trusted outlets; the best fact-check of a claim is often independent of the original source.
    4. Trace the claim. Follow quotes, images, and statistics back to their original context using reverse image search and citation chasing.
    5. Follow with distributed Practice on fresh, authentic pages so the moves become automatic Distributed practice improves retention. [+S]

    Related Strategies¶

    • Lateral Reading — the core reading behavior that SIFT packages into teachable moves
    • CRAAP Test — the checklist tradition SIFT was designed to replace for web evaluation
    • Fact-Checking Routines — professional variants of the same moves

    Examples¶

    • SIFT (Move 1–4), Caulfield's open materials — the original freely licensed description and lesson materials, used in hundreds of library and first-year-experience courses.
    • Civic Online Reasoning curriculum — Stanford History Education Group's free lessons and assessments built on lateral reading research; classroom studies show large gains in source evaluation.
    • Wikipedia's "about" pages as coverage checks — learners use Wikipedia and Snopes as quick third-party coverage sources during the "Find better coverage" move.
