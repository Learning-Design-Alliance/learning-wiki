    Emotional Walking Game¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    The Emotional Walking Game is a kinesthetic warm-up in which learners walk around a shared space while the facilitator calls out (or signals) an emotion — joy, anger, fear, pride, boredom — and learners immediately re-embody it in their walk: pace, posture, facial expression, and gesture. After each round, brief debrief questions ("What did your body do? How did it feel?") convert the physical experience into explicit emotional vocabulary and awareness.

    Design Implications¶

    The game leverages embodied cognition: bodily states and postures feed back into emotional processing, so acting an emotion strengthens recognition and labeling of that emotion in self and others Niedenthal's embodiment account of emotion. [+M]. It also functions as a low-stakes check-in that builds the shared emotional vocabulary on which later social-emotional learning activities depend Universal SEL programming improves social-emotional skills, attitudes, and behavior. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • An open space where learners can walk freely and safely
    • A facilitator who calls emotions, models them first, and paces transitions (Demonstration before learner turns)
    • A short debrief after each round or at the end, so physical experience becomes named emotional knowledge (Articulation)
    • An agreed signal convention (verbal cue, drumbeat, music change) so shifts are unambiguous

    Constraints¶

    • Requires physical mobility; offer seated alternatives (facial expression, hand gestures) so learners with motor impairments can participate fully
    • Learners uncomfortable with public emotional display may disengage or find the activity threatening — participation in portrayal should be invitational, not compulsory [~M]
    • Without a debrief, the activity stays at the level of mimicry and builds little emotional vocabulary [-M]
    • Large groups with poor spatial management produce collisions and off-task behavior; establish walking rules before starting

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Signal modality: verbal labels, music excerpts, or images of faces (the last adds a facial-recognition component)
    • Intensity scaling: the facilitator calls for "slightly worried" vs. "terrified" to build emotional granularity
    • Guessing variant: one walker embodies an emotion while peers infer it, turning the game into a perspective-taking exercise
    • Content-linked variant: emotions are tied to story characters or historical figures, connecting the game to case-based learning
    • Age adaptation: for adolescents and adults, use subtler emotions (ambivalence, anticipation) and slower, more observational pacing

    Target Learners¶

    • Early childhood and elementary students, for whom emotion concepts are still being mapped onto bodily experience [+M]
    • Learners developing emotional vocabulary and self-regulation, including those in SEL programs Universal SEL programming improves social-emotional skills, attitudes, and behavior. [+S]
    • Kinesthetic learners who engage more readily through movement than discussion [+W]
    • Less suitable as-is for learners with strong existing emotion-regulation skills, for whom it functions best as a brief warm-up rather than a core activity [~W]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Emotional awareness and labeling: connecting internal states to names and bodily signatures
    • Empathy and emotion recognition: reading emotion from posture and expression in others
    • Self-regulation: noticing how emotional states change body and behavior, a prerequisite for managing them
    • Physical coordination and body awareness

    Instructions¶

    1. Set up: establish walking rules (space, pace, no contact) and a signal convention.
    2. Model: the facilitator demonstrates one or two emotions in their own walk (Demonstration).
    3. Play: call an emotion; learners walk it for 20–60 seconds; call a neutral signal between emotions to reset (Practice).
    4. Vary: escalate through intensity levels or the guessing variant (Act).
    5. Debrief: ask "What did your body do? How did you know what to change?" and have learners name the emotion–body links they noticed (Articulation).
    6. Assess informally: note accuracy and range of portrayal over time (Assessment).

    Related Strategies¶

    • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a seated sibling activity using the same emotion-labeling goal
    • Acting-Role-Play — extended embodiment of characters and situations rather than single emotions
    • Act It Out — dramatization of content scenarios with an emotional dimension

    Related Elements¶

    • Demonstration — the facilitator's modeled walk gives learners a reference before they attempt their own
    • Practice — repeated emotion shifts build fluency in embodiment
    • Check-Ins — the debrief functions as a group emotional check-in
    • Articulation — verbalizing body–emotion links converts experience into vocabulary
    • Coaching — facilitator feedback sharpens portrayal during play

    Examples¶

    • RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — the walking game serves as a kinesthetic entry into the "Recognizing" and "Labeling" anchors of the RULER SEL framework (https://ei.yale.edu)
    • Drama-based SEL warm-ups — theater-in-education programs commonly open sessions with emotion-walk variants before scene work
    • Early childhood classrooms — teachers use the game during morning circle to build the feelings vocabulary later used in conflict resolution
