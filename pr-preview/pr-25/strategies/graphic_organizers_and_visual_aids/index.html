    Graphic Organizers and Visual Aids¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Graphic organizers and visual aids present content in structured visual formats — concept maps, Venn diagrams, timelines, flowcharts, advance organizers, comparison matrices — that externalize relationships among ideas. They direct attention to the most important information, make structural relationships visible, and offload organization work from working memory onto the display.

    Design Implications¶

    Graphic organizers work by imposing structure on to-be-learned material, which reduces the effort learners spend identifying what matters and how it connects Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Visual formats also recruit a second representational channel, improving recall when words and pictures are well integrated Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. Effectiveness depends on the organizer matching the actual structure of the content — a hierarchy for taxonomies, a flowchart for processes, a matrix for comparisons — and on learners being taught how to read and use it.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Analysis of the content's underlying structure before selecting an organizer type
    • Explicit instruction in how to interpret and complete the organizer (Advance Organizers work only when learners understand their logic)
    • Integration with the text or instruction rather than as a detached add-on; signaling that highlights key relationships supports attention Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M]

    Constraints¶

    • Overly complex or decorative graphics add extraneous load and depress learning [~S] — a cluttered organizer is worse than none
    • Provided organizers can encourage shallow copying; learners who generate their own organizer often learn more than those given one [~S], though generation costs time and can overload novices
    • Poorly matched to content structure (e.g., a linear timeline for a causal system), organizers can instill a misleading model of the domain [-M]
    • Learners with strong prior knowledge may find organizers redundant and gain little [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Instructor-provided organizers: efficient for novices; use as a framing device before or during instruction
    • Learner-generated organizers (concept mapping, Annotating): deeper processing; pair with Self-Explanation prompts to avoid mechanical copying Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
    • Partially completed ("skeleton") organizers: a middle path that structures without doing the work — a form of Fading
    • Cooperative mapping: pairs or groups negotiate the organizer, surfacing disagreements about relationships

    Target Learners¶

    • Novices and learners who struggle with organization, attention, or processing large amounts of text Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
    • Younger learners and English language learners, for whom visual structure reduces language-processing demands
    • Less beneficial for experts, who already possess the organizing schema [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Relational knowledge: seeing how concepts connect, compare, and hierarchize
    • Text comprehension and summarization: extracting macro-structure from extended material
    • Retention of key facts and relationships embedded in a structure
    • Less suited to skill automatization or open-ended creative production

    Instructions¶

    1. Identify the core structure of the content (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, causal chain, part–whole).
    2. Select or design an organizer that mirrors that structure; keep it sparse — key concepts and labeled relationships only (Chunking).
    3. Introduce the organizer before or early in instruction as a framing device (Advance Organizers).
    4. Model completing a portion of it, then have learners complete the rest individually or in pairs, prompting them to explain their placement choices (Self-Explanation).
    5. Fade the organizer over successive tasks as learners internalize the structure (Fading).

    Related Strategies¶

    • Activating Prior Knowledge — organizers can serve as the activation surface, making existing schemas visible before new content lands
    • Chunking Information — organizers are the visual enactment of chunking: grouping related items into a single structured unit
    • Note-Taking Structures — structured notes (Cornell, matrices) are learner-generated organizers applied to lectures and readings

    Examples¶

    • Reading Rockets — published library of story maps, comparison charts, and concept frames used in K– literacy instruction.
    • Inspiration Software / Kidspiration — concept-mapping tools widely used in schools for learner-generated webs and diagrams.
    • Comparison matrices in history instruction — a grid of events × causes × outcomes that turns narrative text into comparable cases; contrasting cases in matrix form supports abstraction Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
