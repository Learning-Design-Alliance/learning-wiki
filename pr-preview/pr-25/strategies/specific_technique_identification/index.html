    Specific Technique Identification¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Specific Technique Identification directs learners' attention to particular features of a text — naming the exact word, sentence, or line where a technique appears — and explains its significance, rather than leaving learners to discover techniques unaided. Questions that quote the text or cite line numbers make the task concrete and accessible. The strategy converts an open-ended "analyze this passage" prompt into a guided search with a known target.

    Design Implications¶

    The strategy works by constraining the search space: novices lack the schemas to distinguish stylistically significant features from incidental ones, so unguided analysis spends working memory on finding what to look at rather than understanding why it matters Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory demands exceed capacity. [+S]. Pointing to specific textual evidence functions like a Demonstration of expert noticing — the teacher models what a skilled reader sees. Naming the location also enables Annotating: learners can mark the identified feature and record its effect, supporting later independent application.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A text rich enough in the target techniques to reward close attention
    • Prepared identification of specific examples (line numbers, quotations) with explanations of significance
    • Time for guided analysis followed by discussion and independent application (Practice)

    Constraints¶

    • Overly directive identification can produce dependence — learners wait to be told what matters rather than developing their own noticing Scaffolding that is not faded undermines development of independent strategy use. [~M]
    • Pre-identifying everything removes the productive struggle of searching; effectiveness drops when learners already have the schemas to find techniques themselves Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
    • Requires careful preparation; ad hoc identification during discussion tends to be inconsistent and hard for learners to record

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Full identification: teacher names every instance — appropriate for first exposure to an unfamiliar technique
    • Partial identification: teacher names one or two instances, learners find the rest — a Fading move toward independence
    • Verification mode: learners propose identifications and the teacher confirms, corrects, and locates missed instances
    • Cross-text transfer: techniques identified in one text are hunted in a second, dissimilar text

    Target Learners¶

    • Novices new to literary or rhetorical analysis, who cannot yet distinguish significant features from incidental ones Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory demands exceed capacity. [+S]
    • Struggling readers, who benefit from reduced search demands and concrete anchors (line numbers, quotations)
    • Less valuable for advanced students, who gain more from open analysis; explicit location-giving becomes redundant Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Close reading: noticing how language works at the sentence and word level
    • Technique recognition: building a repertoire of named devices and their effects
    • Analytical writing: grounding claims in specific textual evidence
    • Reduced search load so discussion can focus on interpretation rather than location

    Instructions¶

    1. Select a text and pre-identify the specific instances of the target technique, noting line numbers or quotations.
    2. Open with a Demonstration: name one instance, quote it, and explain its effect on the reader.
    3. Direct learners to Annotating — mark the identified instance and record its significance in the margin.
    4. Assign Practice: learners locate further instances, individually or in pairs, using the demonstrated criteria.
    5. Use Peer Discussion to compare findings, then whole-class sharing where the teacher verifies, corrects, and supplies missed instances.
    6. Close with Individual Reflection or short writing: learners explain the effect of one technique they identified themselves.
    7. Fade over successive lessons: identify fewer instances, eventually asking learners to justify their own identifications.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Activating Prior Knowledge — recalling known examples of a technique primes learners to spot new ones
    • Think-Aloud Modeling — the teacher verbalizes why a feature matters, not just where it is

    Examples¶

    • A teacher reads a poem aloud, stops at a specific metaphor ("line 7 — 'the sky was a bruised plum'"), and explains how the image sets mood; students then hunt for two further images in pairs and share findings.
    • In a rhetoric unit, the teacher projects a speech with the anaphora pre-highlighted, names it, and models its persuasive effect; students then identify parallelism in a second speech with no highlighting.
    • Sentence stems ("The author's choice of ___ in line ___ suggests…") scaffold students' analytical writing about the techniques identified in class.
