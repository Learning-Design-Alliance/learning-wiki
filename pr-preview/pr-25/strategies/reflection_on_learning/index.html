    Reflection On Learning¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Reflection on learning is the deliberate, structured act of prompting learners to examine what they have learned, how they learned it, and what it means for future action. It is carried out through prompts, journals, exit tickets, structured discussions, or portfolio entries that require learners to articulate their understanding and monitor their own learning processes. Unlike unstructured "think about it" exhortations, effective reflection is scaffolded with specific questions tied to content and process.

    Design Implications¶

    Reflection works by converting experience into articulated knowledge: generating explanations and connections during reflection produces learning gains beyond restudying Self-explanation prompts improve conceptual understanding compared to reading or solving without explanation. [+S]. It also builds the self-monitoring habits central to Self-Regulated Learning, helping learners calibrate confidence against actual performance High-confidence errors are especially persistent, making confidence-checking reflection valuable. [+M]. The quality of reflection depends on prompt specificity — vague prompts ("reflect on today's class") yield vague, low-yield responses.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Specific, content-tied prompts (e.g., "What was the hardest step in today's problem, and why?") rather than generic invitations
    • Time allocated within the learning session, not deferred to optional homework
    • A mechanism for learners to revisit earlier reflections so they can see their own progression
    • Psychological safety if reflections are shared, so learners can surface confusion honestly

    Constraints¶

    • Unprompted reflection is rare; learners left to their own devices typically skip it or produce superficial entries [-M]
    • Reflection on poorly understood material can entrench misconceptions if learners confidently articulate wrong ideas without corrective feedback Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process, not the person. [~M]
    • Overly frequent or formulaic reflection becomes ritual compliance, consuming time without cognitive engagement [-W]
    • For novices, open-ended metacognitive prompts can overload working memory; narrower prompts tied to the immediate task work better [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Written journals or learning logs — sustained across a unit; best for tracking change over time
    • Exit tickets — 2–3 minute end-of-session prompts; low cost, high frequency
    • Structured discussion — paired or group reflection where learners articulate understanding to peers; articulating to others adds a retrieval and organization benefit Learning by teaching improves the tutor's own learning. [+M]
    • Portfolio reflection — learners select and justify artifacts against criteria, connecting reflection to Assessment

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners with some foundational knowledge, who have enough schema to generate meaningful explanations Self-explanation prompts improve conceptual understanding compared to reading or solving without explanation. [+M]
    • Adolescents through adults; younger learners need heavily scaffolded, concrete prompts
    • High-achievers who overestimate their understanding benefit from confidence-calibration prompts High-confidence errors are especially persistent, making confidence-checking reflection valuable. [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Metacognitive skill development: monitoring, evaluating, and planning one's own learning
    • Conceptual integration: connecting new material to prior knowledge
    • Transfer preparation: articulating when and where knowledge applies
    • Dispositional growth: developing habits of examining evidence and revising one's thinking

    Instructions¶

    1. Set the frame. Before the activity, tell learners what reflection is for and model it once with a Think-Aloud of your own reflective process.
    2. Design specific prompts. Target both content ("What is the key idea?") and process ("What strategy did you use, and did it work?"). Tie prompts to the Application learners will do next.
    3. Build in time and structure. Use exit tickets, journal entries, or a Check-In at natural breakpoints; protect the time rather than treating it as filler.
    4. Add social articulation. Have learners share reflections with a partner or group, converting private notes into Articulation for an audience.
    5. Respond and close the loop. Read or sample reflections and adjust instruction; connect reflection to Assessment for Learning so learners see it shapes teaching, not just grading.
    6. Revisit. Return to earlier reflections at unit's end so learners can see growth and revise earlier judgments.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Self-Assessment — reflection focused specifically on judging one's own performance against criteria
    • Learning Journals — the sustained written form of this strategy
    • Exit Tickets — the brief, high-frequency form

    Examples¶

    • Catalina Foothills School District's POGIL-style reflection prompts — after group problem-solving in science, students complete structured role and process reflections on how the group reasoned, not just what it concluded.
    • Medical education portfolios — reflective case write-ups in clinical rotations require students to connect patient encounters to prior cases and identify gaps, a core component of programs accredited by the AAMC.
    • Khan Academy mastery practice — after missed problems, learners are prompted to review the related explanation and re-attempt, embedding a minimal reflect-and-retry cycle into practice.
