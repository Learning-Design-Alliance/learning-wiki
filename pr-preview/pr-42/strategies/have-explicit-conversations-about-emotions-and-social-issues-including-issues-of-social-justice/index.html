    Have Explicit Conversations About Emotions and Social Issues, Including Issues of Social Justice¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    This strategy treats emotions and contested social realities — race, class, gender, disability, power, exclusion — as legitimate, planned subjects of classroom talk, taught with the same deliberateness as any other content. Explicit means named and scheduled: emotion vocabulary is taught rather than assumed, and social issues are raised as planned curriculum rather than only when an incident forces them. The alternative is not neutrality. A classroom that never names these things still teaches something about them — that they are unspeakable here — and the learners for whom they are daily experience are the ones who learn it most clearly.

    Design Implications¶

    The emotions half rests on a fairly direct finding: emotional competence is teachable, and programmes that teach it explicitly and sequentially produce gains in social-emotional skills, behaviour, and academic achievement SEL programs improve social emotional skills [+S] SEL Programs Improve Behavior And Achievement [+S], with effects detectable at follow-up Social-emotional learning benefits persist at follow-up [+M]. Vocabulary is the load-bearing part — a learner with words for what they feel can report it, and a learner without them acts it out.

    The social-issues half rests on a different and less settled base. Jagers and colleagues' argument for transformative SEL is that competence-focused programmes can teach learners to regulate their responses to unjust conditions without ever examining the conditions, and that self-management taught this way functions as compliance training. Adding explicit discussion of power and identity is proposed as the corrective. That argument is well-developed conceptually and only partially tested empirically — treat it as a well-reasoned design commitment rather than an established effect [~W]. What is better supported is that structured, high-quality discussion improves reasoning and comprehension Discussion Quality Drives Comprehension [+M], and that guided perspective-taking shifts intergroup attitudes Building Empathy Improves Intergroup Attitudes [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Established norms for disagreement, set before any contested topic is raised (Norm Setting, Class Discussion Norms)
    • Explicit emotion vocabulary taught as content, not assumed as background (Feelings Vocabulary)
    • Relational groundwork: learners will not speak candidly with a teacher they do not trust Teacher Student Relationships Improve Engagement [+M]
    • Planned curricular slots, so these conversations are not only reactive to incidents
    • A teacher prepared to sit with discomfort rather than close the topic down when it becomes tense
    • Genuine option not to disclose — participation must be possible without self-exposure

    Constraints¶

    • Learners from affected groups are routinely conscripted as spokespeople or living examples; this is the most common and most damaging failure of the practice [-S]
    • Discussion without norms and structure can license harm, leaving those targeted worse off than if the topic had never been raised [-M]
    • Unfacilitated disagreement tends to entrench positions rather than shift them; the structure has to require engaging with the opposing case Structured Discussion Approaches Improve Comprehension [~M]
    • Emotion talk without vocabulary and structure becomes therapeutic in register and instructional in nothing — and teachers are not therapists [-M]
    • Content can be genuinely activating for learners with trauma histories; advance notice and an exit route are requirements, not courtesies [-M]
    • The evidence base is uneven: the SEL-competence half is well supported, the social-justice half considerably less so, and citing the former to justify the latter overstates what is known [~W]
    • Community and institutional context constrains what can be taught, and a teacher who ignores that may lose the ability to teach any of it [-M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Emotion vocabulary curriculum — structured teaching of feeling words embedded in academic content, as in RULER's Feeling Words approach (Emotion Identification and Labeling)
    • Circles — a structured turn-taking format where every voice is heard and no one is required to speak (Community-Building Circles, Restorative Circles)
    • Literature as the entry point — texts carry the social issue, so learners analyse a character's situation before their own (Read-Alouds with Deliberate Topics)
    • Structured controversy — assigned positions and required side-swapping on genuinely contested questions (Structured Academic Controversy)
    • Explicit perspective work — planned examination of how the same situation is experienced from different positions (Explicit Discussion of Perspectives)
    • Seminar formats — text-anchored discussion with defined roles and turn-taking (Socratic Seminar)

    Target Learners¶

    • All learners, but with the caution that costs and benefits fall unevenly: the learners for whom the topic is lived experience carry more risk in the conversation
    • Younger learners, for whom the emotions half is developmentally central and the vocabulary genuinely new
    • Adolescents, who are already conducting these conversations without adults and generally without structure
    • Learners in diverse classrooms, where the issues are present in the room whether or not they are named
    • Learners in homogeneous classrooms, where the absence of the conversation is easiest to mistake for the absence of the issue
    • Requires particular care with learners who have trauma histories connected to the topic

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Emotion vocabulary and the ability to identify and report internal states
    • Self-management and social awareness as taught competencies SEL programs improve social emotional skills [+S]
    • Perspective-taking and reduced intergroup bias Building Empathy Improves Intergroup Attitudes [+M]
    • Belonging — the sense that one's experience is speakable in this room Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes [+M]
    • Reasoning about contested questions, including the ability to state an opposing case fairly Argumentation Improves Reasoning [+M]
    • Reduced exclusionary discipline, where relational and empathic approaches replace punitive ones Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions [+S]

    Instructions¶

    1. Build norms and relationships first. Do not open a contested topic in a group that has not agreed how disagreement works (Norm Setting).
    2. Teach the vocabulary before requiring the talk. Learners need words with useful precision — frustrated, excluded, dismissed — before they can name experience accurately (Feelings Vocabulary).
    3. Plan the topics into the curriculum. Schedule them alongside the content they connect to, so the conversation is not always a response to something going wrong.
    4. Anchor in a text or case. Start from a character, a source, or a scenario rather than from learners' own lives, so participation does not require disclosure (Read-Alouds with Deliberate Topics).
    5. Give notice and an exit. Say what is coming and make it explicit that stepping out or staying silent is a legitimate option.
    6. Structure the turn-taking. Use a circle, a protocol, or assigned positions so airtime is distributed rather than claimed (Community-Building Circles, Structured Academic Controversy).
    7. Never assign a learner to represent a group. Speak from the text and from research; if a learner chooses to speak from experience, that is their decision to make unprompted.
    8. Close deliberately. Summarize what was said, name what remains unresolved, and say what happens next — a conversation left open at the bell does its damage in the corridor (Individual Reflection).

    Related Strategies¶

    • Emotion Identification and Labeling — the vocabulary foundation the emotions half depends on
    • Identifying Feelings — the learner-facing routine for naming internal states
    • Community-Building Circles — the structure that distributes airtime and makes silence acceptable
    • Restorative Circles — the same format applied after harm has occurred
    • Structured Academic Controversy — the format for contested questions that requires engaging the opposing case
    • Explicit Discussion of Perspectives — planned examination of how a situation is experienced from different positions
    • Socratic Seminar — text-anchored discussion with defined roles
    • Norm Setting — the precondition for all of the above

    Examples¶

    Feeling-words curriculum embedded in literature: Emotion vocabulary is taught through the characters in the class text — naming what a character feels and the evidence for it — so the vocabulary is practised at one remove before it is applied to the self.

    Weekly circle in a primary classroom: A fixed, predictable slot where each learner may speak or pass on a prompt; the predictability is what makes the format usable when something difficult actually arises.

    Structured controversy on a curricular question: Learners are assigned positions on a contested historical or civic question, argue them, swap sides, and then attempt a joint statement — the swap being what prevents entrenchment.

    Planned response protocol for current events: An agreed sequence for when something significant happens outside school — acknowledge it, offer a structured space, provide an exit — prepared in advance rather than improvised on the morning.
