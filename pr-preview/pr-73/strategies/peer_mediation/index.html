    Peer Mediation¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Peer mediation trains selected students in a structured conflict-resolution protocol — typically an adapted interest-based negotiation format — so they can serve as neutral facilitators when peers bring disputes to a mediation session. The mediators do not judge or prescribe solutions; they guide disputants through telling their stories, identifying underlying interests, generating options, and drafting a mutually agreed agreement. Programs are usually whole-school: a designated mediation space and referral process, ongoing mediator training, and adult supervision for cases involving violence or serious harm.

    Design Implications¶

    Peer mediation operationalizes Collaborative Learning and Building Empathy by making conflict resolution itself the curriculum: disputants practice perspective-taking and negotiation in authentic, high-stakes situations, while mediators consolidate the skills through repeated facilitation. Reviews of school programs report substantial reductions in recorded conflicts and suspensions, with effects strongest where implementation is sustained and whole-school Johnson & Johnson, 1996 [+M]. Because mediators must model neutral listening and questioning, training should include active_listening practice and role-played simulations before live cases.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Structured mediator training (typically 10–20 hours) covering the protocol, neutrality, questioning, and confidentiality
    • A clear referral process and a scheduled time/place for mediations
    • Adult coordinator to screen cases and supervise sessions
    • Explicit ground rules: voluntary participation, confidentiality, no physical violence or serious rule violations referred instead to adults

    Constraints¶

    • Ineffective — and potentially harmful — for cases involving bullying, power imbalance, or violence, where a neutral forum can re-victimize the less powerful party; these must be screened out Johnson & Johnson, 1996 [-M]
    • Effects fade when mediation is treated as an add-on rather than embedded in whole-school climate work Johnson & Johnson, 1996 [~M]
    • Mediator skill varies widely; untrained or under-trained mediators default to judging or advising, collapsing the neutral-facilitator role [-W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Whole-school model: all students may refer disputes; mediators rotate on a schedule (the dominant US model)
    • Curriculum-embedded model: mediation skills taught to all students as part of social-emotional learning, with a smaller trained mediator corps
    • Restorative adaptations: mediation folded into restorative-conference formats common in UK and Australian schools
    • Online/async variants: structured peer-feedback and dispute protocols in discussion forums, though evidence here is thin

    Target Learners¶

    • Upper elementary through secondary students; disputants as young as grade 3 can follow simplified protocols Johnson & Johnson, 1996 [+M]
    • Mediators gain the most: repeated facilitation builds communication skill and confidence, consistent with evidence that self-efficacy predicts academic persistence [+M]
    • Students with strong status asymmetries in a dispute are poor candidates for peer mediation regardless of age

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Social-emotional: perspective-taking, emotional regulation, negotiation
    • Procedural: fluency with a structured interpersonal protocol
    • Metacognitive: monitoring one's own neutrality and the disputants' movement toward agreement

    Instructions¶

    1. Select and train a diverse mediator corps using role-play and simulation, emphasizing active_listening and neutral questioning.
    2. Establish a referral system, ground rules, and an adult coordinator who screens out bullying and violence cases.
    3. Run mediations using the standard sequence: introductions and ground rules → uninterrupted storytelling by each disputant → joint identification of interests → option generation → written agreement.
    4. Debrief each session with the coordinator to give process-level guidance, consistent with evidence that feedback is most effective at task and process levels [+S].
    5. Fade adult oversight of routine cases as mediators demonstrate competence, following the principle that fading support promotes transfer of responsibility [+M].

    Related Strategies¶

    • acting-role-play — the primary training method for building mediator fluency before live cases
    • active_listening — the core mediator skill; paraphrasing and summarizing drive the protocol
    • class-discussion — related structured-talk format, but without the neutral-third-party role

    Examples¶

    • Resolving Conflict Creatively Program (RCCP), New York City — large-scale K–12 program integrating peer mediation with conflict-resolution curriculum; evaluated by Aber et al. (1998) [+M]
    • Peer Mediators programs in UK secondary schools — coordinated through local authority anti-bullying strategies, typically with restorative adaptations
    • Community Boards (San Francisco) — community mediation center whose school-based training materials are widely adopted in US districts (communityboards.org)
