    Letting students know what order they will be called on¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    The instructor announces — or posts — the sequence in which students will be called on to respond during discussion, rather than selecting speakers unpredictably. This gives each student advance notice of when their turn will come, allowing them to rehearse a response, manage anxiety, and inhibit the impulse to call out or disengage. It converts an unpredictable social demand into a predictable, plannable one.

    Design Implications¶

    Predictability reduces the executive-function load of classroom questioning: students who know when they will speak can allocate working memory to composing an answer rather than monitoring "will I be next?" [~M]. Advance notice also functions as a form of Wait Time distributed across the room — every student gets thinking time before their turn, not just the one who is called on. Structured, predictable turn-taking is a core feature of well-managed classrooms and is associated with higher participation rates than purely voluntary or random questioning Dallimore's cold-calling research [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A visible, consistently followed sequence (seating order, name list, rotating roster, or digital randomizer announced in advance)
    • Questions substantial enough to warrant preparation time, paired with adequate Wait Time before each response
    • Consistency: the announced order must be honored, or trust in the structure collapses
    • A classroom climate where imperfect answers are treated as normal (Class Discussion norms)

    Constraints¶

    • If the sequence is not followed reliably, the predictability benefit disappears and students may experience the practice as arbitrary [-W]
    • Some students fixate on the sequence itself — rehearsing only "their" question and tuning out other turns, reducing attention to peer contributions [~W]
    • Announced order can enable avoidance: students may disengage after their turn has passed, or experience heightened anticipatory anxiety in the minutes before their turn [~M]
    • Less appropriate for genuinely open, emergent discussions where the flow of ideas should determine who speaks next

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Announced rotation — a fixed order (alphabetical, seating) posted before discussion begins
    • Pre-assigned questions — students receive their specific question in advance, not just their turn
    • Randomizer with preview — a digital tool (e.g., a shuffled name wheel) displays the upcoming order so it is random but known
    • Opt-out clause — students may pass once per session, preserving structure while lowering threat

    Target Learners¶

    • Students with inhibition, impulsivity, or self-regulation challenges (e.g., ADHD), for whom waiting unpredictably is disproportionately difficult [~M]
    • Anxious students and English language learners, who benefit from preparation time before speaking publicly [+W]
    • Whole-class use: predictable turn-taking raises overall participation relative to volunteer-only formats, where a small fraction of students dominate Dallimore's cold-calling research [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Self-regulation: practicing waiting, planning, and turn-taking Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
    • Participation equity: distributing speaking opportunities across all students
    • Oral reasoning: improving response quality through advance rehearsal

    Instructions¶

    1. Decide the sequence method (rotation, seating order, or previewed randomizer) and make it visible before discussion begins.
    2. Pose the question to the whole class and provide adequate Wait Time — several seconds of silent thinking, or brief pair rehearsal — before the first call.
    3. Follow the announced order exactly; if a student passes, move on without penalty and return later if desired.
    4. Keep the whole class accountable between turns with follow-up prompts ("Who can build on that?") so attention does not end when a student's turn passes.
    5. Debrief periodically: ask students whether the structure helps them participate, and adjust the sequence method accordingly (Check-Ins).

    Related Strategies¶

    • Cold calling with advance warning — announced order is cold calling made predictable; the warning is what changes its affective profile
    • Think-pair-share — pair rehearsal before the called-on turn improves response quality under the same structure
    • Volunteer-only discussion — the contrast case; equitable but often dominated by a few confident students

    Examples¶

    • A middle-school teacher posts a rotating name wheel at the start of a Class Discussion; a student with impulse-control challenges can see he is fifth, rehearses his answer, and waits without calling out.
    • A law school seminar using modified cold calling (as studied at Harvard Business School) tells students the day's discussion order in advance, so all students prepare all cases rather than gambling on not being called.
    • An ESL discussion course distributes numbered question cards before a seminar; each student knows both their turn and their question, allowing them to draft language in advance.
