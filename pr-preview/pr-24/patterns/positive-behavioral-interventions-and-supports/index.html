    Positive Behavioral Interventions And Supports¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS) is a multi-tiered framework for establishing the social culture and behavioral supports needed for all students to succeed. Rather than reacting to misbehavior with escalating punishment, schools define a small set of positively stated expectations, explicitly teach them like academic content, and consistently acknowledge students who meet them. Support is organized in three tiers: universal expectations for all students (Tier 1), targeted small-group support for students at risk (Tier 2), and individualized intensive interventions (Tier 3). The framework is implemented at the whole-school level, with teams using behavioral data (e.g., office discipline referrals) to guide decisions.

    Implications¶

    PBIS treats behavior as a learned skill rather than a character defect, applying the same instructional logic to expectations as to academics: define, model, practice, and reinforce. This grounding in Behaviorism — reinforcement of desired behavior rather than punishment of undesired behavior — is combined with explicit teaching so that students know exactly what "be respectful" looks like in each setting. Consistent, predictable acknowledgment systems build a sense of belonging and school climate, and randomized trials show school-wide implementation reduces office discipline referrals and suspensions Bradshaw et al. RCT evidence. [+S]. Because punitive discipline disproportionately harms marginalized students, pairing PBIS with empathic rather than exclusionary responses to misbehavior further reduces suspensions Brief empathic-discipline intervention cuts suspensions. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A defined set of 3–5 positively stated, school-wide behavioral expectations
    • Explicit teaching of expectations in each setting (classroom, hallway, cafeteria), with modeling and practice
    • A consistent acknowledgment/reinforcement system for expected behavior
    • A behavioral data system (e.g., office discipline referral data) reviewed regularly by a leadership team
    • Administrative commitment and sustained staff buy-in; partial implementation weakens effects
    • Tier 2/3 supports (check-in/check-out, individualized plans) for students who don't respond to universal supports

    Constraints¶

    • Effects on academic achievement are indirect and smaller than effects on discipline outcomes; PBIS alone is not an academic intervention [~M]
    • Implementation fidelity is essential — schools that adopt the language without the data systems and consistent reinforcement see little benefit [~M]
    • Token economies can feel infantilizing to older students if acknowledgment systems are not developmentally calibrated [~W]
    • Without attention to root causes, Tier 1 supports may not meet the needs of students with trauma histories or significant emotional/behavioral disabilities [~M]
    • Requires 2–3 years of sustained implementation before full effects typically emerge

    Grain Size¶

    Program or whole school — PBIS is designed as a school-wide system, not a classroom strategy, though classroom teachers implement Tier 1 practices daily.

    Target Goals¶

    • Reduction in office discipline referrals, suspensions, and exclusionary discipline
    • Improved school climate and perceived safety
    • Increased instructional time (fewer behavior-related disruptions)
    • Prosocial behavior and self-management skills that support Self-Regulated Learning

    Target Learners¶

    • All students in a school receive Tier 1 universal supports
    • Students at risk of repeated disciplinary contact benefit most from Tier 2 supports
    • Students with intensive behavioral needs receive Tier 3 individualized, function-based interventions
    • Particularly impactful for historically over-disciplined groups when combined with empathic discipline practices Brief empathic-discipline intervention cuts suspensions. [+M]

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Behaviorism — behavior is shaped by its consequences; systematically reinforcing expected behavior increases its frequency
    • Social Learning Theory — students learn expectations through modeling and observation of peers and adults
    • Self-Determination Theory — well-designed PBIS supports competence and relatedness; acknowledgment systems must avoid undermining autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • Self-Determination Theory — extrinsic token-based reinforcement can crowd out intrinsic motivation if rewards feel controlling rather than informational [~M]

    Claims¶

    Supporting¶

    • Brief empathic-discipline intervention cuts suspensions. [+M] — teacher mindset shifts reduce exclusionary discipline, complementing PBIS structures
    • Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M] — predictable, positive school climate supports students' sense of belonging
    • Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S] — acknowledgment systems should be informational, not controlling

    Contradicting¶

    • None identified — though evidence for academic gains is weaker than for behavioral outcomes [~M]

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    1. Define expectations — Leadership team drafts 3–5 positively stated expectations (e.g., "Be safe, be respectful, be responsible") with clear structure
    2. Operationalize — Specify what each expectation looks like and sounds like in every school setting
    3. Teach — Deliver explicit lessons at the start of the year and re-teach after breaks; model and practice expected behavior like academic content
    4. Acknowledge — Reinforce expected behavior consistently through a school-wide acknowledgment system with frequent, specific feedback
    5. Monitor — Collect and review behavioral data (referrals, acknowledgment ratios) on a regular cycle
    6. Tier 2 support — Provide targeted group interventions (e.g., check-in/check-out with daily adult check-in) for non-responders
    7. Tier 3 support — Conduct functional behavioral assessments and build individualized support plans with family involvement

    Affordances¶

    • Behaviorism — the acknowledgment system operationalizes reinforcement of desired behavior instead of punishment of undesired behavior
    • Clear Structure — explicitly defined, taught expectations remove ambiguity about what is required in each setting
    • Belonging — a predictable, positively framed school culture strengthens students' sense of being known and valued
    • Autonomy — mature implementations shift from tangible rewards toward self-monitoring and student ownership of behavior

    Personalization¶

    Young learners (K–5): Use concrete, frequent acknowledgment (token systems, praise) with heavy modeling and role-play; expectations must be taught in small, observable steps.

    Adolescents: Shift from tangible tokens to social recognition, privileges, and self-monitoring tools; involve students in defining expectations to protect autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

    Students with trauma histories: Emphasize predictability and relationship-building over compliance; pair Tier 2 supports with regulation skills rather than purely consequence-based systems.

    Students with disabilities: Tier 3 plans should be function-based (addressing why behavior occurs) and integrated with IEP goals and accommodations.

    Culturally diverse schools: Audit discipline data for disproportionality; combine PBIS with empathic discipline training so corrections are delivered relationally, not punitively Brief empathic-discipline intervention cuts suspensions. [+M].

    Related Patterns¶

    • Direct Instruction — PBIS borrows its instructional logic (model, practice, reinforce) and applies it to behavioral expectations
    • Cooperative Learning — prosocial expectations taught in Tier 1 create the behavioral foundation that collaborative structures require
    • Constructive Alignment — shares the systems logic of aligning stated goals (expectations), activities (teaching), and consequences (acknowledgment)

    Examples¶

    • PBIS Technical Assistance Center: The national OSEP-funded center provides implementation guides, fidelity measures (e.g., TFI, SAS), and state-level implementation data for thousands of U.S. schools.
    • Maryland Safe and Supportive Schools (MDS3): A statewide initiative combining school-wide PBIS with climate measurement; randomized evaluation showed improved climate and reduced suspensions in high-need schools.
    • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO): A widely used Tier 2 program in which a mentor greets the student each morning, reviews goals, and closes the day with feedback and points shared with family — a structured daily check-in cycle.
