    Create a List of Positive Self-Talk Statements¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Learners generate or select a set of affirmative, first-person statements ("I can break this problem into steps") to use when facing difficulty. The list functions as a rehearsed cognitive resource: rather than improvising coping thoughts under stress, learners draw on statements they have already written, evaluated, and personalized. It is typically paired with a reflection activity in which learners identify their common negative thoughts and write counter-statements.

    Design Implications¶

    Self-talk interventions work best when the statements are credible and self-generated rather than generic praise; affirmations that contradict a learner's self-view tend to backfire [~M]. The mechanism is closer to Self-Regulated Learning — a self-instruction routine — than to mood boosting, so statements should be process-oriented and task-specific, not global ("I am smart"). Framing the activity as a choice supports ownership, consistent with evidence that autonomy supports intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Time for learners to identify their actual negative self-talk patterns before writing counter-statements
    • A prompt structure (e.g., "When I get stuck, I will tell myself…") so statements are tied to specific situations
    • A plan for use — when and where learners will read or rehearse the list (before tests, during revision, at a Check-In)

    Constraints¶

    • Abstract, unrealistic affirmations ("I am brilliant") can increase rumination in learners with low self-esteem rather than reduce it [~M] — statements must be believable to the individual
    • Delivered as a one-off worksheet with no follow-up, the list is rarely retrieved at the moment of need; effectiveness depends on rehearsal and integration into routines
    • Not a substitute for skill instruction: positive self-talk about a task the learner cannot yet perform produces frustration, not persistence

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Coping-style statements ("Mistakes show me what to study next") outperform pure positivity for academic tasks
    • Lists can be built around a specific upcoming challenge (exam, performance, presentation) or as a general resource
    • Younger learners may illustrate or record statements; older learners can maintain them in a journal or notes app

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners experiencing anxiety or negative self-talk around a specific task (tests, public performance, writing)
    • Adolescents and adults, who can evaluate whether a statement is credible for them; younger children need adult help crafting realistic statements
    • Belonging- and confidence-focused statements are most valuable for learners at risk of feeling they do not fit, consistent with belonging intervention research Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Self-regulation: building a rehearsed routine for managing difficulty and frustration
    • Metacognition: noticing and naming one's own negative thought patterns
    • Affective outcomes: reducing test anxiety and supporting persistence, not directly improving domain knowledge

    Instructions¶

    1. Learners identify 2–3 recurring negative thoughts in a short written reflection (Individual Reflection or journal entry).
    2. For each, they write a realistic, process-oriented counter-statement in their own words; provide a model list only as a starting point, not a script.
    3. Learners select 3–5 statements that feel true and rehearse them aloud or in writing (Act It Out works for younger learners).
    4. Attach the list to a trigger point — reviewing it before practice, tests, or a scheduled Check-In — and revisit after the challenge to revise statements that did not ring true.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Achievable Micro-Goals — pairing self-talk with small, attainable next steps makes statements actionable rather than purely reassuring
    • Growth Mindset Framing — process-focused self-talk operationalizes mindset language at the moment of struggle

    Examples¶

    • A test-anxiety intervention: before each unit test, students reread a personal list ("I studied for this; I'll start with what I know") and note afterward which statements helped.
    • Sports and performance training: athletes commonly script cue words and coping statements for high-pressure moments; the same structure transfers to academic performance settings.
