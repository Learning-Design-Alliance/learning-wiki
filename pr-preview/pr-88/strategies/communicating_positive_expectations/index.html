    Communicating Positive Expectations¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Communicating positive expectations means deliberately signaling to every learner — through interaction patterns, feedback, and task assignment — that they are capable of success. The strategy counters the well-documented tendency for teacher expectations to become self-fulfilling: learners perceived as low-potential receive fewer opportunities, less wait time, and less substantive feedback, and their performance declines accordingly [~S]. Educators instead monitor their own interactions for bias and distribute high-expectation behaviors equitably.

    Design Implications¶

    Expectation communication works primarily through learner self-efficacy: expressed belief in a learner's capability raises the learner's own belief, which in turn predicts persistence and achievement Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+S]. The mechanism is bidirectional — teachers act on expectations (calling on some students more, waiting longer for others), and learners read these cues as information about their ability. The design task is therefore as much about auditing one's own behavior as about adding encouragement.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Equitable interaction patterns: calling on all learners, distributing wait time evenly, and giving substantive hints rather than giving up early on struggling learners
    • Feedback framed around effort, strategy, and progress rather than fixed ability (Provide Feedback)
    • Tasks that are genuinely achievable with support, so expressed belief is credible rather than empty praise
    • Self-monitoring of interaction data (who gets called on, who receives elaborated feedback) to detect unintended low-expectation signaling

    Constraints¶

    • Expectation effects are typically small-to-moderate and strongest for young learners and stigmatized groups; they do not substitute for quality instruction [~M]
    • Undifferentiated praise ("good job!") can reduce motivation and signal low expectations; praise must be contingent on genuine performance [~S]
    • High expectations without adequate scaffolding produce failure experiences that damage self-efficacy [-M]
    • Teachers' implicit biases resist conscious correction; expectation communication degrades under time pressure and large class sizes [~W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Whole-class framing ("this task is hard, and I expect all of you to get there") versus individual conferencing
    • Structural approaches: assigning demanding tasks to all groups rather than tracking by perceived ability
    • Peer-mediated: training learners to communicate high expectations to each other in collaborative learning settings

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners with low prior achievement or a history of underperformance, who are most likely to receive low-expectation treatment by default [~M]
    • Learners from stigmatized or stereotyped groups, where expectation cues interact with stereotype threat [~W]
    • Younger learners, who are more susceptible to teacher expectation effects than adolescents and adults [~M]
    • Learners with fragile self-efficacy, for whom expressed belief directly supports persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+S]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Motivational outcomes: self-efficacy, task engagement, and persistence Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
    • Behavioral outcomes: on-task behavior and participation
    • Indirect academic gains, mediated through increased opportunity to learn and effort

    Instructions¶

    1. Audit your interaction patterns: track who you call on, how long you wait, and who receives elaborated feedback; correct imbalances.
    2. Extend wait time and follow-up prompts (Coaching) equally across learners, especially after incorrect answers.
    3. Frame feedback in terms of strategy and progress, signaling that ability is malleable rather than fixed.
    4. Assign the same ambitious tasks to all learners, differentiating support rather than the goal.
    5. Express belief explicitly and credibly — tie statements of expectation to specific evidence of the learner's progress.
    6. Use regular Check-Ins to verify that learners are experiencing the tasks as challenging-but-achievable.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Growth Mindset Messaging — the belief-in-malleability framing that makes positive expectations credible
    • Equitable Participation Structures — the interactional mechanics through which expectations are transmitted
    • Mastery-Oriented Feedback — feedback framing that supports rather than undermines expressed belief

    Examples¶

    • Responsive Classroom (https://www.responsiveclassroom.org) — teacher language practices, including "positive imagining," that make high expectations explicit in daily interaction routines.
    • Zearn / Illustrative Mathematics classrooms — all students work the same grade-level tasks with differentiated supports, enacting high expectations structurally rather than rhetorically.
    • Rosenthal & Jacobson's Pygmalion study — teachers told that randomly selected students were "bloomers" showed gains in those students' measured achievement, demonstrating the expectation mechanism (and its ethical stakes).
