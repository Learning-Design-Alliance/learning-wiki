Complex Dynamic Systems Theory frames learner language as hierarchical, interdependent subsystems in dynamic relations over time¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 review), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article presents Complex Dynamic Systems Theory (CDST) as an explanatory framework for second language development in which "a system is composed of hierarchical, interdependent subcomponents (learner, learner language, and environment) in dynamic relations". Growth is conceptualized through van Geert's cognitive growth model, an ecological metaphor in which language components are growers dependent on limited internal and external resources. Because resources are limited and learners cannot prioritize all aspects of performance equally, growth in one subsystem can affect others, producing variability that is inherent to a complex dynamic system.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Longitudinal, process-oriented observation of learners at different time periods, as the CDST studies the review describes adopt

Constraints¶

  • The article notes variability and non-linear trajectories are inherent, so development cannot be read as uniform progress

Target Learners¶

  • second language learners

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • second language development across interrelated subsystems such as vocabulary, grammar, complexity, accuracy and fluency

Claims¶

  • Review identifies gaps: CDST studies isolate learner from environment and define what constitutes a system vaguely [+W]
  • Review reports a precursor relationship in which lexical growth suppressed grammatical growth until a threshold was reached [+W]
  • Language subsystems may show supportive, competitive, or precursor relationships depending on available cognitive resources [+W]
  • Review reports syntactic complexity and accuracy shift from competitive to connected growers over time in Chinese learners of English [+W]
  • Review reports word and noun complexity were connected growers while sentence and noun complexity competed in a Dutch learner of Finnish [+W]
