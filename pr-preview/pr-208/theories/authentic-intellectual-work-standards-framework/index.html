Authentic intellectual work framework: construction of knowledge, elaborated written communication, and connection to student lives¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 8 claims (8 for) · 2 studies (2 associational), q2 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The manual's research strand rests on an intellectual foundation drawn from Fred Newmann and Gary Wehlage's work on authentic achievement. It defines a target of student learning that "includes and goes beyond the acquisition of basic knowledge and skills" toward analyzing and solving real-world problems and communicating ideas in elaborated ways. Rubrics operationalize this as three standards per subject: "construction of knowledge," "elaborated written communication," and "connection to student lives," each scored 1 to 3 or 1 to 4, with slightly different definitions for assignments versus student work and by grade and subject.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Scoring requires subject-specific rubrics whose standards are, by design, applied differently by grade and subject

Constraints¶

  • The framework was applied to written assignments and written student work in mathematics and language arts/English in a purposive sample of Chicago Annenberg schools

Target Learners¶

  • Grade 3, 6, 8, 9, and 10 students in Chicago public elementary and high schools

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Deep subject-matter understanding, authentic intellectual work, and elaborated communication of ideas

Claims¶

  • Authentic Assignments Yield Authentic Student Work [+M]
  • Challenging Assignments Linked To Test Gains [+M]
  • Students showed more authentic intellectual work in writing than in mathematics [+W]
  • Chicago classrooms with high-quality authentic intellectual assignments showed greater one-year ITBS gains than classrooms with low-quality assignments [+W]
  • Exposure to high-quality assignments predicted substantially higher IGAP reading, mathematics, and writing performance (effect sizes 0.43, 0.64, 0.52) [+W]
  • Both high- and low-prior-achieving students benefited from exposure to high-quality assignments [+W]
  • Both high- and low-prior-achieving students benefited from high-quality assignments, with math favoring low achievers and reading favoring high achievers [+W]
  • Classroom composition (prior achievement, race, socioeconomic status, gender) showed virtually no relationship to exposure to high-quality assignments [+W]
  • Assignment quality improved between 1997 and 1999, yet math assignment challenge remained very low [+W]
