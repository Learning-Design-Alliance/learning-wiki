Primary Language Support with Bilingual Texts¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy incorporates texts and resources in a learner's primary (L1) language — bilingual picture books, dual-language editions, and multilingual digital libraries — to scaffold comprehension of new content while the learner develops proficiency in the target language. It rests on the linguistic interdependence hypothesis: skills and knowledge acquired in the L1 transfer to the second language, so continued L1 literacy development supports rather than competes with L2 acquisition Cummins' interdependence hypothesis. [+M]. Beyond comprehension, bilingual texts validate learners' cultural and linguistic identities, supporting engagement and belonging.

Design Implications¶

Bilingual texts let multilingual learners access grade-level concepts through their stronger language while simultaneously building target-language vocabulary and structures, rather than being limited to texts matched to their current L2 proficiency Vocabulary instruction for English learners benefits from L1 supports. [+M]. The strategy works best when L1 and L2 texts are deliberately connected — previewing a concept in the L1, then encountering it in the L2 — rather than offered as parallel, unlinked options.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to high-quality bilingual or dual-language books (classroom libraries, digital collections such as the International Digital Children's Library or Unite for Literacy)
  • Knowledge of learners' home languages and literacy levels in those languages
  • Instructional routines that explicitly bridge the two languages — e.g., Activating Prior Knowledge in the L1 before L2 reading, or Annotating cognates and key terms in both languages
  • An asset-oriented classroom climate where home-language use is welcomed, not merely tolerated

Constraints¶

  • Availability of bilingual materials is uneven; low-incidence languages are often poorly served, and translations vary widely in quality and cultural authenticity [-M]
  • Poorly designed bilingual texts (literal translations, culturally incongruent illustrations) can misrepresent content or stigmatize home languages [-W]
  • If L1 texts are used only as a crutch for content access without any bridge to L2 literacy, transfer of vocabulary and structures is weaker [~M]
  • Over-reliance on translated materials can reduce learners' exposure to comprehensible target-language input, slowing L2 development [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Paired texts: the same book in two editions, read first in L1 then in L2
  • Embedded bilingual format: side-by-side or interlinear dual-language texts
  • Preview–View–Review: content previewed in L1, taught in L2, reviewed in L1
  • Digital audio support: Audiobooks in the L1 paired with L2 print to decouple decoding from comprehension
  • Family engagement: sending bilingual books home extends L1 literacy development beyond the classroom

Target Learners¶

  • Newly arrived immigrants and newcomers with strong L1 literacy but limited L2 proficiency L1 literacy skills transfer to second-language reading. [+M]
  • Emergent bilinguals in early grades, where bilingual picture books support Dialogic Reading-style shared reading at home [+M]
  • Learners at risk of subtractive language shift, where continued L1 literacy protects long-term academic outcomes [~M]
  • Less necessary for learners already proficient in the target language, for whom L1 texts may add little [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Content-area comprehension: accessing science, social studies, and math concepts at grade level before L2 proficiency catches up
  • L2 vocabulary and literacy development through cross-linguistic transfer of cognates and comprehension strategies
  • Identity and belonging: seeing one's language and culture represented in the curriculum Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Inventory learners' home languages and L1 literacy levels (family survey, Check-In conversations).
  2. Curate bilingual texts matched to content units and learner interests; verify translation quality and cultural authenticity.
  3. Preview key concepts and vocabulary in the L1 before L2 instruction (Activating Prior Knowledge, Advance Organizers).
  4. During L2 reading, support cross-linguistic connections through Annotating cognates and bilingual glossaries.
  5. Review and consolidate in the L1, then bridge to L2 output through Class Discussion or Act It Out retellings.
  6. Assess comprehension in whichever language reveals what learners know, not only in the target language (Accommodations).

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — L1 preview activates existing schemas that L2 texts can then build on
  • Accessible Vocabulary and Syntax — bilingual glossaries and cognate instruction complement simplified L2 text
  • Dialogic Reading — bilingual shared reading at home extends the strategy to families
  • Teach in students' home language in primary, assess learning levels and differentiate instruction, and align teacher training with language-of-instruction policy

Related Elements¶

  • Assigned Readings — bilingual editions can serve as the assigned text with L2 follow-up
  • Audiobooks — L1 audio paired with L2 print decouples decoding from comprehension
  • Annotating — marking cognates and dual-language notes makes cross-linguistic transfer explicit
  • Accommodations — L1 assessment responses are an accommodation this strategy enables

Tools¶

  • International Digital Children's Library — free digital picture books in dozens of languages
  • Unite for Literacy — narrated picture books with audio in many home languages
  • Reading A–Z / Raz-Kids — leveled texts with Spanish and other language versions

Examples¶

  • Preview–View–Review with bilingual science texts: a newcomer reads a bilingual picture book on the water cycle in Spanish before the English-language science lesson, then reviews key vocabulary in Spanish afterward — accessing grade-level content while building English science vocabulary.
  • Dual-language classroom libraries: programs such as dual-language immersion classrooms maintain parallel L1/L2 libraries and schedule independent reading in both languages, protecting L1 literacy growth during L2 development.
  • Family literacy take-home kits: schools send bilingual books home with dialogic-reading prompts in the home language, leveraging parents' stronger language for shared reading.
