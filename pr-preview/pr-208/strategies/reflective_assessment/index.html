Reflective Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Reflective assessment treats assessment as a learning activity rather than a terminal judgment: learners examine their own progress over time, articulate what they have learned and how, and grades are positioned as information about growth rather than rewards or sanctions. The instructor designs assessment events that require students to compare current performance against prior performance and against explicit criteria, and uses the results to adjust instruction. This aligns with the broader shift from an assessment of learning culture to an assessment for learning culture Assessment for Learning [+S].

Design Implications¶

Reflective assessment works because self-monitoring and self-evaluation are themselves trainable components of self-regulated learning; students who regularly appraise their own work against criteria show stronger self-regulation and achievement Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]. Feedback delivered as part of reflective cycles is most effective when it addresses the task and the process rather than the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. The grade, when given, should communicate progress against criteria — not rank students or punish early effort.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear, stable criteria or rubrics that students can apply to their own and peers' work (Assessment)
  • Structured opportunities for students to review prior work and document change over time (portfolios, progress logs, Check-ins)
  • Feedback routines that prompt a learner response — revision, re-attempt, or a written reflection — rather than passive receipt (Assess Performance)
  • Instructor willingness to treat early low performance as diagnostic information, not a final verdict

Constraints¶

  • Grading schemes that average early failures into final grades undermine the approach: students rationally stop treating feedback as useful once the grade is already 'spent' [-M]
  • Self-assessment without training or criteria produces inflated, unreliable judgments; accuracy develops only with practice against explicit standards [~M]
  • Overly frequent reflective writing becomes ritual compliance — reflection prompts must connect to specific work samples, not generic feelings [-W]
  • High-stakes accountability contexts can conflict with growth-oriented grading, requiring careful communication with stakeholders about how proficiency and progress are reported

Implementation Variability¶

  • Portfolio models — collections of work over a term with student-written cover reflections arguing for growth against the rubric
  • Revision-based grading — the most recent, revised version of work counts, making improvement itself the graded object
  • Self- and peer-assessment cycles — students score work against rubrics before instructor review, then compare judgments to calibrate their standards
  • Learning journals / exam wrappers — brief structured reflections after major assessments asking what strategies worked and what to change next time
  • Multimodal evidence — allowing visual, oral, or performed demonstrations of learning so that language load does not mask content knowledge, particularly for multilingual learners

Target Learners¶

  • Students who have experienced failure under traditional grading and have learned to ignore feedback; reflective structures rebuild the link between effort, strategy, and outcome Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Multilingual learners, whose content knowledge may exceed what a single written, English-only assessment reveals; multiple modalities of demonstration reduce this measurement bias [+W]
  • Adolescents and adults developing self-regulation; younger learners need heavily scaffolded, criteria-anchored reflection rather than open self-evaluation [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive goals: accurate self-evaluation, strategy selection, and monitoring of one's own understanding Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Long-term skill development where growth over time, not single-point performance, is the meaningful outcome
  • Dispositional goals: ownership of learning, persistence, and healthy response to critique

Instructions¶

  1. Establish criteria first: co-construct or clearly present rubrics before any assessed work, so students know what quality looks like (Assessment)
  2. Collect baseline work early in the instructional cycle without attaching a summative grade to it
  3. After each major task, run a structured reflection: students compare the new work to the baseline and to the rubric, identifying one strength and one specific change (Check-ins)
  4. Deliver feedback at the task and process level, and require a learner action in response — revision, re-attempt, or a written plan Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  5. Grade the trajectory: base summative grades on the most recent evidence of learning, and report progress separately from achievement where the reporting system allows
  6. Periodically have students write a meta-reflection on their strategies across tasks, feeding forward into goal-setting for the next unit

Examples¶

  • Exam wrappers (e.g., used widely in physics and calculus courses per the Carl Wieman Science Education Initiative, https://www.cwsei.ubc.ca) — short post-exam worksheets asking students to categorize their errors and name a strategy change before the next exam
  • Writing portfolio assessment — students select and annotate representative drafts across a term, with a cover letter arguing for their growth against the rubric
  • Standards-based grading in K-12 districts (e.g., Marzano's scale-based models) — report cards communicate proficiency levels per standard with reassessment allowed, decoupling grades from one-shot performance
