Let Students Know What Order They Will Be Called On¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (3 review, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 0 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

The instructor announces — at the start of a discussion or before each round — the sequence in which students will be called on (e.g., alphabetical, by seating order, by a posted roster, or by a shuffled deck shown to the class). This converts unpredictable cold-calling into a predictable turn-taking structure. Students who need more processing time, who experience discussion anxiety, or who struggle with inhibitory control can rehearse or draft a response and hold it until their turn, rather than either blurting out or disengaging.

Design Implications¶

Predictability reduces the cognitive and emotional load of participation: when students are not vigilantly monitoring whether they will be called on, attention can be directed to what is being discussed [~M]. Advance notice of one's turn functions as a brief planning window, which supports more elaborated spoken responses and lowers the arousal associated with public performance [~W]. The strategy trades the accountability benefits of surprise cold-calling — which keeps all students mentally engaged because anyone might be next [~M] — for the accessibility benefits of predictability; instructors should choose deliberately between these trade-offs rather than assuming one is superior.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A visible, fair, and stable ordering mechanism (posted roster, numbered cards, seating chart, shuffled index cards shown face-up)
  • Enough wait time between naming a student and expecting an answer (typically 3+ seconds) for the planning benefit to materialize
  • A norm that the announced order is honored — breaking it destroys the predictability that makes the strategy work
  • An alternative participation channel for students for whom speaking in turn remains a barrier (Accommodations)

Constraints¶

  • Once students know their position in the order, those late in the sequence may stop attending after their turn passes [-W] — mitigate by cycling through the order multiple times or re-shuffling visibly between rounds
  • Removes the "everyone must be ready" pressure that makes cold-calling effective for sustaining whole-class engagement [~M]; in large seminars relying on cold-calling for accountability, announcing the order can reduce preparation rates
  • Predictability does not remove speaking anxiety for all students; some students with social anxiety report that a known turn produces anticipatory dread for the entire class period [-W]
  • If the order tracks a visible attribute (e.g., seating), students may perceive bias or labeling

Implementation Variability¶

  • Announced random order: shuffle cards publicly each round — predictable within a round, unpredictable across rounds
  • Opt-in advance notice: students may request knowing their turn in advance (a private accommodation) while the default remains random
  • Order within small groups: apply the strategy at table level rather than whole-class level, lowering the audience size
  • Written-first variants: students draft responses during the round before their turn (Think-Pair-Share hybrid)

Target Learners¶

  • Students with inhibitory control or impulsivity challenges, who benefit from an external structure that makes waiting purposeful rather than effortful [~W]
  • Students with discussion anxiety or slower processing speed, who gain planning time before public performance
  • English language learners, who can rehearse phrasing before speaking
  • Less beneficial for students already confident speaking extemporaneously, for whom the notice adds little [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Participation and engagement in discussion-based objectives (Class Discussion)
  • Self-regulation: practicing response planning and turn-taking Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Oral elaboration quality: longer, more considered contributions when planning time precedes speaking [~W]

Instructions¶

  1. Choose and publicize the ordering mechanism (roster, cards, seating) and explain why — that predictability is an access measure, not a surveillance tool.
  2. Open the discussion round by announcing the order or displaying it (Advance Organizers can serve as the visible structure).
  3. Pose the question to the whole group first, so all students prepare even though only one will answer.
  4. When calling on a student, allow extended wait time before prompting; resist rescuing too quickly (Coaching cues rather than answers).
  5. After each student's turn, briefly affirm the contribution to sustain self-efficacy [+M] and keep late-order students engaged.
  6. Re-shuffle or rotate visibly between rounds so no student is permanently "last."

Related Strategies¶

  • Cold-calling with random selection — the accountability-oriented counterpart; announcing the order is a deliberate softening of it
  • Wait time extension — the planning window only pays off if the instructor actually pauses after naming the student
  • Talk tokens or equity sticks — alternative turn-management structures serving the same access goal

Examples¶

  • Harvard Business School-style cold-calling, modified: some case-method instructors post the day's calling sequence on the board before class so students can prepare their case analysis for their slot — retaining accountability while adding preparation time.
  • Equity sticks with public draw: an elementary teacher draws name sticks in view of the class and lays each drawn stick face-up in a row, so students can see exactly who is next.
  • CAST UDL Guidelines (Representation & Action/Expression): advance notice of turn order exemplifies UDL's guidance on making expectations and timing predictable to reduce threat and distraction (https://udlguidelines.cast.org).
