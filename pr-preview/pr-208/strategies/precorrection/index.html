Precorrection¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Precorrection is a low-intensity behavioral strategy in which the teacher anticipates a predictable problem situation and intervenes before it occurs — stating the expectation, prompting the desired behavior, and often having students rehearse it. It shifts behavior management from reactive correction to proactive support, and is typically delivered in the natural setting in under a minute.

Design Implications¶

Precorrection works because it makes the expected behavior salient and rehearsed at the moment of highest risk, reducing reliance on punishment after errors [~M]. It is grounded in Behaviorism and applied behavior analysis: antecedent manipulation is generally more efficient than consequence-based correction because it prevents the error chain from starting [~M]. Effective precorrection follows a consistent sequence — identify the predictable context, specify the expected behavior, provide a rationale, rehearse, and deliver a prompt just before the situation — and pairs naturally with specific Praise when students perform the behavior.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Identification of the specific, predictable trigger context (transition, independent work, assembly, first attempt at hard material)
  • A positively stated, observable expectation ("Walk" not "Don't run")
  • Brief behavioral rehearsal or verbal acknowledgment by students, not just teacher statement
  • A delivery point immediately before the risk moment, not at the start of a lesson
  • Follow-up reinforcement when students meet the expectation

Constraints¶

  • Effects fade quickly without reinforcement; precorrection delivered once does not establish durable behavior [-W]
  • Overused or delivered as a lecture, it loses salience and can become background noise students tune out [-W]
  • Less effective when the "problem behavior" actually reflects a skill deficit rather than a performance deficit — students who cannot perform the expectation need instruction, not prompting [~M]
  • Requires accurate prediction of triggers; misidentified contexts waste instructional time and miss the actual antecedent [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class preteaching: brief precorrection before transitions or assemblies for all students
  • Individual precorrection: a private prompt or pre-arranged signal for a student with a specific behavior plan
  • Precorrection with rehearsal: students role-play the expected behavior (e.g., practice lining up) — stronger for new or complex routines
  • Self-precorrection: students self-prompt using a checklist before independent work, building toward self-regulation

Target Learners¶

  • Students with persistent, context-specific challenging behavior, including those with emotional/behavioral disorders [~M]
  • Young learners still acquiring classroom routines and self-regulation
  • Whole classes during high-risk routines (transitions, unstructured time) — precorrection is preventive and non-stigmatizing when delivered universally

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and procedural routines: transitions, materials management, participation norms
  • Self-regulation: anticipating difficulty and pre-loading a coping or work-start strategy
  • Social behavior: entering groups, taking turns, responding to frustration

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the predictable context. Use observation or Check-In data to find when and where the behavior reliably occurs.
  2. Define the expected behavior. State it positively, specifically, and observably.
  3. Give a brief rationale. One sentence on why the behavior matters.
  4. Rehearse. Have students demonstrate or verbalize the behavior (Act It Out); rehearsal outperforms statement alone [~W].
  5. Prompt immediately before the context. Deliver the precorrection in the moment — at the door before the transition, before handing out independent work.
  6. Reinforce. Deliver specific praise or acknowledgment when the expected behavior occurs; without this, precorrection effects decay rapidly.

Related Strategies¶

  • High-Probability Request Sequences — another antecedent strategy that builds behavioral momentum before a low-probability demand
  • Praise — the consequence-side partner; precorrection sets up what to reinforce
  • Routines — precorrection teaches and maintains the routines themselves
  • Choral Responding — a structure that reduces the problem behavior precorrection would otherwise need to address

Examples¶

  • Transition precorrection: Before dismissal to group work, a teacher states, "When you get to your group, sit down and start with the first question. Show me starting right away." Students rehearse the first move, then the teacher reinforces groups that begin within 30 seconds.
  • PBIS — Precorrection is a core Tier 1 and Tier 2 practice in Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports, used before assemblies, hallway transitions, and cafeteria routines.
  • Individual plan: A student who escalates during independent writing receives a private precorrection and a pre-taught "first step" card before writing begins, plus a check-in after five minutes.
