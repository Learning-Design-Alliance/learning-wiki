Annotating Texts¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 6 studies (3 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Annotating texts is a reading strategy in which learners actively mark up a text while reading — highlighting or underlining key ideas, writing margin notes, coding with symbols, posing questions, or summarizing passages in their own words. It converts passive reading into generative processing: the reader must decide what matters, why it matters, and how it connects to prior knowledge. Annotation can be learner-generated or instructor-provided (e.g., pre-annotated exemplars or embedded prompts), and ranges from simple marking to structured schemes (claim/evidence/question codes).

Design Implications¶

Annotation works because it forces selection and organization of ideas rather than verbatim rereading, but its effectiveness depends on how learners annotate: marking alone is weak, while annotation that requires elaboration (paraphrasing, questioning, connecting) is strong Ponce et al. (2020) meta-analysis [+M]. Instructor-provided highlighting can help novices by modeling what experts select, but learners who construct their own annotations generally show deeper comprehension than those given pre-made ones Learner-constructed graphic organizers outperform provided ones. [+M]. Underlining and highlighting as practiced by students is among the least effective study techniques when used without elaboration Dunlosky et al. (2013) [-S] — the marking itself must be paired with generative written response to pay off.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A text worth annotating: dense enough that selection matters, and reusable (print, PDF, or annotation-capable platform)
  • An explicit annotation scheme or rubric, especially for novices — what to mark, how many marks, and what written notes should do
  • Instruction or modeling of expert annotation before independent use (Think-Aloud demonstration of an expert marking a passage)
  • A follow-up use for the annotations (discussion, writing, exam prep) so learners see them as functional, not decorative

Constraints¶

  • Untrained highlighting is near-useless or harmful: learners mark too much, confuse emphasis with understanding, and reread highlighted text instead of self-testing Dunlosky et al. (2013) [-S]
  • Over-marking eliminates the selection benefit — if everything is highlighted, nothing is Experimenter underlining can be effective when selective, but student underlining often is not. [~M]
  • Annotation adds cognitive load for novices who lack the background to judge relevance; without a scheme, marking competes with comprehension Chunking reduces working memory load. [~M]
  • Copying or paraphrasing annotations from provided notes without engaging the text produces shallow processing [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured codes: claim/evidence/question or +/!/ ? schemes that standardize what marks mean
  • Margin summarization: one-sentence gist per paragraph or section, a generative form with stronger evidence than marking alone [+M]
  • Instructor-provided annotation: pre-highlighted or annotated exemplars as models, faded over time
  • Social annotation: shared annotation layers (e.g., Perusall, Hypothesis) where learners read and respond to each other's margin notes
  • Digital annotation: tools like Hypothesis, Perusall, and Kindle notes enable search, aggregation, and reuse of marks

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate readers who can decode fluently but need to learn disciplinary selection criteria; complete novices need a provided scheme first [~M]
  • Students preparing for writing or discussion tasks, where annotations serve as raw material
  • Less beneficial for beginning readers, for whom annotation competes with decoding for limited working-memory resources [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension and retention of expository text: identifying main ideas and supporting detail [+M]
  • Disciplinary reading practices: learning what experts in a field notice and question
  • Preparation for synthesis tasks: annotations as scaffolding for essays, Class Discussion, and Argument Construction

Instructions¶

  1. Model the practice: annotate a short passage on a shared display, thinking aloud about selection criteria (Think-Aloud).
  2. Provide a simple scheme (e.g., underline = main idea; margin ? = confusion; margin ! = surprising or important) and a quota (e.g., no more than 10–15% marked) to force selection.
  3. Require a generative component: a margin paraphrase, question, or connection for each marked passage — marking alone is insufficient [-S].
  4. Have learners use the annotations within 24–48 hours for a discussion, summary, or self-quiz, converting marks into retrieval practice.
  5. Fade the scheme: shift from instructor-specified codes to learner-designed annotation as expertise grows, and compare learner annotations against an expert-annotated exemplar to calibrate.

Related Strategies¶

  • Close Reading — annotation is the primary tool of close reading; the scheme here supplies its method
  • Self-Questioning — margin questions are annotation in its most generative form
  • Summarization — margin gists are micro-summaries; both force selection and restatement
  • Retrieval Practice — annotations become prompts for later self-testing, which is where retention gains materialize

Examples¶

  • Perusall (https://www.perusall.com) — social annotation platform where students annotate course readings collaboratively and AI-scored annotation quality feeds into grading; used widely in flipped and large-enrollment courses.
  • Hypothesis (https://web.hypothes.is) — open web annotation layer used in LMS-integrated reading assignments, enabling instructor-seeded annotation prompts.
  • AP/IB disciplinary reading protocols — history and English courses using claim/evidence/question annotation codes on primary sources before seminar discussion.
  • Reading Apprenticeship (https://readingapprenticeship.org) — WestEd professional-development framework that trains teachers to model metacognitive text annotation across disciplines.
