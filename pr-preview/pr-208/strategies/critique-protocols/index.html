Critique Protocols¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q3 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Critique protocols are structured routines — often with scripted sentence stems, timed rounds, and defined roles — that govern how learners examine one another's drafts or performances and offer feedback. Well-known formulations include Ron Berger's norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and the Critical Friends protocol suite from the National School Reform Faculty. The protocol, not the teacher's discretion, carries the structure: it specifies what is looked at, in what order, and what kind of comments are permitted.

Design Implications¶

Structured critique converts the diffuse social risk of "peer feedback" into a bounded, low-threat routine, which increases both participation and the specificity of comments Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when structured with clear criteria. [+M]. Because learners must analyze someone else's work against criteria, the protocol doubles as an Assessment learning activity: evaluating examples builds the evaluative judgment learners then apply to their own work. Critique also leverages Argumentation — good feedback requires stating a claim about the work and supporting it with evidence from the work itself Argumentation improves reasoning quality. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A draft or incomplete work product — critique targets improvable work, not finished pieces
  • Explicit criteria or a rubric so comments anchor to qualities of the work, not personal taste
  • A protocol script: norms, sentence stems, time limits, and turn-taking structure
  • Time built in for revision after critique; feedback without a revision cycle is inert Feedback must be acted on to affect learning. [+M]
  • A visible model of quality work (exemplar or Non-Examples) so reviewers share a standard
  • Clear criteria or a rubric that the critique references
  • Work-in-progress rather than finished work, so feedback can still change the outcome
  • A taught protocol with sentence stems and time limits, rehearsed before it carries stakes

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured peer feedback ("I liked it") produces negligible learning gains; without criteria and stems, critique degrades into social pleasantries Checklist-style evaluation without criteria is ineffective in online settings. [-M]
  • Critique before learners have enough domain knowledge to judge quality yields confident but inaccurate feedback [~M] — early-stage novices need models of quality first
  • Public critique can threaten belonging for anxious or minoritized learners unless norms are actively enforced and drafts are explicitly framed as unfinished
  • If the same few students always present, the protocol becomes performance rather than feedback
  • Low-trust classrooms suppress honest critique; learners hedge to protect relationships, and feedback quality drops [-M]
  • Novice reviewers can transmit misconceptions; instructor moderation or spot-checking is needed in early iterations [-W]
  • Critique of highly subjective or idiosyncratic work (personal narrative, artistic voice) can flatten expression if criteria over-specify style [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Gallery critique: work is posted; peers leave written comments on sticky notes (low social risk, asynchronous)
  • Author-driven rounds: the presenter asks the group a specific question ("Does my evidence support my claim?"), focusing attention
  • Warm/cool feedback: timed rounds of affirmations then suggestions, common in design studio practice
  • Teacher-modeled critique: the instructor critiques a sample (or their own) work first, demonstrating the moves before students try (Think-Aloud style)
  • Gallery critique: whole-group examination of one work, modeled by the instructor (Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" routine)
  • Critical friends / tuning protocols: small groups follow timed steps — presenter describes, group questions, group discusses while presenter listens, presenter responds
  • Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise–Question–Suggest: lightweight stems for younger or newer critics
  • Studio walk / design crit: rotating stations with written sticky-note feedback, common in art and design education
  • Asynchronous critique: structured comment threads in platforms like Peergrade or Perusall, using the same stems

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have seen models of quality but not yet internalized criteria [~M]
  • Learners in iterative production contexts: writing, design, code, studio arts, research
  • Less suitable for absolute novices, who lack the evaluative schema to say anything useful; pair with Comparing Cases or worked models first
  • Learners old enough to articulate observations against criteria (upper elementary through adult) Peer assessment works across grade levels but needs more scaffolding for younger students. [+M]
  • Learners producing iterative artifacts (writing, design, code, proofs) where revision is expected
  • Reviewers benefit even when their own work is not critiqued — judging others' work builds evaluative judgment [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing what makes work good, not just producing work Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+M]
  • Revision as a habit: normalizing iteration and treating drafts as improvable
  • Communication: articulating specific, evidence-based observations Argumentation improves reasoning quality. [+M]
  • Quality criteria internalization: understanding what makes work good, not just producing work
  • Revision and iteration: treating drafts as improvable Authentic audiences and iterative critique improve the quality of student work. [+M]
  • Evaluative judgment and self-assessment: the capacity to critique one's own work independently

Instructions¶

  1. Establish norms with students: kind, specific, helpful; critique the work, never the person.
  2. Share exemplars and non-exemplars and critique one together as a whole group, modeling the language (Non-Examples).
  3. Distribute drafts and the criteria or rubric; have authors pose a specific question about their work.
  4. Run the protocol: timed observation, then structured feedback using sentence stems ("I noticed…", "Have you considered…").
  5. Authors respond with clarifying questions only — no defending — then plan revisions.
  6. Build in time to revise and, ideally, a second critique round to close the loop.

Related Strategies¶

  • Gallery Walk — a common low-stakes vehicle for written critique rounds
  • Peer Review — the formalized, often rubric-scored version used in writing instruction
  • Studio Critique — the art-and-design tradition from which most protocols derive
  • Peer Feedback — the broader practice; critique protocols are the structured form that makes it reliable
  • Revision Cycles — critique is the feedback event inside an iteration loop
  • Rubric Design — supplies the criteria that anchor critique

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Collaborative Evaluation — critique protocols operationalize peer evaluation with structure
  • Project-Based Learning — critique and revision cycles are a core PBL design feature
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — critique enacts the "articulation" and "reflection" methods

Examples¶

EL Education (formerly Expeditionary Learning) — EL Education's curriculum embeds Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" critique and multiple revision drafts as a standard literacy routine; students critique models before drafting.

High Tech High (San Diego) — project-based school where public critique of drafts is a routine before exhibition; protocols are taught explicitly in advisory.

Design studio education (architecture, product design) — the traditional "crit" is a critique protocol in institutionalized form; recent practice adds written and round-robin variants to reduce the well-documented harshness of open-floor critique. - Ron Berger's EL Education critique lessons (eleducation.org) — the "Austin's Butterfly" routine: kindergarten students give kind, specific, helpful feedback across multiple drafts of a scientific drawing, demonstrating critique with young learners. - Harvard Project Zero "Tuning Protocol" (pz.harvard.edu) — a timed critical-friends structure for examining student or adult work in professional learning communities. - Design studio crits in architecture and art education — the original model: public examination of work-in-progress against disciplinary standards, with the instructor moderating. - Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — asynchronous structured peer review with rubric-anchored comment prompts, used in MOOCs and university courses at scale.
