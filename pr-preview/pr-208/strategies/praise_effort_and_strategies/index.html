Praise Effort and Strategies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

When learners succeed, praise their efforts and strategies rather than their intelligence. Praising intelligence can undermine motivation and performance, as learners may come to view intelligence as a fixed trait; in the face of failure, they may display less task persistence, less task enjoyment, and worse performance [Mueller & Dweck's experiments showed person-praise led to less persistence after setbacks than process-praise]. Effective praise is specific, identifies the strategy or effort that produced the outcome, and orients learners toward controllable causes of success.

Design Implications¶

Praise functions as attributional feedback: it shapes what learners believe caused their success, which in turn governs how they respond to difficulty. Process-focused praise supports incremental beliefs about ability, while person-focused praise ("you're so smart") encourages entity beliefs and fragile, validation-dependent motivation [-M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Instructors must observe learners' actual processes and strategies closely enough to praise something specific and true — generic enthusiasm ("good job!") does not carry the attributional signal
  • Praise should reference controllable, specific behavior: the strategy chosen, the persistence shown, the revision made
  • A learning environment where struggle is normalized, so effort-praise does not read as a signal of low ability [~M] — learners can interpret "praised for effort" as code for "not smart" when praise is delivered only after failure

Constraints¶

  • Generic or inflated praise ("you're brilliant at this!") can backfire, promoting avoidance of challenge and cheating in some learners [-M]
  • Effort-praise alone is insufficient if the effort is unproductive; praising futile struggle reinforces ineffective strategies [~M] — praise should target effective strategy use, not effort per se
  • Effects on academic achievement are smaller and less consistent than effects on motivation and mindset; mindset interventions alone rarely raise grades without accompanying instructional change [~W]
  • In cultures or classrooms where effort is already highly emphasized, additional effort-praise may add little [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Verbal praise in one-on-one, small-group, or whole-class settings; written feedback on work serves the same attributional function
  • Written praise on assignments can be systematized in feedback rubrics that name the strategy used
  • Digital environments can deliver contingent praise messages after productive persistence or strategy use, though automated praise risks feeling hollow if not tied to specific behavior [~W]

Target Learners¶

  • All learner levels, especially those sensitive to failure or who attribute success to innate ability rather than effort
  • Young children are particularly susceptible to person-praise effects; even subtle wording ("you are a good drawer" vs. "you did a good job drawing") shifts persistence and enjoyment in preschoolers [-M]
  • Learners who have experienced repeated failure benefit from feedback that re-attributes outcomes to controllable causes

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Promote a growth mindset and incremental beliefs about ability
  • Increase task persistence and enjoyment in the face of difficulty
  • Build metacognitive awareness of which strategies produce success, complementing Self-Regulated Learning

Instructions¶

  1. Observe the learner's process closely enough to identify a specific strategy or effort worth naming.
  2. Deliver praise that names the controllable cause: "You tried breaking the problem into smaller parts — that's what made it work."
  3. Avoid person-praise ("you're so smart"), especially after easy successes.
  4. When effort was unproductive, redirect rather than praise: acknowledge effort, then coach a better strategy.
  5. Pair praise with actionable next steps, consistent with Action-Oriented Feedback and Assessment for Learning.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — process-praise is a form of feedback that points to what to do next
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — both strategies make the process of learning visible and valued rather than only outcomes

Examples¶

  • Mueller & Dweck (1998) studies — Sixth graders praised for effort after a task subsequently chose more challenging problems and persisted longer after failure than those praised for intelligence.
  • Khan Academy — Mastery-based progress messaging rewards persistence and strategy use ("you stuck with it") rather than labeling learners as talented.
  • Elementary writing conferences — A teacher praises a specific revision move ("your second draft adds evidence to your claim") rather than telling the student they are "a natural writer."
