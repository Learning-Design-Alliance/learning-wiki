Pre Reading Skimming Text¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 2 quant-synthesis), q2–q3 · 2 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Pre-reading skimming asks learners to move quickly through a text before reading it closely — scanning the title, headings, first sentences of paragraphs, figures, and summaries to build a skeletal map of its structure and content. The skim is deliberately shallow and fast (typically 1–3 minutes); the goal is orientation, not comprehension. It is usually paired with a brief prediction or question-setting step that converts the skim into an active plan for the close read.

Design Implications¶

Skimming works because it activates relevant prior knowledge and gives readers an advance organizer — a structure onto which incoming details can be attached during the close read Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]. A reader who knows the text's overall shape spends less working memory on figuring out "where am I?" and more on integrating content Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. The strategy is most effective when the skim ends with a generative step — predicting content, generating questions, or noting what seems unfamiliar — rather than ending at passive scanning Activation improves learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A text with visible structural signals (headings, topic sentences, figures, abstracts); skimming unstructured text yields little
  • A time limit that keeps the skim genuinely fast — extended "skimming" becomes inefficient reading
  • A follow-up prompt: predict, question, or list what to look for in the close read
  • Instruction in how to skim (what to look at, in what order) — the skill is not self-evident to novice readers

Constraints¶

  • Skimming a text with no discernible structure (dense narrative, unfamiliar technical prose) provides few retrieval cues and wastes time [-M]
  • Learners may mistake the gist gained from skimming for full understanding and skip the close read [~M] — the skim must be framed as preparation, not replacement
  • For texts where line-by-line reasoning matters (proofs, legal argument, poetry), skimming can implant a misleading first impression that biases the close read [~W]
  • Weak readers often skim poorly — they fixate on unknown words rather than structural signals — so the strategy requires modeling before independent use [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured skim sheet: learners record headings, one-line gist per section, and two questions before reading
  • Prediction skim: skim, then write a predicted main claim; verify during close reading
  • Vocabulary pre-scan: skim to flag unfamiliar terms, which are pre-taught before the close read
  • Reciprocal variant: in reciprocal teaching-style routines, skimming is one of several strategies students rotate through in groups

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate readers reading expository or academic text above their comfort level Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]
  • Learners with relevant prior knowledge to activate; readers with no background gain less from a structural map alone Activation improves learning. [~M]
  • Beginning readers and learners reading in a second language need explicit modeling of skimming before it helps [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension of extended expository text
  • Strategic reading: self-regulated selection of reading pace and focus
  • Text structure awareness: recognizing how academic texts organize information

Instructions¶

  1. Set a strict time limit (1–3 minutes) and state the purpose: build a map, don't comprehend.
  2. Model the skim aloud on a short text, showing what you look at and in what order (Think-Aloud if available; otherwise narrate directly).
  3. Learners skim independently: title, headings, first/last sentences of paragraphs, figures, summary.
  4. Learners record a one-sentence gist and two questions or predictions — this activates prior knowledge (Activating Prior Knowledge).
  5. Learners read the text closely, checking predictions and answering their own questions, marking key points (Annotating).
  6. Debrief: what did the skim predict correctly? What surprised you? This builds the skimming skill itself.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the skim's main cognitive payoff is activation; this strategy extends it
  • Annotating — the natural close-read follow-on, guided by questions generated during the skim
  • Advance Organizers — the skim functions as a self-generated organizer drawn from the text itself

Examples¶

  • SQ3R (Robinson, 1946) — the classic study routine whose first two steps (Survey, Question) are a structured pre-reading skim; still widely taught in academic study-skills courses.
  • Reading Apprenticeship (WestEd) — metacognitive reading routines in which students survey a text and talk through what they notice about its structure before reading. https://readingapprenticeship.org
  • Academic reading in EAP courses — English for Academic Purposes curricula routinely train skimming/scanning as a paired skill before assigning journal articles.
