Descriptive Feature Chart¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 10 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

A descriptive feature chart is a graphic organizer with horizontal and vertical axes used to develop a descriptive model of an organism. Rows typically represent feature categories (e.g., Body Parts, Number, Color and Shape, Position, Appearance) and columns represent organisms, specimens, or observation sessions. Students record observations in each cell, producing a structured data table that can then support comparison, classification, and written description. It is common in elementary science instruction, where it converts open-ended looking into systematic observation.

Design Implications¶

The chart works by imposing a category structure on observation, which reduces the working-memory demands of holding multiple perceptual details in mind at once Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Its deeper value emerges when the completed chart is used for comparison: contrasting feature profiles across organisms supports abstraction of shared and distinguishing characteristics Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]. The act of generating entries — rather than receiving a pre-filled chart — is what drives learning, consistent with generative accounts of learning from graphics [~S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A pre-designed chart with appropriate categories (e.g., Body Parts, Number, Color and Shape, Position, Appearance)
  • Observation materials: specimens, books, images, or magnifiers; pens and paper or digital tools
  • Teacher modeling of how to fill one row or cell before independent work (Coaching)
  • A follow-on task that uses the chart — a written description, a comparison, or a presentation — so the chart functions as a thinking tool rather than a worksheet

Constraints¶

  • Requires significant initial teacher guidance; young students often record irrelevant details or copy labels without observing [-W]
  • Filling in all cells can become time-consuming busywork that crowds out discussion and analysis [-W]
  • A fixed category scheme can constrain observation — students may stop noticing features that do not fit the rows [-W]
  • Pre-filled or teacher-completed charts substantially reduce the benefit; the generative effort is the active ingredient [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class chart on the board for shared observation, then individual or pair charts for a second organism
  • Partially completed charts (some cells filled) as a scaffold, faded over successive lessons
  • Digital versions with image insertion for learners who struggle with drawing or writing
  • Extended into comparison charts across multiple organisms, bridging toward classification and dichotomous keys

Target Learners¶

  • K–2 students building observational and descriptive language skills, adaptable through upper elementary grades
  • Visually oriented learners and emergent writers, for whom the chart offers a non-prose format for recording knowledge
  • English language learners, who benefit from the pairing of vocabulary labels with direct observation of referents [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Developing a descriptive model of an organism: structure, parts, and appearance
  • Organizing and recording observations systematically (Articulation of what is seen)
  • Comparing and classifying: using feature profiles across cases to identify similarities and differences Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  • Interpreting observations as data — an early step toward evidence-based claims

Instructions¶

  1. Select a target organism and prepare observation materials (specimen, images, books).
  2. Introduce the chart categories and model filling in one or two cells while thinking aloud, naming features and vocabulary (Coaching).
  3. Have students observe and complete their own charts individually, in pairs, or in small groups (Collaboration).
  4. Prompt students to explain their entries — why they classified a feature as they did — to deepen processing beyond transcription Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].
  5. Use the completed charts for comparison across organisms, then as an outline for a written description or class presentation (Assessment via accuracy, completeness, and quality of the derived description).

Related Strategies¶

  • Observation Journals — longer-form alternative that trades the chart's structure for narrative richness
  • Venn Diagram Comparison — a comparison-only organizer; the feature chart supplies the raw observations that feed it
  • Science Notebooks — embeds the chart within a broader record of investigation

Examples¶

  • K–2 students chart a butterfly's body parts, number, color and shape, position, and appearance, then use the chart to write and present a description. Resource: WETA, "Using Graphic Organizers in Literature-Based Science Instruction" (https://www.readingrockets.org)
  • Upper-elementary classes chart two or three local trees across seasons, then compare rows to identify features useful for identification — a bridge to dichotomous keys.
  • FOSS (Full Option Science System) modules (https://www.fossweb.com) use structured observation recording sheets of this kind in their life-science strands.
