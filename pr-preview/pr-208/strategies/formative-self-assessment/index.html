Formative Self Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Formative self assessment asks learners to judge the quality of their own work against explicit criteria — rubrics, checklists, exemplars, or success criteria — while the work is still in progress and revisable. The purpose is not grading but calibration: learners compare their current performance to a target, identify gaps, and act on them. It is carried out through structured activities such as rubric-guided drafts, self-checklists, confidence ratings, or comparing one's work to annotated exemplars.

Design Implications¶

Self assessment works when it functions as an act of Assessment for Learning — evidence gathered by the learner to inform their next step, not a verdict Formative assessment practices improve achievement. [+S]. Accuracy is the central design problem: unguided self-ratings are often inflated and weakly correlated with actual performance, so self assessment must be anchored to external referents such as rubrics and exemplars [~M]. Training and practice improve self-assessment accuracy over time, and accuracy in turn supports self-regulated learning Self-regulated learning theory [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit, learner-accessible success criteria (rubrics, checklists) developed or unpacked before the task
  • Exemplars at multiple quality levels so learners can calibrate their judgments (Comparing Cases)
  • Time and structure for revision — self assessment must be followed by an opportunity to act on the judgment (Practice)
  • Low-stakes framing so honest self-evaluation is not penalized

Constraints¶

  • Unanchored self-ratings ("How do you think you did?") produce inflated, uninformative judgments [~M]
  • Novices lack the knowledge to discriminate quality; without exemplars or criteria their self-assessments are unreliable [~M]
  • If self-assigned grades count toward marks, learners game the ratings and the formative function collapses [-M]
  • Self-assessment consumes time that may exceed its benefit for simple, well-specified tasks where external checks are cheaper

Implementation Variability¶

  • Rubric-guided self-scoring: learners score a draft against a rubric and write one revision goal
  • Exemplar comparison: learners rank their work against annotated samples before revising
  • Confidence-weighted judgments: learners rate confidence per item, not just correctness, surfacing illusions of knowing
  • Self- and peer-assessment pairs: peer feedback first provides an external anchor that improves subsequent self-judgments

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to perceive quality differences; pure novices need exemplars and modeling first [~M]
  • Learners developing self-regulation — self assessment is a core monitoring phase of the learning cycle Self-regulated learning theory [+M]
  • Less effective for learners who are highly anxious or grade-focused unless stakes are explicitly removed

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive monitoring: accurately judging one's own understanding and performance
  • Quality criteria internalization: learning what "good" looks like in a domain
  • Revision and iteration skills: closing the gap between current and target performance

Instructions¶

  1. Unpack the success criteria with learners before the task; co-constructing the rubric improves ownership and comprehension (Advance Organizers)
  2. Study annotated exemplars at several quality levels so learners can recognize the criteria in real work (Comparing Cases)
  3. Have learners produce a draft, then score it against the rubric, citing specific evidence for each judgment
  4. Require one concrete revision action per identified gap, then a second self-check after revision (Practice)
  5. Follow with Action-Oriented Feedback from the instructor or peers to correct persistent miscalibration

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — external feedback that corrects what self assessment misses
  • Check-Ins — lighter-weight monitoring of learner status during instruction

Examples¶

  • Portfolio assessment in writing workshops (e.g., Reading & Writing Project approaches): students score drafts against rubrics and revise before teacher review.
  • Khan Academy mastery practice: learners self-select when to retry skills, with confidence and correctness data surfaced to them.
  • Exam wrappers (widely used in postsecondary STEM, e.g., Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center): students complete a structured self-analysis of their exam preparation and errors, then set goals for the next assessment cycle.
