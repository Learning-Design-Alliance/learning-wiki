Ideal Problem Solving¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Ideal Problem Solving is a strategy built on the IDEAL heuristic (Bransford & Stein, 1984): Identify the problem, Define and represent it, Explore possible strategies, Act on a chosen strategy, and Look back to evaluate the result. Rather than teaching domain-specific procedures, it gives learners a transferable framework for approaching ill-structured and novel problems. It is typically carried out through explicit modeling of each phase, guided practice with prompts, and gradual withdrawal of the scaffold.

Design Implications¶

The strategy operationalizes research on expert–novice differences: experts spend proportionally more time representing a problem before acting, while novices jump immediately to computation [~M]. Teaching the IDEAL steps explicitly slows novices down at the representation phase, where most solution errors originate. The "Look back" phase is the most frequently skipped and the most valuable for transfer, because it prompts comparison of the solved problem to other problem types Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Problems rich enough that the Identify and Define phases are non-trivial (ill-structured or multi-step tasks work better than single-step exercises)
  • Prompts or graphic organizers that cue each IDEAL phase during early attempts
  • Instructor modeling of the full cycle, including revising a failed approach during Look back
  • Multiple problems from the same problem class so learners can practice the cycle repeatedly

Constraints¶

  • General heuristics show weak transfer when taught in isolation from domain knowledge; learners need content knowledge to Explore meaningfully [-M]
  • Overhead of the full cycle can exceed its benefit for simple, well-practiced problem types, where it adds cognitive load without improving solutions [-M]
  • Learners may treat the steps as a ritual checklist rather than genuine analysis, especially if the phases are assessed only for completion [-W]
  • Less effective for learners who already have strong domain-specific strategies, for whom the general framework is redundant [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full-cycle modeling first: instructor solves a problem aloud through all five phases, then fades prompts across subsequent problems
  • Phase-targeted practice: focus a lesson on a single weak phase (commonly Define or Look back) rather than the whole cycle
  • Metacognitive pairing: combine with a think-aloud protocol so learners hear the monitoring and self-questioning that accompanies each phase
  • Collaborative variant: assign phases to roles in group problem solving, then rotate

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who jump to solutions without representing the problem, producing pattern-matching errors [~M]
  • Intermediate learners preparing for transfer to unfamiliar problem types, where the Look back phase builds analogical comparison habits Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M]
  • Less useful for advanced learners with established domain strategies, who find the general cycle redundant [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Problem representation: translating a situation into a defined, solvable form
  • Metacognitive regulation: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own problem solving
  • Transfer: recognizing when problems share deep structure despite surface differences Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Model the full cycle. Solve a problem aloud, making each IDEAL phase explicit — including a false start and revision during Look back, so learners see the cycle as iterative rather than linear.
  2. Identify and Define with prompts. Give learners a graphic organizer or prompt set ("What is given? What is the goal? What type of problem is this?") to force representation before solution attempts.
  3. Explore alternatives. Require at least two candidate strategies before acting; prompt comparison of their likely costs and outcomes.
  4. Act, then Look back. After solving, require learners to check the result against the original goal and to state what other problems this solution method might fit — the comparison step that drives transfer Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning. [+S].
  5. Fade the scaffold. Withdraw phase-by-phase prompts as learners internalize the cycle, moving toward unprompted problem solving.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the Explore phase depends on retrieving relevant prior cases and concepts
  • Worked examples — a completed IDEAL cycle can be presented as a worked example for learners to study before attempting their own
  • Problem-based learning — IDEAL provides the process scaffold that PBL cases often leave implicit
  • Teach prompting as a five-part skill (role, task, audience, constraints, justification) with a structured ask-generate-evaluate cycle

Examples¶

  • IDEAL in mathematics instruction: a teacher models the full cycle on a non-routine word problem, then students work in pairs on a structurally similar problem using an IDEAL organizer, comparing their representation to the modeled one afterward.
  • Writing instruction: students apply Identify/Define to an essay prompt (what is actually being asked?), Explore two possible theses, draft, then Look back by checking the draft against the defined task.
  • Engineering design courses: IDEAL maps onto the design cycle; students document each phase in a design notebook, with Look back entries requiring comparison to prior design problems.
