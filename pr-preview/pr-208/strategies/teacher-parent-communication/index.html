Teacher Parent Communication¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Teacher parent communication encompasses the deliberate practices teachers use to exchange information with families — positive phone calls and texts, progress updates, conferences, newsletters, and digital messaging platforms. Effective communication is two-way and asset-based: teachers share specific evidence of learning and solicit family knowledge, rather than contacting parents only when problems arise.

Design Implications¶

Frequent, personalized teacher-family communication measurably increases student engagement and homework completion, with the strongest effects when contact is positive rather than deficit-focused [~M]. Communication works by aligning the two primary contexts of a child's development; when families know what is being taught and how to respond, home reinforcement matches school expectations rather than competing with them. The relationship-building function matters as much as the informational one — early positive contact builds the trust that makes later difficult conversations productive.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A sustainable, low-friction channel (messaging apps, text, email) matched to what families actually use
  • Specific, evidence-based content — a named behavior or work sample, not generic praise
  • Translation and accessibility support so communication reaches all families equitably
  • A schedule: proactive contact early in the term, before any negative contact is needed

Constraints¶

  • Contact framed around deficits or discipline can damage engagement and increase family distrust [-M] — negative-only contact patterns predict lower subsequent family responsiveness
  • Communication volume without personalization becomes noise; families disengage from generic mass updates [~W]
  • Effects fade when contact is episodic; a single conference or beginning-of-year outreach produces little measurable change [~M]
  • Digital platforms can widen gaps for families with limited connectivity, language access, or flexible work schedules [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Positive phone/text campaigns: brief individualized positive contacts for a subset of students (e.g., 2–3 per week on a rotating roster)
  • Learning updates with evidence: photos of student work with a short note connecting it to learning goals
  • Two-way conferencing: student-led or family-input conferences where families contribute knowledge about the learner
  • Platform-mediated updates: tools like ClassDojo, Seesaw, or Remind for ongoing sharing, supplemented by personal contact for relationship-critical moments

Target Learners¶

  • Students at risk of disengagement, where family-school alignment provides the largest marginal benefit [~M]
  • Early-career and transition points (first year of school, middle school entry) when engagement trajectories are being set
  • Multilingual families, when communication is delivered in the home language — monolingual-English-only outreach systematically excludes the families who most need alignment [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and engagement outcomes: attendance, homework completion, classroom participation
  • Social-emotional development: consistent expectations across home and school contexts
  • Academic outcomes, indirectly — family involvement shows reliable but modest associations with achievement, strongest for engagement-oriented (not homework-supervision) involvement [~M]

Instructions¶

  1. Establish the channel and norms in the first weeks of school, including preferred language and contact times (Check-In with families as you would with students)
  2. Log a positive, specific contact for every student early in the term — a named strength with evidence, not generic praise
  3. Maintain a rotating schedule of brief individualized updates tied to visible learning goals (Assessment evidence shared in family-accessible language)
  4. Before any concern-based contact, ensure a positive-contact history exists; frame concerns around shared problem-solving (Action-Oriented Feedback)
  5. Solicit family knowledge — ask what they observe at home and what works for their child — and visibly incorporate it

Related Strategies¶

  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — the same brief, frequent, individualized contact pattern applied to students rather than families
  • Check-Ins — low-cost recurring contact structures that communication schedules extend to families
  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the feedback framing that makes progress communication actionable rather than evaluative
  • Use universally designed auto-translated two-way messaging so families need only basic text messaging
  • SIG-funded parent liaisons paired with structured parent contact practices to increase family engagement
  • Parents should reinforce on-time attendance, communicate with teachers, and make academics the freshman's top priority

Examples¶

  • Kraft & Dougherty's texting intervention — teachers sent brief individualized messages (positive notes, progress reminders) to families of high school students several times per month; treated students showed increased homework completion and class participation.
  • ClassDojo — platform for sharing photos, videos, and behavior/learning updates with families, with built-in translation into 35+ languages.
  • Seesaw — student work portfolios shared with families, who can view and comment, making the student the mediator of home-school communication.
  • Epstein's partnership programs (e.g., the National Network of Partnership Schools) — school-wide structures for six types of family involvement, including two-way communication channels.
