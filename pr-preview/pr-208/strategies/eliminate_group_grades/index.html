Eliminate Group Grades¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 4 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Eliminating group grades means that no learner receives a summative score based primarily on the collective output of a team. Instead, each learner is assessed on individually demonstrable learning — via individual components, in-class assessments, structured peer assessment, or documented individual contributions — while group work remains a learning activity rather than a grading unit. The strategy targets the well-documented pathologies of shared grades: free-riding, unequal effort, and grade disputes that poison collaboration.

Design Implications¶

Group grades create a social-loafing problem: when individual contribution is invisible, rational learners reduce effort, and higher-ability members compensate or resent Cooperative learning requires group rewards paired with individual accountability to be effective. [+S]. The cooperative-learning literature consistently finds that collaboration improves achievement when group goals are combined with individual accountability — and that group rewards alone, without it, do not reliably produce gains. Removing group grades does not mean removing collaboration; it means decoupling the assessment of learning from the product of the team so that Collaborative Learning can serve its cognitive purpose (elaboration, argumentation, peer explanation) without distorting incentives.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Individual assessment points: individual write-ups, individual exams or quizzes on the group content, or oral defenses of the team product
  • Transparent contribution tracking (e.g., peer ratings, contribution statements, version histories) used formatively rather than punitively
  • Clear communication that the group's purpose is learning and feedback, not grade allocation
  • Alignment with Assessment design so individual evidence maps to the same learning goals as the group task

Constraints¶

  • Removing shared grades can reduce genuine interdependence if the group product no longer matters to anyone [-M] — teams need a shared goal (pride in the artifact, a presentation, a real audience) even when grades are individual
  • Peer assessment of contribution is unreliable when anonymous, low-stakes, or unmoderated; it can be gamed or reflect friendship bias [-M]
  • In large classes without grading capacity for individual artifacts, the strategy may collapse into all-individual work, losing the benefits of collaboration [~M]
  • Cultural and equity considerations: students from collaborative or collectivist backgrounds may experience the shift as distrust; framing matters

Implementation Variability¶

  • Hybrid weighting: group product counts for a small portion; individual component (write-up, quiz, defense) carries the majority
  • Same-task, individual evidence: all members produce individual outputs from shared group work (common in PBL, e.g., individual lab reports from shared data)
  • Peer-evaluated contribution modifiers: individual grade adjusted by structured peer ratings (e.g., CATME-style behavior ratings), used with caution and moderation
  • Group work ungraded entirely: collaboration is formative; all summative assessment is individual Assessment for learning improves achievement when feedback is actionable rather than evaluative. [+S]

Target Learners¶

  • Mixed-ability groups, where shared grades most often penalize high contributors and mask non-learning by low contributors Cooperative learning requires group rewards paired with individual accountability to be effective. [+S]
  • Adolescents and adults in graded programs, where incentive distortion is strongest
  • Less critical in low-stakes or ungraded contexts, where social motivation already sustains participation

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual and procedural objectives that must be individually demonstrated (exams, transfer tasks)
  • Collaborative skills themselves — but assessed via observation and reflection, not group output
  • Not well suited to goals where the collective artifact is the authentic outcome (e.g., a team-designed product for a real client); in those cases, assess individual process and reflection alongside the shared deliverable

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning goals the group activity serves, and identify how each individual will demonstrate them.
  2. Design the group task with genuine interdependence — roles, shared data, or a joint product — so collaboration remains necessary.
  3. Attach an individual accountability component: individual write-up, quiz on the material, or oral defense of the team's decisions.
  4. Use structured peer feedback on collaboration formatively; if used for grading, moderate it and make criteria explicit.
  5. Return individual feedback quickly so group work functions as Assessment for learning, not just measurement.
  6. Debrief with teams on process (who did what, what worked) to preserve shared responsibility without shared grades.

Related Strategies¶

  • Individual accountability within cooperative learning — the claim-level basis: group rewards work only when paired with individual accountability
  • Peer assessment strategies — provide the contribution-visibility mechanism that replaces the shared grade
  • Assessment for learning — reframes group work as feedback-rich practice rather than a grading event

Examples¶

  • Johnson & Johnson's cooperative learning model — specifies "group goals and individual accountability" as defining conditions; teachers assess each member individually on the team's learning material.
  • Project-based science curricula (e.g., PBLWorks exemplar projects) — teams build one product, but each student submits an individual portfolio entry and sits an individual content quiz.
  • Engineering capstone courses using CATME peer evaluation — individual grades derived from individually-authored report sections plus moderated peer ratings of teamwork behaviors (CATME).
  • Oakland-based EL Education schools — crew-based collaborative projects with individual passage presentations defending one's own learning.
