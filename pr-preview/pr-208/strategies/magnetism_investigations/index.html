Magnetism Investigations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 1 causal), q3–q4 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Magnetism investigations are hands-on science activities in which learners test which objects a magnet attracts, explore how magnetic force acts at a distance and through materials, and map how a compass needle responds to nearby magnets and magnetic fields. The cycle of predicting, testing, observing, and explaining drives the learning: learners commit to a prediction, confront the outcome, and revise their mental model of magnetic properties.

Design Implications¶

Hands-on investigation makes an invisible force observable and testable, which is especially valuable for abstract physical-science concepts young learners cannot directly perceive Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone. [+S]. The strategy works best when prediction precedes testing — committing to a prediction creates cognitive disequilibrium when results contradict it, which motivates conceptual change Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]. Investigations should conclude with explanation and discussion, not just manipulation; activity without sense-making produces engagement but shallow learning Hands-on activity alone does not guarantee conceptual learning without reflection and explanation. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Bar magnets, compasses, and a mixed set of test objects (steel, aluminum, copper, plastic, wood, paper) — including non-magnetic metals so learners confront the "all metal is magnetic" misconception
  • A prediction sheet or recording structure so learners commit before testing (Advance Organizers can frame the recording format)
  • A debrief in which learners articulate rules ("magnets attract iron and steel, not all shiny things") and connect compass behavior to Earth's magnetic field
  • Controlled environment: keep stray magnets and electronics away from compass stations

Constraints¶

  • Requires physical materials and per-group supervision; not feasible at scale without kits
  • Young learners frequently overgeneralize from a single trial (e.g., "magnets stick to metal" after testing only steel) — multiple contrasting examples are needed, and a single hands-on session rarely displaces entrenched misconceptions [~M]
  • Compass readings are easily confounded by nearby metal furniture or hidden magnets, producing observations learners cannot explain [-W]
  • Safety: small magnets pose swallowing hazards for young children; strong neodymium magnets can pinch

Implementation Variability¶

  • Prediction-first vs. explore-first: prediction-first produces stronger conceptual change; explore-first suits learners with no prior exposure
  • Add complexity progressively: attraction → force through materials (paper, wood, water) → distance effects → compass field mapping
  • Pair with Analogies (iron filings as "footprints" of the invisible field) to make the field visible
  • Older or advanced learners can extend to plotting field lines with iron filings or building an electromagnet

Target Learners¶

  • K–5 learners encountering physical science for the first time; magnetism's immediate, dramatic feedback suits concrete operational thinkers
  • Learners holding common misconceptions (all metals are magnetic; magnets attract everything shiny) who benefit from direct refutation through testing Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]
  • Less effective as pure free exploration for novices; guided structure with focused questions is needed [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: magnetic vs. non-magnetic materials, action at a distance, Earth as a magnet
  • Science practices: prediction, fair testing, observation, evidence-based explanation
  • Representational skill: mapping invisible fields via compass deflection or filings

Instructions¶

  1. Activate prior experience: "Where have you seen magnets work?" (Activation)
  2. Present the object set and have each group record predictions for every object before touching a magnet (Advance Organizers)
  3. Groups test each object, recording attract/repel/no effect, and re-sort objects by result (Application)
  4. Introduce the compass: learners move a magnet around a compass and record needle deflection at different positions and distances (Practice)
  5. Debrief: groups state a rule supported by their evidence; the class tests edge cases (aluminum coin, nickel) against the rule (Class Discussion)
  6. Assess with novel objects and a "explain the compass" transfer prompt (Assessment)

Related Elements¶

  • Application — testing objects applies the concept of magnetic attraction to concrete instances
  • Practice — repeated compass trials build fluency in reading and interpreting deflection
  • Analogies — filings-as-footprints and Earth-as-magnet analogies make invisible fields tractable
  • Class Discussion — the debrief converts individual observations into shared, evidence-based rules

Examples¶

  • Magnetic Testing (ILS) — Learners observe and predict which objects a magnet will attract, then test and record results.
  • Magnetic Mapping (CCSSO) — Learners investigate how a compass is affected by placing magnets near it at different positions and distances.
  • FOSS Magnetism and Motion module (Lawrence Hall of Science) — A published curriculum sequence of magnet investigations with prediction sheets and embedded assessment. https://www.fossweb.com
