Lesson Plans¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 review, 2 causal, 2 theoretical), q2–q3 · 0 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

A lesson plan is a structured planning document in which an educator specifies learning objectives, sequencing, activities, materials, and assessment for a single instructional session. Planning forces the instructor to work backward from intended outcomes to the activities and checks that will get learners there, converting broad curriculum goals into a concrete, time-bounded sequence. Plans range from detailed scripts (common for novice teachers) to outline-level sketches (common for experts), but the core functions — objective clarity, activity sequencing, and planned assessment — remain constant.

Design Implications¶

Well-structured lessons support learning because they manage the learner's cognitive load through predictable structure and clear goals Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. The strongest empirical support is for plans that begin with review and clear objectives, present material in small steps with practice after each, and include frequent checks for understanding [~S] — Rosenshine's synthesis of effective teaching studies found these features consistently in lessons of highly effective instructors. Objectives framed as what learners will do (process and task goals) outperform vague content coverage statements for novice learners Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit, observable learning objectives stated before activities are selected
  • Alignment among objectives, activities, and assessment (the "constructive alignment" test: every activity serves a stated objective)
  • Planned checks for understanding distributed throughout the lesson, not only at the end
  • Time estimates for each segment, with a contingency for running long

Constraints¶

  • Over-scripted plans reduce teacher responsiveness; lessons that cannot adapt to learner confusion in real time lose the formative-assessment benefit [~M]
  • Plans built around activities first ("what will be fun?") rather than objectives first produce engagement without durable learning [-M]
  • Rigid adherence to a plan when checks for understanding show the class has not learned the material converts the plan from a tool into a liability [-S]
  • Detailed planning benefits novice instructors most; for experts, heavy scripting can crowd out the adaptive, responsive moves that characterize effective teaching [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Scripted plans (e.g., Direct Instruction curricula) specify teacher wording precisely — effective for foundational skills and novice teachers [~S]
  • Outline plans specify objectives, sequence, and checks but leave wording open — suits experienced instructors and discussion-based formats
  • Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe) starts from desired evidence of learning, then plans assessment, then activities
  • 5E plans (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate) structure inquiry-oriented science lessons

Target Learners¶

  • Novice learners benefit most from lessons with clear objectives, small-step sequencing, and high rates of guided practice [~S]
  • Learners with limited self-regulation benefit from visible lesson structure and routine check-ins that externalize monitoring Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Advanced learners may need less structured segments with more open exploration; highly guided lessons can feel redundant to them [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Foundational knowledge and procedural skill: small-step presentation with practice suits well-structured content
  • Conceptual understanding: plans that include Self-Explanation prompts or structured Class Discussion segments
  • Transfer and application: plans allocating substantial time to Practice with varied problems rather than extended presentation

Instructions¶

  1. State 1–3 observable learning objectives; frame them as learner actions, not content coverage Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]
  2. Open with an activating activity or Advance Organizer that connects new material to prior knowledge
  3. Sequence content in small chunks, alternating presentation with guided Practice rather than presenting everything first Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  4. Insert planned checks for understanding (cold call, mini-whiteboards, quick writes) after each chunk; treat results as decision points, not formalities
  5. Plan the Assessment segment to align with the objectives — an Exit Ticket or comparable check that would reveal whether each objective was met
  6. Note timing and a fallback for each segment; identify in advance which segment can be cut or extended

Related Strategies¶

  • Backward Design — the curriculum-level planning method that lesson plans should serve
  • Formative Assessment — the checks-for-understanding segments that make a plan responsive
  • Direct Instruction — a highly structured lesson pattern embodying small-step sequencing and guided practice
  • Flipped Classroom — relocates first exposure out of the lesson so class time can be planned around practice
  • Pair each performance objective with a demonstration-based suggested activity and coded resource references

Examples¶

  • Success for All reading program — tightly scripted daily lesson plans with built-in regrouping based on assessment data; one of the most extensively evaluated structured-planning interventions in K–12 research
  • EngageNY / Illustrative Mathematics curricula — published lesson plans with objectives, timed activity sequences, and embedded checks that teachers adapt
  • Gagné's Nine Events — a classic lesson-plan template: gain attention, inform objectives, stimulate recall, present content, provide guidance, elicit practice, provide feedback, assess, enhance transfer Gagné's 9 Events of Instruction
