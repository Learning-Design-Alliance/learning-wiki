Build Community Through Peer Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 causal, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Students regularly exchange structured feedback on one another's work and use it to revise. The strategy serves two goals simultaneously: improving work quality through iterative critique, and building a classroom community in which students see peers as legitimate sources of expertise and support rather than competitors.

Design Implications¶

Peer feedback works when it is treated as formative assessment rather than evaluation: feedback is most effective when directed at the task and the process, not the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. Structured peer feedback also benefits the giver — critiquing a peer's work requires evaluating it against criteria, which deepens the giver's own understanding of quality Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. The community-building effect depends on psychological safety and repeated, reciprocal exchange; one-off peer review rarely changes classroom culture.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria or rubrics so feedback targets the work, not the writer (Rubrics or annotated exemplars)
  • Training in giving constructive feedback — sentence stems, critique protocols (e.g., "warm/cool feedback"), and norms of respect
  • Time and structure for students to act on feedback through Revision; feedback that is never used teaches students it was pointless
  • Instructor monitoring of feedback quality, especially early on

Constraints¶

  • Untrained students default to vague praise ("good job") or personal comments, which neither improves work nor builds trust [-M]
  • Power and status dynamics can distort exchange: low-status students' feedback is discounted regardless of quality [~M]
  • If feedback is graded or tied to evaluation, students hedge and withhold honest critique [-M]
  • Feedback quality suffers when givers lack familiarity with the criteria or the content domain [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Gallery walks and class discussion critiques for whole-group norm-building
  • Written asynchronous exchange (discussion boards, document comments) for depth and reflection
  • Reciprocal pairs or rotating small groups for relationship-building
  • Author-recipient dialogue ("stars and wishes," critical friends protocols) to close the feedback loop

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 and higher-education learners; particularly valuable for students who experience the instructor as the sole authority [~M]
  • Students with fragile academic confidence benefit from seeing peers struggle and improve Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less effective for novices in a domain who cannot yet judge quality — they need worked examples or annotated exemplars of "good feedback" first [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Formative improvement of drafts, projects, and presentations
  • Development of evaluative judgment — internalizing criteria for quality work
  • Collaborative and communication skills; classroom community and belonging
  • Self-regulation: using external feedback to plan revisions Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Establish norms and train students in constructive critique using exemplars of strong and weak feedback.
  2. Provide clear criteria or a rubric so feedback is anchored to the work, not the person.
  3. Structure the exchange — pairs, rotating groups, or gallery walk — with a protocol and time limit.
  4. Require recipients to respond: identify one change they will make and why.
  5. Build in time for Revision based on the feedback received.
  6. Debrief the process itself (class discussion) to reinforce norms and improve feedback quality over time.

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer critique and revision cycles pair naturally with portfolio and draft-based writing approaches; see Assessment for Learning for the underlying formative-assessment principle.
  • Foster a classroom culture of critical thinking through collective responsibility, trust norms, multiple perspectives, scaffolding, and frequent argumentation opportunities

Examples¶

  • El Education (Expeditionary Learning) "Critique and Revision" protocol — students use kind/specific/helpful norms to critique drafts of expedition products before public exhibition (https://eleducation.org).
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — algorithmically distribute submissions so each student gives and receives calibrated feedback on written work.
  • Writing workshop models (e.g., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project) — regular peer conferring and author's chair sessions embed feedback in the writing cycle.
