Praising Effort and Strategies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Praising effort and strategies (often called "process praise") means giving feedback that names the specific actions, strategies, persistence, and choices a learner used — rather than praising the person ("You're so smart") or offering generic approval ("Good job"). The goal is to reinforce the belief that success comes from controllable, learnable behavior, which supports a growth mindset and sustained persistence after setbacks.

Design Implications¶

Process praise shifts learners' implicit theories of ability toward an incremental view: children praised for effort after failure are more likely to choose challenging tasks and persist, while those praised for intelligence tend to avoid challenge and disengage after failure Mueller & Dweck's six studies [+S]. The mechanism is attributional — praise tells learners what success and failure are about — so the praise must identify controllable causes (strategy choice, practice, revision) rather than stable traits. Praise for effort alone, however, is not a magic word: effort praise that follows failure can implicitly signal low ability, and effort praise is most effective when paired with strategy instruction and outcome feedback [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Genuine, specific observation of what the learner actually did ("You tried breaking the problem into smaller parts") — vague or inflated praise ("You're incredibly brilliant at this!") backfires, especially after failure [~M]
  • A shift in instructor habit from person-focused to process-focused language, applied consistently across success and failure situations
  • Follow-through: process praise works best when paired with concrete strategy suggestions and opportunities to revise (Feedback)

Constraints¶

  • Effort praise after visible failure can be read as a low-ability signal ("they praise effort because I'm struggling"), reducing motivation [~M]
  • Inflated praise given to struggling learners ("amazing!") raises the perceived standard and leads them to avoid challenge [~M]
  • Effects on achievement are smaller and less consistent than effects on mindset beliefs; large-scale replications of mindset interventions show modest, heterogeneous academic effects [~M]
  • Over-praising or praising routine completion can produce complacency and dependence on external validation [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Verbal praise in one-on-one, small-group, or whole-class settings; written feedback on work; rubric language that names process criteria
  • Strategy praise ("your diagram helped you keep track of the quantities") is generally more powerful than undifferentiated effort praise because it teaches what to repeat [~M]
  • In digital environments, process praise can be systematized: hint systems and mastery-based platforms (e.g., Khan Academy) frame errors as "expected steps in learning" rather than deficits

Target Learners¶

  • Children and adolescents, whose implicit theories of ability are still forming; effects of person vs. process praise are documented from preschool onward Mueller & Dweck [+S]
  • Learners who avoid challenge or disengage after setbacks — process praise reframes difficulty as informative rather than diagnostic of ability [~M]
  • Struggling learners need especially careful calibration: inflated or effort-only praise can signal low expectations [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Motivational: growth mindset, persistence, willingness to attempt difficult tasks
  • Metacognitive: attention to strategy selection and self-monitoring (links to Self-Regulated Learning)
  • Not a substitute for cognitive goals — process praise supports the conditions for learning but does not itself teach content

Instructions¶

  1. Observe the learner's work closely enough to name a specific process — a strategy attempted, a revision made, a productive question asked.
  2. Deliver praise that names the controllable action and, where possible, its link to the outcome: "Rereading the problem before solving helped you catch that."
  3. When effort was high but the outcome was poor, pair effort acknowledgment with a concrete strategy suggestion rather than effort praise alone (Feedback).
  4. Avoid person praise ("gifted," "natural") and inflated praise, particularly after failure.
  5. Reinforce the same framing in error responses: treat mistakes as information ("What did this attempt show you?") consistent with Assessment for Learning.

Related Strategies¶

  • Growth-oriented feedback — process praise is the positive-valence end of a feedback practice that frames ability as malleable
  • Normalizing error and productive failure — shares the attributional goal of decoupling mistakes from ability judgments
  • Mastery-oriented grading — structural (rather than verbal) way to reward progress over fixed performance

Examples¶

  • Mueller & Dweck (1998) — After success on Raven's matrices problems, children praised for effort subsequently chose more challenging problems and performed better than children praised for intelligence, who lied about their scores more often to protect a "smart" image.
  • Classroom language routines — Responsive Classroom and similar SEL programs train teachers to use "reinforcing language" that names specific learning behaviors rather than generic approval.
  • Khan Academy — Mastery-based progress displays and messages framing struggle as normal ("mistakes are proof you're trying") operationalize process-oriented feedback at scale.
