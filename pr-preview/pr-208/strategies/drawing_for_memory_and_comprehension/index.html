Drawing for Memory and Comprehension¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 unmarked) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Drawing asks learners to construct a pictorial representation of content they are trying to learn — a diagram, sketch, or illustration of a concept, process, or text. The act of drawing requires learners to select key information, organize it spatially, and translate verbal material into visual form, producing deeper processing than rereading or copying. It is a generative learning activity: the learner, not the instructor, produces the external representation.

Design Implications¶

Drawing works because it combines multiple memory pathways: it engages Dual Coding Theory by creating both verbal and visual codes, and it forces generative processing — learners must decide what to depict and how, which is itself a form of elaboration Drawing improves memory for words and definitions more than writing or visualizing. [+S]. The "drawing effect" on free recall is robust across materials, ages, and cultures Drawing improves memory for words and definitions more than writing or visualizing. [+S]. For comprehension of texts and processes, drawing is most effective when learners are supported in mapping the structure of the content (causal links, sequences, part–whole relations) rather than decorating it with pictures [+M]. Because drawing consumes working memory, it should be applied to small units of content and paired with Chunking [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Content that can plausibly be represented visually (processes, systems, spatial relations, narratives, definitions)
  • Sufficient time — drawing is slower than writing or rereading, so scope must be reduced accordingly
  • Minimal drawing skill demands; the goal is representational, not artistic, quality
  • A follow-up step that connects the drawing back to the verbal content, such as Self-Explanation or explaining the drawing to a peer

Constraints¶

  • Drawing consumes working memory; for novices, complex content plus drawing can overload rather than help Drawing can impose extraneous load when content is complex or drawing skill is weak. [-M]
  • Learners may draw superficially attractive but structurally wrong pictures; without feedback, misconceptions get encoded [-M]
  • Less effective for abstract content with no natural visual analog (e.g., philosophical arguments, abstract algebra) [~M]
  • Learners with low spatial or graphic confidence may spend effort on aesthetics rather than meaning [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured drawing: provide a partially completed diagram or template to scaffold novices, fading support over time
  • Drawing from text vs. from memory: drawing while reading supports comprehension; drawing from memory afterward strengthens retention
  • Interactive drawing: compare learner-generated drawings with expert diagrams and revise — the comparison drives abstraction
  • Group drawing: collaborative concept mapping on shared canvases combines drawing with Collaborative Learning

Target Learners¶

  • Children and novices, who benefit from the dual verbal–visual encoding of new vocabulary and concepts Drawing improves memory for words and definitions more than writing or visualizing. [+S]
  • Learners with weak verbal encoding strategies, for whom the visual route provides an alternative memory path [+M]
  • Less beneficial for learners with high expertise, who already have well-formed mental models and may find drawing redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of facts, vocabulary, and definitions [+S]
  • Comprehension of processes, systems, and causal structures in text [+M]
  • Integration of verbal and pictorial knowledge representations

Instructions¶

  1. Select a small, bounded unit of content (a definition, one process step, one paragraph).
  2. Ask learners to draw a picture that captures the meaning — not an illustration of the words, but a representation of the idea — following the generative logic of Active Learning.
  3. Have learners label the drawing with key terms to bind the verbal and visual codes.
  4. Prompt learners to explain their drawing aloud or in writing, enacting Self-Explanation.
  5. Compare drawings with a reference diagram or peers' drawings and revise; revisit drawings on a spaced schedule using Spaced Repetition.

Related Strategies¶

  • Concept Mapping — a structured, node-and-link variant of drawing focused on relational knowledge
  • Sketchnoting — real-time visual note-taking combining drawing with Annotating
  • Retrieval Practice — drawing from memory is a retrieval activity with a generative visual component

Examples¶

  • Science sketching in elementary curricula: programs such as the Lawrence Hall of Science's FOSS (Full Option Science System, https://www.fossweb.com) embed science notebooks in which students draw observations and mechanisms before writing explanations.
  • Medical education: students sketch anatomical structures and physiological pathways from memory as a study activity, exploiting the drawing effect for high-volume factual learning.
  • Vocabulary learning: Wammes and colleagues' experiments had participants draw word definitions rather than write them, producing substantially better free recall [+S].
