Discipline-Specific Literacy¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q3–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Discipline-specific literacy (also called disciplinary literacy) teaches students how texts are constructed, interpreted, and used within a particular field — how historians source documents, how scientists read data and methods sections, how mathematicians parse proofs. It rests on the finding that literacy skills are not generic: the strategies that make a strong reader in one discipline can be counterproductive in another Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008 [+S]. Instruction typically pairs mentor texts with explicit modeling of disciplinary reading and writing moves, followed by guided practice and feedback.

Design Implications¶

Disciplinary literacy works because it makes the hidden conventions of expert text practices visible and teachable, rather than leaving students to infer them from exposure alone Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008 [+M]. Because experts' tacit practices must first be surfaced, instructors benefit from Think-Aloud modeling of how they actually read and write in their field.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Authentic mentor texts from the discipline (primary sources, journal articles, technical reports)
  • Explicit modeling of disciplinary reasoning — how an expert approaches the text and why (Think-Aloud)
  • Discipline-specific graphic organizers, checklists, and vocabulary supports (Advance Organizers, Annotating)
  • Opportunities to write for authentic audiences and purposes with feedback at the task and process levels Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Instructors are typically trained in content, not in their discipline's literacy practices; without professional development they default to generic "reading strategies" that do not reflect disciplinary norms Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008 [-M]
  • Generic comprehension strategies (summarizing, predicting) show diminishing returns for adolescents who already decode fluently; adding more generic strategy instruction on top of disciplinary work adds little Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008 [~M]
  • Mentor texts and exemplars are unevenly available across disciplines; in less text-rich fields, instructors may need to curate or author materials
  • Overemphasis on discipline-specific conventions can narrow writing instruction; broad writing strategy instruction (planning, revising, sentence combining) carries much of the effect on writing quality Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Read–write–think–discuss cycles: students read a mentor text, analyze its disciplinary moves, discuss, then produce their own text — reduces anxiety through collaborative engagement
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship framing: instructor models, coaches, then fades support as students appropriate disciplinary practices Scaffolding
  • Multiple contrasting cases of disciplinary texts (e.g., a news article vs. a research report on the same event) to make genre conventions visible Cognitive Flexibility Theory

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescent and post-secondary students who decode adequately but struggle with discipline-specific text structures and conventions Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008 [+M]
  • Novices to a discipline, who lack the schemas experts use to parse specialized texts; explicit modeling reduces unguided search Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Students with reading difficulties need foundational supports first; disciplinary literacy supplements rather than replaces intensive intervention for struggling decoders [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of discipline-specific texts and data representations
  • Discipline-appropriate writing and argumentation (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning in science)
  • Epistemic understanding: how knowledge is created and warranted in a field
  • Reduced anxiety and increased engagement with challenging course texts

Instructions¶

  1. Select an authentic mentor text exemplifying the target disciplinary practice.
  2. Model expert reading/writing of the text aloud, narrating decisions and monitoring (Think-Aloud, Direct Instruction).
  3. Provide structured supports — graphic organizers, checklists, Annotating routines, Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax scaffolds.
  4. Have students apply the practice collaboratively, then independently (Practice, Collaborative Learning).
  5. Give process-focused feedback and fade supports as competence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — disciplinary comprehension depends heavily on relevant prior knowledge being retrieved before reading
  • Annotating — a core disciplinary reading routine (e.g., sourcing and annotating in history)
  • Case-Based Learning — uses authentic disciplinary materials as the object of analysis

Examples¶

  • Reading Like a Historian (Stanford History Education Group, sheg.stanford.edu) — students source, contextualize, and corroborate primary documents, then write evidence-based arguments; extensively studied and freely available.
  • Science writing heuristic approaches in secondary chemistry, where students write claim–evidence–reasoning explanations modeled on how scientists report findings.
  • Writing in the disciplines programs in higher education (e.g., WID initiatives at Michigan State and Harvard), which pair faculty from across departments to articulate and teach field-specific writing conventions.
