Creating Space for New Ideas¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Creating space for new ideas means deliberately structuring the conditions under which educators can experiment with unfamiliar approaches: protected time, access to resources and expertise, and — most critically — psychological safety, so that trying and failing is treated as data rather than deficiency. It is carried out through routines such as scheduled innovation time, small-scale pilots, peer observation, and public framing of failure as informative.

Design Implications¶

Innovation in schools is fundamentally a team learning problem, and team learning depends on members believing that interpersonal risk-taking — asking questions, admitting uncertainty, proposing untested ideas — will not be punished Psychological safety enables learning behaviors such as asking for help and experimenting. [+S]. Edmondson's research shows that teams with high psychological safety report more errors and discuss them more openly, not because they make more mistakes but because they surface and learn from them [+S]. Improvement-science approaches operationalize this by treating each classroom trial as a small, fast test within a larger improvement cycle rather than a high-stakes adoption decision Rapid-cycle improvement makes failure cheap and informative. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Leadership that explicitly frames experimentation as expected and failure as informative — norms set at the top shape whether teachers take interpersonal risks [+S]
  • Protected, scheduled time for planning, piloting, and debriefing new approaches
  • A feedback structure that examines the work (task and process level) rather than the person Feedback most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Small-scale pilots with defined success measures, so evidence rather than opinion drives scale-up

Constraints¶

  • Where evaluation regimes tie experimentation to performance ratings, teachers rationally avoid risk and revert to familiar practice [-M]
  • Innovation space without follow-through — pilots that are launched but never debriefed or resourced — breeds cynicism and reduces future participation [-M]
  • Psychological safety without accountability produces comfortable but low-performing teams; Edmondson's data show safety must pair with high standards [+S]
  • Time pressure and coverage mandates crowd out experimentation regardless of stated norms [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Improvement cycles: Plan-Do-Study-Act routines in which teachers test one change in one class for two weeks (Bryk et al.'s Networked Improvement Communities)
  • Peer observation partnerships: reciprocal non-evaluative classroom visits that normalize seeing others' practice
  • Innovation time: scheduled blocks (e.g., 20% time, genius-hour structures for staff) for developing new units or methods
  • Failure forums: structured sessions where teachers present what didn't work and what they learned, modeled on error-management training

Target Learners¶

  • Practicing teachers and instructional teams, particularly those implementing new curricula or technology [+M]
  • Early-career educators, who benefit from norms that make asking for help safe Self-efficacy predicts persistence in the face of early difficulty. [+M]
  • School leaders designing professional learning systems

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Adaptive expertise: developing the disposition to modify practice based on evidence rather than habit
  • Collective efficacy: building shared belief that the team can improve outcomes
  • Reflective practice: systematic analysis of one's own teaching decisions

Instructions¶

  1. Establish explicit norms that experimentation is expected and that honest reporting of failure is valued — leaders go first by sharing their own missteps [+S]
  2. Schedule protected time for pilots and debriefs; treat it as non-negotiable as instructional time
  3. Frame each trial as a small test with a clear measure, following improvement-science routines Rapid-cycle improvement makes failure cheap and informative. [+M]
  4. Pair experimenting teachers with Coaching support so risk-taking is scaffolded, not solitary
  5. Use structured Check-In routines and team meetings to surface obstacles early, before they harden into disengagement
  6. Debrief at the task and process level — what did the change do to student learning? — rather than evaluating the teacher Feedback most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  7. Scale what the evidence supports; retire what it doesn't, publicly and without blame

Related Strategies¶

  • Professional Learning Communities — the collaborative structure within which experimentation becomes shared learning rather than isolated risk
  • Coaching — provides the individual support that makes risk-taking survivable for the teacher and their students
  • RPIP focus-group professional learning cycle: consultancy, four-week try-out with commitment and hypothesis, then artifact debrief
  • Provide teachers protected, substantial time away from their day, with meals, to commune with students
  • Schedule protected release and preparation time for educators completing pre- and between-session toolkit work

Examples¶

  • Networked Improvement Communities (Carnegie Foundation) — Bryk et al.'s model in which schools across a network run rapid PDSA cycles on shared problems, with failures documented as learning for the whole network (Carnegie Foundation)
  • Lesson Study (Japan) — collaborative planning, public teaching of a research lesson, and structured observation; the "lesson" is expected to be imperfect because its flaws are the object of study
  • Google's 20% time adapted for schools — some districts allocate regular staff time for teacher-initiated pilots, with a showcase for sharing results regardless of outcome
