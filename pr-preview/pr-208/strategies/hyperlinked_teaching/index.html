Hyperlinked Teaching¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (3 for, 2 against) · 13 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 6 of 13 report an effect size

Description¶

Hyperlinked teaching provides students with direct avenues for further inquiry and exploration within a topic. Rather than a single linear path through content, materials contain links to deeper explanations, alternative perspectives, and supplementary resources, and students are expected to pursue, evaluate, and synthesize these connections with evidence from multiple sources. The instructor curates the link ecology and structures time for exploration, discussion, and product creation.

Design Implications¶

Hyperlinked teaching leverages learner choice and self-direction, which supports motivation when students perceive genuine autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. Its effectiveness, however, depends on structure: unguided exploration of linked resources is a form of minimally guided instruction and works poorly for novices Minimal guidance is less effective than guided instruction for novices. [-S]. Curated link sets, guiding questions, and a required synthesis product convert free browsing into purposeful inquiry.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A curated set of high-quality, varied resources (articles, videos, datasets, primary sources) mapped to the topic's key ideas
  • Instructional time for exploration, discussion, and project development
  • A guiding question or task that gives the exploration direction (Advance Organizers help frame it)
  • A synthesis requirement — a report, concept map, or presentation — that makes students integrate across sources Concept mapping improves learning. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Open hypermedia environments frequently produce disorientation ("lost in hyperspace") and extraneous cognitive load, degrading learning relative to structured sequences Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-M]
  • Learners with low prior knowledge struggle to judge link relevance and source quality; without curation they flounder or settle on superficial resources [-M]
  • Curation is labor-intensive: links rot, and quality control across many resources is an ongoing burden
  • Choice without constraints can overwhelm working memory; too many options reduces rather than increases engagement [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Curated pathways: instructor-selected link clusters with recommended sequences — lower autonomy, lower risk
  • Choice boards: students select from a bounded menu of exploration options (Choice Boards)
  • Student-built hypertext: learners author the links themselves, annotating why connections matter [~M]
  • Layered depth: core content for all, with optional "go deeper" links for students who finish early or want more

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who have enough schema to evaluate which links are relevant [~M]
  • Self-directed adolescents and adults pursuing inquiry connected to personal interests Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Novices need more scaffolding — guiding questions, recommended starting links, and Coaching during exploration [-S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Research and information-evaluation skills: locating, assessing, and citing multiple sources
  • Knowledge integration: connecting ideas across perspectives and sources Multiple varied cases support flexible knowledge structures. [+M]
  • Learner autonomy and self-regulated inquiry

Instructions¶

  1. Define a guiding question or problem that the linked resources can genuinely illuminate.
  2. Curate a bounded set of resources (roughly 5–15), annotated with why each is worth visiting (Assigned Readings).
  3. Provide an advance organizer or concept map skeleton students will fill in as they explore (Advance Organizers).
  4. Give structured exploration time with checkpoints — brief Check-Ins where students report what they found and how sources agree or conflict.
  5. Require a synthesis product that cites at least three sources and makes an argued connection (Case Studies can anchor the product).
  6. Debrief: have students share the most valuable link they found and justify its relevance, building collective curation skills.

Related Strategies¶

  • Inquiry-based learning — hyperlinked teaching is a resource-supported variant; the same guidance caveats apply
  • Case-based learning — cases give hyperlinked exploration a concrete anchor
  • Web-supported flipped learning — linked pre-class resources enact the same curation function
  • Curate and connect open education resources for making, re-formatted and linked to standards, to meet champions' demand for student project guides

Examples¶

  • Wikipedia — the canonical hyperlinked text; classroom uses that assign a bounded "link walk" with a synthesis note outperform unstructured browsing.
  • HyperDocs (hyperdocs.co) — teacher-designed documents with curated links, tasks, and reflection prompts; a packaged form of hyperlinked teaching common in K-12.
  • Khan Academy (khanacademy.org) — related-concept links alongside practice let students branch to prerequisite or extension content on demand.
