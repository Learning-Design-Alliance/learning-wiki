Draw Time When Felt Emotion¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 causal), q3–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners are given an emotion prompt (e.g., "a time you felt proud," "a time you felt frustrated"), draw a picture depicting a personal episode when they experienced that emotion, and then share or discuss their drawings with classmates. Drawing externalizes an autobiographical emotional memory into a concrete, discussable artifact, lowering the verbal and social barriers that pure discussion of feelings can impose.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines Activation of personal experience with structured emotional reflection. Externalizing emotion through drawing supports emotion regulation and processing: expressive engagement with emotional material is associated with improved well-being and self-understanding Expressive writing about emotional experiences improves physical and psychological health outcomes. [+M], and drawing offers a parallel, lower-verbal channel for the same mechanism. Sharing drawings with peers builds perspective-taking and Building Empathy, since learners must interpret others' emotional experiences from visual cues Empathy-focused interventions improve intergroup attitudes and understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Drawing materials (paper, markers, or a digital canvas)
  • A clear, specific emotion prompt — one emotion per round, not "draw how you feel" in general
  • A sharing structure with explicit norms (voluntary disclosure, respectful listening) (Check-In)
  • Time for individual reflection before and after sharing (Individual Reflection)

Constraints¶

  • Learners who are introverted, culturally reserved about emotional disclosure, or processing trauma may find public sharing of emotional memories distressing; sharing must be voluntary [~M] — forced disclosure of negative emotional events can increase distress rather than relieve it
  • Drawing skill anxiety can divert attention from the emotional content; facilitators should stress that artistic quality is irrelevant
  • Without a debrief or naming step, the activity stays at the level of recall and does not build emotional vocabulary or regulation strategies [-W]
  • Group sharing of negative-emotion drawings can create social comparison or contagion effects in poorly normed classrooms [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Private variant: drawings are made but shared only in pairs or kept private, with a written reflection instead of public sharing
  • Prompt ladder: begin with positive emotions (pride, joy) before moving to harder ones (frustration, loneliness)
  • Literature integration: learners draw the emotion of a character in a text, then connect it to a personal episode — bridging Case Studies and personal experience
  • Digital variant: drawing apps or whiteboard tools for remote settings

Target Learners¶

  • Young learners and emergent writers, for whom drawing is a more accessible expressive channel than prose [~M]
  • Learners building emotional vocabulary and self-awareness, including those in social-emotional learning programs
  • Multilingual learners who can communicate experience visually before they can narrate it verbally
  • Less suitable as-is for learners with acute emotional distress around the prompted emotion; offer an alternative prompt

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: recognizing, naming, and distinguishing emotions
  • Self-assessment and metacognitive awareness of one's own affective responses
  • Empathetic understanding of peers' experiences
  • Narrative and descriptive language development when drawings are later described in words

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce the target emotion with a brief definition and a Think-Aloud-style example from the facilitator ("A time I felt nervous was my first day teaching…").
  2. Give quiet individual reflection time (2–3 minutes) for learners to recall a specific episode (Individual Reflection).
  3. Learners draw the episode (8–10 minutes); emphasize content over artistic quality.
  4. Learners share in pairs or small groups, describing what happened and how it felt (Act It Out or verbal narration both work).
  5. Debrief as a whole class: name the emotion, note how the same emotion can arise from different situations, and connect to the day's learning topic (Check-In norms apply).

Related Strategies¶

  • What's My Emotion? Game Check-In — a faster, game-based alternative for building emotion vocabulary
  • Acting-Role-Play — enacts emotional episodes physically rather than visually
  • Individual Reflection — the private-processing component that pairs with sharing

Examples¶

  • Responsive Classroom morning meeting — a feelings check-in where students draw or select an image representing their current emotion before academic work begins (https://www.responsiveclassroom.org).
  • Second Step (SEL curriculum) — emotion-recognition lessons that pair visual representations of feelings with personal connection prompts (https://www.secondstep.org).
  • Art therapy–informed classroom practice — drawing-to-regulate interventions in which students depict a stressful event and then reframe it, based on findings that structured art-making supports mood repair (Drake & Winner, 2012).
