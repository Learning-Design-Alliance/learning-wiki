Goal Setting With Students¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Goal setting with students is the practice of jointly establishing specific, measurable, and appropriately challenging learning targets — rather than imposing them — so that students understand what success looks like and can monitor their own progress toward it. It is carried out through structured conversations, written goal records, and periodic check-ins in which goals are revisited, revised, and connected to feedback.

Design Implications¶

Goal setting is one of the most robustly supported motivational interventions in organizational and educational psychology: specific and challenging goals reliably produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory. [+S]. In educational settings, its power comes from coupling goals to self-regulated learning: goals give learners a reference standard against which to evaluate progress, which feeds the forethought–performance–reflection cycle [+M]. Involving students in setting the goals matters — participation supports perceived autonomy, which sustains intrinsic motivation and commitment to the goal Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Goals that are specific and proximal ("complete three practice problems with correct unit conversions by Friday") rather than distal and vague ("get better at chemistry")
  • A challenge level calibrated to current ability — goals must be difficult enough to motivate but attainable enough to sustain commitment
  • A mechanism for monitoring progress and connecting it to feedback, so goals function as evaluation standards rather than decoration
  • Student voice in the goal-setting conversation, not merely ratification of teacher-set targets

Constraints¶

  • Goals imposed without student input can undermine autonomy and reduce intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [-M]
  • Overly difficult or unattainable goals lead to abandonment and disengagement, especially for low-efficacy learners [~S]
  • Goals focused narrowly on performance outcomes (grades, rankings) can encourage surface strategies and reduce interest compared with learning-oriented goals [~M]
  • Public goal sharing can backfire for some learners: announcing goals can create a premature sense of accomplishment [~W]
  • Goal setting alone, without progress monitoring or feedback, shows weak effects — the goal must be embedded in a self-regulation cycle [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual goal conferences: brief one-to-one conversations setting a weekly learning target, common in writing conferences and advisory systems
  • Goal-setting journals / learning logs: students record goals, monitor progress, and reflect at intervals — a core component of self-regulated learning instruction
  • Contract grading / learning contracts: goals formalized in an agreement between student and instructor
  • Proximal sub-goals within larger projects: breaking a term project into staged targets with checkpoints
  • Digital dashboards: platforms like Khan Academy display progress against goals, externalizing the monitoring function

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling or low-achieving students benefit disproportionately: a structured goal-setting intervention improved GPA and satisfaction for academically struggling college students Morisano et al. (2010) elite-struggling students goal-setting intervention. [+S]
  • Adolescents and adults with emerging self-regulation skills, who need external structure to scaffold the forethought phase [~M]
  • Less effective as a standalone for very young children, who lack the metacognitive capacity to monitor against abstract targets [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: building the habit of forethought, monitoring, and self-evaluation
  • Sustained engagement on long-horizon tasks (projects, portfolios, independent reading)
  • Metacognitive awareness of one's own progress and gaps
  • Not well suited to rapid, discrete skill acquisition where immediate practice dominates

Instructions¶

  1. Open with a diagnostic conversation or check-in to establish the student's current performance and what they want to achieve.
  2. Co-construct one to three specific, proximal goals; make the success criteria explicit and visible (link to assessment for learning practices, which improve achievement when criteria are transparent Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]).
  3. Break distal goals into proximal sub-goals with scheduled checkpoints.
  4. Establish a monitoring routine — learning log, dashboard, or conference — where progress is compared against the goal and feedback is actioned.
  5. Revise goals based on evidence: raise challenge when goals are met early, recalibrate when they are out of reach.

Related Strategies¶

  • Achievable micro-goals — the proximal-goal variant: very short-horizon targets that keep commitment high
  • Action planning — the "how" companion: converting goals into concrete steps and schedules
  • Check-ins — the recurring touchpoint that keeps goals alive rather than set-and-forgotten
  • Academic choice (planning, working, reflecting) — a broader structure in which goal setting is the planning phase

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy — learners set mastery goals per skill; the dashboard tracks progress against them, externalizing the monitoring loop.
  • Morisano et al. (2010) — struggling university students wrote structured goal-setting essays (elaborated goals, plans, contingencies); the intervention raised GPA relative to controls. doi:10.1037/a0020720
  • Expeditionary Learning / EL Education (https://eleducation.org) — K-12 crews set and track individual and collective learning targets each expedition cycle, with revision at checkpoints.
  • Writing conferences in workshop models (e.g., Units of Study, https://www.unitsofstudy.com) — teacher and student set one specific writing goal per conference, revisited at the next.
