Co-Design Tools for Standards and Interest Balancing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 unmarked) · 1 study (1 qualitative), q2 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Curriculum co-design teams often face two goals in tension: covering required standards, and connecting to what students and their communities actually find compelling. This strategy sequences a specific set of tools that make both goals visible and negotiable at the same time, rather than treating standards coverage as a fixed constraint decided in advance and student interest as an afterthought layered on top. The tools work through three interacting mechanisms: deliberation (confronting different perspectives on what standards mean and what counts as compelling), anticipation (rehearsing a unit's launch to test whether it actually elicits the intended questions), and reflection (explicit group review of how well each tool served its purpose).

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A mixed-role design team (the source study used researchers, classroom teachers, and a district science coordinator) so that standards expertise, classroom-practice knowledge, and system-level constraints are all represented
  • Electronic student-interest surveys administered to co-designers' own current students, disaggregated by demographic group (race, gender, home language) so the team can see whose interests are and aren't being captured
  • Dedicated workshop time to rehearse a unit's opening ("Anchoring Phenomena Routine") with other co-designers role-playing students, to surface anticipated questions before the unit reaches real students

Constraints¶

  • A single interest survey is not sufficient on its own — the source team found survey wording could bias results (an active-voice framing of a question outranked a passive-voice version of the same idea) and recommended supplementing surveys with focus groups or interviews
  • Team demographic homogeneity limits the phenomena the team can imagine as compelling to non-represented student groups, even with disaggregated survey data
  • Standards alignment sometimes had to override strong measured student interest (a highly-rated phenomenon was dropped because it matched middle-school, not high-school, standards)

Target Learners¶

  • Co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data, and launch rehearsal support balancing standards alignment with student interests

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Producing curriculum units (see Storyline Science Curriculum Design) that are simultaneously standards-aligned and grounded in genuine student and community interest

Instructions¶

  1. Bundle standards: a small team groups related performance expectations into a teachable unit.
  2. Unpack standards: the full unit-lead team analyzes the bundle in detail (standard text, clarification statements, assessment boundaries) to identify what students must know and do, and how understanding should build lesson by lesson.
  3. Brainstorm candidate phenomena: generate 7-10 possible anchoring phenomena that are both potentially compelling and require the target ideas to explain.
  4. Write target explanations (backward design): draft what students should be able to explain by the unit's end for each candidate phenomenon; discard any phenomenon that doesn't actually require the target ideas.
  5. Survey student interest: administer an electronic survey to co-designers' current students rating interest in and perceived community importance of each remaining candidate phenomenon, disaggregated by demographic group.
  6. Decide on the driving question: as a full group, analyze the unpacking, target explanations, and disaggregated survey data together; use straw polls and discussion to reach consensus on one anchoring phenomenon serving both standards and interest goals.
  7. Rehearse the launch: unit teams draft and test their unit's opening activity with other co-designers acting "in student hat," capturing anticipated student questions.
  8. Co-create the storyline: using a template (lesson-level questions, phenomena, standards, and student "punchlines"), draft the unit from the student perspective.

Examples¶

  • A unit-lead team of 12 co-designers (researchers, teachers, a district coordinator) plus 15-19 additional workshop teachers ran this process across two annual workshops (2019 in-person, 2020 virtual) over roughly two years, ultimately selecting a space-exploration anchoring phenomenon over an equally interest-rated air-pollution phenomenon because air pollution better matched middle-school standards.
