Through the Eyes of the People in a Story¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (5 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners select a character from a short story or novel and retell or re-enact the main events from that character's point of view, articulating the character's thoughts, emotions, and perceptions of other characters and events. The strategy combines close reading, reflective writing, and role-play or presentation, converting narrative comprehension into perspective-taking work. It is carried out through individual reflection on the chosen character, structured discussion with peers who adopted other characters, and optional in-character performance.

Design Implications¶

Perspective-taking tasks deepen narrative comprehension by requiring learners to build a causal model of a character's motivations rather than merely tracking plot events, and reading literary fiction has been linked to improved theory-of-mind performance Kidd & Castano's literary fiction finding. [+M]. The strategy also functions as an empathy intervention: sustained engagement with characters' inner lives supports empathy and perspective-taking gains, particularly when learners must articulate rather than merely infer those inner states Fiction reading is associated with empathy and perspective-taking. [+M]. Because learners must justify their interpretation from textual evidence, the activity doubles as a Self-Explanation task Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A short story or book rich enough to support multiple defensible character perspectives (multiple viewpoints, internal conflict, or an unreliable or peripheral narrator)
  • Individual reflection time with a written or spoken artifact (monologue, diary entry, letter) that makes the character's reasoning explicit
  • A discussion structure in which learners who chose different characters confront the same events from conflicting viewpoints (Class Discussion)
  • Instructor guidance distinguishing text-grounded inference from projection of the learner's own biases

Constraints¶

  • Learners frequently impose their own values and biases on the character, producing projection rather than perspective-taking; without evidence-based prompts the exercise can reinforce rather than challenge existing assumptions [~M]
  • Weak readers may lack the comprehension baseline needed to model a character's mental states, causing the task to collapse into plot summary [-M]
  • Empathy gains from a single session are fragile; effects on dispositional empathy require repeated engagement across texts [~W]
  • If all learners choose the same protagonist, the contrasting-perspectives discussion — the mechanism driving abstraction about the story — is lost Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written form: in-character diary, letter, or interior monologue (lower-risk, suitable for introverts and large classes)
  • Performance form: hot-seating, monologue, or in-character debate (Act It Out)
  • Comparative form: two learners relive the same scene from opposing characters, then reconcile the accounts
  • Extension: relive events from a minor or silent character (a servant, a bystander), which maximizes inferential demand

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescent and adult literature students who can sustain inference about unspoken mental states [~M]
  • Learners developing social-emotional competencies; fiction-based perspective work shows the strongest theory-of-mind effects for readers of literary (character-driven) rather than plot-driven texts Reading literary fiction improves theory of mind. [+M]
  • Emerging readers need scaffolding (character trait charts, evidence prompts) before the perspective task is tractable [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Narrative comprehension: causal modeling of character motivation and theme
  • Perspective-taking and empathy: adopting and articulating another's viewpoint Fiction reading is associated with empathy and perspective-taking. [+M]
  • Textual evidence use: grounding interpretation in specific passages Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]
  • Oral communication and discussion skills

Instructions¶

  1. Assign the text and let learners choose (or draw) a character; deliberately distribute choices across minor and major characters (Assigned Readings, Choice Boards).
  2. Learners re-read key scenes marking every passage that reveals what their character knows, wants, or feels (Annotating).
  3. Learners produce a first-person artifact — monologue, diary entry, or letter — reliving the main events and explicitly stating the character's reasoning.
  4. Convene a mixed-character discussion of a shared pivotal scene; each learner speaks and answers questions in character (Class Discussion).
  5. Debrief out of character: compare accounts, identify where perspectives conflicted, and evaluate which interpretations the text best supports.

Related Elements¶

  • Assigned Readings — supplies the shared text
  • Class Discussion — the contrasting-perspectives forum where the strategy's main payoff occurs
  • Choice Boards — structures character selection with autonomy
  • Self-Explanation — the evidence-based justification step
  • Act — in-character performance option

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Case-Based Learning — shares the structure of adopting a stakeholder position within a rich scenario
  • Discussion-Based Learning — the mixed-character debate enacts this pattern with narrative material

Examples¶

  • Students read To Kill a Mockingbird and relive the trial from the perspective of Scout Finch, Atticus Finch, or Tom Robinson, then debate the testimony in character.
  • Students read The Great Gatsby and reconstruct the same party scene as Nick Carraway, Jay Gatsby, or Daisy Buchanan, comparing how each account assigns blame and motive.
  • Facing History and Ourselves (https://www.facinghistory.org) uses identity-centered perspective-taking routines on historical narratives, pairing close reading with in-character reflection and discussion.
