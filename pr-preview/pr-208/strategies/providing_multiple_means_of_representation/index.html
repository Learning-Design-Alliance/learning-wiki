Providing Multiple Means of Representation¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 14 studies (7 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 14 report an effect size

Description¶

Providing multiple means of representation is the first principle of Universal Design for Learning (UDL): content is offered in more than one format — text, audio, video, diagrams, worked models — so that perceptual, linguistic, and prior-knowledge differences do not become barriers to access. It goes beyond redundancy: each representation should be pedagogically functional, not merely a duplicate, and should include options that adjust vocabulary, syntax, and background demands.

Design Implications¶

Multiple representations work because they distribute information across verbal and visual channels and reduce the chance that a single format's demands (decoding, listening comprehension, unfamiliar vocabulary) block access to the content itself Presenting material across visual and verbal channels improves learning over single-channel presentation. [+S]. But added representations are not free: each channel consumes working memory, so formats must be integrated and coordinated rather than scattered Cognitive overload degrades learning when total demands exceed working memory capacity. [+S]. Irrelevant or decorative media actively harms learning Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • At least two genuinely functional representations of core content (e.g., text plus annotated diagram, not text plus decorative image)
  • Accessible formats: captions, alt text, screen-reader-compatible structure, transcripts
  • Vocabulary support — glossaries, definitions, plain-language alternatives (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  • Activation of relevant background knowledge, since a representation only helps if learners can connect it to what they know (Activation)

Constraints¶

  • Redundant on-screen text read aloud verbatim impairs learning by overloading the visual and auditory channels simultaneously [-S] — narration plus identical text is worse than narration alone for fluent readers
  • Adding formats without integration (e.g., a diagram far from its explaining text) forces costly visual search and splits attention [-S]
  • Multiple representations increase production and maintenance cost; applying them to low-stakes or easily-accessed content wastes effort better spent elsewhere [~M]
  • For learners with strong prior knowledge, extra scaffolding representations can slow processing Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts (expertise reversal). [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full UDL implementation: all materials designed from the start with format options (Accommodations built in rather than retrofitted)
  • On-demand alternatives: a primary representation with optional supports — glossaries, translations, Audiobooks, Advance Organizers
  • Learner-selected format: Choice Boards letting students pick text, audio, or video versions of the same content
  • Segmenting and chunking: breaking dense media into learner-paced segments (Chunking)

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with sensory, language, or processing differences who are blocked by a single default format [+M]
  • Novices, who benefit from concrete visual models paired with verbal explanation Presenting material across visual and verbal channels improves learning over single-channel presentation. [+S]
  • Multilingual learners, who benefit from vocabulary scaffolds and visual support alongside text
  • Less beneficial to over-scaffold experts, who may find redundant formats inefficient Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts (expertise reversal). [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Foundational knowledge acquisition: making content accessible regardless of entry format
  • Conceptual understanding: multiple representations support building flexible mental models Analogical and multi-case comparison improves transfer. [+M]
  • Transfer: seeing the same idea in multiple forms supports recognizing it in novel contexts Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the core content and the barriers embedded in the default representation (reading level, sensory modality, assumed background).
  2. Design a second, functionally different representation — e.g., an annotated diagram, a narrated model, or an Analogy — and integrate it spatially and temporally with the first.
  3. Add vocabulary and background supports: glossaries, plain-language summaries, Advance Organizers, or Activation prompts.
  4. Ensure accessibility: captions, transcripts, alt text, logical heading structure.
  5. Manage load: segment media, apply Chunking, and cut decorative media Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning. [+S].
  6. Offer format choice where feasible (Choice Boards) and check that each option leads to the same learning goal.
