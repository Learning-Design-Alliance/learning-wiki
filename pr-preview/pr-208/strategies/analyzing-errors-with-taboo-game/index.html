Analyzing Errors (with Taboo game)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 1 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners play Taboo — describing a target term without using its most obvious associated words — to practice choosing and deploying keywords, then the instructor leads a structured analysis of the errors and dead ends players encountered. The instructor names the underlying strategies (finding synonyms, giving precise descriptions, abandoning low-yield keywords) so learners connect their mistakes to generalizable search and vocabulary tactics rather than treating them as one-off failures.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines game-based practice with error analysis: the game generates authentic errors under time pressure, and the debrief converts those errors into instructional material. Studying one's own errors is most effective when learners explain why an approach failed, not just that it did Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M], and self-explaining during that analysis deepens conceptual understanding beyond what corrective feedback alone achieves Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. Errors that learners commit with confidence are especially valuable for analysis, because they persist unexamined otherwise High-confidence errors improve retention when analyzed. [+W].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A Taboo-style game (physical cards or a digital adaptation) with target terms relevant to the discipline
  • Sample keyword lists for commonly searched topics, so the debrief can contrast effective and ineffective choices
  • A facilitator who can name strategies in the moment and guide reflection afterward (Provide Feedback)
  • Time for structured reflection — the error analysis, not the game itself, is where the learning consolidates

Constraints¶

  • The game may not transfer well to other contexts; keyword strategies learned in Taboo do not automatically generalize to database searching or other retrieval tasks without explicit bridging [-W]
  • If the debrief is skipped or rushed, the activity collapses into entertainment with little durable learning [-M]
  • Learners with very limited vocabulary in the target language may experience the game as high-anxiety performance rather than productive practice [~M]
  • Time pressure that is too intense increases cognitive load and produces random guessing rather than strategic choices [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reverse Taboo: learners identify the target term from a description, practicing recognition rather than production
  • Team play with rotating describers increases Practice opportunities per learner
  • Digital adaptations (e.g., an online taboo-card generator) allow asynchronous or remote use
  • The error-analysis frame generalizes beyond Taboo to any activity that surfaces learner errors — search-log reviews, failed database queries, incorrect problem solutions

Target Learners¶

  • Learners at any grade level who are developing search and vocabulary strategies; the game format lowers the affective cost of making errors publicly
  • English language learners, for whom synonym generation and precise description are directly transferable language strategies [+W]
  • Learners who hold confident but ineffective search habits — the game exposes these for analysis High-confidence errors improve retention when analyzed. [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural skill: choosing effective keywords for information retrieval
  • Strategic knowledge: knowing when to abandon a keyword and switch to synonyms or description
  • Metacognitive awareness: recognizing one's own error patterns and strategy choices

Instructions¶

  1. Prepare taboo cards using terms learners will actually need to search for in the discipline; include sample keyword lists for the debrief.
  2. Play rounds of Taboo in small teams, with learners rotating as describer (Practice).
  3. During play, note specific moments where a describer's keyword choice succeeded or failed (Provide Feedback).
  4. After each round, pause for individual reflection: learners write which keyword attempts failed and why (Individual Reflection).
  5. Debrief as a group: the instructor names the strategies observed — synonym substitution, narrowing by context, precise description — and contrasts them with the errors (Peer Discussion).
  6. Bridge to transfer: have learners apply the named strategies to a real search task in the course context and compare results against their pre-game habits.

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — the game provides repeated, low-stakes retrieval and production attempts
  • Individual Reflection — the private written step where errors are explained before group discussion
  • Peer Discussion — comparing strategy choices across players widens the pool of analyzed errors
  • Provide Feedback — instructor naming of strategies targets the process level, where feedback has its largest effects Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]

Tools¶

  • Physical Taboo card decks or discipline-made card sets
  • Online taboo-card generators for custom vocabulary
  • Shared documents or whiteboards for recording keyword attempts during the debrief

Examples¶

  • An information-literacy session in a first-year composition course: students play Taboo with research topics, then the librarian debriefs failed keyword attempts and has students redo a database search using the named synonym and narrowing strategies.
  • An ESL vocabulary class uses Taboo with academic vocabulary; the error analysis focuses on circumlocution — describing around an unknown word — as a compensatory communication strategy.
