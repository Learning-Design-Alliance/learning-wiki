Relationship Skills Identification and Support¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (3 review, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 0 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy involves educators systematically observing students to identify strengths and challenges in relationship skills — effective communication, cooperation, conflict management, and resisting inappropriate social pressure — and then implementing targeted supports. The approach is strengths-based: existing social competencies are leveraged as entry points for addressing weaker areas. Supports include pairing students with positive role models, designing group work around common interests, making communication protocols visible, explicitly teaching skills through literature and role-play, and having students reflect on which strategies work for them.

Design Implications¶

Identification must precede support: generic social-emotional programming misses students whose needs are specific (e.g., conflict de-escalation vs. initiating collaboration). Social and emotional competencies are malleable and teachable, and targeted SEL programming produces measurable gains in skills, attitudes, and academic performance Durlak et al.'s meta-analysis of 213 school-based SEL programs. [+S]. Supports work best when they are embedded in authentic academic and social tasks rather than delivered as isolated lessons [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Structured observation routines and rubrics that define what competent communication, cooperation, and conflict management look like behaviorally
  • A repertoire of targeted strategies (role assignment, peer pairing, explicit protocols, literature-based discussion) matched to specific skill gaps
  • Student reflection on which supports help, so adjustments are data-driven rather than assumption-driven
  • A safe classroom climate; students will not practice vulnerable social skills where peer risk is high

Constraints¶

  • Misidentification is costly: labeling a student as "lacking relationship skills" can become a self-fulfilling expectation and depress the student's own social self-concept [-M]
  • Supports fail when the identified skill is practiced only in artificial settings; skills trained in pull-out sessions often do not transfer to unstructured peer contexts (playground, lunchroom) [-M]
  • Grouping struggling students together for "social skills practice" without skilled modeling can reinforce maladaptive peer norms [-W]
  • Effectiveness depends on student engagement and on educator capacity for accurate, non-judgmental observation; under-resourced classrooms may lack time for individualized supports [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class universal protocols (e.g., sentence stems for disagreement) vs. individualized supports for identified students
  • Peer-mediated approaches (structured pairings, cooperative roles) vs. adult-mediated approaches (Coaching, conferencing)
  • Literature-based and case-based teaching, where characters' social dilemmas become objects of analysis, vs. direct behavioral rehearsal through role-play

Target Learners¶

  • All students benefit from visible communication norms, but students with identified social challenges benefit most from targeted, strengths-based supports [+M]
  • Students with strong relationship skills can serve as peer models; pairing leverages their competence while building the partner's skills [~M] — pairing works only when the stronger peer is coached to scaffold rather than dominate
  • Students whose social difficulties stem from skill deficits (not motivation) respond best to explicit instruction; students with performance avoidance need low-risk practice structures first [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Communication: expressing ideas, listening, negotiating meaning
  • Cooperation and collaboration: shared goals, role negotiation, mutual accountability
  • Conflict management: de-escalation, perspective-taking, repair
  • Social awareness and self-efficacy: students who experience social success persist more in academic settings Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Observe and identify. Use structured observation and rubrics across multiple contexts (structured work, transitions, unstructured time) to map each student's relationship-skill strengths and challenges; avoid single-incident judgments.
  2. Name strengths explicitly. Communicate identified strengths to the student to build social self-efficacy before targeting gaps.
  3. Select matched supports. Choose from: peer pairing with a positive role model; group assignments built around common interests; visible communication protocols (sentence stems, turn-taking norms); literature and case studies that externalize social dilemmas for analysis.
  4. Model and rehearse. Demonstrate target interactions (e.g., disagreeing respectfully) through role-play and active listening practice before students apply skills in real group work.
  5. Coach in context. Use Coaching during authentic collaborative tasks, prompting and debriefing in the moment rather than after the fact.
  6. Reflect and adjust. Have students reflect on which strategies helped (self-monitoring improves self-regulation [+M]); the educator adjusts supports based on reflection and continued observation.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active listening — the core communication behavior most relationship-skill supports target
  • Acting-role-play — low-risk rehearsal of social interactions before live application
  • Check-ins — routine structures that surface relational states and give students practice naming them
  • Building empathy — perspective-taking underlies conflict management and cooperation

Examples¶

  • Digital Promise Learner Variability Project — documents relationship skills as a mappable learner factor with research-grounded supports for educators.
  • Responsive Classroom — embeds relationship-skill supports in daily structures (morning meeting, teacher language, collaborative norms) rather than pull-out lessons.
  • Edutopia SEL collection — practitioner accounts of strengths-based identification and peer-role-model strategies.
  • CASEL — framework defining relationship skills and schoolwide implementation guidance.
