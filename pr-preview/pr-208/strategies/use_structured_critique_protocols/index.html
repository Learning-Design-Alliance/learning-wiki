Use Structured Critique Protocols¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q3 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Structured critique protocols are explicit routines for peer or instructor critique of work-in-progress. Rather than asking learners to "give feedback," the protocol specifies a sequence — e.g., clarify, then value, then raise concerns and suggest ("ladder of feedback"); or T-A-G (Tell something you liked, Ask a question, Give a suggestion) — so critique is specific, actionable, and socially safe. Protocols are typically paired with criteria or rubrics and a revision opportunity, making critique a formative loop rather than a verdict.

Design Implications¶

Structure converts critique from opinion exchange into criterion-referenced analysis, which improves both the quality of feedback given and the quality of revisions made Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, what next?" improves achievement. [+S]. Protocols also make evaluative reasoning itself visible and practicable, so learners build judgment, not just receive it Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality. [+M]. The revision step is essential: critique without a subsequent opportunity to act on it produces little learning.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A shared, explicit set of criteria or a rubric so critique targets the work, not taste (Assessment)
  • A named protocol with defined turns, sentence stems, and time limits
  • Work that is genuinely in progress — critique of finished work invites defensiveness and wastes the revision window
  • A scheduled revision or iteration step following the critique (Practice)

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured or checklist-only evaluation online is ineffective; generic checklists without exemplars or dialogue do not improve work Checklist-based evaluation without exemplars or dialogue is ineffective online. [-M]
  • Critique of finished, unrevisable work yields little benefit — the formative value depends on a live revision loop
  • Novice critics often focus on surface features unless the protocol and criteria direct attention to substance
  • Without norms of psychological safety, critique triggers defensiveness; protocols with "value first" steps mitigate but do not eliminate this

Implementation Variability¶

  • Ladder of feedback (Perkins): clarify → value → concerns → suggestions; suits design and writing critique
  • TAG / Two Stars and a Wish: lightweight stems for younger or novice critics
  • Gallery walk / silent critique: written sticky-note critique around displayed work; scales to large groups
  • Critical friends / tuning protocols: extended, facilitator-led examination of one piece of work against shared standards
  • Studio critique (art/design education): public, instructor-moderated critique of work-in-progress

Target Learners¶

  • Learners producing authentic artifacts (writing, designs, code, arguments) who can revise Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality. [+M]
  • Adolescents and adults with enough evaluative vocabulary to apply criteria; younger learners need heavily scaffolded stems
  • Less effective for learners with no prior exposure to the domain's quality standards — criteria and exemplars must come first

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Quality judgment: internalizing criteria for "good work" in a domain
  • Revision and iteration skills
  • Giving and receiving actionable feedback (a transferable professional skill)
  • Deepening understanding through explaining and defending work Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Establish criteria and, ideally, show exemplars at multiple quality levels before any critique (Assessment for Learning).
  2. Ensure work is at a revisable draft stage; publish the protocol and sentence stems in advance.
  3. Run the critique in the protocol's sequence — e.g., clarify, value, concerns, suggestions — with time limits per turn (Class Discussion or written gallery format).
  4. Have authors paraphrase or record the feedback rather than rebut it; defer defense to a later turn.
  5. Schedule immediate revision time so critique converts into action (Practice).
  6. Debrief the critique itself — which comments were most useful and why — to build evaluative skill over time (Articulation).

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer Review / Peer Feedback strategies — structured critique is the protocol layer over peer feedback
  • Use Rubrics and Exemplars — supplies the criteria that make critique criterion-referenced
  • Revision and Iteration cycles — the follow-on step that makes critique formative

Examples¶

  • High Tech High (San Diego) — project-based schools use gallery walks and critique protocols ("kind, specific, helpful") with multiple draft cycles before public exhibition of student work (https://www.hightechhigh.org).
  • Harvard Project Zero "Ladder of Feedback" — a widely used protocol sequencing clarify → value → concerns → suggestions for classroom and professional critique.
  • National School Reform Faculty protocols — tuning and critical-friends protocols used for examining student and professional work (https://www.nsrfharmony.org).
  • RISD studio critique — art and design education's long-standing model of public, criterion-anchored critique of work-in-progress.
