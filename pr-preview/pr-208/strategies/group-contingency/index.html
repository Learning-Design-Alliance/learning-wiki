Group Contingency¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

A group contingency is an arrangement in which access to a reward or privilege is contingent on behavior at the group level, rather than on each individual's behavior alone. The instructor announces a clear behavioral criterion, monitors performance, and delivers (or withholds) the shared consequence for the entire class or team. Three canonical forms exist: dependent (the group's outcome hinges on one designated student's behavior), interdependent (the group must collectively meet a criterion, e.g., a class average or shared goal), and independent (each member meeting the criterion earns the reward for themselves, but the arrangement is administered to the whole group) [Litow & Pumroy, 1975].

Design Implications¶

Group contingencies apply operant reinforcement at scale, converting peer attention into a support for rather than a distraction from target behavior Behaviorism. Meta-analytic evidence finds group contingencies among the most effective classroom management interventions, with large reductions in disruptive behavior across grade levels [Stage & Quiroz, 1998] [+S]. Their power comes from mobilizing positive peer pressure: classmates prompt and encourage the behaviors on which the shared reward depends, so the teacher no longer supplies all monitoring and reinforcement.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A precisely defined, observable target behavior and a measurable criterion (e.g., "all rows ready within 30 seconds")
  • A reward with genuine group appeal, varied over time to prevent satiation
  • Frequent, fair monitoring with visible progress indicators so students can see how the group is doing
  • Criteria calibrated so success is attainable but not automatic

Constraints¶

  • Dependent contingencies can single out one student for scapegoating or harassment if the group resents their behavior [-M] — use cautiously and pair with support for the target student
  • Overly difficult criteria produce learned helplessness and group disengagement [~M]; overly easy criteria lose their motivating function
  • Rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks students already find interesting when the reward is tangible and expected [~M] — prefer activity-based or natural reinforcers
  • If monitoring is inconsistent or perceived as unfair, the arrangement collapses into resentment rather than cooperation

Implementation Variability¶

  • Good Behavior Game: teams earn penalties for disruptive behavior; the team with fewest wins — one of the most replicated interdependent formats, with effects persisting into later grades [+S]
  • Mystery Motivator: the reinforcer is concealed behind a random reveal, adding uncertainty to boost responding
  • Randomized dependent contingency: the student whose behavior determines the group's outcome is named only after the observation period, so every student must perform as if chosen
  • Level systems: criteria escalate as the group succeeds, fading external control over time

Target Learners¶

  • Whole classes, especially in elementary settings where group identity and peer salience are high [+S]
  • Students with attention or self-regulation difficulties, who benefit from peers' prompting and from externalized criteria [+M]
  • Use caution with dependent contingencies for students already socially marginalized; interdependent or independent formats distribute risk more evenly

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Classroom conduct and engagement: on-task behavior, transitions, noise reduction
  • Academic responding: rate and accuracy of completed work when contingencies are tied to academic criteria [+M]
  • Prosocial behavior: cooperation, turn-taking, and mutual accountability

Instructions¶

  1. Define one or two observable target behaviors and state them positively ("voices off during independent work").
  2. Choose the contingency type — interdependent for shared goals, dependent only when one student needs targeted support with peer goodwill secured.
  3. Set a criterion the group can meet roughly 70–80% of the time initially, then raise it gradually.
  4. Select a reinforcer the group values; rotate options and include activity-based rewards (extra choice time, a cooperative game).
  5. Announce the contingency, model the target behavior, and post a visible progress display.
  6. Monitor and deliver the consequence immediately and consistently; narrate group progress during the interval.
  7. Fade by lengthening intervals, shifting to delayed rewards, and eventually replacing the external contingency with self-monitoring routines.

Related Strategies¶

  • Token Economy — group contingencies are often delivered through a shared token system
  • Precorrection — prompting expected behavior before the contingency period begins reduces failures
  • Praise — teacher praise for group progress reinforces the contingency's criteria

Examples¶

  • Good Behavior Game (PAX Good Behavior Game, https://www.paxis.org) — teams of students earn points for on-task behavior; widely studied with effects on both behavior and later academic outcomes.
  • Classroom Dojo-style point systems (https://www.classdojo.com) — digital group point tallies toward a class reward; effective when criteria are collective rather than individual leaderboards.
  • Reading fluency sprint: an interdependent contingency where the class must reach a combined words-read-correctly goal to earn a shared story time.
