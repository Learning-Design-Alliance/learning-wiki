Find the Total¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Find the Total is an early-numeracy activity in which learners are shown two dot cards and asked to determine the total number of dots. In a number-talk format, learners then explain how they found the total, making addition strategies (counting all, counting on, known doubles, making ten) publicly visible and comparable. The activity pairs a concrete visual representation with verbal explanation, building fluency, mental math, and part-whole understanding simultaneously.

Design Implications¶

The strategy exploits the concreteness of dot arrangements to offload counting onto perception, reducing working-memory demand while learners construct addition schemas Chunking reduces working-memory load. [+M]. Well-structured dot configurations (e.g., dice patterns, ten-frames) allow subitizing of parts so learners can practice combining rather than counting. The explanation phase is where much of the learning occurs: articulating a strategy consolidates it and exposes learners to alternative approaches from peers Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dot cards with clear, countable arrangements (subitizable patterns, not random scatter)
  • A brief routine structure: show cards briefly (encouraging mental math rather than counting one-by-one), collect answers, then have several learners explain their strategies
  • Teacher facilitation skill: rephrasing, connecting strategies ("Who used a method like Maria's?"), and representing strategies with equations or drawings
  • Initial guidance on what an explanation sounds like, since young learners may need modeling of strategy talk
  • Dot cards with clear, countable arrangements (ten-frames, dice patterns, linear rows)
  • A brief silent-thinking period before sharing, so all learners attempt a strategy
  • A facilitation routine for eliciting and recording multiple strategies (Think-Aloud norms, revoicing, strategy labeling)
  • A way to record strategies visually (equations, arrows, annotated card images)

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on dot arrangements that are easy to count; cluttered or ambiguous arrangements push learners back into unreliable one-by-one counting [-M]
  • Learners with no addition strategies yet need explicit initial instruction before the open discussion format is productive [~M]
  • Brief flash-and-remove timing can frustrate learners who still count all; the number range and exposure time must match current fluency
  • If only one strategy is affirmed, learners stop generating flexible approaches and the routine degrades into answer-checking [-M]
  • Random or unstructured dot arrangements force one-by-one counting and undermine subitizing and strategy use [-M]
  • If the teacher demonstrates a single "correct" strategy first, learners converge on it and the strategy-comparison benefit is lost [-M]
  • Dot ranges far above learners' subitizing capacity (typically 3–5 items for young children) overload perception and reduce the activity to rote counting [~M]
  • Effectiveness depends on sustained, distributed use; a single session produces little durable fluency Distributed practice improves retention. [+S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Adjust number range (totals within 5, 10, or 20) to match learner level
  • Vary representations: ten-frames, rekenreks, finger patterns, or two separate cards vs. combined arrangements
  • Extend to subtraction ("find the difference") or missing-addend versions once addition is secure
  • Use think-pair-share before whole-group explanation to increase participation
  • Ten-frame totals: cards within ten-frames to surface "make ten" strategies
  • Doubles and near-doubles: paired identical or near-identical cards to prompt derived-fact strategies
  • Missing-addend variant: show total and one card, ask learners to find the hidden card
  • Student-created cards: learners design their own dot cards and challenge peers, shifting to production
  • Rekenrek or dot-slide variant: physical manipulatives replace cards for learners needing more concrete support

Target Learners¶

  • Pre-K to Grade 1 learners developing addition concepts and counting-on strategies
  • Learners building number sense and part-whole relationships before formal symbolic addition
  • Multilingual learners benefit from the visual anchor paired with structured math language practice [~M]
  • Pre-K through Grade 1 learners developing addition concepts and counting-on strategies
  • Learners who rely on counting-all and need structured visuals to move toward counting-on and derived facts [~M]
  • English language learners benefit from the visual anchor paired with structured math-language routines [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency: efficient, flexible mental addition
  • Conceptual understanding: part-whole relationships and composition of numbers
  • Mathematical communication: explaining and comparing solution strategies
  • Conceptual: part-whole relationships, cardinality, composition of numbers
  • Procedural fluency: mental addition within 10–20
  • Metacognitive/communication: articulating and comparing solution strategies

Instructions¶

  1. Show two dot cards briefly (2–5 seconds), then hide them so learners must form a mental image rather than count one-by-one.
  2. Ask learners to find the total silently, using Practice routines already established with smaller quantities.
  3. Collect several answers, then invite learners to explain their strategies; restate and represent each with an equation or drawing.
  4. Provide Feedback by naming the strategy ("You counted on from the bigger number") and highlighting its efficiency.
  5. Compare two strategies side by side so learners evaluate when each is useful.

  • CGI problem types — the strategy-explanation discussion mirrors Cognitively Guided Instruction, in which teachers elicit and build on children's informal strategies.
  • DreamBox Learning — digital lessons use flashed dot and ten-frame arrangements with strategy feedback, adapting number range to learner performance.
  • Number Talks (Parrish, Math Solutions) — the canonical classroom routine: dot images and ten-frame cards with whole-class strategy sharing; "Find the Total" is a standard dot-image string.

  • CGI classrooms (Carpenter et al., Children's Mathematics) — teachers pose dot-total problems and build instruction from the strategies children volunteer.
