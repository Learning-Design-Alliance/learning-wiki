Cognitive Strategy Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 13 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational), q1–q4 · 3 of 13 report an effect size

Description¶

Cognitive strategy instruction (CSI) makes the hidden mental procedures of skilled performers — summarizing, predicting, decomposing problems, monitoring comprehension — explicit and teachable. The instructor names the strategy, models its use (Think-Aloud), guides practice, and gradually transfers responsibility to learners until they deploy the strategy independently. The goal is not content mastery alone but durable, transferable procedures learners own.

Design Implications¶

CSI works because strategies reduce the working-memory and search burden of complex tasks, letting learners allocate effort to understanding rather than figuring out how to proceed Cognitive load management. Effects are strongest when instruction is explicit and sequenced — model, guided practice, independent practice — rather than when strategies are merely named or suggested Palincsar & Brown's reciprocal teaching combined modeling, guided practice, and fading to produce large comprehension gains. [+S]. Teaching the when and why of a strategy (conditional knowledge), not just its steps, is what produces transfer to new tasks [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-specified strategy with named, teachable steps (e.g., reciprocal teaching's predict–question–clarify–summarize cycle)
  • Modeling that makes expert reasoning visible, not just the strategy's outcome
  • Guided practice with coaching and corrective feedback before independent use
  • Deliberate fading of support so learners take ownership Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+S]
  • Explicit discussion of when, where, and why to use the strategy

Constraints¶

  • Strategies taught as rote sequences without conditional knowledge are applied indiscriminately or abandoned once instruction ends [-M]
  • Ineffective when the strategy is redundant with what learners already do — stronger readers gain little from comprehension-strategy training Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can burden those with more prior knowledge. [~M]
  • Overloading novices with multiple new strategies at once exceeds working memory and produces shallow acquisition of all of them [-M]
  • Requires sustained practice over weeks; brief one-off "strategy lessons" show negligible effects [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reciprocal teaching — learners take turns leading the strategy dialogue in small groups, adding peer modeling
  • Self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) — pairs strategy steps with goal-setting and self-monitoring for writing Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Embedded vs. standalone — strategies can be taught within subject-matter lessons or as separate lessons then applied; embedded instruction improves transfer
  • Prompt cards and checklists — externalize strategy steps during the guided phase, then fade

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and struggling learners who lack effective procedures for the task Strategy instruction produces its largest gains for lower-achieving students. [+S]
  • Learners with adequate foundational knowledge but poor task approach — CSI cannot substitute for missing prerequisite skills [-M]
  • Less valuable for advanced learners who already use effective strategies Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can burden those with more prior knowledge. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency in complex tasks: reading comprehension, writing, mathematical problem solving
  • Metacognitive development: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own cognition
  • Transfer: applying learned procedures to unfamiliar tasks when conditional knowledge is taught

Instructions¶

  1. Assess current approach — identify what learners already do and where their procedures break down.
  2. Name and justify the strategy — state the steps and explain when and why it helps.
  3. Model with a Think-Aloud — the instructor performs the task while verbalizing decisions, monitoring, and self-correction.
  4. Guide practice — learners execute the strategy with prompts, checklists, and coaching; feedback should target the process, not just the answer Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  5. Fade support — remove prompts and hand over the dialogue as competence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+S].
  6. Promote maintenance and transfer — have learners apply the strategy across varied tasks and self-monitor continued use.

Related Strategies¶

  • Reciprocal teaching — the canonical CSI program for reading comprehension
  • Self-regulated learning — CSI is the cognitive half; SRL adds motivational and monitoring components

Examples¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown, 1984) — small reading groups rotate the roles of predictor, questioner, clarifier, and summarizer; the teacher models each role first, then fades. Produced 30+ percentile-point comprehension gains with seventh graders.
  • SRSD for writing (Graham & Harris) — students learn genre-specific planning strategies (e.g., POW + TREE for opinion essays) alongside goal-setting and self-instruction; widely validated for students with writing difficulties.
  • Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI for math) — teachers elicit and formalize children's informal problem-solving strategies, then build instruction around them.
