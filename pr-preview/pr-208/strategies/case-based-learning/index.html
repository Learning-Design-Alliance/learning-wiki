Case Based Learning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 11 studies (6 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 4 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Case Based Learning (CBL) places learners in the role of analyst or decision-maker confronting a realistic scenario — a patient history, a business dilemma, a legal dispute, an engineering failure. Learners examine the case's details, identify the central problem, apply relevant concepts, and defend a course of action, typically through structured discussion and argumentation. Unlike Problem-Based Learning, CBL usually supplies a fuller, pre-constructed narrative rather than an open problem statement.

Design Implications¶

CBL works because it situates abstract concepts in the messy, contextualized conditions under which they must later be applied, supporting transfer and retention Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the case being analyzed, not merely read: structured prompts, small-group discussion, and required justifications convert a story into reasoning practice Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. Multiple varied cases are more effective than a single exemplar for building flexible, adaptable knowledge structures Multiple varied cases support flexible knowledge under Cognitive Flexibility Theory. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-written case with authentic complexity, ambiguity, and enough data to support multiple defensible positions
  • Explicit analysis scaffolds: guiding questions, advance organizers, or an analysis framework (e.g., issue → evidence → options → recommendation)
  • A facilitation structure — small-group work followed by whole-class debrief — so learners compare reasoning rather than only conclusions
  • Clear linkage between the case and the target concepts; cases chosen for conceptual fit, not just narrative appeal
  • A well-documented, realistic case with genuine decision points and no obvious single answer
  • Preparation materials that establish the prerequisite concepts before analysis begins
  • Structured discussion protocols or analysis frameworks (e.g., issue → analysis → recommendation)
  • A facilitator skilled at probing reasoning rather than lecturing (Coaching, Class Discussion)
  • Closure: an expert debrief or model analysis so learners can calibrate their reasoning
  • A well-written case with genuine decision points, not a thinly disguised illustration of a textbook answer
  • Preparation structures (pre-reading questions, response submissions) that ensure learners arrive having committed to an analysis
  • A facilitation plan: probing questions, board plans, and willingness to let discussion go somewhere unplanned
  • Explicit connection back to course concepts during or after discussion (Debrief)

Constraints¶

  • Cases that are engaging but conceptually thin produce lively discussion with little durable learning; alignment to learning goals must be explicit
  • Without structure, novices flounder in case complexity and extract surface stories rather than underlying principles [~M] — scaffolds should fade as analytical skill grows
  • Large-enrollment settings struggle with the discussion-intensive format; written case analyses or structured online threads are partial substitutes [~W]
  • Learners with minimal prior knowledge may lack the domain schema to interpret the case at all; brief pre-reading or activation activities are needed first Activation of prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]
  • Cases without closure or expert comparison leave novices with unresolved misconceptions — learners may encode flawed reasoning as readily as sound reasoning [-M]
  • Novices without sufficient domain knowledge can flounder in unstructured case discussion, engaging in trial-and-error search rather than principled analysis [~M]
  • A single memorable case can induce overgeneralization; learners may treat one context's solution as universal [-W]
  • Group discussion dominated by a few voices reduces the retrieval practice that drives the effect [-M]
  • Cases with an obvious single "right answer" collapse into recall exercises and forfeit the judgment-building benefit [-M]
  • Novices without adequate domain knowledge can flounder in open discussion, encoding surface details rather than principles [~M] — some prior instruction or scaffolding is usually needed first
  • Large classes without discussion infrastructure reduce the method to lecture with a story attached [-M]
  • Poorly facilitated discussion can entrench misconceptions if errors go unchallenged [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Harvard method: cold-call-driven whole-class case discussion typical of business and law schools — see Case-Based Learning: Harvard Method
  • Small-group CBL: used extensively in medical education, often with a facilitator per group (Thistlethwaite et al., 2012)
  • Trigger-short cases: brief vignettes that launch discussion, blending CBL with problem-based formats
  • Asynchronous online CBL: cases posted with staged releases of information and structured discussion boards
  • Progressive disclosure (Harvard method): information is released in stages; learners must commit to decisions before seeing consequences
  • Contrasting case sets: several short cases varying one dimension, supporting abstraction of underlying principles
  • Erroneous or flawed cases: learners diagnose what went wrong, leveraging error analysis
  • Student-authored cases: learners construct cases from their own experience, shifting the goal toward synthesis
  • Discussion-first (Harvard method): cold-calling and board-driven whole-class analysis of a case learners have prepared in advance
  • Small-group analysis: teams work a case then report out; scales better and adds peer explanation
  • Progressive disclosure: the case unfolds in stages and learners commit to decisions before seeing consequences, mirroring real decision pressure
  • Multimedia/anchored cases: video-based cases (e.g., the Jasper Woodbury series) embed data in rich narrative contexts

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have foundational concepts and need practice applying them to realistic conditions Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]
  • Professional students (medicine, law, business, teaching) preparing for judgment-intensive practice
  • Pure novices benefit less without substantial scaffolding; experts may find single well-structured cases redundant [~M]
  • Professional learners (medicine, law, business, teaching) preparing for ill-structured real-world decisions Whole-task performance improves transfer. [+M]
  • Pure novices benefit less without scaffolding; the expertise reversal effect means heavy case-based formats can outstrip what beginners can extract [~M]
  • Professional students (medicine, law, business, teaching) preparing for ill-structured practice
  • Complete novices benefit only with heavy scaffolding; pure discovery in an unfamiliar domain overloads working memory [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Applied reasoning: connecting principles to situated decisions
  • Diagnostic and judgment skills: weighing incomplete, conflicting evidence
  • Argumentation and justification: defending and revising positions under peer scrutiny
  • Flexible knowledge structures: seeing one concept across many contexts Multiple varied cases support flexible knowledge under Cognitive Flexibility Theory. [+M]
  • Applied reasoning and diagnosis: connecting principles to ambiguous situations
  • Decision-making under uncertainty: weighing trade-offs and justifying choices
  • Professional identity and judgment: practicing the role, not just the content
  • Applied reasoning: using concepts to diagnose, decide, and justify
  • Professional judgment and argumentation: weighing competing values and evidence
  • Perspective-taking: understanding stakeholder positions within a situation

Instructions¶

  1. Select or write a case aligned to target concepts, with genuine ambiguity and decision points (Case Studies)
  2. Activate relevant prior knowledge with a short pre-brief or framing question (Activation)
  3. Have learners analyze the case in small groups using an explicit framework, producing a position with justification (Application of Knowledge)
  4. Debrief in whole class, contrasting groups' reasoning and surfacing the underlying principles (Comparing Cases)
  5. Follow with a second, structurally different case so learners must adapt rather than repeat their first solution Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]

Related Strategies¶

  • Problem-Based Learning — sibling strategy; PBL starts from an open problem, CBL from a narrated situation
  • Role Play — cases can be enacted rather than analyzed, adding perspective-taking
  • Jigsaw — distributes case information across group members, forcing interdependence
  • Debrief — the reflection phase where case learning is consolidated
  • Debate — structured argumentation over case decisions
  • Peer Instruction — a scalable way to inject commitment-and-discussion cycles into large classes

Examples¶

  • Harvard Business School case method — MBA students analyze ~500 published cases (e.g., from HBP Education) in cold-call discussion sections
  • Case-Based Learning in medicine — pre-clinical courses use patient cases with staged lab results; a systematic review found CBL favored over lecture for exam performance (Thistlethwaite et al., 2012)
  • Learning by Design™ (Kolodner et al.) — middle-school science units in which learners design artifacts (vehicles, containers) and use case libraries of peers' attempts to reason about science principles
  • National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science (University at Buffalo) — peer-reviewed STEM cases with teaching notes, spanning clicker-based to discussion formats
  • Harvard Business School case method — MBA students analyze a new case daily in section discussion, with cold-calling to ensure broad participation; the canonical implementation of progressive-disclosure case teaching
  • National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science — Peer-reviewed science cases (e.g., "The Case of the Tainted Taco Shells") with teaching notes, used across undergraduate STEM
  • Problem-based medical curricula (e.g., McMaster University) — Patient cases drive tutorial-based learning of basic and clinical sciences
  • Harvard Business School — the canonical discussion-based MBA case method; every course session centers on student analysis of a prepared case (hbs.edu)
  • Harvard Business School Publishing case collections — curated cases with teaching notes used across business schools worldwide (hbsp.harvard.edu)
  • National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science (University at Buffalo) — peer-reviewed science cases with teaching notes for undergraduate STEM (nccsts.org)
  • Anchored Instruction (Jasper Woodbury series) — video-based adventure cases requiring mathematical problem solving embedded in narrative
