Connection of Prior Knowledge to New Learning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (1 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 8 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy deliberately surfaces what learners already know and links it to new content before, during, and after instruction. New information is encoded more durably when it is integrated into existing knowledge structures rather than stored in isolation; conversely, new learning that conflicts with unexamined prior conceptions is often distorted to fit them. The strategy is carried out through activation activities (pre-assessments, brainstorming, analogy, advance organizers) followed by explicit bridging — showing how the new concept extends, refines, or corrects what learners already hold.

Design Implications¶

Prior knowledge is among the strongest predictors of learning outcomes, but it cuts both ways: relevant, accurate prior knowledge accelerates learning, while misconceptions and partial knowledge can actively interfere Prior knowledge is one of the strongest individual-difference predictors of learning outcomes. [+S]. Activation alone is insufficient — learners must be prompted to elaborate the connection between old and new, not merely recall the old Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension and retention. [+M]. Because prior knowledge changes the effectiveness of any given instructional treatment, the strategy must be calibrated to the learner's actual starting point Instructional guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [~S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A diagnostic step: pre-assessment, discussion, or brainstorming that reveals what learners actually know, including misconceptions
  • An activation activity that brings relevant knowledge into working memory at the moment of instruction (Activation, Analogies, Advance Organizers)
  • Explicit bridging: the new material must be visibly connected to the activated knowledge, not presented as a separate topic afterward
  • A plan for handling misconceptions, since activation surfaces them but does not resolve them

Constraints¶

  • Activating irrelevant or tangential prior knowledge consumes working memory and can degrade learning Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes. [-M] — activation must be selective, not exhaustive
  • Learners with genuine gaps or inaccurate schemas cannot "connect" to material they do not have; activation without remediation leaves them behind
  • Over-reliance on familiar analogies can entrench misconceptions when the analogy's limits are never addressed [~M]
  • For learners with high prior knowledge, extensive activation and scaffolding become redundant and can reduce engagement Instructional guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Advance organizers — a brief expository or comparative overview presented before the material (Ausubel's original formulation)
  • Analogical bridging — mapping new concepts onto familiar domains (Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation)
  • Misconception-focused — eliciting predictions, then confronting them with evidence (used in physics and science education)
  • Experience-based — drawing on learners' lived experience and cultural knowledge, particularly valuable in diverse classrooms
  • KWL and similar routines — structured Know/Wonder/Learned charts that make connections explicit and revisitable

Target Learners¶

  • Novices with some relevant but fragmented knowledge benefit most from structured activation and bridging Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension and retention. [+M]
  • Learners with misconceptions benefit when activation is paired with cognitive conflict and correction
  • Learners with strong, well-organized prior knowledge need minimal activation; heavy scaffolding can be counterproductive Instructional guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: integrating new ideas into existing schemas
  • Retention: meaningful connections support durable memory
  • Transfer: knowledge connected to multiple contexts and prior structures is more available for application
  • Conceptual change: surfacing and revising inaccurate prior conceptions

Instructions¶

  1. Diagnose: use a pre-assessment, quick-write, or discussion to establish what learners know and believe about the topic.
  2. Activate: run a brief Activation activity (brainstorm, analogy, prediction) that brings relevant knowledge into working memory.
  3. Organize: present an Advance Organizer that frames the new material as an extension or correction of what learners already hold.
  4. Bridge: during Direct Instruction, explicitly reference the activated knowledge — "this is like X, except…" — and contrast with misconceptions where they exist.
  5. Consolidate: have learners Practice applying the new concept and articulate the connection themselves; Provide Feedback targeted at the quality of those connections.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the activation component in isolation; this strategy adds the bridging and misconception-handling work
  • Activate Background Knowledge — classroom routine variant emphasizing learner experience sharing
  • Accessing Students' Background Knowledge — diagnostic and culturally responsive variant

Examples¶

  • Bransford & Johnson's passage studies — learners who were given a relevant title ("washing clothes") before reading a vague passage comprehended and recalled far more than those given it afterward, demonstrating that activation must precede encoding.
  • Physics "Predict–Observe–Explain" routines — students predict an outcome based on their intuitive conception, observe the actual result, and reconcile the difference; widely used in interactive lecture demonstrations.
  • Annenberg Learner video-based science courses open lessons by eliciting learners' existing explanations before presenting the scientific account.
  • KWL charts in literacy instruction — students record what they Know and Want to know before reading, then return to record what they Learned, making the prior-knowledge connection explicit and assessable.
