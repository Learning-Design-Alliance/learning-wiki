Establish Guidelines for Discussing Race¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Establishing guidelines for discussing race means co-creating explicit agreements with learners before engaging in race-related dialogue, so that conversations remain respectful and productive even when emotionally charged. Common agreements include staying engaged, experiencing discomfort, speaking your truth, accepting non-closure, being verbally and physically considerate, and never asking students to speak for anyone but themselves. The practice also includes facilitation moves such as introducing a "pause of respect" after an especially honest or hard statement.

Design Implications¶

Race talk triggers identity threat and emotional arousal that can shut down productive discourse; explicit norms reduce ambiguity about acceptable behavior and support the psychological safety that open discussion depends on [~M]. Guidelines work best when co-authored rather than imposed, because negotiated norms increase learner ownership and willingness to stay in discomfort. They are a precondition for, not a substitute for, substantive content — norms alone do not build racial literacy.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A facilitator prepared to hold the space, model the agreements, and intervene when they are violated
  • Guidelines co-constructed with participants at the outset, revisited periodically
  • A physical or virtual environment where participants can speak without interruption
  • A commitment to revisit and repair when agreements are broken, not just a one-time posting of rules

Constraints¶

  • "Safe space" framing can suppress honest engagement; participants avoid risk-taking to keep the peace, producing shallow conversation [-M] — reframing toward "brave spaces" that normalize discomfort mitigates this
  • Asking students of color to educate peers or serve as racial spokespeople imposes an emotional burden and undermines the guidelines' purpose [-M]
  • Guidelines without enforcement become performative; violations that go unaddressed erode trust faster than having no guidelines at all [-W]
  • One-shot norm-setting before a single discussion has little durable effect; norms require ongoing reinforcement [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured frameworks such as Courageous Conversations About Race provide four fixed agreements (stay engaged, experience discomfort, speak your truth, accept non-closure)
  • Co-created agreements generated by the class itself, which increases buy-in but may omit protections the facilitator would include
  • Complementary structures such as Assigned Positions or written rounds that reduce the spontaneity demands of open dialogue

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents through adults; effective across K-12, higher education, and professional development contexts [~W]
  • Particularly important for mixed-race groups where identity threat and misreading of intent are most likely [~M]
  • Learners low in prior experience with interracial dialogue benefit most from explicit norms; experienced participants may find heavy structure constraining [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Building capacity for productive dialogue across difference (Building Empathy)
  • Perspective-taking and understanding of diverse racial experiences
  • Metacognitive and affective self-regulation: tolerating discomfort, monitoring one's own reactions

Instructions¶

  1. Before the first discussion, facilitate a norm-setting session in which participants propose and agree to guidelines; add facilitator-suggested agreements (e.g., "speak from the I," "what is shared here stays here") that the group misses.
  2. Post the agreements visibly and open each discussion with a brief re-reading or Check-In to re-anchor the group.
  3. Structure the dialogue itself with Class Discussion protocols or Assigned Positions so that turn-taking is governed by the norms, not by status or confidence.
  4. Model the agreements as facilitator — especially the "pause of respect" after a hard statement, and redirecting any request that a student speak for their racial group.
  5. Close with reflection on how the agreements held, and revise them as the group's capacity grows.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Listening — the individual skill the group agreements are designed to protect and require
  • Address Biases in the Use of Language and Symbols — a content-level complement to the procedural norms

Examples¶

  • Courageous Conversations About Race (Singleton) — a widely used professional development framework whose four agreements and six conditions structure race dialogue in school districts across the US (Pacific Educational Group).
  • "Brave spaces" reframing — college instructors replacing safe-space language with agreements that explicitly name and normalize discomfort, per Arao & Clemens' chapter used in intergroup dialogue courses.
  • Intergroup dialogue courses (e.g., University of Michigan's IGR program) — semester-long, facilitator-guided dialogues that open with co-created guidelines before structured race and identity discussion (IGR).
