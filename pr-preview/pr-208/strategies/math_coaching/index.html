Math Coaching¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 14 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 5 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 6 of 14 report an effect size

Description¶

Math Coaching is a peer-mediated strategy in which small groups of learners work together to solve word problems. One learner reads a word problem aloud while the others act as "math coaches," advising on which mathematical operation to use, underlining key words in the problem, and confirming the choice by "flashing" an operation flashcard. Roles rotate so every learner alternates between solver and coach.

Design Implications¶

Math Coaching converts word-problem practice into structured peer tutoring, combining verbal rehearsal of problem representation with immediate peer feedback. Requiring coaches to articulate why an operation fits the problem pushes learners toward self-explanation rather than answer-giving Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S], and peer feedback delivered at the level of strategy choice targets the process level where feedback is most effective Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. The structure also enacts Cognitive Apprenticeship at the peer level: articulation of reasoning and reciprocal role-taking replace the expert–novice dyad with a guided dialogue between near-peers.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A set of word problems calibrated to the group's skill level, ideally ten or more per session
  • Operation flashcards (add, subtract, multiply, divide) used to make the coach's recommendation explicit
  • A shared cueing routine: read aloud, underline key words, propose an operation, justify it, solve, verify
  • Teacher modeling of the coach role before independent group work (Coaching)
  • Individual accountability mechanisms — each solver computes and each coach justifies — so participation cannot be absorbed by one dominant member

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness collapses when one member dominates or passively copies; without role rotation and individual accountability, group rewards alone do not produce gains Cooperative learning requires group rewards combined with individual accountability. [~S]
  • Key-word underlining can mislead: problems phrased with cue words that do not match the required operation (e.g., "left" implying subtraction when addition is needed) can reinforce a shallow cue-matching schema [-M]
  • Coaches with weak conceptual understanding can entrench errors if the teacher does not circulate and check group reasoning [-M]
  • The flashcard routine adds procedural overhead; for learners still building computation fluency, the social protocol can consume working memory needed for the mathematics itself [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pairs instead of small groups, with stronger solver–coach reciprocity
  • Written justification variant: coaches record why the operation fits before flashing the card
  • Schema-broadening variant: mix in problems where key words are absent or misleading to prevent over-reliance on cue matching; contrasting such cases side by side supports abstraction of the underlying structure Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  • Fading variant: begin with the full cueing script and flashcards, then withdraw the flashcards and written cues as groups internalize the routine Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]
  • Digital variant: shared documents replace flashcards, with coaches annotating the problem text directly (Annotating)

Target Learners¶

  • Learners in grades 3–8 who have basic computation fluency but struggle to select operations in word problems
  • Low-achieving learners benefit from the verbal scaffolding of the coach role; high-achieving learners deepen understanding by explaining Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Less suitable for learners who cannot yet decode the problem text; pair them with stronger readers or pre-teach vocabulary

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Translating verbal/real-world situations into mathematical operations (problem representation)
  • Discriminating among the four operations and justifying the selection
  • Mathematical communication: explaining reasoning to a peer

Instructions¶

  1. Model the full coaching cycle with the whole class, thinking aloud as solver and as coach (Coaching)
  2. Form heterogeneous groups of 3–4 and assign the first solver; rotate roles each problem (Collaboration)
  3. Solver reads the problem aloud; coaches underline key words and discuss what the problem is asking (Application)
  4. Coaches agree on an operation, flash the corresponding card, and must justify the choice — the solver may challenge the justification (Articulation)
  5. Solver computes; group verifies the answer against the problem context before moving on
  6. Teacher circulates, listens for justification quality, and addresses misconceptions in a brief whole-class debrief (Class Discussion)

Related Strategies¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction — shares the focus on learners' verbal reasoning about word problems, but teacher-directed rather than peer-mediated
  • Peer Tutoring — the broader family of strategies; Math Coaching is a math-specific, flashcard-structured variant
  • Reciprocal Teaching — analogous role rotation and dialogue structure applied to reading comprehension; Math Coaching adapts its predict–question–clarify–summarize cycle to operation selection

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the core interaction; the coach role is a peer enactment of guided support
  • Application — each problem requires applying operations to a situated context
  • Collaboration — the group structure that enables coaching
  • Articulation — coaches must verbalize the reasoning behind operation choices

Examples¶

  • Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS) for Math (Fuchs et al., Vanderbilt University) — the best-researched relative: structured pair work in which students alternate coach and player roles on math problems, with substantial gains in fluency and problem solving. https://pals.vanderbilt.edu
  • A grade 4 classroom runs a weekly "problem relay": ten word problems per session, with role rotation every two problems and a closing debrief on which key words were misleading
