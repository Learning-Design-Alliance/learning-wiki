Apply Student-Centered Strategies to Support Learning Differences¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 7 studies (3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy involves building a data-driven, student-centered plan and then implementing specific instructional strategies matched to a selected student's learning differences — both strengths and challenges. It moves from assessment data (formal or informal) to a hypothesis about what the student needs, selects strategies accordingly, and monitors whether the strategies actually change learning.

Design Implications¶

The core move is matching strategy to profile rather than applying generic accommodations: meta-analytic work shows that structured, explicit, strategy-based instruction produces reliable gains for students with learning disabilities, but only when the strategy targets the student's actual processing difficulty [~S]. Strengths should be treated as access routes, not rewards — e.g., using strong verbal reasoning to scaffold weaker written output.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Data on the student's learning profile: assessment results, work samples, observation of engagement and persistence
  • A written plan that names the specific strategies, the rationale linking each to the profile, and success criteria
  • Instructional strategies that can be adjusted; a single fixed plan defeats the purpose
  • Regular progress monitoring with a feedback loop back into the plan (Assessment, Check-ins)

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness drops when strategies are selected from a menu without diagnostic data — mismatched support adds cognitive load rather than reducing it [~M]
  • Over-accommodation can suppress productive struggle and reduce autonomy; supports that remove all challenge undermine motivation and self-efficacy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [-M]
  • Plans built on unvalidated "learning styles" labels do not produce gains and consume planning time [X]
  • One-to-one tailoring does not scale without systems; whole-class frameworks like UDL reduce the need for individual retrofitting

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individualized (IEP/504-style): strategies specified in a formal plan with legal accountability
  • Classroom-level differentiation: teacher adjusts chunking, pacing, and output modes within a shared task
  • Student-led: older students co-select strategies and monitor their own progress, building self-regulation

Target Learners¶

  • Students with identified or suspected learning differences (e.g., dyslexia, ADHD, processing-speed differences) [~S]
  • Students whose strengths and challenges diverge sharply across modalities or task types
  • Benefits any student when implemented as responsive differentiation, but is most critical where a mismatch between instruction and profile is blocking progress

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Access goals: removing barriers so the student can engage with grade-level content
  • Compensatory goals: building strategies that leverage strengths to route around challenges
  • Self-advocacy and metacognitive goals: the student learns to name their own needs and select supports Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Gather profile data. Combine assessment results, work samples, and observation; note where the student succeeds and stalls.
  2. Identify one or two priority differences. Avoid plans that list every possible accommodation; focus on the barrier with the largest current impact.
  3. Select matched strategies. For working-memory limits, use chunking and advance organizers; for processing-speed differences, adjust pacing and reduce redundant copying; for decoding challenges, use accessible vocabulary and syntax and audiobooks while keeping comprehension demands high.
  4. Teach the strategy explicitly. Model it (Coaching, Cognitive Apprenticeship) rather than merely permitting it — strategy instruction works when the student is taught how, not just allowed to [~S].
  5. Monitor and adjust. Use short-cycle formative assessment and check-ins; fade supports as independence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Accommodating processing speed challenges — a concrete instance of profile-matched strategy selection
  • Accessible syntax — language-level support for students with reading or language differences
  • Activate background knowledge — leverages existing strengths to anchor new content

Examples¶

  • CAST UDL Guidelines — a framework for designing instruction with multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression, reducing the need for after-the-fact individual accommodation.
  • IRIS Center (Vanderbilt) — free case-based modules walking teachers through data-driven strategy selection for students with learning and behavioral differences.
  • Tier 2/3 reading interventions (e.g., systematic phonics plus explicit strategy instruction) — the best-evidenced example of profile-matched, explicitly taught strategies producing measurable reading gains.
