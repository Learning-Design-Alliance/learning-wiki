Peer Editing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer editing (a form of peer review or peer assessment) has learners exchange drafts and critique one another's work against explicit criteria before revising. It is carried out through structured protocols — rubrics, comment prompts, or review forms — that direct reviewers' attention to specific qualities of the work rather than general impressions.

Design Implications¶

Peer editing works because evaluating someone else's text forces learners to apply quality criteria analytically, which benefits the reviewer as much as the author Peer assessment can improve achievement when structured with criteria. [+M]. In writing contexts, structured peer feedback improves the quality of subsequent revisions and final drafts Collaborative and peer-supported writing improves text quality. [+M]. Unstructured "read my essay and tell me what you think" exchanges produce little benefit; the criteria and the revision requirement carry the effect.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit evaluation criteria or a rubric that reviewers are trained to apply (Rubrics or annotated exemplars)
  • Training or modeling of the review act itself — e.g., the instructor critiques a sample draft aloud (Think-Aloud, Coaching)
  • A required revision step, so feedback has a consequence (Revision)
  • Time for at least one full draft–feedback–revision cycle

Constraints¶

  • Untrained reviewers default to vague praise ("good essay") or surface-level copyediting, giving authors little usable guidance [-M]
  • Reviewers with low expertise in the domain may misjudge quality; author–reviewer agreement is moderate at best and drops for complex, judgment-heavy criteria Peer assessment accuracy varies with the complexity of the criteria judged. [~M]
  • If peer comments are treated as grades or verdicts rather than revision input, students respond defensively and the exchange becomes adversarial [-M]
  • Anonymity and small group size (2–4 reviewers per author) mitigate social loafing and friendship bias; whole-class open review without structure does not [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Calibrated peer review: reviewers first rate an instructor-anchored sample and receive accuracy feedback before reviewing peers (e.g., the Calibrated Peer Review platform)
  • Reciprocal vs. one-way: reciprocal exchange builds reviewer ownership; one-way expert-to-novice review suits large skill gaps
  • Staged focus: early rounds target global issues (argument, structure), later rounds target sentence-level concerns — reversing this order wastes reviewer effort
  • Technology-mediated: systems like SWoRD/Peerceptiv distribute drafts, aggregate ratings, and score reviewers on helpfulness

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students in writing-intensive courses [+M]
  • Intermediate learners benefit most as reviewers — novices lack the criteria knowledge to evaluate, and experts gain little Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • Students who resist instructor feedback often accept peer comments more readily, lowering feedback anxiety [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: revision, audience awareness, and criterion application Collaborative and peer-supported writing improves text quality. [+M]
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing the standards used to assess quality in a domain
  • Critical reading and Annotating of others' work
  • Self-regulation: using external feedback to plan one's own revision

Instructions¶

  1. Publish the rubric and critique an anonymous sample draft aloud, modeling specific, actionable comments (Think-Aloud).
  2. Have reviewers practice on the sample and compare their judgments to the instructor's, calibrating criteria understanding.
  3. Distribute drafts to 2–4 reviewers with a structured comment form (Rubrics, Annotating).
  4. Require authors to summarize received feedback and state a revision plan before revising (Articulation).
  5. Collect the revised draft with a cover note explaining how peer feedback was used; assess the revision, not just the product (Assessment).

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer Tutoring — same reciprocal structure applied to skill practice rather than drafts
  • Collaborative Writing — co-authoring as an alternative route to shared criteria and text quality gains
  • Formative Feedback Loops — peer editing is one cycle within a broader feedback system

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — web system where students calibrate against instructor-anchored texts before reviewing peers, widely used in STEM writing assignments.
  • Peerceptiv (https://peerceptiv.com) — anonymized multi-reviewer workflow that grades both documents and review helpfulness.
  • Writing workshop model (elementary/secondary ELA) — students exchange drafts in pairs with author's-checklist prompts, then confer verbally before revising.
