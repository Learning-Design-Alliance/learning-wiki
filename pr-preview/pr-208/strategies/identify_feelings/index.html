Identify Feelings¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 theoretical), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Identify Feelings is a strategy in which students recognize, name, or match emotional states — typically from facial expressions, body language, tone, or situational cues — and label them with accurate vocabulary. It is carried out through activities such as emotion-card matching, feelings check-ins, analyzing characters' emotions in stories, or photographing and categorizing expressions. The core move is converting an internal or observed affective state into a precise verbal label.

Design Implications¶

Labeling emotions is the foundational skill of emotional literacy: students cannot regulate or empathize with feelings they cannot name. Affect labeling research shows that putting feelings into words reduces amygdala reactivity and increases prefrontal regulation Lieberman et al. (2007) [+M], and broader social-emotional learning programs that include emotion identification improve both behavior and academic outcomes Durlak et al. (2011) [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A graded vocabulary of emotion words, from basic (happy, sad, angry) to granular (frustrated, anxious, proud), introduced progressively (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  • Varied exemplars — photos, video clips, story characters, real classroom situations — so students generalize beyond a single face or context
  • A low-stakes routine for practice, such as a daily Check-In
  • Teacher modeling of accurate, non-judgmental labeling of both student and adult emotions

Constraints¶

  • Static facial-expression matching overstates how readable emotions are; emotion recognition depends heavily on context, culture, and individual differences, so decontextualized face-matching tasks transfer weakly [~M]
  • Students with alexithymia or limited interoceptive awareness may accurately match pictures without connecting labels to their own felt experience — pairing with body-cue attention (Act It Out) is needed
  • Treating some emotions as "bad" teaches suppression rather than literacy; all labels should be framed as acceptable, with behavior — not feeling — as the target of guidance

Implementation Variability¶

  • Matching format: emotion cards, sorting games, or digital tools for younger learners
  • Narrative embedding: identifying characters' feelings during read-alouds, which adds perspective-taking to labeling
  • Self-report routines: mood meters (e.g., the RULER approach's Mood Meter) where students locate their own state on valence–arousal axes
  • Multimodal: voice tone, posture, and situational cues, not faces alone

Target Learners¶

  • Early childhood and primary students, for whom emotion vocabulary is still developing [+S]
  • Students with autism or language delays, for whom explicit instruction in emotion cues is more effective than incidental exposure [~M]
  • Older students benefit from granularity — finer emotion distinctions predict better regulation — rather than basic matching [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: building a vocabulary for internal states
  • Self-awareness: recognizing one's own affect as input to self-regulation Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: reading others' states as a prerequisite for Building Empathy

Instructions¶

  1. Model: Teacher names emotions aloud in authentic moments ("I'm feeling frustrated because the projector failed"), demonstrating that labeling is normal and safe.
  2. Introduce vocabulary: Present 3–5 target emotion words with faces, body cues, and situation examples (Chunking the set to limit load).
  3. Match and sort: Students sort photos, cards, or story events by emotion, justifying their choices with observable evidence (Articulation).
  4. Apply to self: Students use a routine Check-In (mood meter, feelings chart) to label their own current state.
  5. Apply to others: During stories, role-plays, or Case Studies, students infer and label characters' or peers' feelings and discuss how they knew.
  6. Extend granularity: Gradually replace broad labels with precise ones (mad → annoyed, frustrated, furious) as vocabulary grows.

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a game-based variant that embeds identification in a daily routine
  • Acting/Role-Play — students enact emotions, deepening the connection between label and felt experience

Examples¶

  • RULER (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — the Mood Meter asks students to plot their state on valence and energy axes, then select a precise word; used school-wide at https://ei.yale.edu
  • Second Step (Committee for Children) — early-lesson "emotion cards" and photo-sorting activities teach recognition of facial and body cues; https://www.secondstep.org
  • Zones of Regulation — students sort feelings into four color-coded zones and match them to regulation strategies; https://zonesofregulation.com
