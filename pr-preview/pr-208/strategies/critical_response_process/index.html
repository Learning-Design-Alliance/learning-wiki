Critical Response Process¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 mixed) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), q4 · 2 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

The Critical Response Process (CRP), developed by choreographer Liz Lerman, is a structured feedback protocol that puts the work's creator in control of the conversation. It proceeds through four steps: Statements of Meaning (responders state what was meaningful, evocative, or striking in the work); Artist as Questioner (the creator asks questions of the responders); Neutral Questions (responders ask questions framed without embedded opinions, and the creator responds); and Opinion Time (responders offer opinions, but only with the creator's permission, which can be declined). The sequence deliberately subverts the traditional power dynamic between expert and learner by making feedback a negotiated exchange rather than a unilateral judgment.

Design Implications¶

CRP operationalizes learner agency in feedback, which matters because feedback that learners do not act on produces little learning Feedback interventions can decrease performance when they direct attention away from the task. [~M]. By sequencing meaning-making before critique and requiring permission before opinions, the process reduces the defensive reactions that blunt feedback uptake, and the question phases push learners to articulate their own intentions — a form of self-explanation that supports deeper processing.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear, taught guidelines for each of the four steps; responders need practice framing neutral questions
  • A facilitator (often the instructor) to enforce the sequence, especially early on
  • A work-in-progress to respond to — the process is designed for unfinished work, not final products
  • A climate where declining feedback is genuinely safe to do

Constraints¶

  • Time-intensive: a full cycle for one work can take 20–40 minutes, limiting throughput in large classes
  • Learners accustomed to grade-oriented critique may find the process frustrating or "soft," and may not invest in neutral questioning Feedback interventions can decrease performance when they direct attention away from the task. [-W]
  • The permission structure can be circumvented if responders disguise opinions as "neutral" questions; facilitation quality determines whether the protocol holds
  • Poorly suited to evaluative contexts where a verdict is required (grading, acceptance decisions) — mixing CRP with assessment undermines the safety it depends on

Implementation Variability¶

  • Abbreviated versions (Statements of Meaning + Artist as Questioner only) work for early drafts or time-limited sessions
  • Written/async adaptation: responders post meaning statements and neutral questions in a discussion forum; the creator replies before opinions open
  • Discipline-specific: used in writing programs, design studios, research labs, and capstone courses, not only performing arts

Target Learners¶

  • Advanced learners (upper-level undergraduates, graduate students, professionals) revising substantial creative or scholarly work
  • Learners in studio, workshop, or peer-review contexts where work is shown before completion
  • Less appropriate for novices who lack the vocabulary to formulate questions about their own work; they may need prior exposure to Peer Feedback/Peer Review conventions

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Revision skill: using feedback to improve work-in-progress
  • Metacognition and articulation: learners must state intentions and diagnose their own work's gaps
  • Feedback literacy: learning to give, receive, and negotiate critique
  • Community norms: building a culture of constructive peer response

Instructions¶

  1. Establish the protocol and model each step with a low-stakes example before applying it to real work.
  2. Statements of Meaning: responders describe what was meaningful or striking — no advice, no "I liked it because…" evaluations.
  3. Artist as Questioner: the creator asks the group specific questions about the work; responders answer without steering into opinions.
  4. Neutral Questions: responders ask questions free of embedded judgment ("What led you to structure it this way?" rather than "Why is it so long?"); the creator answers.
  5. Opinion Time: the creator names the areas where opinions are wanted and grants permission; responders offer opinions, which the creator may decline.
  6. Close by having the creator state what they will do next, converting the exchange into a revision plan.

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer Review — CRP is a structured alternative to conventional peer review that shifts control to the author
  • Workshop Model — CRP supplies the feedback protocol within writing and studio workshops

Related Elements¶

  • Peer Feedback/Peer Review — the broader practice CRP structures and disciplines
  • Peer Discussion — the conversational medium through which each step runs
  • Provide Feedback — the generic element CRP restructures around learner agency

Examples¶

  • Liz Lerman Dance Exchange — the original context: choreographers showing works-in-progress to company members and community responders using the four-step cycle (see Lerman & Borstel, Critique Is Creative, Wesleyan University Press).
  • University writing programs — MFA workshops adopting CRP in place of traditional critique, with the author questioning readers before any opinions are offered.
  • Design studio courses — students present prototypes and use neutral questions to probe usability issues before peers offer design opinions.
