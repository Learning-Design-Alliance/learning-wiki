Learning Walks¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

A learning walk is a brief (typically 5–15 minute), non-evaluative observation in which a small team of educators visits several classrooms in sequence, gathering evidence of instructional practice and student learning against a shared focus question. Unlike formal evaluation, the purpose is formative: identifying patterns across classrooms, surfacing effective practices worth spreading, and informing professional learning. Observations are followed by structured debriefs in which the team shares only descriptive evidence, not judgments.

Design Implications¶

Learning walks work because they make teaching practice observable and discussable, converting private craft knowledge into shared professional learning Communities of practice improve professional learning through shared repertoire. [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on a tight, narrow focus (one practice, e.g., questioning or student engagement), descriptive rather than evaluative language, and a debrief that connects evidence to next actions Feedback is most effective when directed at task and process levels rather than the person. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A shared, narrow focus question agreed before the walk (e.g., "How are students explaining their reasoning?")
  • A normed protocol for recording descriptive, non-judgmental evidence
  • Protected time for the walk and an immediate structured debrief
  • Trust norms: non-evaluative purpose stated clearly to all observed teachers
  • A follow-up mechanism — practice, coaching, or PLC cycles — so observations lead to changed practice

Constraints¶

  • If perceived as evaluative or tied to appraisal, teachers perform for the walk and the data becomes invalid [-M]
  • Brief snapshots without a shared focus produce anecdote, not pattern; teams that visit classrooms without a protocol reliably over-generalize from single moments [-M]
  • Walks without follow-up (no debrief, no coaching, no practice) rarely change instruction Pairing observation with practice and feedback is necessary for transfer. [+S]
  • High-stakes or punitive cultures suppress honest practice and reduce the value of what is observed [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructional rounds (City, Elmore, Fiarman & Teitel, 2009): larger-scale, district-level walks producing a "problem of practice" statement
  • Peer learning walks: teacher-led pairs within a department, often reciprocal
  • Student learning walks: students or mixed teams observe from the learner's perspective
  • Virtual walks: recorded lessons annotated against the focus, used where scheduling or trust makes live visits hard

Target Learners¶

  • Teachers and instructional leaders, not students directly; the "learners" on a walk are the observing educators
  • Most beneficial for early-career teachers building a repertoire of concrete practices Modeling and observation of expert performance supports novice skill acquisition. [+M]
  • School leaders calibrating their understanding of instructional quality across classrooms
  • Less useful for isolated expert teachers with no team structure to act on findings [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Building shared, concrete understanding of what a target practice looks like in context
  • Calibration of instructional expectations across a faculty
  • Identifying school-wide professional learning priorities from evidence rather than assumption

Instructions¶

  1. Set the focus. Leadership or a PLC selects one narrow practice tied to an improvement goal; keep it descriptive ("what students are doing"), not evaluative.
  2. Norm the protocol. Train the team on recording only observable evidence — time-stamped notes, student work, verbatim questions — using Check-In routines to establish trust norms before entering classrooms.
  3. Walk. Visit 3–6 classrooms for 5–10 minutes each; do not interact evaluatively with the teacher; talk briefly with students where appropriate.
  4. Debrief descriptively. Within 24 hours, share evidence only, sort it for patterns, and resist premature judgment; connect patterns to the focus question.
  5. Act. Convert findings into next steps: Coaching cycles, demonstration lessons, or PLC inquiry; schedule a follow-up walk to check for change Contingent follow-up support improves implementation of new practices. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Lesson Study — a deeper, collaborative cycle of planning, observing, and revising a single lesson; learning walks are broader and shallower
  • Peer Observation — reciprocal, single-classroom observation with richer feedback than a walk
  • Professional Learning Communities — the team structure that typically hosts and acts on walk findings

Examples¶

  • Instructional Rounds in Action (Harvard Graduate School of Education): networks of schools conducting rounds visits with a defined problem of practice and networked debriefs (https://www.gse.harvard.edu)
  • Uncommon Schools' "Look-Fors": highly specified observation foci (e.g., "100% of students tracking the text") used in walks and coaching across their charter network
  • Australian Principals' instructional leadership walks: state systems (e.g., NSW DoE) provide learning-walk templates with a "notice and name" evidence protocol (https://education.nsw.gov.au)
