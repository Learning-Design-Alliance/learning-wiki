Document Outline¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The "Document outline" tool in Google Docs can be an easy and effective way to ask students to pick out evidence that supports the main ideas they've found. Students write a heading for a paragraph or group of paragraphs and select "Heading 1"; when they find a sentence or phrase representing supporting evidence, they summarize it and label it "Heading 2." An outline automatically populates in the sidebar with main ideas and nested evidence, and can be exported as a table of contents to serve as a jumping-off point for writing.

Design Implications¶

The strategy works because it forces selection and hierarchical organization — two acts that require learners to distinguish central ideas from detail, which is the core comprehension move in expository reading Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. The sidebar outline provides immediate structural feedback: if the outline looks flat or disorganized, the student's thinking is flat or disorganized, making an otherwise invisible planning process visible and revisable.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to Google Docs (or a word processor with equivalent heading/outline features)
  • Brief instruction on applying Heading 1/Heading 2 styles and reading the sidebar outline
  • A source text or draft with identifiable main ideas and evidence

Constraints¶

  • Tool-dependent: without Google Docs the workflow must be rebuilt manually with styles or a separate outliner
  • Students who apply headings mechanically (labeling every paragraph) get little benefit; the teacher must model selective labeling first [-W]
  • Heading-based structure suits hierarchical, evidence-based genres (essays, reports) poorly suited to narrative or creative writing [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reading mode: students outline an assigned text to extract its argument structure
  • Drafting mode: students outline their own draft to diagnose organization before revision
  • Collaborative mode: pairs negotiate which ideas earn Heading 1 status, forcing discussion of relative importance
  • The exported table of contents can serve as a pre-writing scaffold or as a quick formative check for the teacher

Target Learners¶

  • Middle and high school students, particularly for essay writing and research-based reports
  • Developing readers who struggle to separate main ideas from supporting detail; the two-level heading constraint makes the hierarchy explicit Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Students who find blank-page outlining intimidating; the outline accumulates from work they have already done, lowering initiation cost

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension: identifying main ideas and distinguishing them from evidence
  • Writing planning: organizing claims and support into a coherent structure
  • Revision: using the outline as a diagnostic view of an existing draft's organization

Instructions¶

  1. Model the process on a shared document: read a paragraph, state its main idea, and apply a Heading 1 (Think-Aloud modeling makes the selection criteria visible).
  2. Have students read their text or draft and apply Heading 1 to each main idea, summarizing rather than copying (Summarization forces compression and judgment).
  3. Have students mark supporting evidence with Heading 2, nested under the relevant main idea (Annotating in a structured, machine-readable form).
  4. Students review the sidebar outline, reorder or merge headings until the structure is coherent (Practice with immediate structural feedback).
  5. Export the outline as a table of contents and use it as the skeleton for drafting or revising the essay.

Related Strategies¶

  • "Headings and Highlight" strategy — the same heading-as-structure move applied to text marking rather than outlining
  • 3-Source Rule — a complementary constraint on how much evidence students gather per main idea

Examples¶

  • A middle school ELA class outlines an argumentative essay draft in Google Docs; the teacher scans the sidebar outlines in one glance to spot essays with claims but no nested evidence, targeting conferences accordingly.
  • Students in a social studies research unit outline their source documents with Heading 1 (author's claim) and Heading 2 (evidence), then compare outlines across sources to plan a synthesis essay.
