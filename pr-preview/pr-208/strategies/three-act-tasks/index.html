Three Act Tasks¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

A Three Act Task is a mathematics lesson structure popularized by Dan Meyer in which a short multimedia story unfolds in three acts. Act 1 presents a brief video or image ending on a question-worthy conflict (e.g., "Which will run out first, the water or the tank?"); students generate their own questions and estimates. Act 2 provides the information students request to resolve the conflict, prompting them to model and compute. Act 3 reveals the resolution via video, followed by a discussion comparing student solutions to the actual outcome. The structure deliberately delays formal information and procedure until learners feel a need for them.

Design Implications¶

Three Act Tasks operationalize the sequencing insight that learners learn more from formal instruction when it follows a period of grappling with a problem A time-for-telling sequence — problem exploration before direct instruction — improves learning from the telling. [+S]. Act 1 activates prior knowledge and creates a felt need for the mathematics, while the withheld information in Act 2 forces learners to identify what they need to know — a modeling decision that traditional word problems make for them. The structure also manages cognitive load by presenting the problem context visually rather than as dense text Reducing extraneous load from text-heavy problem presentation frees capacity for modeling. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A compelling multimedia hook with a genuine, visually verifiable conflict — not a contrived scenario
  • A teacher prepared to elicit, record, and sequence student questions and estimates in Act 1
  • Act 2 information released on request, not all at once, so students must decide what is relevant
  • Time for students to attempt the problem before the Act 3 reveal (Practice precedes telling)
  • A closing discussion that connects multiple student approaches to the canonical solution

Constraints¶

  • Weak if Act 3 is shown before students attempt the problem — the reveal becomes passive entertainment rather than feedback Problem exploration before instruction is what drives the benefit; skipping it removes the effect. [-S]
  • Less effective when the task's conflict is artificial or the Act 3 reveal is unverifiable, because students learn that the scenario is decorative rather than informative
  • Requires substantial teacher facilitation skill; poorly orchestrated Act 2 can collapse into unguided exploration with high failure rates Unguided or minimally guided exploration is less effective for novices than structured guidance. [~M]
  • Time-intensive relative to worked-example-first instruction; the payoff depends on students having enough prior knowledge to make productive progress during Act 2

Implementation Variability¶

  • Sequel tasks extend the same context with a new question, reusing the setup for transfer practice
  • Student-created Three Act Tasks (students filming their own Acts 1 and 3) shift the work toward modeling and data collection
  • Adapted beyond mathematics for science (phenomenon-based anchoring events) and estimation-focused warm-ups

Target Learners¶

  • Middle and secondary mathematics students, where the structure was developed and most evidence accumulates
  • Students with low engagement in conventional word problems; the visual hook and low-floor Act 1 (anyone can guess) broadens participation
  • Novices still need structured support during Act 2 — the format is not a substitute for guidance Minimally guided approaches disadvantage learners with low prior knowledge. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Mathematical modeling: translating a real situation into quantities, relationships, and operations
  • Problem posing and question formulation: deciding what is worth asking and what information is needed
  • Conceptual understanding and number sense, especially through estimation and reasonableness checks
  • Procedural fluency applied in context rather than in isolation

Instructions¶

  1. Act 1 — Hook and question. Show the short video/image and stop at the conflict. Ask "What questions do you have?" and "What's your guess — too high or too low?" Record estimates publicly. This enacts Activation by surfacing prior knowledge and committing students to a prediction they want to test.
  2. Act 2 — Information on demand. Ask "What do you need to know?" and release only requested data (measurements, rates, dimensions). Students then work the problem individually or in pairs, with the teacher circulating and selecting approaches for discussion.
  3. Act 3 — Resolution and comparison. Play the reveal video; students compare their computed answer to the actual outcome and diagnose discrepancies. Sequence student solutions from common to sophisticated and connect them to the target mathematics.
  4. Sequel (optional). Pose a follow-up question in the same context to consolidate and extend (Application of the just-built model).

Examples¶

  • Dan Meyer's Three Act Task library — the original collection, including "Dueling Discounts" and "Sugar Packets," with sequels and Act 2 data files.
  • Graham Fletcher's elementary Three Act Tasks — K–5 adaptations such as "The Cookie Thief," aligned to grade-level standards.
  • Andrew Stadel's Estimation 180 — daily estimation images that function as single-act warm-ups feeding into longer Three Act lessons.
