No Hands Up¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

No Hands Up (also called "cold calling" or "no opt out" questioning) removes the raised-hand norm from classroom questioning. Instead of allowing volunteers to self-select, the instructor selects respondents deliberately — by name, by random draw (name cards, popsicle sticks, digital pickers), or by structured turn-taking — and typically builds in thinking time before the name is chosen. The goal is to distribute participation, keep all students accountable for thinking, and give the instructor a valid sample of class understanding.

Design Implications¶

Hand-raising lets a small minority of confident students dominate discourse while others disengage; removing it converts questioning from a volunteer performance into a whole-class accountability mechanism, consistent with evidence that active participation improves exam outcomes Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. Effectiveness depends on pairing selection with adequate wait time and a safe response culture — cold calling without scaffolding raises anxiety and produces shallow or refused answers [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A question worth every student thinking about (genuine, open or reasoning-based, not trivial recall)
  • Sufficient wait time (3+ seconds) between question and selection, and after the answer, so students formulate and refine responses
  • A psychologically safe climate: normalize "I don't know yet," allow think-pair rehearsal before public response, and follow up supportively rather than punitively
  • A fair, visible selection method (random draw, name cards, or planned rotation) so students perceive the process as impartial

Constraints¶

  • Cold calling without wait time or rehearsal raises student anxiety and can suppress participation, particularly for anxious or marginalized students [~M] — Dallimore et al. found voluntary participation drops when cold calling is perceived as punitive rather than routine
  • Ineffective if questions are low-level recall; accountability for trivial answers wastes the technique's leverage
  • Fails in low-trust classrooms where students fear public error; must be introduced gradually with rehearsal structures
  • Less suitable for very large lectures without discussion structures to support individual response

Implementation Variability¶

  • Random selection: name sticks, wheel apps, or LMS rosters; maximizes unpredictability
  • Warm calling: announce the name after the question and wait time, or let students discuss in pairs first, then select — reduces threat while keeping accountability
  • No opt out: a student who cannot answer hears a peer's answer, then repeats or builds on it, ensuring every selected student ends with a successful response
  • Whole-class response systems: mini-whiteboards, clickers, or finger signals achieve the same universal accountability without individual exposure

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 and higher-education students in discussion-based classes; benefits are largest for students who would otherwise never volunteer [~M]
  • Anxious students benefit from the accountability only when paired with rehearsal (pair-share) and a no-shame response culture; without these supports the same students are harmed [~M]
  • Particularly valuable for checking understanding of students whose silence would otherwise be misread as mastery

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Formative assessment: obtaining an accurate, representative sample of class understanding
  • Disciplinary reasoning and verbal articulation of ideas
  • Engagement and attention: keeping all students cognitively active during whole-class instruction

Instructions¶

  1. Pose a genuine, reasoning-level question to the whole class — link to Class Discussion
  2. Provide wait time (3–5 seconds) or a brief pair rehearsal so every student drafts an answer
  3. Select a respondent via a fair, visible method (random draw or rotation), not by perceived ability
  4. If the student cannot answer, apply no-opt-out: another student responds, then return so the first student can restate or extend
  5. Probe the answer ("How do you know?", "What if…?") rather than immediately evaluating it
  6. Record what responses reveal about understanding and adjust instruction accordingly — link to Formative Assessment

Related Strategies¶

  • Wait Time — the pause that makes cold calling a thinking technique rather than a gotcha
  • Think-Pair-Share — rehearsal structure that de-risks individual response
  • Cold Calling — the selection technique at the core of this strategy
  • Classroom Response Systems — anonymous whole-class alternative achieving the same universal accountability

Examples¶

  • Uncommon Schools / Teach Like a Champion classrooms — Lemov's "Cold Call" and "No Opt Out" techniques are standard practice; teachers use name-stick randomization with pair rehearsal before selection (teachlikeachampion.com)
  • Dylan Wiliam's formative assessment work — "No hands up (except to ask a question)" is one of his five key formative assessment strategies, using random selection (e.g., Popsicle sticks) to make every student accountable (Dylan Wiliam Centre)
  • Harkness-style seminars (Phillips Exeter Academy) — structured turn-taking replaces hand-raising so discussion flows student-to-student rather than student-to-teacher
