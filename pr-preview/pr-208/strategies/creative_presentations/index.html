Creative Presentations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 5 studies (3 causal, 2 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 5 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Creative presentations apply visual design principles — color, imagery, layout, typography, and animation — to instructional materials so that attention is directed to what matters and comprehension is supported rather than undermined. The core discipline is coherence: every visual choice either reduces extraneous load or adds nothing, and decoration that competes with content measurably hurts learning.

Design Implications¶

Well-designed visuals exploit the dual channels of working memory — pairing words with relevant images improves learning over words alone Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+S]. But the effect is conditional: seductive details, decorative images, and gratuitous animation reliably depress retention and transfer by diverting limited attentional resources [~S]. Design for signal, not spectacle.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Graphics that are relevant to the learning goal, not merely attractive (Multimedia Learning coherence principle)
  • Consistent, restrained color use that highlights structure — emphasis placed on what learners should attend to Emphasis directs learner attention to the emphasized content. [+M]
  • Text and images integrated spatially (labels near diagrams) rather than separated
  • Segmented, chunked content rather than dense single slides Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Decorative images, background music, and redundant on-screen text read verbatim from narration reduce learning [~S] — the coherence and redundancy effects are among the most replicated findings in multimedia research
  • Overly novel or visually striking styling can impair memory for the content itself [~W] — the von Restorff advantage for the distinctive element comes at the cost of surrounding material
  • Learners with high prior knowledge extract less benefit from supportive graphics, and may be actively harmed by added guidance [~M] (expertise reversal)
  • Heavy visual design raises production cost and can reduce accessibility if contrast, font size, or screen-reader compatibility are sacrificed [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static diagrams with integrated labels vs. narrated animation — animation is not inherently superior; simple statics often match or beat animation [~M]
  • Slide-based lectures, infographics, video lessons, and interactive visualizations all instantiate the same principles with different production demands
  • Learner-controlled pacing (click-through slides) substitutes for segmenting when pre-programmed pacing is impossible

Target Learners¶

  • Novices benefit most from relevant visuals and from the removal of extraneous decoration Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+S]
  • Low-prior-knowledge learners are most vulnerable to seductive details, which they cannot yet distinguish from core content [-M]
  • Experts need less visual support and may find integrated guidance redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: diagrams and illustrations that depict relationships and processes
  • Retention of structured content: chunked, emphasized layouts that cue organization
  • Attention management: directing limited working memory to essential material

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning goal and identify the minimal content that serves it; cut everything else before designing anything.
  2. Select or create graphics that explain rather than decorate — process diagrams, comparisons, annotated examples (Analogies, Advance Organizers).
  3. Apply coherence: remove background music, decorative clip art, and text that duplicates narration verbatim.
  4. Integrate words and images spatially; place labels on the diagram, not in a separate legend.
  5. Use color and emphasis sparingly and consistently to mark structure Emphasis directs learner attention to the emphasized content. [+M].
  6. Segment the presentation so learners process one chunk at a time Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M].
  7. Check accessibility: contrast ratios, font sizes, alt text, and color-independent encoding.

Related Strategies¶

  • Chunked Content Delivery — segmentation is the structural half of the same load-management goal
  • Visual Note-Taking — learners apply the same dual-coding principles to their own encoding
  • Storyboarding — planning presentations around narrative and visual sequence before production

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy — deliberately plain visuals: a single dark canvas, minimal color, handwriting synchronized with narration; design choices that embody the coherence principle.
  • 3Blue1Brown — mathematical animations where every visual element carries explanatory weight; motion illustrates the concept rather than decorating it.
  • Duarte Design's slide-deck guidelines — professional presentation design built on one-idea-per-slide and image-dominant layouts, widely used in corporate training.
