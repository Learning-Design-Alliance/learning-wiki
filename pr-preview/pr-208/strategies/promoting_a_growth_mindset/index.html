Promoting a Growth Mindset¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Promoting a growth mindset encourages learners to believe that intelligence and ability are not fixed traits but can be developed through effort, effective strategies, and help from others. The strategy is carried out through process-focused feedback, framing of mistakes as informative, explicit teaching about how learning changes the brain, and task designs that make persistence worthwhile. It is most associated with Dweck's implicit-theories research and is widely applied in mathematics, where fixed-ability beliefs are especially prevalent.

Design Implications¶

Growth mindset interventions work by changing how learners interpret difficulty: setbacks become signals to change strategy rather than evidence of low ability. Effects on achievement are real but modest and highly conditional — the largest gains appear for at-risk and low-achieving students, and only when the intervention is paired with instructional practices that actually make effort productive [~M]. Praise and messaging must be genuine and specific; students detect and discount empty effort praise.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Feedback that names the process — strategies used, progress made, next steps — rather than the person (Provide Feedback)
  • Tasks that are challenging but attainable, so effort visibly pays off; effort praise in the absence of progress teaches students that effort is a consolation prize
  • Normalization of error: opportunities to revise work and see mistakes as data (Check-In routines, revision cycles)
  • Consistency between the mindset message and grading, grouping, and task-difficulty practices — a "you can grow" message undermined by fixed-ability tracking is worse than no message

Constraints¶

  • Effects on academic achievement are small on average and near zero for well-supported students; meta-analysis finds meaningful gains concentrated in low-SES and academically at-risk groups [-S] for the general population
  • Effort praise that is vague, unsolicited, or attached to failure can backfire, signaling low expectations [-M]
  • A growth mindset cannot compensate for missing prerequisite knowledge or inadequate instruction; telling struggling students to "try harder" without strategy support increases frustration [-M]
  • Single-session interventions fade without environmental reinforcement from teachers and peers [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Direct instruction about malleability and neuroplasticity (brief in-class lessons)
  • Process-focused feedback routines embedded in ordinary grading practice
  • Revision-friendly assessment structures that reward improvement over one-shot performance
  • Teacher-facing variants: training teachers to hold growth expectations, which changes their feedback and task selection

Target Learners¶

  • Low-achieving or at-risk students, who show the most reliable gains when they are taught that ability is improvable [~M]
  • Students who attribute failure to fixed ability and disengage after setbacks
  • Students whose sense of Belonging is fragile — mindset framing reduces the threat of difficulty in stereotype-relevant domains Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students. [+M]
  • Least effective as a standalone for high achievers, who typically already hold growth beliefs

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Dispositional: persistence, resilience after failure, willingness to attempt challenging tasks
  • Metacognitive: attributing outcomes to strategy and effort rather than fixed ability
  • Motivational: supporting the autonomy and competence needs that sustain engagement Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Audit existing messages: identify where grading, grouping, or praise communicates fixed ability, and replace ability labels with strategy- and progress-focused language (Provide Feedback).
  2. Teach the core idea explicitly — one or two short lessons on how practice changes the brain and why struggle signals learning, not limitation.
  3. Design tasks at the edge of competence so effort produces visible improvement, and build in revision so early errors are recoverable (Practice).
  4. Give specific process feedback: name the strategy that worked, the progress since last attempt, and the next step — never generic "good effort."
  5. Model fallibility: instructors work through mistakes publicly and revise, normalizing error as part of expert performance.
  6. Reinforce over time through routines (Check-In reflections on what strategies were tried), since one-off interventions decay without environmental support.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the feedback mechanism through which mindset messages are delivered
  • Formative Assessment — revision-friendly assessment structures make effort visibly productive

Related Elements¶

  • Provide Feedback — the primary vehicle for process-focused messaging
  • Belonging — mindset framing works partly by protecting students' sense of belonging when difficulty strikes
  • Coaching — sustained strategy support that makes effort effective, not just enthusiastic
  • Check-In — routine reflection on strategies tried and progress made

Tools¶

  • PERTS Mindset Kit — free lesson materials and teacher training for growth mindset practices
  • Khan Academy — "You can learn anything" framing with mastery-based progression that lets learners see effort convert into progress
  • Brainology — Mindset Works' online curriculum teaching neuroplasticity and study strategies

Examples¶

  • Instead of "You're so smart at math," a teacher says: "You tried drawing the problem first, and that's why you caught your error — use that strategy on the next one."
  • A middle-school math class replaces one-shot quizzes with a revise-and-resubmit cycle; grades reflect improvement, so early mistakes carry no penalty.
  • Jo Boaler's YouCubed program combines mindset lessons with open, low-floor/high-ceiling mathematics tasks so students experience struggle leading to success.
