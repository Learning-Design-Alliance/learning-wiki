The Disciplinary Steps¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

The Disciplinary Steps are a scripted sequence for handling learner misbehavior: (1) review what happened, (2) identify and accept the learner's feelings, (3) review alternative actions the learner could have taken, (4) explain the building policy as it applies to the situation, (5) state that all learners are treated the same, (6) invoke an immediate and meaningful consequence, (7) express disappointment at having to invoke the consequence, and (8) communicate an expectation that the learner will do better in the future. The sequence deliberately separates emotional validation from consequence delivery, so the learner feels heard before the policy is applied.

Design Implications¶

The sequence works because it combines relational repair with behavioral accountability: acknowledging feelings preserves the relationship and reduces defensiveness, while the consistent, policy-anchored consequence provides the predictability that behaviorist research identifies as necessary for behavior change [~M]. Ending with an explicit expectation of future success is a form of teacher expectation communication that supports learner self-efficacy rather than eroding it Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]. The steps function as a form of Coaching delivered at the moment of failure, converting a disciplinary incident into a rehearsal of better decision-making.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A trained instructor who knows the sequence and can deliver it calmly under pressure
  • A clear, published building policy so step 4 refers to a real, consistent rule rather than an ad hoc judgment
  • A repertoire of immediate, meaningful, proportionate consequences to draw on
  • Time and a semi-private setting; the conversation cannot be done well in a single shouted sentence

Constraints¶

  • The full sequence is time-consuming and cannot be executed during an active safety threat or major disruption — de-escalation must come first
  • Public delivery of the steps can humiliate the learner and damage peer relationships; the conversation should be private where possible
  • Expressing personal disappointment (step 7) can shade into shame induction; if the relationship is weak or the learner has a history of exclusionary discipline, this step risks disengagement rather than reform [~W]
  • Rigid, identical treatment of all learners (step 5) can conflict with individualized needs; learners with trauma histories or disabilities may require adapted responses, and strict uniformity can produce inequitable outcomes [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Steps 1–3 can be delivered as a restorative conversation with the learner doing most of the talking, shifting the sequence from instructor-led to learner-led reflection
  • In younger grades, steps 2–3 can use visual aids or role-play (Act It Out) rather than verbal review
  • The sequence can be compressed into a brief version (feelings → policy → consequence → expectation) for minor infractions, with the full version reserved for serious or repeated incidents

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 learners in structured school settings with a published behavior policy
  • Learners who respond to relational authority; the feelings-acknowledgment step matters most where the instructor–learner relationship is still being built
  • Less effective, without adaptation, for learners with chronic trauma exposure or those for whom exclusionary discipline has already damaged trust [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral self-regulation: reviewing alternative actions builds a repertoire of better choices Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Procedural fairness: learners perceive the process as legitimate when policy is applied consistently
  • Repair of the instructor–learner relationship after conflict

Instructions¶

  1. Review what happened. Ask the learner to recount the event; establish facts before judging (Class Discussion in miniature, one-on-one).
  2. Identify and accept the learner's feelings. Name the emotion and validate it without validating the behavior — this is Coaching applied to the emotional layer of the incident.
  3. Review alternative actions. Prompt the learner to generate what they could have done instead; self-generated alternatives are retained better than instructor-imposed ones Feedback is most effective when directed at the process, not the person. [+S]
  4. Explain the building policy as it applies here, connecting the consequence to a rule rather than to personal displeasure.
  5. State that all learners are treated the same, establishing procedural fairness.
  6. Invoke an immediate and meaningful consequence — immediate, proportionate, and connected to the offense.
  7. Express disappointment at having to invoke the consequence, targeting the situation, not the learner's character.
  8. Communicate the expectation that the learner will do better, ending on a forward-looking, efficacy-supporting note Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Related Strategies¶

  • Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports — the school-wide framework within which individual disciplinary conversations gain consistency
  • Restorative Questions — a restorative alternative that shares steps 1–3 but replaces the consequence with repair

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the steps are corrective coaching delivered at the point of failure
  • Assessment — reviewing what happened is a formative assessment of the behavior and its triggers

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Direct Instruction — the scripted, teacher-led structure mirrors direct instruction's explicit sequencing
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — steps 2–3 make the instructor's reasoning about behavior explicit, modeling decision-making

Examples¶

  • A middle school teacher uses the full eight-step sequence after a student throws a binder in frustration: facts are established, frustration is named, alternatives (asking for a break) are rehearsed, the classroom disruption policy is cited, a lunch-detention consequence is applied, and the conversation ends with "I expect you to use the break card next time."
  • Schools implementing PBIS embed this sequence as the minor-infraction conversation script, ensuring every adult in the building applies consequences through the same relational steps.
