Outlining¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 11 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review), q2–q4 · 3 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Outlining asks learners to represent content as a hierarchical structure — main ideas, sub-ideas, and supporting details — before producing a full text or while processing source material. It can be instructor-provided (a skeleton to complete), learner-generated (a plan built before writing), or co-constructed. The outline functions as an external planning device that offloads organization from working memory.

Design Implications¶

Outlining reduces the attentional overload of juggling ideation, organization, and transcription simultaneously, which is why outline strategies improve writing quality and efficiency Kellogg's work on planning strategies shows outlines reduce attentional load during composition. [+M]. As a reading or study tool, an outline acts as an Advance Organizer that gives learners a structure into which new material can be assimilated Advance organizers improve learning and retention. [+M]. Because outlines impose a single hierarchy, they work best when the content genuinely has a hierarchical structure; forcing linear outlines onto web-like content can distort understanding Concept mapping improves learning. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Source material or a writing task with identifiable main-idea structure
  • An explicit model of what a good outline looks like (levels, labels, granularity)
  • A follow-on task that uses the outline (drafting, summarizing, presenting) — an outline made and abandoned adds little Pairing planning with application supports transfer better than planning alone. [+M]
  • For novice writers, instruction in how to select and order ideas, not just how to format them Explicitly taught planning strategies improve writing quality. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Learners with weak topic knowledge cannot outline meaningfully — they lack the basis for judging what is a main idea; pre-teaching or source scaffolds are needed first
  • Overly detailed outlines can consume planning time that drafting needs, and can lock writers into a structure they cannot revise [~W]
  • For creative or exploratory writing, a rigid outline can suppress discovery-based composing; looser "working plans" fit better [~W]
  • Copying an instructor's outline without generating the hierarchy themselves produces shallow engagement [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Completion outlines: instructor provides headings; learners fill in details (lower load, good for novices)
  • Reverse outlining: learners extract an outline from a finished draft to diagnose structure (powerful revision tool)
  • Sentence outlines vs. topic outlines: sentence outlines force fuller commitment to claims; topic outlines are faster
  • Digital outliners (e.g., Workflowy, Roam Research, Notion) allow restructuring, mitigating the rigidity cost
  • Collaborative outlining: groups negotiate hierarchy, surfacing disagreements about what is central

Target Learners¶

  • Novice writers, whose planning is typically sparse and reactive Explicitly taught planning strategies improve writing quality. [+S]
  • Learners with high working-memory demands in the task (long texts, multi-source synthesis) Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less beneficial for expert writers, who often plan mentally or in fragments and may find formal outlining redundant [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Text structure and discourse organization: understanding how ideas relate hierarchically
  • Reading comprehension: extracting and sequencing main ideas from expository text Advance organizers improve learning and retention. [+M]
  • Study skills: converting material into a reviewable structure for later Distributed practice improves retention. [+M] (an outline is a natural spaced-review artifact)

Instructions¶

  1. Model the skill: outline a short text or plan a sample essay aloud, showing how you decide what is a main idea (Think-Aloud if available; otherwise narrated modeling).
  2. Provide a partial outline and have learners complete it, using Chunking to keep each level manageable.
  3. Fade support: move from completion outlines to learner-generated outlines of new material (Fading if available; otherwise gradual release).
  4. Require use: learners draft, summarize, or present from their outline, then compare their structure against the source or a peer's (Annotating the outline during comparison).
  5. For revision, have learners reverse-outline their own drafts to expose structural gaps.

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — the instructor-provided counterpart: a structure given before learning rather than built by the learner
  • Concept Mapping — the non-hierarchical alternative when relationships are cross-cutting rather than tree-like
  • Summarization — outlining and summarizing both force selection of main ideas; outlining preserves structure, summarizing compresses it

Examples¶

  • SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development) writing instruction — explicitly teaches students to plan essays with outlining/brainstorming before drafting; strong evidence base for improving writing quality (see De La Paz & Graham, 1997).
  • Reverse outlining in writing centers — University of North Carolina's Writing Center handout has students outline their own drafts to diagnose organization problems.
  • Textbook chapter outlines — platforms like Perusall and instructor-built reading guides ask students to reconstruct a chapter's hierarchy before seminar discussion.
