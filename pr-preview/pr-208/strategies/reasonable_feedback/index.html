Reasonable Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Reasonable feedback involves providing feedback that is manageable and actionable rather than exhaustive. The teacher deliberately selects a limited focus for each feedback cycle — such as transitions, evidence use, or thesis clarity — and often pairs one strength ("Glow") with one improvement ("Grow"). Students then carry these focused comments forward as goals for their next attempt, converting feedback from a verdict into a work plan.

Design Implications¶

Feedback has among the largest documented effects on achievement, but only when students can and do act on it; voluminous or unfocused commentary is frequently ignored or misread Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self. [+S]. Limiting feedback to one or two process-level targets keeps it within what a learner can hold in working memory and act on during revision, and pairing praise with a next step protects self-efficacy while still demanding change.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A deliberate decision by the teacher about the single focus for each feedback cycle, aligned to the assignment's learning goal
  • Balanced comments: one specific strength and one specific, actionable improvement
  • A mechanism for students to record and revisit their goals before the next attempt (Check-Ins, goal sheets, or portfolio reflection)
  • Time built in for students to apply the feedback through revision (Practice)

Constraints¶

  • Feedback delivered at the self level ("you're a good writer") rather than the task or process level does not improve performance and can undermine motivation Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self. [-S]
  • Overly narrow feedback can mislead when the underlying problem lies outside the chosen focus — a "transitions" comment will not fix a paper with a flawed argument
  • Students who do not engage with or trust the feedback will not act on it, regardless of how reasonable its scope; feedback without a revision opportunity is largely wasted effort
  • Limiting feedback increases teacher judgment load: choosing the right focus requires accurate diagnosis of the student's current need

Implementation Variability¶

  • Glow and Grow: one strength plus one growth area per assignment; scalable to large classes
  • Feedback focus rotation: the whole class works on one dimension per cycle (e.g., evidence this week, organization next), simplifying grading and building shared vocabulary
  • Student-selected focus: learners request feedback on a dimension they choose, building Self-Regulated Learning and ownership
  • Deferred feedback: comments recorded but withheld until revision, so students attempt self-assessment first

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers and novices, who lack the schema to triage long lists of comments and benefit from a single prioritized target Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self. [+S]
  • Students with low self-efficacy, for whom exhaustive criticism is discouraging Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Advanced learners may prefer more comprehensive feedback; the constraint should loosen as expertise grows

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing and other iterative production skills where revision is central
  • Goal-setting and self-regulation: students learn to carry targeted goals across tasks
  • Formative growth rather than summative judgment

Instructions¶

  1. Diagnose the student's current work against the assignment's primary learning goal and select one focus area (Assess Performance).
  2. Write one specific strength ("Glow") tied to that focus and one specific, actionable improvement ("Grow") phrased as a next step (Provide Feedback).
  3. Have the student record the goal and revisit it before the next attempt (Check-Ins).
  4. Require application: the next draft or task must show movement on the goal, and feedback on the new attempt should acknowledge that movement.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-oriented feedback — reasonable feedback is a scoping discipline applied to action-oriented comments
  • 5-minute writing conferences — a natural setting for delivering one-focus feedback orally
  • Achievable micro-goals — the "Grow" comment functions as a micro-goal students carry forward

Related Elements¶

  • Assess Performance — the diagnostic step that determines the feedback focus
  • Check-Ins — the mechanism for revisiting goals between attempts
  • Coaching — sustained one-focus feedback over time is the core of coaching cycles

Examples¶

Glow and Grow conferencing (elementary writing workshops) — Teachers using workshop models (e.g., Units of Study, Heinemann) give each student one strength and one growth comment per draft; students keep the comments on a goal card referenced during the next writing session.

Feedback focus rotation (secondary English) — A department agrees that essay cycles alternate focus: argument in cycle one, evidence in cycle two, style in cycle three, so every comment lands on the dimension being taught.

Draft-limited comments (higher education) — Instructors cap margin comments at three per draft and require a revision memo explaining how each comment was addressed, making feedback uptake visible and assessable.
