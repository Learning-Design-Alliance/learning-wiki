6to16 college-readiness and social capital curriculum¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 5 claims (2 for, 2 mixed, 1 against) · 2 studies (1 qualitative, 1 design), q2 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

6to16 is a college-readiness and social capital-based curriculum for sixth through eighth graders, described by program staff as the cornerstone of the Success Project and originally developed by the University of Chicago Charter School. "The 6to16 curriculum was divided into five units: College and Life Vision, Student Success Skills, Strong Student Profile, Social Capital, and School Awareness and Selection." Lessons ranged from short 15-minute small-group pullout formats to 90-minute blocks, and coordinators revised lessons during Year 2 to make them more accessible and relevant.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Protected time within the school schedule and principal buy-in to teach the curriculum

Constraints¶

  • The curriculum had no final version until the end of Year 2, which made planning difficult
  • Initial lessons assumed more college knowledge than students had and required extensive scaffolding

Target Learners¶

  • Low-income middle grades students in urban public schools

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Belief in the ability to graduate from college
  • Goal-setting and student success skills
  • Building a strong student profile
  • Social capital and networks
  • High school awareness and selection

Affordances¶

  • Success Project Three Lever Theory Of Action

Claims¶

  • Students' attendance and middle grades on-track rates in Success schools improved during the first two program years, especially in Year 2 [+W]
  • Success schools' student GPAs did not show much improvement in the first two years [-W]
  • After two years, fewer graduates attended career academies and neighborhood high schools and more attended charter schools; these shifts were no different from comparison schools [~W]
  • Students reported improved school-work priorities, peer academic behavior, teacher connection and support, and school connectedness after two years [+W]
  • Students overwhelmingly relied on GoCPS, CPS, and school websites to learn about potential high schools, but found websites out of date and lacking student-voice information [~W]
