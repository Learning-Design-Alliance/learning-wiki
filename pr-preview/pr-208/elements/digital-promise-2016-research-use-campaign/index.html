Digital Promise 2016 ed-tech research-use campaign and scoring rubric¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 8 claims (8 for) · 3 studies (1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), q2 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

Description¶

In summer 2016 Digital Promise ran a campaign asking ed-tech companies to share how they use research to inform product development and evaluate efficacy. Companies submitted in three categories; 53 companies submitted in total. Researchers at Teachers College, Columbia University and Digital Promise scored responses with a rubric assessing three main areas: rigor and appropriateness of research methods, whether the company worked with or employed a researcher, and whether it shares research publicly, with multiple reviewers per application and averaged scores.

Claims¶

  • Among Evaluation category submitters, 67 percent used a comparison group and 23 percent used randomization [+W]
  • Among Evaluation category companies, 57 percent publicly shared evaluation results but only four published in a peer-reviewed journal [+W]
  • Only 24 percent of top literacy apps mentioned research in their app store descriptions, per a 2015 study the report cites [+W]
  • Among 2016 Learning Science category submissions, 85 percent of companies had a researcher on staff or worked with an external researcher [+W]
  • Learning Science submitters that scored well drew on research from multiple disciplines and translated findings into product features [+W]
  • Nearly all (94 percent) user research submitters engaged more than one stakeholder group, most often students and teachers [+W]
  • Design Tech High School partnered with a neuroscientist to apply learning sciences research across school and classroom levels [+W]
  • Students used their assigned ed-tech tools far below recommended levels, and usage declined over the summer [+W]
