Supporting Gifted and Talented Students¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 23 claims (6 for, 17 mixed) · 20 studies (8 review, 4 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 3 theoretical, 1 associational), q1–q4 · 2 of 20 report an effect size · 19 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns how a teacher or designer plans ordinary class instruction for a student who already does, or quickly learns, what the class is about to be taught: a student identified as gifted or talented, or one who simply shows it on the current unit. For such a student, whose response to the unit's own work before or early in teaching shows the target already done unaided, replacing the practice they do not need with work at their level, either moving on to the next content sooner (acceleration) or going deeper and wider on the same content (enrichment), chosen from that response rather than from the label alone, is expected to produce continued progress on the subject (general-achievement, near-transfer) and continued engagement (motivation-affect, persistence) compared with keeping the student on grade-level practice they have already shown. The earlier page's warning stands beside it: without that accommodation, a student who started ahead may make little further progress and grow bored.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship. None compares acceleration, enrichment, a blend of the two, or compacting the curriculum with keeping gifted students on grade-level work, and none follows a gifted student's progress or boredom under either. The claims the wiki holds bear on neighbouring parts: guidance that helps novices becoming redundant for learners who already hold the schema (cognitive-load experiments and a narrative review, not with students identified as gifted); adaptive tutoring systems that adjust difficulty to performance (meta-analyses, general populations); mastery pacing (positive on examinations, but apparently strongest for the weaker students in a class); cooperative learning with high-ability and gifted students (a digest reporting other researchers' work, which also records that no long-term research exists); praise for intelligence rather than effort (fifth graders); and, on identification, one issue brief, one narrative review and one witness's testimony on under-identification. The design below is therefore the earlier page's guidance, made concrete and labelled step by step.

The relationship is conditional on four things. What the student actually shows on this unit's work: a gifted label or a test score from another year does not say whether the student already does this unit's target; the response does, and expertise is domain-specific, so a student advanced in mathematics may be a novice in the next writing unit. Whether the replacement work is harder or only more: extra worksheets at the same level, or enrichment the teacher cannot engage with, is what the earlier page calls busywork, not accommodation. The kind of target: acceleration suits cumulative subjects where the next unit builds on this one (procedure, principle); enrichment suits targets that open outwards (complex-skill, a talent in music, art or writing). Who is identified at all: under-identification of culturally and linguistically diverse students, and of girls where a test score alone gates entry, is reported in the wiki's identification claims, so a plan that serves only labelled students may miss students who show the same response.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Mastery Learning holds the general gate-correct-recheck relationship; this page applies the same gate at the top, letting a student who already meets the criterion move on rather than wait. Adaptive Learning holds adjusting task difficulty and support to measured performance, which is how acceleration within a class is usually run. Standardized Test Fairness and Bias holds the fairness of an identification decision made from a test score, including gifted identification; this page does not repeat its model, and follows it for who gets into a programme. Supporting Students with Intellectual Disabilities sits at the other tail of the same distribution and shares the rule that a classification sets neither a ceiling nor a floor. Provide a continuum of gifted services is the school-level version of the same idea.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a teacher can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal with its evidence status beside it.

  1. Find out what the student already does, on this unit, before teaching it. At the start of each unit give every student the same short pre-check built from the unit's end-of-unit task (10–15 minutes, the hardest items included), and record which parts each student does unaided. Do not plan from the label, an IQ score or last year's test. Untested proposal; it follows the earlier page's requirement to know the student "as an individual" and the domain-specific reading of expertise in Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [+M] (cognitive-load experiments with trainees and a narrative review; not with students identified as gifted).
  2. Compact what the student has shown. As a labelled proposal: where a student does at least four in five of the pre-check's items unaided, excuse them from the practice on those parts (keep any part they missed, and keep the unit's end-of-unit check). Expect this to free roughly a third to a half of the unit's class time for that student. Untested numbers. The move rests on the redundancy of guidance for learners who already hold the schema (expertise reversal [+M]; Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases [+M]), carried from lab and training studies to classes.
  3. Choose acceleration, enrichment or both from the response and the subject. If the student also does the next unit's pre-check well and the subject is cumulative, move them on in that subject (subject acceleration: work from the next unit or the next year's course, in class or online). If not, or the target opens outwards, give enrichment that is harder, not more: a task with an open problem, a real audience or a professional's standard (a proof of a general case, an original composition, a source analysis). Blend the two in most cases, as the earlier page advises. Untested proposal. Adaptive learning [+M] (tutoring systems that adjust difficulty to performance beat teacher-led large-group instruction in meta-analyses; general populations, not gifted students) supports running in-class acceleration through work that adjusts to the student's responses.
  4. Make the replacement work something the teacher can follow. Choose enrichment the teacher (or a named mentor) can read, question and give feedback on; set a contract with the student naming the task, the standard, the check-in dates and what the student hands in. The earlier page's constraint is that enrichment the teacher cannot engage with reads as busywork. Untested proposal.
  5. Keep the student in the class's shared work for part of each week. Plan whole-class discussions, labs and group tasks where the student takes a role that stretches them (the hardest sub-problem, the reviewer's role, the first draft of a general rule), not the role of explaining to others for most of the lesson. Evidence: cooperative learning with gifted and high-ability students [+W] (one 1993 digest reporting Johnson and Johnson's studies: higher mastery and more higher-level reasoning in cooperative than competitive or individual work; it also records that no long-term research exists). A position essay argues that all-day mixed-ability grouping asks gifted students to help slower learners (homogeneous grouping for reading, heterogeneous for discussion [~W]: the author's experience, no data).
  6. Name what the student did, not that they are gifted. Feedback names the strategy, effort or choice ("you tested the general case before trusting it"), and work is set hard enough that real struggle, and real effort to name, happens. Evidence: praise for intelligence rather than effort [+M] (fifth graders and a review of children's praise studies: ability praise left children more fragile after a later failure; general population, not tested with gifted students).
  7. Watch for boredom and underachievement as signals of a mismatch. When a student who did well starts handing in little or behaving badly, check first whether the work is below their level, before treating it as a conduct or motivation problem (the earlier page's point). Offer an adult who takes an interest (a mentor, a club, an extracurricular in the area of talent). Evidence: extracurricular involvement and adult mentorship are associated with reversal of gifted underachievement [+W] (one issue brief reporting earlier studies; associational).
  8. Open the door by response, not only by label. Give the pre-check and the enrichment option to every student, and let any student who shows the response take the replacement work, labelled or not. Each year compare who is in gifted provision with the school's enrolment by group. Evidence: culturally and linguistically diverse students are underrepresented in gifted identification nationwide [~W] and teachers under-refer them [~W] (one 2008 issue brief); for decisions from a test score see Standardized Test Fairness and Bias.
  9. Plan an arc across a term, not one lesson. A default arc, with untested quantities. In weeks 1–2 of a unit: pre-check, compact, and teacher-set extension work with a short check-in (5 minutes) twice a week. Weeks 3–6: the student chooses an enrichment or acceleration task from two or three the teacher can follow, works on it in the time freed by compacting (two to three class periods a week as a proposal), and meets the teacher or mentor weekly for 10 minutes with a draft. End of the unit: the student sits the same end-of-unit check as the class, and presents or hands in the extension product, to the class or a real audience where there is one. At the next unit, start again from the pre-check: a student who compacted one unit does not automatically compact the next. If after two weeks the student is still finishing everything easily, raise the level (the next unit's content, or a harder version of the task), not the amount.

When a condition is not met: if no time is freed because the student did not pass the pre-check, keep them in the class's teaching on the parts they missed and offer enrichment on the parts they showed; if the teacher cannot follow any enrichment the student wants, use subject acceleration through existing course material instead, or find a mentor before starting.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the student does now on this unit's own work. A student who does the end-of-unit task before teaching needs compacting and replacement work; one who learns faster but does not yet do it needs faster pacing within the unit, not removal from it. If a brief omits it, ask: what does the student do, unaided, on this unit's end-of-unit task before it is taught?
  • Whether the subject is cumulative and whether harder work is available. Acceleration needs a next unit, a next course or adaptive material to move into; enrichment needs a task the teacher or a mentor can follow. If omitted, ask: what comes after this unit, and who can read and judge the student's harder work?
  • How the student came to be identified, and who else shows the same response. Provision tied to a label and a test cut-off can miss students who show the response. If omitted, ask: how are students chosen for this provision, and could a student who is not labelled take it on the same evidence?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response to a task under stated conditions: the pre-check items done and missed, the time taken, the quality of the extension work against its standard, what the student did when the work became hard, and the end-of-unit check. "Gifted", "bored", "unmotivated" and "underachieving" are inferred states, and so is "has mastered the unit": a pre-check shows what the student did on those items that day.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares acceleration, enrichment or compacting with grade-level instruction for gifted students. The claims below test neighbouring parts.

  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows [+M]: three entries (a narrative review of cognitive-load experiments across five paradigms, three experiments with electrical trainees reading circuit diagrams, and a conceptual paper restating the review). Integrated explanations and worked examples helped less experienced learners and lost their advantage, or reversed, for learners who already held the schema; no effect sizes are printed. It supports removing practice and guidance a student has shown they do not need. It was not run with students identified as gifted, and it defines expertise per topic, not per student. Held at M, below the claim's cap, because it is carried to a different population.
  • Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases [+M]: two entries (the same review and a synthesis on the transition from example study to problem solving); advanced learners benefit less from full worked examples, and example-heavy guidance should be reduced as learners move to independent problem solving. Same scope as above.
  • Adaptive learning improves outcomes [+M]: two meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring systems: higher achievement than teacher-led large-group instruction (g = 0.42), other computer-based instruction (g = 0.57) and textbooks or workbooks (g = 0.35), but no advantage over individual human tutoring or small-group instruction; a median of 0.66 SD over conventional instruction in 50 evaluations, depending heavily on whether tests were locally developed. It supports running acceleration through work that adjusts to performance; it is not a test with gifted students, and held at M for that reason.
  • Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [+M]: experiments with fifth graders and a narrative review of children's praise studies; ability praise led to performance goals and worse performance after a later failure than effort praise, moderated by age and gender. It bears directly on how a teacher talks to a student labelled gifted; general population.
  • Cooperative learning shows benefits for gifted and high-ability students, though long-term evidence is lacking [+W]: one 1993 ERIC digest (q2, review) reporting Johnson and Johnson's nine studies of high-ability and gifted students over 15 years (higher mastery and retention, more higher-level reasoning when working cooperatively), and Slavin's statement that there is no long-term research on ordinary cooperative learning with the gifted. No effect sizes; second-hand. It supports keeping gifted students in shared work for part of the week, and limits it to the short term.
  • Extracurricular involvement and adult mentorship are associated with reversal of gifted underachievement [+W]: one 2008 issue brief (q2, review r1) reporting earlier studies that gifted students in extracurricular activities are typically not underachievers and that students who reversed underachievement often cite an adult. Associational; not a test of offering a mentor.

The limiting claims. Algebra ability level differences appear on both criteria with no treatment-by-ability interaction [~M]: in one 1972 comparison of two strategies for teaching verbal problems in algebra, high-ability students outscored intermediate and low-ability students on both criteria, but neither strategy worked better for one ability group than another. It is a reminder that "gifted students need a different method" is not established by the wiki's evidence; what differs here is the level and pace of the content, not a method. And Mastery learning improves outcomes [~M]: a meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations found positive effects of mastery programmes on examinations, apparently stronger for the weaker students in a class, with more student time on instructional tasks. A group-paced gate helps weaker students; it says nothing about the students who already meet the criterion, and a class that waits for everyone to pass holds them where they are, which is the problem this page addresses.

Learners, settings, outcomes and comparisons differ across these claims (trainees in a lab, tutoring-system users, fifth graders, students in 1970s algebra classes, digests reporting other studies), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures progress or boredom for gifted students under acceleration or enrichment.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

The earlier page cited no claim pages. These claims bear on single rows or on the earlier page's description, and have not been read against the whole model.

  • Teachers underrefer culturally and linguistically diverse students for gifted identification [~W] and Culturally and linguistically diverse students are underrepresented in gifted identification nationwide [~W]: one 2008 issue brief (q2, review r1) reporting NCES enrolment and identification shares and summarising referral research. They bear on who reaches gifted provision, not on what it does.
  • A narrative review, citing Ford (2003), attributes the underrepresentation of culturally and linguistically diverse students in gifted education to identification measures biased toward another cultural background [~W]: a narrative review presenting no data on representation rates or on bias in any measure; the attribution is the review's position, reported from Ford, not a tested cause.
  • Traditional gifted identification tests cannot fully determine learning potential and omit classroom-learning components [~W]: an argument in the same review (q2, theoretical), citing earlier authors. It supports adding classroom evidence to identification; it is not a test of any measure.
  • Testimony at a 1987 congressional hearing reported that the Johns Hopkins talent search, which admitted by SAT score, invited over 2,500 boys but 1,081 girls in one summer although equal numbers took the test [~W]: one witness's written testimony reporting invitation counts for one programme in one summer; it does not compare admission routes. Related testimony on the SAT and girls is held on Standardized Test Fairness and Bias.
  • Even the most talented performers need around 10 years of intense involvement to reach international level [~W], Amount of accumulated deliberate practice distinguishes more from less accomplished musicians [~W], Relative-age effects, not innate talent, bias selection into elite youth sports [~W] and Early training produces neural and physiological adaptations that can be misattributed to innate talent [~W]: one review from the expert-performance perspective (Ericsson et al., 2005), reporting other studies. They qualify the earlier page's account of talent as fixed: early selection and training produce part of what is read as talent. They bear on talent development and selection, not on classroom provision. See also perceived giftedness as access to training resources.
  • Children scoring highly on one intelligence tend to score highly on others, consistent with general intelligence [~W] and The Theory of Multiple Intelligences is weak in stressing science and social sciences as fields of talent [~W]: a review reporting a finding second-hand, and a position essay. They qualify the earlier page's link to Multiple Intelligences Theory as support for the broadened definition of giftedness; neither is evidence about provision.
  • Homogeneous grouping for reading instruction and heterogeneous grouping for discussion groups each serve distinct purposes [~W]: a position essay (q1) arguing against all-day mixed-ability grouping because gifted students are asked to help slower learners; the author's experience, no data.
  • Teacher expectation bias predicted Dutch secondary students' performance up to five years later, after controls for prior achievement, IQ and motivation, in one large correlational cohort study [~M]: associational, about 11,000 Dutch secondary students; the effect was larger for students with higher prior achievement. It bears on the risk that a label, or its absence, shapes what a teacher offers; it is not a test of gifted provision.
  • Differentiated instruction shows a medium positive pooled effect on academic achievement (REM ES = 0.729) with a confidence interval spanning negative values [~W]: a pooled estimate over three studies whose confidence interval runs from −3.659 to 5.117; it does not establish an effect of differentiation, for gifted students or anyone.
  • No significant impact of frustration or boredom (or anxiousness, discouragement, distractedness) on practice performance was found [~W]: an exploratory affect model in one retrieval-practice study; self-rated boredom did not predict practice performance there. It is a caution against reading boredom as a measure of mismatch on its own; it says nothing about gifted students.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually continued progress in a subject at the student's level (the next unit's content, a harder standard), or a talent developed towards a performance, measured on a stated task at a stated time. Elicit separately what the student wants: to go faster, to go deeper into one interest, to stay with friends, not to be singled out, not to be given more work for finishing early. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A student who values being with friends may refuse a pull-out group or a grade-skip that serves the objective well; one who wants to pursue an interest may work hard on an enrichment project and little on accelerated content in the same subject.

For example, a 12-year-old who finishes every mathematics unit early may want to build games, while the designer's objective is the next year's algebra. Algebra applied in a game the student designs serves both; an accelerated textbook chapter serves only the objective. Record the student's own goal in their contract in their words, and check progress on it as well as on the objective.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons with students who already meet a unit's criterion show that compacting and replacement work produce no more progress on later units or on a delayed check than staying with grade-level practice, or that the gain shows only on the extension work itself. If enrichment that is harder rather than more does no better than more work at the same level, the distinction this page leans on is not real. If the boredom and underachievement the earlier page describes do not fall when the work's level changes, they are not signals of mismatch and the diagnostic advice is wrong. If opening replacement work to any student who shows the response does not change who takes it up, the identification rows matter less than the label suggests.

Progress on the next unit, progress on enrichment work, retention over time, engagement and social adjustment are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no pre-check criterion, no amount of time to free and no rule for choosing acceleration over enrichment.
