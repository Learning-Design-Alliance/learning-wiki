Strengths-based Approach¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 16 claims (10 for, 5 mixed, 1 against) · 19 studies (9 review, 4 causal, 2 theoretical, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), q1–q4 · 2 of 19 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns how a teacher or designer reads and uses what a learner can already do. For a learner who has been described mainly by gaps (a label, a deficit-framed referral, a record of failure, a history of interrupted schooling), finding specific capabilities the learner has shown, naming them to the learner as concrete performances, and building the next task, role and support plan from them is expected to change three things: the expectations and treatment the teacher brings to the learner; how much of the learner's competence an assessment can see (assessment-accuracy); and the learner's task-specific self-efficacy and persistence on harder work (motivation-affect, persistence). A gain in achievement (general-achievement) or at a delay is not established by anything recorded here.

The relationship is conditional on four things. The strength must be a specific, demonstrated performance, not a trait: praise for being smart can make a learner more fragile after a later failure, while feedback that names what the learner did on this task, and sets a near next target, is reported to raise self-efficacy. Barriers and needed support stay in the plan: an asset lens is added to an accurate account of what the learner cannot yet do, not used in place of it. Explicit teaching and challenging, attainable tasks remain: a changed belief, in the teacher or in the learner, does little unless the work and the help change too. Strengths are treated as starting points, not fixed roles: the learner who "is good at drawing" should not draw for the group every time. It is proposed for learners of any age (child to adult), any expertise from novice upward, and goals from attitude-motivation and metacognitive-strategy to complex-skill, in classroom, workplace-clinical and online settings over days-weeks or term-plus.

No claim in the wiki compares a strengths-based approach with the same instruction organized around deficits. The recorded evidence is associational (teacher expectation bias predicting later performance; self-efficacy predicting persistence), qualitative or design-based (teachers' changed expectations after household research; tasks built from family practices letting young children show competence), and second-hand (a review's report of experiments on proximal goals and attributional feedback). Two bodies of randomized evidence, on praise and on brief belief-changing interventions, limit it rather than support it. The default design below is the earlier page's guidance made concrete; each step is labeled with its evidence status.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page. Cultural & Life Experiences Connections holds the relationship of using a learner's own cultural, family and work experience as the material of tasks and assessments; when a strength is a body of experience, use its model for building tasks from it (and its family-interview method), and use this page for how the strength is named, recorded and built on. Autonomy holds choice within structure; offering several ways into a task is one of its moves and is not repeated here. Community-Based Learning holds giving work a real role in a community. Self-Regulated Learning holds the learner's own monitoring against a criterion, which this page uses when learners start naming their own effective strategies. The unconverted neighbours Foster Growth Mindset and Wise Feedback Across Difference cover the learner's beliefs about ability and critical feedback that conveys high standards with assurance; both touch the feedback step below.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (deliberate asset identification, feedback that names competence, strengths shaping grouping, roles, entry points and support plans, and the cautions against generic praise, ignored barriers and static labels), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise.

  1. Collect evidence of strengths before planning, from more than one source. In the first week, a 10–15 minute strengths conversation or written intake per learner (what tasks have gone well, what helped, what they do outside class that takes skill), plus one low-stakes task that lets learners show a competence in more than one way (talk, draw, demonstrate, write, use their home language). For young children, ask families about everyday practices. Record each strength as an observed performance ("sequenced the six steps of the bus route and justified the order"), never as a trait ("visual learner", "a natural leader"). Evidence: tasks built from family-identified practices let K-2 children show computational thinking (Assessment tasks built on family-identified funds of knowledge elicited computational thinking from K-2 children in a small design-based study that made no comparison with standard assessment [+M]; 13 children, no comparison with a standard task). The intake for older learners and the timing are untested proposals.
  2. Write barriers and needed supports into the same record. Beside each learner's strengths, note what the learner cannot yet do on this course's targets and what support (accommodation, explicit teaching, more practice) they need. A strengths record that hides a reading difficulty, a disability or a missing prerequisite is not this approach. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's caution against ignoring barriers.
  3. Check your own expectations against the evidence you collected. Before grouping, tracking, referral or choosing who gets the harder task, compare your expectation for each learner with their performance on step 1's task and their prior record; where they disagree, give the learner the harder task. Evidence: teacher expectation bias, the part of a teacher's judgment not explained by prior achievement, IQ or motivation, predicted student performance five years later in a Dutch cohort of about 11,000 (Teacher expectation bias predicted Dutch secondary students' performance up to five years later, after controls for prior achievement, IQ and motivation, in one large correlational cohort study [+M]; correlational, no standardized effect size); teachers who studied their students' households report raising their expectations (Teachers who took part in funds of knowledge household research came to view their minority students as competent and raised their expectations of them. [+W]; one qualitative digest, no measure).
  4. Name strengths as specific performances, tied to the next target. In feedback, say what the learner did that worked on this task, why it worked, and the next target it makes reachable ("You checked each step against the diagram, which is why the circuit worked; next, do the same check on a circuit with two branches"). Prefer this to praise of the person. Evidence: in a review of experiments, feedback crediting early, rapid learning to the learner's ability raised self-efficacy and performance more than effort feedback, and close-at-hand goals did more than distant ones (Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [+M]); praise for intelligence after success made fifth graders more helpless after a later failure (Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [-S]). The template sentence is an untested proposal.
  5. Start each new task from a strength, then stretch it. Give each learner an entry point that uses a recorded strength (a familiar representation, a role, a context they supplied), then a step that uses it on harder or less familiar material, with the support from step 2 in place. Keep tasks challenging but attainable. Evidence: self-efficacy, built mostly through genuine success on graded tasks, predicts persistence and effort (Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]; narrative reviews); specific, difficult goals outperform vague ones when the learner is committed and able (Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [~M]; one narrative review). The sequence is an untested proposal.
  6. Use strengths in roles, and rotate the roles. In group work, give a learner a role their strength fits for the first task, then rotate every task or two so each learner also takes a role that needs a skill they are still building. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's role-based collaboration and its caution against static labels.
  7. Hand the noticing over to the learner. Ask learners to keep a short record of what worked: after each substantial task, two lines on a strategy that worked and the evidence that it did, and one target for the next task. Evidence: self-monitoring against a goal helps learners notice gaps and adjust strategy (Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions. [~M]; a review and a theoretical synthesis, no effect size). The record's form is an untested proposal.
  8. Plan the arc across a unit or term. A default for several weeks: in the first two or three sessions, the teacher collects evidence (step 1) and names strengths in feedback on every piece of work (one or two sentences each); in the middle sessions, a 5-minute strengths conference with each learner every two or three weeks, where the learner names the strength first and the teacher adds evidence, and the learner's own record (step 7) replaces most teacher naming; in the last sessions, the learner chooses which strength to bring to an unfamiliar task and states their next growth target unprompted. Move a learner on from teacher-named to self-named strengths once they can, without prompting, name a strategy that worked with evidence from their own work, and use it on a task they have not seen. The timings and the move-on rule are untested proposals.
  9. Assess in a familiar and a standard form, and record both. Include at least one task set in a form or context the learner has shown competence in and one in the form the final assessment uses, so a gap between them is visible and not read as lack of ability. Untested proposal, built on the K-2 finding in step 1.

When a condition is not met: if the teacher cannot speak with each learner (a large or anonymous group), use step 1's low-stakes task and a short self-report, and name strengths in written feedback only; if there is no time for an arc, use steps 4 and 5 within one lesson and claim nothing about lasting change; if barriers are severe or a learner is failing, do not replace intervention with encouragement: keep the explicit teaching and support of step 2, and use strengths only to choose how it is delivered.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learner has actually been seen to do, and who has been describing them. A learner with a record of deficit labels, referrals or failure needs strengths collected from new evidence, not read from the file; one already well regarded needs little framing and mainly harder tasks. If unstated, ask: what has this learner been seen to do well on a task, by whom, and what labels or records will the teacher meet first?
  • What the learner cannot yet do, and what support that needs. The approach adds strengths to an accurate account of barriers; it does not decide whether support is needed. If unstated, ask: on this course's targets, where is the learner now, and what accommodation or explicit teaching is already planned?
  • Who sees the learner's work, and how often. Naming specific performances needs someone who sees them: a teacher with five learners can hold a conference each fortnight; one with two hundred, or none, cannot. If unstated, ask: who will look at each learner's work, how often, and through what channel will feedback reach them?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: a learner's performance on a task under stated conditions (the task, its form and language, the help given), what the learner says worked, and how they respond to feedback and to the next, harder task. "Has a strength in X", "has low self-efficacy" and "has been underestimated" are inferred states. A performance in a familiar form shows what the learner can do in that form; it does not show they can do it in the form the course will assess.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Shows competence on a task built from a familiar practice, not on the standard task The standard task's language or format hides the competence; the familiar task is easier and the competence is not there; the familiar task rests on knowledge the standard one does not need Give a standard task with the language simplified but the demands unchanged, and a familiar task made as demanding as the standard one; compare.
Dismisses or disputes the strength named ("I was just lucky") Low task-specific self-efficacy; the feedback was too general to believe; the learner does not value that strength Ask what they think made it work, and point to the specific step in their work; ask which of their skills they would want recognized.
Takes the same role in every group and avoids others The strength has become a label; anxiety about the skill they lack; the group assigns it to them Rotate roles and watch who reassigns them; ask the learner privately which role they would try with support.
Persists less after strengths-focused feedback than before The praise was of the person and a failure followed; the feedback lowered the challenge; the next task was out of reach without support Look back at what the feedback said (person or performance); give a matched task with the planned support and a near goal.
Teacher's strengths record and the learner's own self-record disagree Teacher has misread how the learner works; the learner underrates (or overrates) themselves; they are describing different tasks Hold a short conference with a piece of work in front of both and agree what it shows.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. Naming a strength is itself an intervention that can change what it describes, so record what was said and when, and keep the explanations open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares a strengths-based approach with deficit-organized instruction on any outcome. The claims below bear on parts of it.

  • Teacher expectation bias predicted Dutch secondary students' performance up to five years later, after controls for prior achievement, IQ and motivation, in one large correlational cohort study [+M]: in a Dutch cohort of about 11,000 students followed for five years, teacher expectation bias (the part of the teacher's track recommendation not explained by prior achievement, IQ, motivation and background) predicted later performance after controls; the effect partly dissipated in the first two years, then held. Correlational (correlational), no standardized effect size. It supports checking expectations against evidence; it does not show that changing expectations changes achievement, and the claim's own Discussion says belief change without changed teaching shows little benefit.
  • Teachers who took part in funds of knowledge household research came to view their minority students as competent and raised their expectations of them. [+W]: an ERIC digest's qualitative report of teacher change after household research; no measure of expectations or of student achievement. It supports collecting strengths outside the classroom as a way of changing the teacher's reading of the learner.
  • Assessment tasks built on family-identified funds of knowledge elicited computational thinking from K-2 children in a small design-based study that made no comparison with standard assessment [+M]: a design-based study with 13 K-2 children in a low-income, majority Hispanic and Black district; tasks built from family-identified practices let children show sequencing, data reasoning and abstraction. No within-child comparison with a standard task, so "misses" is an inference; teachers who interviewed families themselves shifted toward an asset view of those families but not always of others.
  • Learners' task-specific self-efficacy beliefs predict whether they initiate, persist through, and recover from difficulty on academic tasks. [+M]: two narrative reviews (Bandura 1997, Pajares 1996), no effect size. Mastery experiences are reported as the strongest source of self-efficacy; the claim's Discussion warns that telling learners they are "smart" does not build task efficacy.
  • Proximal goals and ability attributional feedback for early learning raise self-efficacy more than distant goals and effort feedback, by a review's report of earlier studies [+M]: Schunk's (1996) review reporting earlier experiments: proximal goals raised self-efficacy and skillful performance more than distant ones (children learning fractions), and ability-attributional feedback for rapid or early learning more than effort feedback. Second-hand, no effect size. It supports crediting early success to the learner's skill, specifically for success that has happened.
  • Limiting: Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [-S]: in six experiments with fifth graders, praise for intelligence after success led children to prefer performance goals and to score lower after a later failure than children praised for effort; a review finds person praise can raise helplessness after failure, moderated by age and gender, while sincere process-focused praise can help. It is the reason a named strength must be a performance and not a trait. Read with the Schunk claim: crediting ability for real early success helped, praising intelligence before a failure hurt; the difference may lie in what is credited and what follows, which no claim here tests directly.
  • Limiting: Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [~S]: a pre-registered national experiment with ninth graders found a brief online mindset intervention raised lower-achieving students' GPA by a negligible standardized amount (0.11), larger where peer norms supported it; two meta-analyses found weak effects overall, with more for at-risk students. Carried here: a change of belief alone, without changed tasks and feedback, should not be expected to do much.
  • Limiting: A brief values-affirmation writing exercise raised African American seventh-graders' grades in two small field experiments, but a large same-district replication found no effect [~S]: affirming a valued part of the self helped in two small randomized studies and not in a larger replication with the same protocol. Brief identity-affirming exercises are fragile and are not a substitute for the design moves above.
  • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions. [~M] and Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [~M]: the two claims the earlier page cited, a review and a theoretical synthesis on monitoring, and one narrative review on goal setting. They bear on handing the noticing to the learner and on pairing named strengths with a hard, specific next target; neither tests strengths framing. The earlier page marked the goals claim [~S]; its one recorded study is coded q2, so it is capped at M here.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (Dutch secondary students, K-2 children, fifth graders in the lab, US ninth graders, student teachers), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures achievement or delayed outcomes under a strengths-based design against a comparison.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a course capability, measured in a stated form at a stated horizon; the strengths-based moves are means to it, with intermediate targets in self-efficacy, persistence and participation. Elicit separately which of their own capabilities the learner wants recognized and what they want to be able to do. Agreement and divergence are both observations: a learner may not value the strength a teacher has noticed (being "good with numbers" when they want to be seen as a writer), or may want a skill recognized that the course does not assess.

For example, an Aboriginal woman in her first year at university may value persisting to a degree that her family and community can see, while the designer's objective for the unit is a written argument in a disciplinary register. Naming her strengths in oral argument and in drawing on community knowledge serves the learner's goal of persisting and her confidence on the next task; the written argument is still the objective, taught explicitly from that starting point. Record both, so that recognition is not read as the capability the course requires, and the course requirement is not read as the measure of the learner.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of strengths-based against deficit-organized instruction, with comparable learners and aligned outcomes, show no difference in self-efficacy, persistence or achievement, or show that gains in self-report do not carry into performance. If naming specific performances works no better than generic encouragement, step 4 is a preference, not a mechanism. If teacher expectations changed by strengths collection do not change what teachers give learners (harder tasks, more wait time, richer feedback), the expectation pathway does not run through this design. If learners labeled by a strength come to avoid other roles, the role step should be dropped. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not really strengths-based" after the fact.

Teacher expectations, recognized competence in a familiar form, self-efficacy, persistence, standard-form performance and delayed outcomes are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no dose of strengths conferences, no wording of feedback and no criterion for handing the noticing to the learner.

Related Principles¶

  • Learner Choice — strengths-based design often creates multiple legitimate pathways into a task.
  • Foster Growth Mindset — asset framing helps learners interpret challenge as workable rather than identity-defining.
  • Developing Your Cultural Awareness — instructors need cultural awareness to recognize strengths accurately.
  • Authentic Audiences & Purposes — learners' strengths are easier to see when work resembles meaningful practice.
  • Take students' previous knowledge and experience as the growth point of new knowledge
  • Cultural & Life Experiences Connections — owns using learners' own experience as the material of tasks; this page owns naming and building on demonstrated capability.
  • Wise Feedback Across Difference — critical feedback that conveys high standards and assurance the learner can meet them.
  • Engage teachers in literacy events learner-to-learner to build trusting coaching relationships

Examples¶

  • Strengths conference: Instructor and learner identify effective strategies, prior experiences, and useful supports before setting goals.
  • Role-based collaboration: Teams distribute responsibilities based on emerging strengths while still rotating opportunities to grow.
  • Asset-based feedback: Instructor names what the learner already does well and connects it to the next improvement target.
  • Experience-informed task entry: Learners can begin with examples from work, family, language, or community knowledge.
  • Choose fading starting points by identifying a response already in the child's repertoire
  • Ground coaching conversations in the work of children
  • Leveraging Strengths in Relationship Skills
  • Take a listening tour of classrooms when beginning work in a new school
  • Leverage what students do well and enjoy to address their learning challenges
  • Strength-based guidance: recognizing and nurturing learners' inherent strengths rather than emphasizing deficits
