Expressive Writing Improves Exam Performance¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 causal r2, 1 quant-synthesis r3 · q3–q4 · i0–i2

Brief expressive writing about exam-related worries, completed shortly before a high-stakes test, can offload anxious thoughts and free working-memory resources for the exam task itself. The claim concerns performance under pressure — helping students show what they already know — not the acquisition of new knowledge.

Evidence¶

Ramirez and Beilock 2011¶

Ramirez, G., & Beilock, S. L. (2011). Writing about testing worries boosts exam performance in the classroom. Science, 331(6014), 211–213. doi:10.1126/science.1199427

q3 · peer-reviewed experiment (2 lab experiments + 2 randomized field experiments, not pre-registered) · i2 · medium effect, d=0.57 · n=106 (51 + 55 ninth-graders across two classroom cohorts) · causal · r2

Two laboratory studies (Ns=20, 47) and two randomized field experiments in ninth-grade biology classrooms (Ns=51, 55) tested a 10-minute expressive-writing exercise done immediately before a high-pressure math test or the first high-school final exam. In the classroom studies, test anxiety predicted lower final-exam scores in the control condition (combined r=−.51) but not in the expressive-writing condition (combined r=−.14), and among students high in test anxiety, writers outscored controls on the final exam by about 6 percentage points [t(52)=2.08, P<0.05, d=0.57]; low-anxiety students showed no writing-condition difference. The lab studies (d=0.47–2.48) showed that writing specifically about exam-related worries — not writing about an unrelated topic — prevented the usual pressure-induced accuracy drop.

O'Meara and Lovett 2026¶

O'Meara, P., & Lovett, B. J. (2026). Single-session expressive writing interventions for test anxiety: A meta-analysis. Anxiety, Stress, & Coping, 39(1), 21–34. doi:10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857

q4 · meta-analysis (21 studies, 15 documents) · i0 · negligible effect, r=.06 (n.s.) on performance after outlier removal; r=−.05/−.10 on anxiety · n=1,457 participants across 21 studies, 30 effect sizes · quant-synthesis · r3

A meta-analysis of the single-session expressive-writing literature (the paradigm Ramirez and Beilock's classroom studies exemplify) found only a negligible, nonsignificant effect on test anxiety (r=−.05, p=.57; r=−.10, p=.08 after removing an extreme outlier) and an effect on test performance that was significant before outlier removal (r=.09, p=.02) but shrank to nonsignificance after it (r=.06, p=.06). Heterogeneity across studies was substantial and the moderators tested did not explain it robustly. The authors conclude the broader literature "fails to support single sessions of expressive writing as a treatment for test anxiety," which qualifies rather than confirms the original classroom finding as a general effect.

Discussion¶

Proposed mechanism. The claim rests on a working-memory account: test anxiety consumes working-memory capacity with intrusive, ruminative worry, and writing those worries down externalizes them so they no longer compete with task-relevant processing. This aligns with the broader finding that cognitive overload degrades learning and performance — see Cognitive overload degrades learning. — and with evidence that reducing unnecessary load frees capacity for the target task, as in Chunking reduces working memory load..

Boundary conditions. The effect is expected to be strongest for students who are chronically anxious about tests and for high-stakes situations; for low-anxiety students, writing about worries may be neutral or even counterproductive by directing attention to failure. Timing matters — the writing must occur close to the exam, before the pressure peaks, and should focus on thoughts and feelings rather than essay-quality prose or study content. The intervention is a performance lever, not a learning lever: it changes how well students can express what they know under pressure, not what they know — unlike Active learning improves exam performance., which operates on encoding.

Practical implementation. In classroom terms, the intervention is cheap and scalable: roughly ten minutes of unstructured writing about worries immediately before an exam, framed as a routine opportunity to "download" concerns rather than as a therapeutic exercise. Framing matters — if students perceive the writing as an assessment or as an implicit signal that they are expected to be anxious, the offloading benefit may be undermined.

Open questions. The recorded evidence is mixed: the original classroom experiments found a medium effect for highly test-anxious students, and a later meta-analysis of 21 studies found a negligible effect on test performance. Open issues include dosage (how long a writing session must be), durability across repeated exams, and whether effects hold in low-stakes or online assessment contexts. Replication across age groups and subject domains is also needed before the claim can be generalized beyond the populations in which it was first tested.
