Coherence Principle Irrelevant Material Hurts Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 3 studies · q4 meta-analysis and experiments · i1–i3 · interesting-but-irrelevant material costs transfer

Adding interesting-but-irrelevant text, images, sounds, or music to instructional materials typically impairs learning by consuming working-memory resources without supporting the learning goal.

Evidence¶

Harp & Mayer 1998¶

Harp, S. F., & Mayer, R. E. (1998). How seductive details do their damage: A theory of cognitive interest in science learning. Journal of Educational Psychology, 90(3), 414–434. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414

q3 · i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may · causal · r?

Experiments adding seductive details — vivid, interesting, topically related but explanatorily irrelevant material — to a lesson on lightning formation. Transfer suffered. The authors' account is that the details prime an inappropriate schema, which makes this a claim about interference rather than about attention alone.

Mayer et al. 2001¶

Mayer, R. E., Heiser, J., & Lonn, S. (2001). Cognitive constraints on multimedia learning: When presenting more material results in less understanding. Journal of Educational Psychology, 93(1), 187–198. doi:10.1037/0022-0663.93.1.187

q3 · i? · the abstract prints no effect size; the full text may · causal · r?

Three experiments adding extraneous video, narration or music to a multimedia explanation. Each addition reduced problem-solving transfer. The title states the result directly: presenting more material can result in less understanding.

Rey 2012¶

Rey, G. D. (2012). A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect. Educational Research Review, 7(3), 216–237. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003

q4 · i? · no source text available to check; the entry prints no effect size · quant-synthesis · r?

A review and meta-analysis of the seductive-detail literature. The effect held on retention and transfer measures, and the pooled magnitude was smaller than the headline experiments implied. Recorded here at i1 for that reason: the direction is well supported, and a designer should not expect a large effect from removing one embellishment.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The coherence principle follows from cognitive load theory: working memory has severely limited capacity, and seductive details — emotionally interesting but tangential material — compete for those resources, diverting attention away from essential content and disrupting the construction of a coherent mental model. This connects directly to Cognitive overload degrades learning and Chunking reduces working-memory load.

Moderators and boundary conditions. The effect is not uniform. Learners with higher prior knowledge may be less harmed by, or even benefit from, interesting extraneous material because they can more easily separate essential from inessential content — consistent with the expertise reversal effect. The harm also appears strongest when seductive details are inserted adjacent to (rather than separated from) core explanations, and when the material is high in perceptual salience (e.g., background music, decorative animation) rather than merely thematically related.

Open questions. Whether "interesting" adjuncts can ever pay for their cognitive cost through increased motivation and engagement remains contested; some researchers argue motivational gains may offset load costs under certain conditions, but the weight of experimental evidence favors removal of irrelevant material for novices.

Design implication. The constructive counterpart to deleting the irrelevant is cueing the relevant: rather than adding decorative content, use signaling to direct attention to essential material. In multimedia design this means pruning decorative images, background audio, and verbose anecdotes that do not serve the stated learning objective — see Multimedia learning improves outcomes when aligned with cognitive theory.
