Manipulatives Improve Math Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · quant-synthesis r2 · q4 · n=7,237 students, k=55 studies

Physical or visual objects that learners manipulate (counters, base-ten blocks, fraction tiles) can support mathematics learning by making abstract relationships concrete and actionable.

Evidence¶

Carbonneau et al. 2013¶

Carbonneau, K. J., Marley, S. C., & Selig, J. P. (2013). A meta-analysis of the efficacy of teaching mathematics with concrete manipulatives. Journal of Educational Psychology, 105(2), 380–400. doi:10.1037/a0031084

q4 · meta-analysis · i? · effect size reported as "small to moderate" but no pooled d value appears in what was read · n=7,237 students, k=55 studies · quant-synthesis · r2

Systematic search identified 55 studies comparing mathematics instruction using manipulatives against a control condition using only abstract math symbols, spanning kindergarten to college. Overall results were statistically significant, favoring manipulatives, with small-to-moderate effect sizes (Cohen's d). The manipulatives-learning relationship was moderated by both instructional and methodological characteristics of the studies. Separate analyses by outcome type found moderate-to-large effects on retention (k=53, N=7,140) and small effects on problem solving (k=9, N=477), transfer (k=13, N=3,453), and justification (k=2, N=109), all favoring manipulatives.

Discussion¶

The proposed mechanism is that manipulatives offload and concretize mathematical relationships, reducing demands on working memory in the same general way described in Cognitive Load Theory — but concreteness is a double-edged sword. If the surface features of the object are salient while the target mathematical structure is not, learners may attend to the wrong features, and the manipulation itself can add extraneous load rather than reduce it, consistent with the general finding that cognitive overload degrades learning. Effective use therefore depends on the manipulative being well matched to the concept, on learners being supported to connect the concrete representation to symbolic notation, and on the manipulation being genuinely reasoning-driven rather than procedural.

A key moderator is learner expertise. For novices, a well-designed concrete representation can anchor understanding; for more advanced learners, the same representation may be redundant or even interfere with abstract reasoning, mirroring the expertise reversal effect. Duration and fading also matter: long-term reliance on a specific manipulative without fading toward diagrams and symbols risks learners binding understanding to that object rather than to the underlying mathematics.

Design implications follow directly from these moderators. Manipulatives should be chosen so their perceptual features map transparently onto the target structure (e.g., fraction tiles whose relative sizes encode the part–whole relation), introduced alongside — not after — symbolic notation, and faded deliberately toward drawings and abstract symbols as competence grows. This progression from concrete to pictorial to abstract representation is the design logic of concrete-representational-abstract sequencing. Teachers should also guard against "hands-on without minds-on": physical activity with an object does not guarantee mathematical reasoning about it, and procedural manipulation can mask conceptual gaps. This connects to the broader principle that active learning benefits come from cognitive engagement, not physical activity per se.

This claim currently lacks evidence entries. Studies establishing when manipulatives help, for whom, and at what age still need to be added before the claim can be treated as supported. Until then, the claim should be read as a plausible, mechanism-grounded hypothesis with well-specified boundary conditions rather than an established effect.
