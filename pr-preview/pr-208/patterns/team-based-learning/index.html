Team Based Learning¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 9 claims (7 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 18 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 4 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 8 of 18 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

Team-Based Learning is a unit-level cycle in which learners study material before class, take a closed-book individual readiness test, retake the same test in a stable team with immediate feedback, appeal items in writing, receive clarification targeted at what the tests showed, and then spend most of class time on team application exercises. Its readiness-assurance sequence is a response-dependent policy: what the instructor does next depends on the individual and team responses. It instantiates part of the retrieval practice principle.

Which parts the retrieval evidence bears on, and which it does not. The individual test is a retrieval attempt after study, and the team retest supplies corrective feedback; the retrieval and feedback claims bear on those two steps, and on reusing readiness items later. They say nothing about team formation, team size or permanence, appeals, application exercises, simultaneous public reporting, peer evaluation or the grading weights. Those parts rest on other evidence (cooperative learning and active learning, below), on argument, or on nothing recorded in this wiki. No claim page in this wiki reports a study of Team-Based Learning itself. The study configurations below are evidence about components; the policy and its branches are an untested design proposal.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Prior study of this content, the reading or media actually assigned, an individual response on a closed-book probe (item format, time since study), confidence, and language or access needs. Preserve unknowns; "did the reading" is an inference from responses, not an observation.
Context and activities Class size, team size and how teams are formed, time per unit, item format (multiple choice is usual), how team feedback is given (scratch card or digital), what counts toward grades, and every earlier exposure to the items. Setting is usually classroom, duration term-plus.
Learner-valued goal Ask what learners want from the unit and from working in a team, and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective; some learners value the grade, others a professional use.
Designer objective The intended capability: recall of the pre-class content, its application to cases, teamwork, or several. Say which the readiness test is meant to assure, and which the application exercise is meant to build.
Outcome Instrument, local criterion and horizon for each target: individual delayed recall of readiness content, individual performance on a new application item, a team product. A team score is not an individual outcome.

A request to "use TBL" does not specify these. Elicit them before choosing item counts, grading weights or a predicted gain.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

  1. Set the course frame. Form teams and agree the grading scheme and peer evaluation. No retrieval evidence bears on this step.
  2. Pre-class study. Learners study assigned material. Retrieval evidence assumes material has been studied; it does not say that reading alone is enough first instruction for every learner or topic.
  3. Individual readiness test. Closed-book, individual, usually multiple choice. Retrieval evidence bears here: testing against restudy, item format, initial success. Multiple choice without feedback carries a recorded risk of learning the lures, which the next step is placed to correct.
  4. Team readiness test with immediate feedback. The same items, retaken by the team until correct. Feedback evidence bears here, though team discussion and feedback arrive together and the studies separate neither from the other in this form.
  5. Appeals. Teams argue in writing that an item was flawed or their answer defensible. No retrieval evidence bears on this step.
  6. Targeted clarification. The instructor explains only what the individual and team responses show was not understood. Evidence on combining peer discussion with instructor explanation bears by analogy.
  7. Application exercises, public reporting, peer evaluation. Teams work a shared problem and report simultaneously. Retrieval evidence does not bear on these. Active-learning evidence bears on lecture replacement in general, not on this exercise design.
  8. Reobserve. Probe individual recall and application of earlier units' content at a delay, with new items. This step is a proposal added here; it is not part of the canonical sequence.
Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Individual score low, team score high on the same items Was the material studied, or studied and not retained, or were wrong lures chosen? Give an individual short-answer probe on the missed items at the next session and ask what was read. If the individual probe is now correct, the team feedback may have corrected it; keep the cycle and recheck later. If it is still wrong, the team result was carried by others: offer individual follow-up retrieval with feedback. Neither branch is validated.
Individual and team both wrong on an item A shared misconception, a flawed item, or reading that did not cover it. Read the appeals, check the item against the reading, and ask a team to explain its choice. Revise or drop a flawed item. Otherwise give targeted clarification on that concept and a new retrieval item on it later; do not reteach the whole reading.
Team reaches the correct option only after several scratches Elimination by guessing, or discussion that changed members' understanding. Give an individual isomorphic item later with no discussion. If the isomorphic item fails, treat the team result as not yet understood and plan another retrieval opportunity with feedback.
Readiness scores high, application exercise weak Recall of facts without their use; an ambiguous problem; team process. Give an individual application item with the needed facts supplied. If it succeeds with facts supplied, the gap is in use, not recall: work the application with modelling. If it fails, revisit the concept. Retrieval evidence does not predict this case.
One member dominates, others are silent Team dynamics, uneven preparation or language demand. Compare members' individual test results and private explanations; read peer evaluations. Adjust norms or roles, or team composition. This is a team-process judgement; the retrieval evidence offers nothing here.

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Keep the individual test closed-book and low-stakes enough to be a learning event. Classroom quizzing [+M] improved classroom exam performance in eighth-grade science, college statistics and introductory psychology, including on completely new questions in one comparison of two sections; these are reported second-hand in one review chapter and not yet checked against their sources. None was a team-based course.
  • Follow multiple-choice readiness items with feedback. Multiple-choice tests without feedback [-M] led students to produce the lures they had selected on a later cued-recall or short-answer test, even with an overall benefit; second-hand from the same chapter, one of two entries passes the judge. This is the case for the team retest's immediate feedback, and for feedback on the individual test too if the team retest is ever dropped.
  • Expect feedback to matter most for errors. Feedback after retrieval [+M] raised retention after incorrect responses and added little after correct ones in a word-pair experiment, and classroom quizzes with feedback showed larger effects than those without, in a between-study comparison another meta-analysis did not reproduce. Team feedback in this pattern comes with discussion, so the studies do not isolate it.
  • Combine team discussion with targeted instructor explanation. In genetics courses using clicker questions, peer discussion [+W] improved performance on a later isomorphic question, and peer discussion followed by instructor explanation gave larger gains than either alone. These are classroom studies of peer instruction (one with no control group, one quasi-experimental), not of team readiness tests. One entry passes the judge on its abstract; the other could not be confirmed from its abstract.
  • Treat the application phase as active learning, not as retrieval. Active learning [~M] beat traditional lecturing on undergraduate STEM exams and concept inventories (0.47 SD across 158 studies), and only high-intensity active learning narrowed achievement gaps. Team-Based Learning is one form among many in those syntheses, which do not estimate it separately. Not settled on the abstracts.
  • Base team rewards on individual learning. Group rewards with individual accountability [+W] were associated with larger achievement effects (median +.32 against +.07) in a best-evidence synthesis of school studies, which also reports structured interaction working without rewards. These are school-age cooperative-learning studies, not this pattern; they bear on its grading, not its retrieval steps.

Do not rank these against one another, and do not read an effect from a laboratory word-pair or a lecture course as a forecast for a team-based course. A precise abstention names the missing comparison: no recorded study compares this sequence with the same content taught otherwise.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented for illustration; not a tested course. A second-year pharmacology unit: students read a chapter on one drug class, take a ten-item closed-book individual test, retake it as teams of six with scratch cards, and may appeal. The designer's objective is one-week recall of mechanisms and their use in choosing a drug for a case; several students say they value being able to explain a choice to a patient. The instructor sees most teams scratch twice on one interaction item while the individual test shows a common wrong option, clarifies that interaction for ten minutes, and runs a case exercise. A week later each student answers two new short-answer items on the unit, one recall and one application, with no notes.

Record: item and format → time since study, notes allowed → individual response and confidence → team response and number of attempts → appeal, if any → candidate interpretations → clarification given → individual response to a new item at the stated horizon. Keep individual and team records separate; a team score is not evidence about any one member. Preserve the learners' stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise the record, but must not fill missing fields or assign probabilities to states without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Assigned readings, quizzes, retrieval practice, practice testing, feedback, peer discussion, argumentation, application, class discussion and peer assessment.

The retrieval evidence supports the readiness tests as retrieval with feedback; it does not support the claim that the whole cycle beats other designs, nor any team size, grading weight or application format. The response-dependent branches above, and the delayed reobservation step, are proposals to be tested with learners.

Related Patterns¶

  • Cooperative Learning — shares positive interdependence and individual accountability; TBL adds the readiness assurance cycle and simultaneous reporting
  • Case-Based Learning — TBL's application phase is typically case-based; the Harvard method variant pairs naturally with TBL team structure
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — complementary rather than competing: TBL assumes content is acquired before class, so expert modeling must happen in pre-class media or targeted mini-lectures

Examples¶

Medical education — pharmacology: Many medical schools (e.g., Vanderbilt, Duke-NUS) use TBL for pre-clerkship sciences: students complete assigned readings, take iRATs on drug mechanisms, then teams work patient-case application exercises choosing and defending treatment decisions.

Business education — management courses: Michaelsen's original context: teams of 5–7 in large organizational-behavior courses work through group-dynamics cases, with peer evaluation feeding into grades each unit.

Large undergraduate STEM courses: Introductory biology and chemistry courses at research universities have adopted TBL to convert lecture halls into team-application sessions, using digital tRAT tools such as InteDashboard or Top Hat for immediate team feedback. - Two-week fruitless gene TBL laboratory module on aggression and courtship in Drosophila melanogaster - Use IRAT as an indicator of subsequent assessment performance and exam-condition practice in pharmacy calculations
