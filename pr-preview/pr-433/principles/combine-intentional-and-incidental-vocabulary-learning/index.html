Combine intentional and incidental vocabulary learning rather than relying on incidental learning alone¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 25 claims (16 for, 8 mixed, 1 against) · 17 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 1 associational), q2–q3 · 2 of 17 report an effect size · 20 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the balance between deliberate word study and learning words from meaning-focused input over a course. For a second-language learner (and, by extension, a first-language reader) who is building general vocabulary, a combination of the two modes is expected to produce more words known and better retention than either mode alone: deliberate study of selected words (meaning given, then recalled and used) supplies the words that make texts comprehensible, and reading or listening for meaning, with glosses and recurring words, then adds and deepens words that would take too long to teach one by one. The intentional share should be largest while the learner knows too few of the surrounding words to infer meaning from context, and should shrink as coverage of the learner's texts rises. The capability is word knowledge (verbal-association for form and meaning) and its use in comprehension and production; the outcomes are immediate-performance on taught and met words and delayed-retention.

Two claims test the combination itself, and both are weak tests. One second-hand report of a study of 93 Chinese university ESL students found that both incidental and intentional learning produced gains and the combined instruction produced greater gains and better retention (Combining intentional and incidental vocabulary learning produces greater gains and better retention than either mode alone [+M]). A 1986 meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction, read from its abstract, concluded that the most effective methods combined definitional and contextual information, gave more than one or two exposures and asked for deeper processing, without an effect size for that comparison (Pairing Contextual Encounters With Explicit Instruction Produces Stronger Vocabulary Outcomes Than Either Alone [+M]). One small trial with first-year ESL students added mobile listening to usual vocabulary teaching and found higher end-of-unit exam scores than usual teaching alone (Listening to vocabulary lessons while physically active improves End-of-Unit exam performance over traditional intentional learning alone [+M]), though the added component also changed time and activity. The two halves are better supported on their own: incidental learning from meaning-focused input is reliable but picks up only a small share of the unknown words met (Incidental vocabulary exposure produces reliable learning, but learners pick up only a small share of the unknown words they meet [+S]), and one second-hand direct comparison found intentional learning gave more, faster gains and better retention than incidental learning (Intentional vocabulary learning yields higher, faster gains and better retention than incidental learning through reading [+M]). No claim in the wiki tests the order of the two modes, the ratio between them, the coverage at which to shift the balance, or how many encounters a word needs; those parts of the design below are proposals. One narrative review in the wiki argues the opposite direction, that incidental acquisition is more powerful than intentional learning (Incidental vocabulary acquisition through comprehensible input is more powerful than intentional vocabulary learning [-W]); it is weighed below.

The relationship is conditional on four things: how many of the surrounding words in the learner's texts the learner already knows (inference from context needs most of them; Correct word inference in reading requires knowing roughly 95-98% of surrounding words, corresponding to about 3,000-5,000 word families [+M], second-hand); how much meaning-focused input the learner actually gets each week; how often target words recur in it; and the outcome and horizon (recognising a word's meaning, recalling it, or using it in speech and writing, now or weeks later). The evidence here is mostly from second-language learners at university; carrying it to children or to adults learning the terms of a job is an extrapolation.

How the converted sibling sits beside this page: Explicit Instruction: Vocabulary holds how to teach a selected set of words (selection, introduction, active use, spaced review, a "known" criterion). This page does not repeat that model; it holds the decision around it: how much of a learner's vocabulary time goes to deliberate study and how much to reading and listening for meaning, how the two are joined within a session, and how the balance shifts across a course. Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax holds which words to remove from a task or text so that it can be read at all.

Default design¶

Two claims test the combination and one small trial adds input to deliberate study; none tests the order, ratio or dose. Each step names its evidence status, and the quantities are proposals to set locally and record.

  1. Check coverage before choosing the mode. Take a 100-word sample of the next text the learner will read or hear and have the learner mark the words they do not know (or test a sample of them). If fewer than about 95 of 100 are known, do not rely on inference from context: teach or gloss more. Comfortable reading needs about 98 of 100. The 95% and 98% figures are reported second-hand from one review (Correct word inference in reading requires knowing roughly 95-98% of surrounding words… [+M]); the 100-word sampling procedure is an untested proposal.
  2. While coverage is low, lead with deliberate study of the most frequent words. A learner who does not yet know roughly the first thousand words (the earlier page's threshold) gets most vocabulary time as deliberate study: 10–15 minutes a session on 5–8 high-frequency words, with meaning, examples and recall, and spaced review on cards or an app. Input in this phase is graded or simplified text, or text with most unknown words glossed. The threshold is from the earlier page and untested here; computer-based spaced review beside classroom teaching gave much higher end-of-semester credit than classroom teaching alone in one study of EFL cadets (Supplemental computer-based spaced repetition activities nearly triple long-term vocabulary retention… [+M]).
  3. Pre-teach a few key words, then read or listen for meaning. In each session with a text: 5–8 minutes on 3–5 words the text needs and the learner will meet again, then 15–20 minutes reading or listening for meaning, with a comprehension purpose rather than a word hunt. Combining definitions with contextual encounters is supported by the two combination claims above (Pairing Contextual Encounters… [+M]; Combining intentional and incidental vocabulary learning… [+M]). Which mode comes first is untested in the wiki; the minutes are untested proposals.
  4. Gloss the other unknown words in the margin; leave a few for the dictionary. Give a short marginal gloss (first-language or simple target-language meaning, a picture for concrete words) for unknown words that matter to the text and were not pre-taught, and ask learners to look up two or three more. Glosses and dictionary use each helped incidental learning in a randomised study of 78 advanced university learners of French, reported in a review (Marginal glosses, dictionary use, and repeated occurrence of unknown words each positively affect incidental vocabulary learning [+M]); a second-hand report of a study of 125 Chinese ESL freshmen found that tasks with more search and evaluation (glosses plus dictionary) gave better retention and that marginal glosses were the more efficient aid (Tasks with higher involvement load… [+M]). Meta-analyses of glossing are reported second-hand as finding medium-to-large effects, larger for text-plus-picture glosses (Glossing yields medium-to-large positive effects on L2 vocabulary learning… [+W]).
  5. Choose or write texts in which the target words recur. A word met once is rarely learned from context; plan texts, or sets of short texts on one topic, in which each target word appears several times, ideally spread over days rather than in one passage. A default of at least five or six encounters across a week is an untested proposal. The number of encounters goes with incidental learning across second-language studies, a correlation rather than a tested dose (The more often learners meet a word while reading, the more likely they are to learn it incidentally… [+M]; Vocabulary Knowledge Grows Incrementally With Repeated Encounters In Varied Contexts [+M]).
  6. After the input, turn met words into studied words. 5–10 minutes: learners pick 3–5 words from the text they want to keep, write the sentence they met it in, recall its meaning without the gloss, and use it in a sentence of their own or a short spoken answer. This is the intentional half applied to words the input supplied. Supported in direction by the combination claims; a second-hand report found that adult ESL students who read a short story could use the new words in sentences while students who had studied dictionary meanings in isolation could not (Adult ESL students who read a short story could use incidentally acquired words in sentences… [~M]), which argues for keeping the context with the word. The routine is an untested proposal.
  7. Bring met and taught words back on a spaced schedule. Words from the input lose ground over time if nothing brings them back (Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems, with losses over time moderated by feedback timing and topic interest [~M]). Review each kept word after about 1, 3, 7 and 14 days, recall first and meaning shown after, 5 minutes at the start of a session or on an app (an untested schedule; spaced review per step 2).
  8. Protect the volume of meaning-focused input. Because each encounter teaches a small share of the unknown words met, gains from input come from volume: plan at least 60–90 minutes a week of reading or listening for meaning at a level where coverage is about 95% or higher, in or out of class (an untested dose). Listening and reading-while-listening yielded proportions of words learned similar to reading in one meta-analysis (L2 learners pick up vocabulary incidentally from meaning-focused input… [+M]; Incidental vocabulary exposure produces reliable learning, but learners pick up only a small share… [+S]). Let learners choose topics they find interesting where the course allows: one second-hand report found growth from interesting passages lasted (Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems… [~M]).
  9. Shift the balance across the course, and move on by coverage and recall. A default arc, untested: early (coverage of graded texts below about 95%): about two thirds of vocabulary time on deliberate study of high-frequency words, input graded and heavily glossed, the teacher supplying meanings. Middle: about half and half; fewer glosses, learners infer a meaning from context first and check it against the gloss or dictionary. Late (about 95% coverage or more of the texts the learner needs): about a third on deliberate study, kept for words input will not supply often enough (low-frequency academic or technical words) and for the after-input routine of step 6; most time is extensive reading and listening with few or no glosses. Move a learner to the next text level when a 100-word sample of it shows about 95% known and they recall the meaning of most reviewed words (say four in five) at the two-week review. The ratios and criteria are untested proposals; the direction (intentional learning first builds the words later incidental learning depends on) is from the earlier page and the coverage claim.

When a condition is not met: if the learner cannot read the script or decode the words, run the input as listening with pictures and do the deliberate study orally; if there is no time for input in class, set the reading or listening as homework and keep class time for steps 3, 6 and 7; if the learner's coverage is already high, skip step 2 and spend the deliberate share on the words their texts use rarely.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How much of the learner's texts they already understand. Below about 95% coverage, inference from context fails and the intentional share must be larger; above it, input can carry more of the load. If unstated, ask: in a typical text the learner will read or hear next, how many words in a hundred do they not know?
  • How much meaning-focused input the learner gets, and where. A course with an hour of reading a week can rely on input more than one with ten minutes; input outside class (a phone, a library, work documents) counts. If unstated, ask: how many minutes a week does the learner read or listen to the language for meaning, in and out of class, and on what?
  • What the learner must do with the words, and when. Recognising a meaning, recalling it, and using a word in speech or writing at a delay need different shares: productive use needs the after-input routine and spaced use. If unstated, ask: what should the learner be able to do with these words, and how long after the course?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed: which words a learner marks as unknown in a text sample, their answers to comprehension questions, the glosses they open or the words they look up, the words they choose to keep after reading, their recall of a word's meaning at a review, and their use of the word in a sentence or task. "Knows the word", "has partial knowledge", "infers well from context" and "is not reading enough" are inferred states. A word recognised at the end of a session is not a word recalled two weeks later or used in writing.

Evidence and qualifications¶

Two claims test the combination and both are thin, and a third adds an input component to deliberate study in one small trial; the halves are better supported. Learners, outcomes and horizons differ across them (Chinese university ESL students, advanced Dutch university learners of French, adult ESL students, pooled second-language studies), so do not rank them by effect label.

  • Combining intentional and incidental vocabulary learning produces greater gains and better retention than either mode alone [+M]: a review describes Guo's (2010) study of 93 Chinese university ESL students in two groups, with texts containing 26 target words and vocabulary exercises for the experimental group; both modes gave significant gains, and the combined instruction gave greater gains and better retention. Second-hand, no effect size printed. The entry says "two groups", so how three conditions were compared is not clear from the claim page.
  • Pairing Contextual Encounters With Explicit Instruction Produces Stronger Vocabulary Outcomes Than Either Alone [+M]: Stahl and Fairbanks's 1986 meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction (abstract read) found d = .97 on comprehension of passages containing taught words and d = .30 on global comprehension, and concluded that methods combining definitional and contextual information were the most effective; no effect size for combined against single methods. Its table gathers 7 distinct studies on the combination question, 2 of them with an effect size coded, none of which tests context-only, instruction-only and combined conditions against each other.
  • Incidental vocabulary exposure produces reliable learning, but learners pick up only a small share of the unknown words they meet [+S]: about 15% of unknown words learned during normal reading across 20 experiments; in 24 controlled second-language studies, a large effect against controls (g = 1.14 on first posttests) yet only 9–18% of target words learned immediately and 6–17% at a delay; encounters correlate r = .34 with learning across 26 studies. It is the case for the intentional half: input alone is slow for any particular word.
  • Intentional vocabulary learning yields higher, faster gains and better retention than incidental learning through reading [+M]: a review reports Peters et al.'s (2009) direct comparison: incidental acquisition gave less and slower gains and worse retention. Second-hand, no effect size; it has no combined condition, so it does not test this page's relationship.
  • Marginal glosses, dictionary use, and repeated occurrence of unknown words each positively affect incidental vocabulary learning [+M]: Hulstijn et al. (1996), 78 advanced university students of French randomly assigned to marginal-gloss, dictionary or control conditions while reading a short narrative, 16 target words; reported second-hand, no effect size. It supports glossing as the bridge between the modes, for advanced learners.
  • Listening to vocabulary lessons while physically active improves End-of-Unit exam performance over traditional intentional learning alone [+M]: 51 first-year Ukrainian ESL students in one experimental and two comparison classes; the experimental class added mobile audio listening while physically active to its usual (intentional) vocabulary teaching and scored higher on the end-of-unit exam (mean 81.9 against 63.2). It is a primary test of adding an input component to deliberate study, but the added component also changed time on task and physical activity, rigour is r1, and no effect size is printed.
  • Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems, with losses over time moderated by feedback timing and topic interest [~M]: second-hand reports that all groups lose some gains in the long run, that a short study-test lag with delayed feedback retained better, and that growth from interesting passages tended to last. A reason to add spaced review to input.

What limits the model.

  • Incidental vocabulary acquisition through comprehensible input is more powerful than intentional vocabulary learning [-W]: a narrative review (rigour r1) concluding that incidental acquisition outperforms intentional learning per unit of time and in retention. It runs against the intentional half, and with Incidentally acquired vocabulary is retained over time while intentionally learned (focus-on-form) words are rapidly forgotten [~W] (a re-analysis, reported in the same review, in which focus-on-form words were forgotten within 16 days while words from reading were kept; the entry's table also has an "integrated" group whose result the claim page does not give) it warns that deliberate study of isolated words, without reading, may not last. Both come from one review arguing one hypothesis. The model takes the warning as a design constraint (keep the context with the word, step 6; review on a schedule, step 7) rather than as evidence against combining.
  • Adult ESL students who read a short story could use incidentally acquired words in sentences, while students who learned word meanings in isolation could not [~M]: gains of 6.73 against 0.57 on sentence-production tests (t = 4.48, df = 50), reported in the same review. It qualifies the intentional half: dictionary meanings studied in isolation did not give productive use.
  • Correct word inference in reading requires knowing roughly 95-98% of surrounding words… [+M]: the coverage thresholds are reported second-hand from Huckin and Coady (1999), not measured here; the default's 95% switch-point leans on them and should be checked locally.

None of the claims varies the ratio of intentional to incidental time, the order of the two, or the coverage at which to shift; none follows learners across a course.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does combining deliberate word study with learning from meaning-focused input give more words and better retention than either alone? Pairing Contextual Encounters With Explicit Instruction Produces Stronger Vocabulary Outcomes Than Either Alone [+M]
  • Do glosses and dictionary use during second-language reading raise the words learned from it? Earlier meta-analyses, as reported second-hand, find that glosses improve L2 vocabulary learning and that text-and-picture glosses outperform single-mode glosses [+W]
  • How much do repeated encounters add, and do words met in input last without review? Vocabulary Knowledge Grows Incrementally With Repeated Encounters In Varied Contexts [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Every claim the earlier page cited is used in the model above. Claims found while rewriting, read but not built into the model:

  • Reading with marginal glosses is the most beneficial task condition for incidental vocabulary acquisition gains [+M]: Xu's (2010) study of 125 ESL freshmen, four reading tasks, reported second-hand through a different review; no external aid was least efficient and marginal glosses most beneficial. It appears to be the same study as the involvement-load claim used in step 4, described with a different set of four tasks, so it is not counted as a second study.
  • Reading and listening yield similar proportions of incidental vocabulary learning, with reading ahead only on some immediate posttests [+S]: proportions of words learned were similar for reading and listening (17% and 15% immediately); reading had the larger effect size on first posttests only. It bears on the low-literacy row (listening as the input mode).
  • Extensive reading improves vocabulary acquisition and spelling through repeated meaningful exposure [+W]: a curriculum review (rigour r1) with no effect sizes; bears on step 8 in direction only.
  • After eight encounters in reading, L2 learners recognize the form and meaning of unknown words better than they can recall their meaning [~M] — attached 2026-10-16 from Pellicer-Sánchez (2016), which proposed "Design extensive reading materials so that unknown vocabulary items recur about eight times for substantial incidental learning".

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually a vocabulary large enough to read and listen to the course's texts at high coverage, plus known and usable target words at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to read a particular kind of text (novels, news, manuals at work), to follow conversations, to pass a test with a set list, to talk about their own life. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants to pass a listed-word exam will favour deliberate study and may not read; a learner who wants to read for pleasure may resist word lists. The input half is where the learner's own goal can most easily be served (choice of texts), and the evidence that interesting passages give longer-lasting growth is a reason to let them choose.

For example, an adult learner of English may want to read football news on a phone, while the course objective is the first two thousand words of a frequency list. Graded football reports with glosses, chosen by the learner, can serve both: the frequency-list words recur in them and are studied after reading. Record both the list words recalled and whether the learner reads the news unaided by the end, so success on one is not read as the other.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if primary studies comparing combined, intentional-only and input-only conditions with comparable learners, aligned outcomes and a delayed test find no advantage for the combination. If deliberate study of words, given with their contexts and reviewed on a schedule, is still forgotten as quickly as the focus-on-form words in the re-analysis above, the intentional share should shrink and its role change to glossing and noticing. If learners below 95% coverage turn out to learn as much from glossed authentic texts as from deliberate study of frequent words, the coverage switch-point should go. If a study varies the ratio or order of the two modes, its result replaces the default arc. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "not enough reading" or "not enough review" after the fact.

Words recognised at the end of a session, words recalled at a delay, words used in writing or speech, and growth in general reading are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no ratio, no order, no coverage switch-point tested as such, and no number of encounters per word.

Claims¶

  • The probability of learning an unknown word from a single natural-context exposure is roughly .05 to .11, supporting an extrapolated 750 to 5,500 words learned per year from reading [~W]
  • Total reading time independently contributed to the learning of word meaning [~W]
  • Classroom experiments test whether instructional procedures that increase the salience and cognitive processing of targeted words in reading texts can support L2 vocabulary acquisition [~W]
  • Teachers with high incidental exposure described expanded opportunities for word use and connecting words to topics [~W]
