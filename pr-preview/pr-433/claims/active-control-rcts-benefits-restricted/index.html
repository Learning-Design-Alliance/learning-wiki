Evidence¶

Dunning 2019¶

Dunning, D. L., Griffiths, K., Kuyken, W., Crane, C., Foulkes, L., Parker, J., & Dalgleish, T. (2019). Research Review: The effects of mindfulness-based interventions on cognition and mental health in children and adolescents – a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12980

q3 · i2 · quant-synthesis · r3

Separate random effects meta-analysis of the 17 studies (n = 1,762) using an RCT design with an active control condition. The review reports benefits "restricted to the outcomes of Mindfulness ( d = .42), Depression ( d = .47) and Anxiety/Stress ( d = .18) only."

"when considering only those RCTs with active control groups, significant benefits of an MBI were restricted to the outcomes of Mindfulness ( d = .42), Depression ( d = .47) and Anxiety/Stress ( d = .18) only."

Related Claims¶

  • The evidence base for school mindfulness interventions is promising but too variable for definitive practice recommendations — the general claim this finding bears on, with the studies behind it
  • Publication bias is evident for Negative Behaviour and Anxiety/Stress across all RCTs and for Mindfulness in active-control RCTs, suggesting overestimation of MBI effects — related
  • Effect sizes vary by control group type, with wait-list controls yielding the largest mean effect and no-contact controls the smallest — related
  • Age significantly moderates MBI effects on executive functions across all RCTs (larger benefits with older age) and on negative behaviour in active-control RCTs (larger benefits with younger age) — related
  • Across all RCTs, mindfulness-based interventions significantly improve mindfulness, executive functioning, attention, depression, anxiety/stress and negative behaviours in youth, with small effect sizes — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Total training hours significantly moderate MBI effects on negative behaviour across all RCTs, with more hours associated with larger reductions — related
  • Studies using a no-input control group yield significantly smaller effect sizes, protecting against overestimation — related
  • Mindfulness interventions in schools show the largest between-group effects for cognitive performance (g = 0.80) and smaller significant effects for stress (g = 0.39) and resilience (g = 0.36) — reports the opposite
  • Cognitive-only coaching increased deep and achieving learning approaches, academic self-concept, and reduced test anxiety and anxiety/depression, but academic performance declined relative to control; behavioural-only coaching decreased test anxiety and increased academic performance with no other effects. — related
  • Large-scale RCTs show school mindfulness training does not benefit overall well-being or social-emotional functioning and can harm those most at risk — related
