Evidence¶

Holz J 2012¶

Holz J, Piosczyk H, Landmann N, Feige B, Spiegelhalder K, Riemann D, Nissen C, Voderholzer U. (2012). The Timing of Learning before Night-Time Sleep Differentially Affects Declarative and Procedural Long-Term Memory Consolidation in Adolescents. PLoS ONE 7(7): e40963. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0040963

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Randomized parallel-group experiment (25 per group) on a five-element finger-tapping sequence. Table 2 reports speed differences of 4.8 vs 6.1 sequences on Day 2 (P = 0.020) and 6.8 vs 9.0 on Day 8 (P = 0.002); no standardized effect size is printed.

"Univariate ANOVAs revealed a significantly greater improvement in finger-tapping speed in the EG compared to the AG across the first retention interval from Day 1 to Day 2 (Tab. 2, Fig. 3a), as well as for the interval from Day 1 to Day 8 (Tab. 2, Fig. 3b)."

Discussion¶

Related Claims¶

  • Evening-trained adolescents show greater gains in finger-tapping accuracy (reduced error rate) than afternoon-trained adolescents at both retention intervals
  • Learning timing produces Group X Time interactions in both memory systems: declarative consolidation time courses differ between afternoon and evening groups, and finger-tapping speed and accuracy show highly significant Group X Time interactions
  • Polysomnographic sleep parameters did not differ between afternoon- and evening-trained groups and did not correlate with memory consolidation measures
  • Adolescents trained on word pairs in the afternoon show higher word-pair retention after 24 hours than evening-trained adolescents, but the difference is not sustained at 7 days — related
  • Participants improved tapping speed from the first block to the block where 95% of learning was achieved (d = −1.72) — related
  • Twenty-four-hour consolidation of motor skill differs according to the time of day of training, with deterioration in the morning, stabilization in the afternoon, and improvement in the evening — related
  • Skill consolidation after evening training is comparable when retested 2 h versus 12 h after waking, independent of time awake the day after training — related
  • Skill acquisition during a finger-tapping training session does not differ according to time of day of training — related
  • Mere passage of time awake after evening training does not produce offline skill improvement; the night of sleep is the key factor — related
  • The evening group's 24 h skill improvement reflects offline learning rather than a simple continuation of practice — related
