Related Claims¶

  • Double-dose algebra substantially increased interactive pedagogy for low-skill students (.55 SD, p<.001), unexplained by classroom compositional changes — possibly the same claim (merge candidate)
  • Double-dose algebra policy increased academic demand for both high- and low-skill students, with below-norm students' gains not attributable to peer ability changes — related
  • The double-dose policy led schools to track algebra classes by entering math skills, making classrooms more homogeneous in ability — related
  • Chicago's double-dose algebra policy raised algebra test scores for both low-skill and high-skill students — related
  • Extended learning time interventions, including week-long acceleration academies with highly effective teachers and some double-dose math structures, show strong evidence of effectiveness — related
  • Between- or within-class homogeneous ability grouping has a small negative effect on low-ability primary students but no effect on other students — related
  • Students in active classrooms scored higher on tests of learning than students in passive lectures (0.46 SD higher, P < 0.001) — related
  • Algebra failure rates rose post-policy for above-norm students (~3 percentage points) but fell for below-norm students (~4 points); classroom environment and peer ability explained most of the above-norm rise but not the below-norm fall — related
  • Below-norm students' algebra test scores improved despite having lower-skill peers post-policy, consistent with additional supports offsetting compositional decline — related
  • Evidence that classroom network structure relates to course outcomes under interactive pedagogies is mixed — related
