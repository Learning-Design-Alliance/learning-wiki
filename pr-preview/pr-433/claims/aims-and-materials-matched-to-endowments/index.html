Evidence¶

J. D. Heilman 1916¶

J. D. Heilman. (1916). Psychology in the Schoolroom. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0073918

q1 · i? · theoretical · r2

The article reports that psychological studies on children's mental endowments made clear that, for the school to do its work economically and well, it must not strive for the ideal aims for all classes of children; it also states specific aims are needed for children of superior or very different capacities.

"There are classes of children in the public schools for whom the aims must be very low, such as training in right conduct, but not in the comprehension of right and wrong"

J. D. Heilman 1916 (2)¶

J. D. Heilman. (1916). Psychology in the Schoolroom. The Journal of Educational Psychology. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0073918

q1 · i? · theoretical · r2

The article describes this as a change in school management practice influenced by psychological studies of retardation and endowments, naming the lock-step system as retarded for both bright and dull children; no statistics are printed.

"In many cities the lock-step system of promotion has been broken up through a realization of the fact that it retarded both the bright and the dull child; and special classes and schools have been established in recognition of the widely different endowments of children."
