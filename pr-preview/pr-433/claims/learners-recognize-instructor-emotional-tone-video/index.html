Evidence¶

Lawson 2021¶

Lawson, A. P., Mayer, R. E., Adamo-Villani, N., Benes, B., Lei, X., & Cheng, J. (2021). The positivity principle: do positive instructors improve learning from video lectures? Education Tech Research Dev. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-021-10057-w

q3 · i3 · causal · r2

Experiment 1 (96 students, 2×2 between-subjects ANOVA on emotion ratings) found large valence effects on happy, content, bored, and frustrated ratings, e.g. happy ratings "d = 3.25". Learners distinguished positive from negative emotional tones.

"Overall, there is consistent evidence for the first hypothesis of the positivity princi- ple: students were able to distinguish between the instructor’s positive and negative emo- tional tones."

Related Claims¶

  • Recognizing a frustrated instructor's emotional tone required the embodied onscreen agent, contradicting the voice hypothesis for that emotion — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Voice alone conveys the emotional tone of happy, content, and bored instructors as accurately as voice plus an embodied onscreen agent — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Participants detected emotional valence but did not distinguish between discrete emotions of the same valence — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Participants rated happy and content instructors higher on positive than negative emotion scales, supporting the positivity hypothesis — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Participants rated frustrated and bored instructors higher on negative than positive emotion scales, with the frustrated effect stronger when the agent was on screen — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Learners report paying more attention during learning with a positive instructor than with a negative instructor. — related
  • Instructor competencies were the most critical factor in students' emotions and attention during live online lectures — related
