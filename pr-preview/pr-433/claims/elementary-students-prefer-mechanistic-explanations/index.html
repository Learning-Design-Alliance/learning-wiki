Elementary students prefer and can evaluate mechanistic explanations using explanatory power and accuracy criteria¶

Claim · All claims
Evidence · 1 study · causal r1 · q2 · i2 medium · n=53 students, grades 2-6
Currency · newest study 2025 · not yet checked against its sources · not reviewed by a person

Elementary-age children, across grades 2-6, accept and prefer mechanistic explanations of biological phenomena over non-mechanistic (teleological or circular) ones significantly more often, applying an explanatory-power criterion tied to "how" question framing and an information-accuracy criterion grounded in their existing biological knowledge.

Evidence¶

Shtechman, Ergazaki & Haskel-Ittah 2025¶

Shtechman, Y., Ergazaki, M., & Haskel-Ittah, M. (2025). Mechanistic reasoning in biology among elementary school children: Epistemic criteria for evaluation and construction. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 34(1), 35-70. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2024.2432682

q2 · mixed-methods exploratory study, systematic coding of interview justifications (inter-rater agreement refined to 98%), no control/comparison group · i2 · a statistically significant, moderate-sized difference in acceptance rates (71% vs. 53%, p<.001) · n=53 students, grades 2-6 · causal · r1

Students evaluated two mechanistic and two non-mechanistic explanations for each of three biological phenomena (hydrotropism, phototropism, the withdrawal reflex, blowfish defense), then justified their judgments in semi-structured interviews. Justifications were coded for two epistemic criteria: explanatory power (whether an explanation answers "how") and information accuracy (whether an explanation matches the student's existing biological knowledge). Mechanistic explanations were accepted significantly more often than non-mechanistic ones, and explanatory-power language increased with grade level. The accuracy criterion was applied about equally to mechanistic and non-mechanistic explanations overall, but was used specifically to reject mechanistic-form explanations that conflicted with what students already knew — suggesting explanatory power, not accuracy, is the primary criterion that distinguishes acceptance of mechanistic from non-mechanistic explanations.

Discussion¶

This is a single exploratory study (q2: systematic coding, but not an experiment with a manipulated condition) with a modest sample (n=53) drawn from one context; there is no comparison to an instructional intervention, so the study demonstrates that this reasoning capacity exists and can be elicited by question framing, not that a particular teaching method reliably produces it. The finding that explanatory-power language increased with grade is a cross-sectional association, not a tracked developmental change in the same children. The core practical implication — that explicitly framing questions as "how" rather than "what/why" helps elicit mechanistic over teleological reasoning, even in young children — is well-supported within this study but untested as an instructional intervention in its own right.
