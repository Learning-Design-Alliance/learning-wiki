Evidence¶

Alshehri OAO 2026¶

Alshehri OAO, Lamouchi A, Abdellatif MS, Al-Dosari MNA, Mekheimer M and Nemt-allah MA (2026). From tool to scaffold: structured human–AI collaboration and its effects on academic writing and digital critical thinking among Saudi EFL learners. Front. Psychol. 17:1830103. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1830103

q1 · i? · causal · r2

Qualitative thematic analysis (open coding, category consolidation, constant comparison in NVivo) of open-ended reflections from the 31 experimental-group students. Theme 1 appeared in 100% of participant reflections per Table 5.

"Theme 1, AI as a flexible writing scaffold, captured students' consistent framing of AI tools as supports for their own thinking and composing process rather than substitutes for authorship."

Alshehri OAO 2026 (2)¶

Alshehri OAO, Lamouchi A, Abdellatif MS, Al-Dosari MNA, Mekheimer M and Nemt-allah MA (2026). From tool to scaffold: structured human–AI collaboration and its effects on academic writing and digital critical thinking among Saudi EFL learners. Front. Psychol. 17:1830103. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1830103

q2 · i? · causal · r2

Descriptive analysis of the structured reflection instrument (Table 4): AI improved content/organization M = 3.48 (SD 0.68, scale 1–4); language accuracy M = 3.39 (SD 0.50); reliance beyond course guidance M = 2.10 (SD 0.30, scale 1–3).

"Structured reflection items from the experimental group (n = 31) indicated that students perceived AI as helpful for improving both organizational quality and language accuracy, while reporting relatively moderate levels of reliance on AI output and high rates of output verification prior to incorporation into their writing."

Related Claims¶

  • PGR students rejected GenAI generation of research ideas, original arguments, or whole papers as violating authorship norms, while accepting surface-level support — related
  • Nearly all postgraduate research students in the study used GenAI across multiple research stages, positioning it as a supportive assistant with human oversight retained — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Planning-stage AI ideas were often redundant with or rejected in favor of writers' own ideas: idea attribution was 17.8% among users versus 2.9% among non-users, but ownership did not differ between users and non-users within ai plan. — related
  • AI support shifts perceived attribution of ideas and text toward the AI, most strongly for AI-generated drafts, and an AI draft based on the writer's own outline contributed more ideas than expected (27%). — related
  • Students framed AI as replacement rather than scaffolding in 48 of 49 essays — reports the opposite
  • GenAI functions as productive self-scaffolding when students retain executive control, and becomes unproductive when reasoning is outsourced — related
  • Hybridization of authorship blends human and AI-generated content, often without learners' explicit awareness of the boundary, normalizing formulaic rhetorical structures — related
  • Experimental-group students detected AI errors and enacted verification and ethical self-regulation as routine practices — related
  • ChatGPT-assisted students reported substantially lower ownership of their submitted code across self-attributed ownership, psychological ownership, and authorship attribution — related
  • Minority of undergraduate health science students reported using GenAI for reflective writing, mostly to support rather than replace reflection — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • AI ghostwriter effect: users do not claim ownership of AI-generated texts yet fail to publicly acknowledge the AI's contribution — reports the opposite
  • Focus-group participants preferred creating their own essays while viewing ChatGPT as an initial resource for fundamentals and idea generation — possibly the same claim (merge candidate)
