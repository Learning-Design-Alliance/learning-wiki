Evidence¶

Amrita Ganguly 2026¶

Amrita Ganguly, Aditya Johri, Nora McDonald, Areej Ali, Umama Dewan, and Aayushi Hingle Collier. (2026). A Comparative Analysis of Institutional and Course Generative AI Policies within Higher Education: Implications for Instruction in Computing Education. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12296 doi:10.48550/arxiv.2607.12296

q2 · i? · qualitative · r3

Document analysis of 98 computer science syllabi from 54 R1 universities, coded by three researchers with a 16-code codebook. The course-level analysis found "half (50%, N=49) outright prohibiting use" and 41% permitting partial use for specified activities.

"Almost all (92%, N=90) syllabi provided explicit guidelines about permissions to use GenAI in their course, with half (50%, N=49) outright prohibiting use and a few only allowing it with explicit permission (N=6)."

Related Claims¶

  • Both institutional and course-level guidance emphasize transparency and acknowledging or citing GenAI use, with 83% of syllabi providing explicit citation guidance — related
  • Comprehensive institutional GenAI guidelines translate to course-level guidelines at higher rates (48.3–71.4%) than moderately comprehensive ones (30.8–31.2%) — related
  • Institutional guidance cautions instructors about privacy (60% of institutions) while syllabi more often flag veracity of GenAI output and hindering learning — related
  • Course syllabi anthropomorphize GenAI — 39% attribute living characteristics such as a GenAI Assistant — a theme absent from institutional guidelines — related
  • Course syllabi name specific GenAI tools — ChatGPT in 90 of 98 — while institutional guidelines contain no code for specific tools — related
  • Perceived institutional AI regulation differs significantly across STEM subjects, with mathematics and natural sciences perceived as prohibitive and computer science as permissive — related
  • Universities converge on disclosure and attribution expectations as the condition distinguishing legitimate AI assistance from misrepresentation — related
  • Among the 50 most innovative U.S. universities, instructor-set syllabus-level governance is the dominant GenAI convergence, with 33 of 50 providing syllabus or course-policy guidance — related
  • Most undergraduate students in computer science–related programs are unaware of their institution's GenAI regulations (62.25% not aware; 25.83% aware) — related
  • Strict AI prohibitions can intensify students' moral conflict without preventing AI use — related
  • Educators' interpretations of students' AI use evolved from perceived AI stigma toward emphasizing AI usage transparency and explicit AI guidance — related
  • A majority of faculty believe student evaluation methods require significant change, and preferences tilt toward disclosure-based permission regimes over bans — related
  • Nearly all top-50 universities provide faculty GenAI guidelines, with 94% emphasizing establishing and communicating course-specific GenAI policies — related
  • Most students favour allowing ChatGPT in assessments if tasks require original thinking or guidance is provided — related
  • Most university AI policies are written as neutral suggested guidelines rather than strongly subjective or objective mandates — related
  • Institutions diverge in default GenAI permission stances, ranging from unauthorized-unless-authorized to program-level carve-outs where instructors may not ban AI use — related
