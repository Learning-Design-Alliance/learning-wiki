Evidence¶

Jongbloed 2024¶

Jongbloed, J., Chaker, R., & Lavoué, E. (2024). Immersive procedural training in virtual reality: A systematic literature review. Computers & Education, 221, 105124. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105124

q3 · i2 · quant-synthesis · r2

Meta-analysis of media-comparison studies (16 experimental results from 11 studies) using Hedges' g with analytic weighting in Stata. The review reports "a positive overall effect size (theta)" of g = 0.58 for IVR versus less immersive controls.

"In the sixteen sets of results included in the meta-analysis, we find a positive overall effect size (theta) when comparing IVR with less immersive learning conditions: Hedge's g = 0.58, indicating a medium positive effect of IVR as compared to controls on learning outcomes"

Related Claims¶

  • Only half of reviewed IVR procedural-training studies framed their application with an explicit learning theory, and only two used IVR-specific theories (CTML or CAMIL) — related
  • Immersive lessons show a small effect size advantage over less immersive technology, but increased presence does not necessarily lead to more learning — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Prior general meta-analyses of IVR learning found small positive effects (g = 0.24 and g = 0.38), supporting the hypothesis that procedural skills respond particularly well to IVR — a broader claim this one bears on
  • Knowledge transfer outcomes show the largest effect sizes of immersive procedural training (g = 1.80), while knowledge gain and retention effects are not significant — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • VR improved movement-based transfer test performance versus reading in security training, while learning in VR without the possibility to act was detrimental — related
  • Increased perceptual fidelity fails to reliably improve serious game learning outcomes (the fidelity fallacy) — related
