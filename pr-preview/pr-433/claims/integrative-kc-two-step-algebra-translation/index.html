Evidence¶

Kenneth R. Koedinger 2010¶

Kenneth R. Koedinger, Albert T. Corbett, Charles Perfetti. (2010). The Knowledge-Learning-Instruction (KLI) Framework: Toward Bridging the Science-Practice Chasm to Enhance Robust Student Learning. Carnegie Mellon University Technical Report. http://learnlab.org/research/wiki

q2 · i? · theoretical · r2

The article reports, citing Heffernan and Koedinger (1997), a performance difference between two-step and matched one-step algebra translation tasks, attributed to a missing integrative KC (an algebra grammar rule for composing expressions). It adds that instruction targeting this hidden KC 'significantly improved student performance' (Koedinger & McLaughlin, in press).

"Heffernan and Koedinger (1997) found students were significantly worse at translating two-step algebra story problems into expressions (like 800-40x) than they were at translating two closely matched one-step problems"

Discussion¶

Related Claims¶

  • Simpler knowledge components are executed faster: correct application times rise from about 3-6 seconds for constant-constant KCs to 10-14 seconds for variable-variable KCs
  • KLI complexity-matching hypothesis: instructional principles of a given complexity are effective for knowledge components of equal or greater complexity but not less complex ones
  • Personalizing algebra story problems to student interests improved accuracy on easy knowledge components within the tutoring unit — related
  • Personalization to student interests improved accuracy on hard knowledge components involving algebraic expression-writing within the tutoring unit — related
  • In a small subgroup of struggling students (n = 25), interest personalization greatly improved expression-writing accuracy within the tutoring unit — related
  • Personalization reduced time spent on hard expression-writing steps, and students who had received it were still faster at writing expressions in a later non-personalized unit — related
  • An IES practice-guide presentation recommends personalized problem contexts, asserting without cited studies that students engage more actively and perform better in them — related
