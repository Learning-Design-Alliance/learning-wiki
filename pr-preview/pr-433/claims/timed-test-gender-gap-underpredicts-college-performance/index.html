Evidence¶

Halpern 2007¶

Halpern, D., Aronson, J., Reimer, N., Simpkins, S., Star, J., and Wentzel, K. (2007). Encouraging Girls in Math and Science (NCER 2007-2003). Washington, DC: National Center for Education Research, Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/5

q2 · i? · associational · r?

Descriptive NAEP trend analysis (Figures 3 and 4) reported in the overview, citing the 2005 NAEP High School Transcript Study. The comparison names girls and boys as the two sides; no effect sizes are printed.

"on the 2005 NAEP math and science assessments, girls scored lower than boys when controlling for highest course completed at all levels, except the lowest level (see figures 3 and 4)."

Halpern 2007 (2)¶

Halpern, D., Aronson, J., Reimer, N., Simpkins, S., Star, J., and Wentzel, K. (2007). Encouraging Girls in Math and Science (NCER 2007-2003). Washington, DC: National Center for Education Research, Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/5

q2 · i? · review · r2

The guide's overview cites Wainer and Steinberg (1992) for this finding, using it to argue that "it is not ability, per se, that hinders girls and women from pursuing careers in math and science." Reported as cited evidence, not reanalyzed.

"Researchers have found, for instance, that SAT math scores underpredict young women's performance in college math courses."
