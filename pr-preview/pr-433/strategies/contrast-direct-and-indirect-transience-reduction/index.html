Design experiments contrasting direct learner offloading versus indirect transience reduction by the presenter¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 review), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that future research should explore the benefits of allowing an active role for the individual in choosing how and when to reduce transience during complex learning tasks, either through direct offloading or through avoiding transience. It suggests contrasting direct offloading on the part of the learner versus indirect offloading via transience reduction by the presenter, examining impacts on learning of otherwise transient information.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Research designs must manipulate who controls transience reduction: the learner through direct offloading or the presenter through presentation design

Constraints¶

  • The article frames this as a direction for future research rather than a tested instructional approach

Target Learners¶

  • learners in complex learning tasks, including e-learning contexts

Target Learning Goals¶

  • learning of otherwise transient information

Claims¶

  • Both cognitive offloading and the transient information effect reduce extraneous cognitive load by making information more accessible or less transient [+W]
  • Shifting information to external aids enhances task performance with greater gains under high cognitive load [+W]
  • Cognitive offloading does not guarantee representational permanence and memory can suffer if the external record becomes unavailable [~W]
  • Element interactivity moderates the effectiveness of cognitive offloading, with greater benefit for high element interactivity materials [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Design experimental studies that systematically vary how GenAI outputs are presented, comparing full solutions against structured hints or constructive feedback guardrails.
  • Design future developmental observational-learning studies to manipulate social context, direct versus indirect observation, and active versus passive learning
