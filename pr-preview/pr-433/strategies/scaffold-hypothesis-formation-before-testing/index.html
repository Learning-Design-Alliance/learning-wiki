Scaffold hypothesis formation before testing and provide tools for explicit hypothesis statements in games¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 associational), q3 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Based on the finding that confusion after scan outcomes reflected disconfirmed hypotheses, the authors recommend that intelligent systems respond to contextualized emotions by scaffolding reasoning. They write: "if we know that higher levels of confusion is a result of forming inaccurate hypotheses, the system can scaffold the student to ensure the hypothesis is correct prior to scanning the food item or submitting the final diagnosis". They further suggest designing a tool that lets students explicitly state hypotheses so they can monitor hypothesis formation and testing during gameplay.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Real-time detection of contextualized emotions and in-game action outcomes that the system can respond to adaptively

Constraints¶

  • The recommendation is grounded in correlational/regression findings from a single study with 61 participants; the emotion-score correlations themselves were not significant after correction

Target Learners¶

  • college students learning scientific reasoning in game-based environments

Target Learning Goals¶

  • hypothesis generation and testing
  • self-regulated learning
  • emotion regulation during learning

Claims¶

  • Students experienced less confusion after positive scan results than after negative scan results [+W]
  • Joy during relevant book reading and confusion after positive scans positively predicted overall game score, while total worksheet submissions negatively predicted it [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Embed the ReACT reasoning-acting-observing-reflecting cycle in tutoring systems to mirror inquiry-based learning
  • Simulations + Structured Activity
  • Provide explicit prompting instruction and expert scaffolding before or during novice LLM activities
