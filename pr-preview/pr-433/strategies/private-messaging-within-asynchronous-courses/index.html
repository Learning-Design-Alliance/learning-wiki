Strategies

Offer a private messaging function inside asynchronous online courses to support community formation and interactivity¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 2 mixed) · 1 study (1 associational), q2 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends providing students a synchronous private messaging mechanism within an asynchronous course environment, on the grounds that the personal nature of messaging appears to strengthen community without privatizing participation. The authors state that "A significant relationship between the number of private messages and the number of asynchronous notes might also suggest that the personal nature of messaging increases the sense of community among class participants". In their study, instructors sometimes primed use by sending personalized messages at course start or encouraged messaging to coordinate group activities.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • The course environment must provide a private messaging tool that notifies online recipients immediately and delivers messages to offline recipients at next access.
  • Asynchronous participation should remain a graded or otherwise expected activity, as in the studied courses where forum participation was required for part of the grade.

Constraints¶

  • The authors warn that adding a communication mode cannot be assumed to increase interaction, calling the belief that increased connectivity deterministically leads to increased interaction a powerful myth of online learning.
  • The study is observational and could not contrast single-mode against dual-mode courses, so causal benefits of adding messaging are not established.

Target Learners¶

  • graduate education students in fully online courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • sustained asynchronous knowledge construction and discussion participation
  • community formation and social presence in online courses

Claims¶

  • LIWC analysis shows synchronous messages carry more social and emotional language while asynchronous notes carry more cognitive-processing language [+W]
  • Frequent private-message writers show a lower note scan rate, scanning fewer opened notes (r = −.18) [+W]
  • Students who write more synchronous messages also write more asynchronous notes and read more notes (r = .60 and r = .36) [+W]
  • Students who write more synchronous messages spend more time online (r = .61) [+W]
  • No significant correlation between number of messages written and average length of notes written [~W]
  • Synchronous private messages are shorter, easier to read, and use fewer academic words than asynchronous notes in dual-mode online courses [~W]
