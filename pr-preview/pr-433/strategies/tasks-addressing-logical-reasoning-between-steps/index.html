Design hypothesis-testing tasks that require students to reason about relations between steps rather than fill in pre-given steps¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), q3 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that learning environments include tasks in which students construct whole hypothesis-testing solutions — selecting and ordering steps themselves — so that feedback can address the logic connecting steps, rather than splitting the procedure into separate items with pre-given steps. The authors state that from an educational perspective, availability of tasks addressing the logical reasoning "would have been valuable as well, to avoid that students rely too much on the ITS feedback". Such tasks would also allow better research into transfer of feedback effects.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Tasks in which students can freely select and order steps, with a system monitoring the internal consistency of the solution so far

Constraints¶

  • The study's follow-up tasks did not have this character, which the authors name as a limitation possibly explaining the lack of transfer

Target Learners¶

  • first-year university students in introductory statistics courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • understanding the logic of and relations between steps in hypothesis testing

Claims¶

  • The combined-paradigm ITS allows easy creation of new hypothesis-testing tasks because model-tracing components generate solutions and intermediate steps [+W]
  • Students receiving intelligent ITS feedback made significantly fewer errors in hypothesis-testing logic than students receiving verification feedback only [+W]
  • Students receiving intelligent ITS feedback spent significantly more time and made significantly more steps on the hypothesis-testing tasks than controls [+W]
  • ITS feedback availability interacted with task number so that experimental-group students increasingly outperformed controls in solving tasks over time [+W]
  • The number of tasks students worked on and attempted step construction for did not differ significantly between feedback conditions [~W]
  • Effects of intelligent ITS feedback did not transfer to follow-up hypothesis-testing tasks without intelligent feedback [-W]
