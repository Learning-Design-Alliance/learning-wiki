Debrief and redirect attributions during computational thinking teacher education: normalize struggle, debunk stereotypical attributions, and reframe causes as controllable and temporary¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's implications for inclusive computer science education within teacher education programs are an enumerated set of actions teacher educators can take when preservice teachers struggle with computational thinking. They include to "normalize struggle during problem solving", "address and/or debunk both stereotypical and non-stereotypical attributions", and "redirect attributions to controllable and unstable factors such as effort and persistence rather than innate abilities". The strategy also calls for mastery-oriented goals, role models that challenge stereotypical narratives, and attention to intersecting factors such as race, gender, socioeconomic status, and prior access to technology.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Teacher educators must attend to attributions expressed during struggle and intervene to debunk them, per the article's implication that attributions be addressed rather than left unchallenged.

Constraints¶

  • The article states extended and unsupported struggle can be detrimental to motivation, so support must accompany struggle.

Target Learners¶

  • preservice teachers in teacher education programs

Target Learning Goals¶

  • computational thinking
  • computer science attitudes and belonging

Claims¶

  • Stereotypical attributions to struggle reproduce dominant discourse that excludes minoritized populations from computer science [+W]
  • Negative dispositional attributions persisted even after successful task completion [+W]
  • Stereotypical attributions framed struggle as dispositional, uncontrollable, and permanent, while non-stereotypical attributions were situational, controllable, and temporary [+W]
  • Preservice teachers' attributions to struggle with computational thinking were predominantly stereotypical and negative [+W]
