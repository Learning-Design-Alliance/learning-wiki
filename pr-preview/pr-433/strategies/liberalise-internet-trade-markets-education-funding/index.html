Liberalise internet trade markets through legal frameworks to de-monopolise internet service provision and reduce the costs of digital infrastructure for education¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (2 review, 1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

The article proposes that governments of developing countries move beyond traditional donor and stakeholder quota funding for the digitalisation of higher education. Its recommendation is to "liberalise internet trade markets through legal frameworks to de-monopolise the provision of internet services", which it argues would reduce the costs of erecting digital infrastructures and provision of services. The strategy is addressed to governments rather than to individual institutions.

Context¶

  • Government-level legal frameworks that open internet service provision to competition

Constraints¶

  • Proposed in the context of developing countries, with Zimbabwe as the studied case; the article presents it as a proposal rather than a tested intervention

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Access to online teaching and learning and the institutional capacity to deliver it

Claims¶

  • Zimbabwean universities faced trenchant institutional needs for online teaching — untrained personnel, poor digital infrastructure and cyber security — mostly emanating from inadequate funding [+M]
  • Digital infrastructure readiness determines the scalability and effectiveness of AI systems in higher education, with disparities between high-income and low-income regions [+M]
