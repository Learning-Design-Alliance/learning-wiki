Latent change score (LCS) modeling framework for comparing developmental theories¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 2 studies (2 associational), q2–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article uses bivariate latent change score (LCS) models, which "conceptualize differences between successive measurements as latent change factors," allowing within-subject change to be modeled via self-feedback (β) and coupling (γ) parameters. This framework let the authors translate g-factor, investment, and mutualism accounts into competing psychometric models and compare them directly on the same longitudinal data — something prior studies had not done. The coupling parameter indexes whether change in one domain is affected by the prior level of the other domain.

Accounts¶

  • Latent change score modeling framework for comparing developmental theories: The article uses bivariate latent change score (LCS) models, which "conceptualize differences between successive measurements as latent change factors," allowing within-subject change to be modeled via self-feedback (β) and coupling (γ) parameters. This framework let the authors translate g-factor, investment, and mutualism accounts into competing psychometric models and compare them directly on the same longitudinal data — something prior studies had not done. The coupling parameter indexes whether change in one domain is affected by the prior level of the other domain. (Kievit et al. (2017))
  • Bivariate latent change score modeling as a method for testing dynamic co-development of two constructs: The article's methodological framework combines cross-lagged panel and parallel-process growth modeling. "Bivariate latent change score (LCS) models make use of longitudinal data to define the nature of dynamic relations between two variables over time," with change decomposed into a constant change component (α ∗ η), a proportional autoregressive component (β), and a cross-lagged component (γ) that "estimates whether one construct is a leading indicator of change in the second construct." The authors argue this approach detects bidirectional influences that growth-curve models miss, because "LCS models have a parallel process growth curve model embedded in them, which allows for testing of bidirectional influences between two constructs above and beyond the influence of correlated intercepts and slopes and proportional change parameters." (Quinn et al. (2020))

Claims¶

  • Higher starting scores in vocabulary predicted greater gains in matrix reasoning and vice versa (bidirectional positive coupling) [+M]
  • For typically developing students, vocabulary knowledge and reading comprehension show bidirectional leading influences over time in kindergarten through fourth grade [+M]
  • For students with a school-identified learning disability, there were no significant direct cross-lagged pathways between vocabulary knowledge and reading comprehension [+M]
