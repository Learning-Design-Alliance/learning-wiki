CID framework for the cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs¶

Research Method · All research methods
Evidence · 8 claims (6 for, 2 mixed) · 4 studies (3 causal, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 4 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The CID framework (a framework for the cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs) explains how learning in one phase affects subsequent phases by focusing on the learner's knowledge state between phases. The editorial states it analyzes "intermediate knowledge"—the knowledge between phases—and how it shapes further learning, constituting a preparation effect. It builds on Koedinger et al. (2012) by positing analysis on three levels: instructional design, learning processes, and knowledge components, supported by research designs that isolate single mechanisms.

Accounts¶

  • The CID framework explains preparation effects in composite instructional designs through intermediate knowledge: The CID framework (a framework for the cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs) explains how learning in one phase affects subsequent phases by focusing on the learner's knowledge state between phases. The editorial states it analyzes "intermediate knowledge"—the knowledge between phases—and how it shapes further learning, constituting a preparation effect. It builds on Koedinger et al. (2012) by positing analysis on three levels: instructional design, learning processes, and knowledge components, supported by research designs that isolate single mechanisms. (Katharina Loibl et al. (2025))
  • CID framework linking knowledge, learning, and instruction levels across phases of composite instructional designs: The CID framework extends the knowledge–learning–instruction approach to multi-phase designs. As the authors state, it is "a framework for conductin g cognitive analyses of composite instructional designs (CID) that links the three levels – (K) knowledge components, (L) learning processes, and (I) instructional designs". Its core element is intermediate knowledge (K1) generated by the first phase, which shapes learning processes in the second phase; modelling prior knowledge (K0) additionally allows explanation of aptitude-treatment-interactions such as expertise-reversal effects. (Katharina Loibl · Timo Leuders · Inga Glogger-Frey · Nikol Rummel (2025))
  • CID framework for the cognitive analysis of composite instructional designs: The CID framework (cognitive analyses of composite instructional designs) is presented as "a useful means to systematically describe the elements of such an instructional design and its theoretical assumptions" for multi-phase designs like PS-I. It requires theoretical specification on the levels of knowledge, learning, and instruction, and introduces the notion of intermediate knowledge acquired in the first phase, which can alter learning processes in the second phase and thereby affect outcomes. The article uses it to align the replication's materials and mechanisms with the original study. (Loibl et al. (2024))
  • Composite Instructional Design (CID) framework: intermediate knowledge links exploration phases to subsequent instruction: The CID framework, introduced by Loibl et al. (2024), provides "a structured lens for interpreting how the sequence of instructional phases shapes the intermediate knowledge students acquire during exploration and impacts their cognitive processes during instruction". Its central construct is intermediate knowledge — the state of knowledge after the initial phase — which can include target-knowledge components, activated prior knowledge, erroneous knowledge requiring refutation, knowledge-gap awareness, and procedural knowledge. The framework holds that the effectiveness of subsequent direct instruction depends heavily on the intermediate knowledge and cognitive states fostered during prior exploration. This study uses CID to interpret Exploration-first versus Instruction-first sequences for MVC learning. (Saba et al. (2025))
  • Typology of intermediate-knowledge types and their interplay with second-phase learning processes: The article distinguishes types of intermediate knowledge (K1) generated in the first phase: partial or erroneous knowledge serving integration in the second phase (partial knowledge integrated into target knowledge, erroneous knowledge contributing via refutation, specific negative knowledge focusing on relevant aspects), and instrumental knowledge that supports rather than becomes target knowledge (situational knowledge as a concrete anchor, unspecific negative knowledge as global awareness of knowledge gaps generating motivation, and supportive knowledge such as strategies or fluency freeing cognitive capacity). As illustrated in Fig. 5, these types "contribute differently to the learning processes (L2) initiated by the instructional design of the second phase". (Katharina Loibl · Timo Leuders · Inga Glogger-Frey · Nikol Rummel (2025))

Claims¶

  • PS-I instructional designs can improve conceptual understanding and transfer when design principles are met [+M]
  • PS-I effects did not replicate in all domains: null sequence effects in Bayesian reasoning, javelin throwing, and inquiry-skill sequencing [~W]
  • Worked-example preparation deepens subsequent learning via more elaboration during the future-learning phase [+W]
  • Inventing-phase failure was unproductive: invented solutions did not correlate with transfer, but suboptimal inventions related to lower interest and learning time [~W]
  • In the Bayesian-reasoning problem-solving phase, most students generated only one solution attempt, far fewer than in the original variance study [+M]
  • Students' generated solutions rarely matched the erroneous solutions included in the explicit instruction phase [+M]
  • Exploration-first students transfer MVC-strategy Efficiency better, with condition-by-Task-2 interactions for Efficiency and Quality [+M]
  • Exploration-first students show more advanced MVC reasoning on the transfer task than Instruction-first students [+M]
