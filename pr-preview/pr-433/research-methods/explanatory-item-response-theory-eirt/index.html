Explanatory Item Response Theory (EIRT)¶

Description¶

EIRT is described as "a special name given to models of multilevel, categorical responses, in which latent estimates are generated for both item and person parameters and explanatory variables can be modeled as predictors of responses across levels of analysis". It combines experimental design with observational measurement to yield explanatory measurement. The article maps SCM postulates onto crossclassified, mixed-effects, and bivariate EIRT specifications, using random intercepts and slopes for items and persons in adolescent addition data.

Accounts¶

  • Explanatory Item Response Theory (EIRT) as a psychometric framework for jointly estimating item and person effects on problem solving: EIRT is described as "a special name given to models of multilevel, categorical responses, in which latent estimates are generated for both item and person parameters and explanatory variables can be modeled as predictors of responses across levels of analysis". It combines experimental design with observational measurement to yield explanatory measurement. The article maps SCM postulates onto crossclassified, mixed-effects, and bivariate EIRT specifications, using random intercepts and slopes for items and persons in adolescent addition data. (Rhodes et al. (2018))
  • Multilevel explanatory IRT based on Embretson's Multidimensional Rasch Model for Learning and Change: The article applies an item response theory approach based on Embretson's Multidimensional Rasch Model for Learning and Change, extended with multilevel and explanatory components, to model children's pretest-to-posttest change in analogy solving. Ability is built up in a summation term over testing occasions, with an initial ability factor and modifiabilities representing change. Person covariates such as age and working memory are entered with regression parameters on the latent scale, and nested models are compared with likelihood ratio tests. The authors state this "allowed for reliable estimates of learning from feedback" where classical gain scores are unreliable. (Stevenson (2017))

Claims¶

  • The effect of min-counting on solution time and accuracy varies significantly across both items and persons [+M]
  • Interactive tutoring feedback leads to greater progression in children's analogy solving than multiple-try feedback, which is more effective than no feedback [+M]
  • Working memory moderates feedback effects: higher working memory children gain more from tutoring feedback and no feedback, but gains in the multiple-try condition are unrelated to working memory [+M]
  • Children who initially used non-analogical strategies improved more from pretest to posttest than analogical reasoners, especially with tutoring feedback [+M]
