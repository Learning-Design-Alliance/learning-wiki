Community schools approach¶

Product or Programme · All products and programmes
Evidence · 11 claims (11 for) · 5 studies (3 qualitative, 2 review), q2 · 0 of 5 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

Description¶

A whole-child, whole-school, whole-community framework organized around integrated student supports, expanded learning opportunities, family and community engagement, and collaborative leadership, used by schools and community partners.

Components¶

  • The four-pillar framework of community schools: The report organizes the community schools approach around four common features, or pillars: integrated student supports, expanded learning time and opportunities, family and community engagement, and collaborative leadership and practice. The review states that although community schools vary by local context, four features—or pillars—appear in most community schools and support the conditions for teaching and learning found in high-quality schools. The framework structures the report's evidence review: each pillar is reviewed in its own chapter, and the pillars are mapped onto characteristics of high-quality schools (Table 3, Figure 1) and onto addressing out-of-school barriers to learning. (Maier et al. (2017))
  • Community schools strategy with four key pillars: The report describes the community schools approach as "an evidence-based approach for increasing the school success of students living in communities of poverty that should become the norm in New Mexico's high-poverty schools." Although community schools vary to reflect local conditions, "they are built with four key pillars: integrated supports, expanded learning time and opportunities, family and community engagement, and collaborative leadership." The report recommends making the strategy the norm in schools where at least 80% of students come from low-income families, and notes Albuquerque, Las Cruces, and Santa Fe have created more than three dozen community schools. (Oakes et al. (2020))
  • Community schools as a place-based integrated-support strategy: Community schools are "a place-based strategy in which schools partner with community agencies and allocate resources to provide an integrated focus on academics, health and social services, youth and community development, and family and community engagement". Many operate on all-day and year-round schedules serving both children and adults, functioning as academic and social centers for innovative learning, recreation, health care, and social services. The report describes them as associated with reduced absenteeism, improved academic outcomes, and more positive school climates, and notes statewide initiatives in California, Maryland, New Mexico, New York, and Vermont. (Cookson et al. (2022))
  • New Mexico's six key practices framework for community schools: The New Mexico Public Education Department's community schools framework identifies six site-level practices for implementation: collaborative leadership, shared power, and voice; powerful student and family engagement; rigorous, community-connected classroom instruction; expanded, culturally enriched learning opportunities; a culture of belonging, safety, and care; and integrated systems of support. The brief states these practices are "grounded in research and the expertise of community school practitioners participating in the national Community Schools Forward task force" and were integrated into the strategic vision and daily operation of the profiled schools. (Germain et al. (2025))
  • Community schools as a whole-child strategy partnering with local organizations and families: The brief defines community schools, quoting the New Mexico PED, as "a whole child, comprehensive strategy to transform schools into places where educators, local community members, families, and students work together to strengthen conditions for student learning and healthy development." Schools achieve this by partnering with local organizations and families to organize in- and out-of-school resources such as health services, meals, and after-school programming. The strategy is positioned as an equity-centered, evidence-based approach to school transformation. (Germain et al. (2025))
  • New Mexico's six key site-level practices of community schools: The New Mexico Public Education Department defines a community school as "a whole child, comprehensive strategy to transform schools" where educators, families, community members, and students organize in- and out-of-school resources so young people thrive. PED identifies six key site-level practices grounded in research and practitioner expertise: expanded culturally enriched learning opportunities; rigorous community-connected classroom instruction; a culture of belonging, safety, and care; integrated systems of support; powerful student and family engagement; and collaborative leadership with shared power and voice. Los Padillas's transformation is organized around these practices. (Germain (2024))
  • New Mexico PED community school framework with six key site-level practices: The New Mexico Public Education Department defines a community school as "a whole child, comprehensive strategy to transform schools" where educators, families, community members, and students organize in- and out-of-school resources so young people thrive. PED identifies six key site-level practices: expanded culturally enriched learning opportunities; rigorous community-connected classroom instruction; a culture of belonging, safety, and care; integrated systems of support; powerful student and family engagement; and collaborative leadership, shared power and voice. The Roswell profile documents implementation of several of these practices at Sierra and University High. (Espinoza (2024))
  • Community schools as a whole child transformation strategy anchored by a cross-boundary coordinator role: The report frames community schools as an evidence-based school transformation strategy that unites students, families, educators, and community partners to improve learning and well-being through a whole child approach. Its central organizing concept is the coordinator: "a school-based staff member not traditionally found in schools, who is charged with organizing access to a range of public and private resources tailored to student and family needs and weaving them into the fabric and daily operations of the school." Coordinators act as cross-boundary leaders and connectors between schools, community partners, and families, and require targeted capacity building because the role is new and distinct from typical school positions. (Germain et al. (2026))

Claims¶

  • Integrated student supports are associated with improvements in attendance, behavior, social functioning, and academic achievement [+M]
  • Thoughtfully designed expanded learning time and opportunities are associated with positive academic and nonacademic outcomes, with the best-designed studies showing the strongest effects [+M]
  • Meaningful family and community engagement is associated with reduced absenteeism, improved academic outcomes, and more positive school climates [+M]
  • Collaborative leadership and practice can create conditions for improving student learning, with social capital and teacher-peer learning explaining the link to achievement [+M]
  • Community schools are associated with positive student outcomes including reduced absenteeism, improved academics, and more positive school climates [+M]
  • Los Padillas reduced chronic absenteeism by more than half, from 65.5% in 2021–22 to 31.3% in 2023–24, a rate below other APS community schools [+M]
  • 66% of Los Padillas students reported a sense of belonging in 2023–24, well above the 44% for students in the rest of the district [+M]
  • University High School's suspensions dropped 78% and fights fell from more than 10 per semester to one in the 2023–24 school year after adopting the community school strategy [+M]
  • Sierra Middle School students reporting a caring adult who regularly checks on them rose from 4% to 31% over three years [+M]
  • In Lynwood Unified, chronic absence fell from 37.1% to 24.5% and average daily attendance rose from 87.4% to 91.5% during the first two years of districtwide community schools implementation. [+M]
  • After becoming community schools, 11 of 12 Lynwood elementary schools increased the percentage of students meeting or exceeding the ELA proficiency standard, and 10 did so in math. [+M]
