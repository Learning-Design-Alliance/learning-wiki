Description¶

A school-improvement measurement framework developed by the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research that organizes school survey profiles around five domains and their related measures.

Components¶

  • Essential supports framework for educational technology use in schools: A theory developed and tested in a previous Consortium report on educational technology availability and use in Chicago's public schools, holding that teacher assignment and student use of technology depend on factors beyond hardware and software availability. The brief states, "Chief among these were the professional development opportunities in technology for teachers and the professional community within the school supporting technology use," plus technology support staff and, most important, principal leadership for technology use. The Grow analysis applies this framework and finds its Grow findings "very similar" to the earlier technology findings. (John Q. Easton (2004))
  • Five essential supports framework for urban school improvement: The Consortium's organizing framework for Chicago school improvement, based on broad research on urban school improvement. It "posits that improvements in student learning require effective school leadership, parental involvement, professional development and collaboration among teachers, a climate focused on student learning, and quality instructional programs." This report examines the fourth support, a student-centered learning climate, encompassing school safety and order, teachers' personal concern for students, and expectations for serious academic work, and synthesizes findings on four of the five supports as they relate to the city's lowest-performing schools. (Sebring et al. (1996))
  • Five essential supports framework for organizing school improvement self-analysis: The report organizes survey results according to five essential supports for student learning: School Leadership; Parent and Community Partnerships; Student-Centered Learning Climate; Professional Development and Collaboration; and Quality Instructional Program. Each support involves a number of best practices, and for most practices one or two scales from student or teacher responses summarize the state of the practice in a school. The framework was deliberately tailored so individualized school reports could serve as a companion to a district self-analysis guide. (Sebring et al. (1995))
  • Five essential organizational supports for improving student learning: The article reports a Chicago framework in which schools effective at improving student learning have strong organizational structures across five areas: "leadership, professional capacity, partnerships with parents and community, learning climate, and instruction." The framework treats the school as an organization whose supports, not just individual teacher skill, set the stage for instructional effectiveness and improvement in mathematics and reading. (Elaine Allensworth (2012))
  • Five Essential Supports framework for organizing school improvement conditions: The report organizes all school survey profiles around five domains: school leadership, parent and community partnerships, student-centered learning climate, professional capacity, and quality of the instructional program. These five domains are frequently referred to as the Essential Supports for Student Learning, and the report states that schools strong in these supports are more likely to improve academically while weak schools are more likely to be non-improving. It emphasizes that it is the systemic blending of initiatives that makes a material difference in student learning, so profiles should be read in relation to one another. (Luppescu et al. (2001))
  • Five Essential Supports for School Improvement (5Es) framework: The 5Es is a framework developed by the Consortium on Chicago School Research detailing what school leaders should attend to when improving schools. The article states it was "First developed in the mid-1990s" and that consortium studies "continued to elaborate the Five Essential Supports for School Improvement (5Es)". It still guides improvement efforts in Chicago and has been adopted by many other districts, functioning as a system-level map of the conditions school leaders must manage. (Sharon Greenberg et al. (2024))
  • The Five Essential Supports framework organizes school climate measurement into five key components of student learning: The report organizes its school measures under an 'Overview of the Five Essential Supports and Corresponding Consortium Profiles and Measures' diagram listing five 'ESSENTIAL SUPPORTS' key components: School Leadership, Parent and Community Partnerships, Student-Centered Learning Climate, Professional Capacity, and Quality Instructional Program. Each support is subdivided into named measures such as Teacher-Principal Trust, Safety, Professional Community, and Quality of Student Discussion. Some measures are marked as applying only to elementary ('E') or high school ('H') teachers and students. (Luppescu et al. (2005))
  • Five Essential Supports framework for organizing school improvement measures: The Consortium's framework holds that five domains—school leadership, parent and community partnerships, a student-centered learning climate, professional capacity, and quality of the instructional program—are "frequently referred to as the Essential Supports for Student Learning in improving student achievement." The report uses these supports as the organizing structure for school survey profiles, with each support described by a set of related measures such as trust, program coherence, and professional development. (Luppescu et al. (2003))
  • School survey profiles comparing a school to demographically similar schools and system trends over time: The report's display format charts a school's score on each measure across five survey years alongside two reference trends. It "compares your school both to other schools that are demographically similar to yours, and to the Chicago public school system as a whole," with a gray box marking the middle two-thirds of schools and a star locating the school's standing. Time-trend data show whether reform efforts are improving, and trend bands distinguish somewhat from substantially above- or below-average reports. (Luppescu et al. (2003))
