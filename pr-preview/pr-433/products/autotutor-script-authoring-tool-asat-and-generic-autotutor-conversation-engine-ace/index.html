AutoTutor Script Authoring Tool (ASAT) and generic AutoTutor Conversation Engine (ACE)¶

Product or Programme · All products and programmes
Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 review), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The AutoTutor project’s ASAT authoring tool and ACE conversation engine let organizations create and deploy scripted conversational tutoring applications across domains.

Components¶

  • AutoTutor Script Authoring Tool (ASAT) and generic AutoTutor Conversation Engine (ACE) licensed for new content and applications: The AutoTutor Script Authoring Tool (ASAT, Cai et al. 2015) lets researchers at other universities, businesses, and organizations develop their own content and integrate it with the generic AutoTutor Conversation Engine (ACE). The article reports that Wolfe et al. (2015) used the authoring tools to build a website on genetic risk factors for breast cancer (BRCA) and reported learning gains above the existing website on the same topic. Educational Testing Service is licensing ASAT for assessment in virtual worlds, and the Army Research Laboratory has incorporated AutoTutor in its open source Generalized Intelligent Framework for Tutoring (GIFT). (Graesser (2016))
  • AutoTutor family of intelligent tutoring systems: natural conversation between student and automated tutoring agent: The AutoTutor family comprises more than two dozen systems developed over almost two decades that help students learn subjects including physics, biology, computer literacy and math. Its core approach is "to organize effective learning based on a natural conversation between a student and an automated tutoring agent," with design following "pedagogical strategies exhibited by human tutors." It takes on a more challenging task than most ITSs by supporting deep reasoning, critical thinking and self-regulated learning. (Sergey Sosnovsky et al. (2014))
  • Expectation-misconception tailored discourse framework with the 5-step tutoring frame: AutoTutor's core explanatory framework stores anticipated correct answers (expectations) and frequent invalid answers (misconceptions), and uses semantic matching to evaluate student input against them. Dialogs follow a 5-step frame in which the tutor poses a question, the student answers, the tutor gives brief feedback, collaboration improves the answer, and the tutor checks understanding. Discourse moves such as pumps, hints, prompts, assertions, and summaries guide students to cover each expectation, with assertions as a bottom-out response. The review presents this framework as the shared design inherited across the AutoTutor family. (Nye et al. (2014))
