    Sensory Tools and Fidgets¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Sensory tools and fidgets are small, quiet, manipulable objects (stress balls, putty, textured strips, wobble cushions, foot bands) or environmental sensory supports offered to learners during instruction. The rationale is that channeling excess motor energy or providing tactile input frees attentional and working-memory resources for the learning task itself, rather than leaving the learner to suppress movement or seek stimulation in more disruptive ways.

    Design Implications¶

    The evidence base is narrower and more conditional than popular adoption suggests. Fidgets plausibly help learners whose attention lapses stem from under-arousal or a need for movement, but they can become a distraction for learners who do not need them [~M]. Effects are strongest when the tool is chosen by the learner, introduced with clear rules of use, and matched to a specific regulation need rather than distributed indiscriminately. Movement opportunities with stronger evidence — such as brief physical activity breaks — should be considered alongside or instead of static fidget objects Classroom physical activity improves attention. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A clear purpose: the tool addresses an identified regulation, attention, or sensory need (Accommodations)
    • Explicit norms for use (silent, hands-only, does not leave the learner's desk) so the tool does not become a toy
    • Learner choice among a small set of options (Choice Boards) rather than teacher-assigned tools
    • A plan to monitor whether the tool is helping or distracting, with willingness to withdraw it

    Constraints¶

    • For learners without a regulation need, fidgets function as novel distractors and reduce attention to instruction [~M] — novelty effects dominate when tools are introduced to a whole class at once
    • Fidgets do not treat underlying attention disorders; they are a support, not a substitute for evidence-based interventions
    • Poorly matched tools (noisy, visually stimulating, requiring visual attention) divert working memory from the task Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M]
    • Whole-class distribution without individual need assessment tends to produce off-task manipulation rather than regulation

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Individual accommodation: a tool specified in a learner's support plan, used consistently
    • Classroom station or "sensory corner" learners visit briefly rather than tools at desks
    • Movement-based alternatives: standing desks, foot bands, scheduled activity breaks, or active recess options
    • Environmental supports: adjusted lighting, noise-reducing headphones, seating options

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners with ADHD or sensory processing needs, for whom controlled movement or tactile input may support sustained attention [~W]
    • Learners who benefit from autonomy over their regulation strategies Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
    • Least appropriate as a universal, whole-class intervention without individual need

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Attention and self-regulation as prerequisites for academic objectives, not academic objectives themselves
    • Executive function development: learners practicing selecting and monitoring their own regulation tools
    • Access: enabling participation in Active Learning for learners who would otherwise be disregulated

    Instructions¶

    1. Identify the need — observe when and for whom attention or regulation breaks down; involve the learner and, where relevant, families or specialists (Check-In)
    2. Offer a small, curated set of quiet, low-visual-stimulation options and let the learner choose (Choice Boards)
    3. Co-create use norms: silent, unobtrusive, tool stays with the learner
    4. Frame the tool as a regulation strategy the learner monitors, building toward independent self-management (Self-Regulated Learning)
    5. Review after 2–3 weeks: is on-task time or participation improving? Withdraw or swap tools that have become distractions

    Related Strategies¶

    • Brain Breaks — movement-based alternative with stronger attention evidence
    • Flexible Seating — environmental variant addressing the same regulation need
    • Accommodations — the broader framework into which individual sensory tools fit

    Examples¶

    • A learner with ADHD keeps a silent putty tin on their desk during independent reading, per their support plan; the teacher tracks on-task intervals to verify benefit.
    • A primary classroom offers a "regulation station" with wobble cushions and textured strips that learners access via a choice board during seatwork.
    • Occupational therapy practice embeds foot bands and weighted lap pads for individual students in inclusive classrooms (see Understood guidance on fidgets as accommodations).
