Process Writing Approach¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review), q3–q4 · 3 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

The process writing approach treats composing as a recursive cycle — planning, drafting, revising, editing, publishing — rather than a single-shot product. It includes many related activities: a greatly increased quantity of writing (only some of which is taken to publication), writing integrated throughout the course, mini-lessons on embedded skills (e.g., sentence combining), instructor modeling of writing and revision, and structured peer and teacher feedback. It is also a professional development model: student writing gains correlate with the depth of teachers' training in the approach, most visibly through the National Writing Project.

Design Implications¶

The approach works because it distributes cognitive load across the composing cycle — writers plan and revise with feedback rather than attempting to plan, compose, and edit simultaneously — and because frequent low-stakes writing builds fluency and automaticity in transcription, freeing working memory for higher-order composition Collaborative writing with feedback improves text quality. [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence (Graham & Perin, 2007) identifies process writing, strategy instruction, and sentence combining among the most effective practices for adolescent writing improvement [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Sustained time blocks for writing across multiple sessions, with a recursive (not linear) cycle
  • Instructor modeling: drafting and revising in front of learners, and responding to feedback visibly (Think-Aloud modeling of author decisions)
  • Mini-lessons targeting embedded skills (sentence combining, transitions, conventions) delivered just-in-time
  • Structured feedback from teacher and peers, with revision expected — feedback without opportunity to revise does not improve writing Collaborative writing with feedback improves text quality. [+M]
  • An authentic audience or publication purpose for at least some pieces (Authentic Audiences & Purposes)
  • Sustained, protected writing time with regular opportunities to draft and redraft
  • Explicit instruction in specific processes — planning, revising strategies, sentence combining — not just workshop structures Graham & Perin, 2007
  • Feedback directed at ideas and text structure rather than surface errors Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • A classroom community where peer response and collaborative learning are taught and normed, not merely assigned

Constraints¶

  • Requires significant instructional time; a full cycle per piece is difficult in courses with heavy content coverage [-M]
  • Effectiveness depends on instructor training; untrained implementation often collapses into "draft twice and fix commas," losing the revision focus [-M]
  • Heavy emphasis on process can shortchange explicit mechanics instruction for learners with weak transcription skills, whose limited sentence-level fluency consumes the working memory revision requires [~M]
  • Peer feedback is unreliable without training and protocols; unstructured peer review can reinforce errors [~M]
  • Weak implementations — workshop routines without explicit strategy instruction or substantive teacher feedback — show markedly smaller effects Graham & Sandmel, 2011 [~M]
  • Unstructured peer response often devolves into praise ("I liked it") unless response protocols and coaching are explicitly taught [-M]
  • Overemphasis on process can shortchange transcription fluency; struggling writers whose handwriting and spelling are effortful have little working memory left for revision, so mechanics instruction must not be omitted [-M]
  • Full process cycles for every piece are time-expensive; forcing the full cycle on low-stakes writing dilutes both coverage and quality [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Writers' Workshop (Calkins; Units of Study): mini-lesson → independent writing with conferring → sharing
  • Writing Process portfolios: multiple drafts and reflective cover letters as assessment artifacts
  • Content-area integration: short process cycles on disciplinary writing tasks rather than a standalone English class
  • Scaled-down cycles: single-session plan–draft–revise loops for frequent low-stakes writing
  • Writers' workshop (Calkins/Units of Study model): mini-lesson → independent writing → conferring → sharing
  • Strategy-infused process (e.g., Self-Regulated Strategy Development): process stages combined with explicit strategies, self-talk, and goal setting — the highest-effect variant Graham & Perin, 2007
  • Digital process writing: versioned drafting in platforms like Google Docs makes revision history visible and enables asynchronous peer and teacher comment
  • Scaffolded fading: teacher support moves from heavy modeling and shared writing toward independent management of the full cycle Support fades as responsibility transfers to the learner. [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents and adults developing compositional competence; effects are strongest in grades 4–12 Process writing instruction improves writing quality. [+M]
  • Struggling writers benefit especially from explicit mini-lessons and modeled revision, provided sentence-level skills are also addressed [~M]
  • Beginning writers (K–2) need more structured scaffolds within the cycle; a fully open process can overwhelm them [-W]
  • Adolescents and older students with enough transcription fluency to benefit from revising Graham & Perin, 2007
  • Struggling writers, when the process structure is paired with explicit strategies and scaffolds rather than open-ended immersion Graham & Sandmel, 2011 [~M]
  • Young or novice writers need more direct modeling and shared writing before managing the cycle independently

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: organization, elaboration, coherence, audience awareness
  • Writing fluency and volume: building comfort and automaticity with composing
  • Self-regulated writing: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own text (Self-Regulated Learning)
  • Revision as a disposition: internalizing that writing is re-writing
  • Writing quality: coherent, well-developed, audience-appropriate compositions
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own composing Flower & Hayes, 1981 [+M]
  • Metacognitive knowledge of writing as a craft, including revision as re-seeing rather than proofreading

Instructions¶

  1. Launch with frequent low-stakes writing to build volume and fluency (Practice)
  2. Model the target genre and revision moves by composing and revising in front of learners, verbalizing decisions
  3. Guide planning: audience, purpose, and organization before drafting
  4. Support drafting with Provide Feedback — teacher conferences and structured peer review focused on content before conventions
  5. Deliver mini-lessons on needs revealed by the drafts (e.g., sentence combining); have learners apply them immediately in revision
  6. Require revision, not just editing; publish or share selected pieces with an authentic audience
  7. Assess growth through portfolios and Enhance Retention and Transfer by applying the cycle to new genres

Related Strategies¶

  • Strategy Instruction in Writing (SRSD) — adds explicit self-regulation strategies to the process cycle; the two combine well
  • Sentence Combining — a high-impact embedded mini-lesson technique within the approach
  • Writers' Workshop — the most common classroom structure for enacting the approach
  • Peer Review / Feedback Protocols — structures that make peer feedback reliable
  • Break the writing process into explicit components students move among flexibly
  • 5-minute writing conferences — the conferring mechanism at the heart of workshop implementations
  • Activating prior knowledge — planning in the process model is fundamentally retrieval and organization of prior knowledge
  • Action-oriented feedback — conference and peer feedback should specify what to do next, not just evaluate
  • Use GenAI across the writing process stages of pre-writing, drafting language support, and revision feedback, and for discussion questions, differentiated explanations, and scaffolded practice

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — high writing volume is the fluency engine of the approach
  • Provide Feedback — feedback paired with revision opportunities drives quality gains
  • Modeling — instructor demonstration of drafting and revision makes author decisions visible

Tools¶

  • National Writing Project — professional development network and local chapters for teachers implementing the approach
  • Google Docs — version history makes revision visible and supports conferencing and peer comment
  • Padlet / class blogs — low-stakes publishing and authentic audiences

Examples¶

  • National Writing Project summer institutes — teachers experience the process approach as writers, then implement it in their classrooms; student gains correlate with teacher participation
  • Units of Study (Heinemann, Lucy Calkins) — a published writers' workshop curriculum enacting mini-lessons, conferring, and publication
  • College first-year composition — multi-draft essays with peer review workshops and portfolio assessment, the standard process-based course design
  • Units of Study (Heinemann, Lucy Calkins) — a widely adopted K–8 writers' workshop curriculum built on the process approach.
  • National Writing Project (nwp.org) — teacher professional network whose summer institutes model the process approach for teachers as writers.
  • Google Docs revision history — makes recursive drafting visible, enabling teachers to assess process, not just product.
