Explicit Instruction: Online Reading Strategies¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 25 claims (15 for, 10 mixed) · 40 studies (15 causal, 10 quant-synthesis, 8 review, 3 associational, 2 qualitative, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 9 of 40 report an effect size · 15 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one capability: reading and making sense of linked online texts once they have been found — deciding which links serve the reading goal, keeping track of meaning across pages and tabs, resisting drift, and putting what several pages say together. For a learner who reads comparable print text adequately (decoding is reasonably automatic) but is a novice online reader, and whose observed starting response on an open online reading task is to click whatever looks interesting, lose track of the question after two or three pages, end with notes copied from one page, or be unable to say where a piece of information came from, explicit instruction in a small set of online reading moves (set a goal and questions before opening any link; choose links against the goal; keep a running record of pages and how they connect; notice drift and return to the goal; combine pages into an answer), modelled by thinking aloud on real web pages, practised on authentic linked texts with prompts and feedback, and faded across sessions, is expected to produce better comprehension and integration of information from several linked pages, and more goal-directed navigation, on new online reading tasks (immediate-performance, near-transfer) than reading online without such instruction. The target is a metacognitive-strategy used in service of comprehension of concept and principle content; whether the gain lasts (delayed-retention) or transfers to unlike tasks (far-transfer) is not settled by anything here.

No claim in the wiki tests this relationship. No claim here compares taught online reading strategies with untaught online reading, or compares any version of such teaching with another. The nearest evidence comes from four directions, each carried to online reading from somewhere else and labelled where it is used:

  • explicit reading-strategy instruction on print texts improves comprehension, by a medium-to-large amount for English second-language learners and by a very small amount on standardised tests when ordinary teachers deliver it to whole classes (Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]);
  • prompts to plan, monitor and evaluate in computer-based learning environments improve learning by a medium amount (Metacognitive prompts improve learning [+S]); in one study of 28 college students in MetaTutor, a computer environment that prompts self-regulation, following the prompts to set subgoals, review notes and revise a summary went with larger gains, while several other prompts did not (Higher compliance with MetaTutor's suggest-subgoal prompt is associated with larger proportional learning gains [+M]);
  • a review of learner-controlled hypermedia names disorientation, distraction and cognitive overload as the usability problems that make its benefits hard to show, and learners' prior knowledge and self-regulatory skills as moderators (Usability problems — disorientation, distraction, and cognitive overload — hinder demonstrating effectiveness of self-controlled hypermedia learning [~M]; Learner characteristics … moderate hypermedia learning effectiveness [~M]);
  • general findings the old page used: chunking, process goals, self-explanation and contingent support (below).

The relationship is conditional on the learner's print reading (strategy instruction does not make up for effortful word recognition), prior knowledge of the topic and self-regulation (both named as moderators of learning from hypermedia), the kind of online reading task (skimming to orient, reading for evidence and synthesising across pages call for different moves), how open the link environment is (a curated set of pages against the open web), and whether anyone or anything sees the learner's path while it happens.

How this sits beside the converted siblings. Explicit Instruction: Internet Search owns finding the pages (framing a query and revising it); this page starts where the learner has results or a start page in front of them. Evaluating Sources owns whether to trust a page, including the move off the page; this page hands over to it whenever a reading decision turns on credibility and does not repeat its model. Pre-reading Questioning owns questions attempted before reading a single text; the goal-setting step here borrows that move for a set of pages. Note-taking, Annotating and Graphic Organizers own how notes, marks and maps are made; the running record here is one use of them. Self-Regulated Learning holds the general plan–monitor–evaluate relationship of which this is an application to linked texts, and Guided Practice and Scaffolding and Fading hold the practice phase and how support is withdrawn. Clear Structure and Cognitive Load Management hold the designer's side: building digital material that needs fewer navigation decisions in the first place.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (define a goal and questions before reading, map the information while reading, limit the links explored, model skilled choices, explain why one clicked, skipped or combined), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step says what stands behind it; where a claim is cited, it is carried from print reading, computer-based learning or general learning research, not tested on online reading strategy instruction.

  1. Check print reading and the online starting response first (10 minutes, once). Give one short print passage with two comprehension questions and one short online task (a start page with four or five links, one question). Watch: does the learner read the print text adequately, and online do they state a purpose, pick links, lose the question, or copy? If print comprehension or word reading is weak, work on that first or reduce the reading load (shorter pages, text read aloud); online strategy teaching will not make up for it. Basis: the discussion on Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [~S] says strategy instruction cannot compensate for weak word recognition (a qualification stated in the claim's discussion, not in its two entries); the probe itself is an untested proposal.
  2. Set the goal and two or three questions before opening any link (2–3 minutes per task). The learner writes what they need to find out and what they will do with it, as process steps ("find two explanations of X and say how they differ"), not only a product ("finish the worksheet"). Basis: Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] (84 high-school girls learning a writing-revision skill; carried); in one MetaTutor study, following the prompt to set subgoals went with larger gains (suggest-subgoal compliance [+M], associational, 28 college students). The questions-before-reading move is modelled on Pre-reading Questioning.
  3. Model the moves by thinking aloud on real pages (8–10 minutes, first two sessions). On a projected or shared screen, read a small set of linked pages against a stated goal and say each decision aloud: what I expect behind this link and whether it serves my question; why I am not opening that one; "I have drifted — back to my question"; how this page connects to the last one. Name each move after modelling it. Basis: explicit, teacher-modelled strategy instruction is what the print evidence tests (Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S], print texts; carried); the think-aloud for online pages is an untested proposal. The routine is spelled out in the strategy Think-Alouds for Online Reading Strategies.
  4. Limit the links, then widen them. Early sessions use a curated set of three to five linked pages and a budget ("open at most four links beyond the start page for this question"); middle sessions a larger set with some irrelevant and distracting links; late sessions the open web with the learner setting their own budget. Basis: disorientation, distraction and overload are the usability problems a hypermedia review names (Usability problems … hinder … self-controlled hypermedia learning [~M], review, no effect sizes); working memory holds few new units (Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S], carried from memory research). The budget numbers are untested proposals. Handing learners control by itself is not the active ingredient: across 18 studies, learner control in educational technology had an effect near zero (Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]).
  5. Keep a running record while reading. One line per page opened: where it is, what it says for my question, and how it links to a page already read; in later sessions, a node-and-link map built by the learner. Before answering, the learner reviews the record and revises their summary. Basis: learners who construct concept maps from what they study learn more than in most comparison conditions, though not more than time-matched retrieval practice (Concept mapping improves learning over most comparison conditions by a moderate average amount, but not over time-matched retrieval practice [+S], carried from map construction on study texts); in the MetaTutor study, following prompts to review notes and revise a summary went with larger gains (review-notes compliance [+M]; revise-summary compliance [+M]; both associational, one study), while following the prompt to open the diagram did not (no effect for four explicit prompts [~M]).
  6. Ask for the reason at two or three decision points per task. The learner says or writes why they clicked, skipped, left a page, or joined two pages ("I left because it repeated page 2"; "this explains the cause the first page only named"). Basis: Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] (carried from explaining worked examples and expository text, including a print text read by eighth graders; immediate tests). Explaining navigation choices is an untested proposal.
  7. Prompt and respond during practice, then fade. While learners practise, the teacher or the software prompts at visible drift or stalls: a plan prompt ("what is your question?"), a monitor prompt ("does this page answer it?"), an evaluate prompt ("what do you still need?"), with feedback on what the learner does next. Give more help to those who need it and less after a success. Basis: Metacognitive prompts improve learning [+S] (computer-based environments; effects vary with whether prompts carry feedback, how task-specific they are and whether they adapt to the learner; in one reviewed study, lower-prior-knowledge learners benefited only once trained to use the prompts); Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] (mostly one-to-one tutoring; carried).
  8. End each task with a synthesis that uses two or more pages (5 minutes). The learner answers the goal questions in a few sentences, names the pages used, and says how they relate (agree, add, conflict). Untested proposal; from the earlier page's objective of integrating information across pages. If the answer turns on whether a page is trustworthy, hand over to Evaluating Sources.
  9. Plan the arc across sessions, not one lesson (a default of six sessions of 40–50 minutes over two to four weeks). Sessions 1–2: check (session 1 only), goal-setting 3 minutes, teacher think-aloud 8–10 minutes, guided practice on a curated set of three to five pages with a printed prompt card 20–25 minutes, synthesis 5 minutes, debrief on one common drift 5 minutes. Sessions 3–4: no full think-aloud (a 3-minute reminder of one move), a larger set with distracting links, learners explaining choices to a partner, prompts only on request or at visible drift. Sessions 5–6: open-web tasks with the learner's own goal and link budget, prompt card withdrawn, the running record kept unprompted; end with one unaided task on a new topic. Move a learner on (to open-web tasks, or out of the sequence) when on two consecutive unaided tasks their synthesis answers the goal questions from at least two relevant pages and their record shows a return to the goal after any drift; keep a learner on the curated set while they lose the question or copy from one page. The arc, the minutes and the threshold are untested proposals. Learners who already read online this way should skip the modelling and do one check task: guidance that helps novices can become redundant as expertise grows (Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]).
  10. Judge progress on an unaided online task like the target, not on assisted practice or a strategy quiz. Whole-class print strategy programmes showed a very small effect on standardised comprehension tests but larger ones on researcher-made tests and on strategy measures (Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [~S]), so "uses the strategies" is not the same result as "comprehends better". Untested as a rule for online reading.

When a condition is not met: if no one can watch learners' paths (a large class, self-paced online work), build the goal entry, the page record and two or three prompts into the environment and review records afterwards; if learners already read online purposefully, skip to open-web synthesis tasks.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • The learner's print reading and their observed online behaviour. A learner who struggles to read the words needs reading support before online strategies; one who already keeps to a goal and joins pages needs little modelling. If unstated, ask: can they read a page of this difficulty in print and answer two questions on it, and what do they do with a start page and five links when given one question?
  • How open the link environment is, and who sees the path. A curated set of pages with a teacher watching, a self-paced course page with links, and the open web with nobody watching need different supports. If unstated, ask: which pages will learners read, can they leave them, and who (or what) will see the pages they open and the notes they keep, and how soon?
  • The reading task and what counts as success. Orienting on a topic, finding evidence for one point, and combining several sources into an answer use different moves, and each needs a different check. If unstated, ask: what must the learner produce from their reading, for whom, judged how, and when?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a reading path under stated conditions: the goal written (if any), the pages opened and in what order, the time on each, the record kept, where the learner left the question, the prompts given and what the learner did next, and the synthesis produced. "Disoriented", "distracted", "overloaded" and "lacks the strategy" are inferred states, and the same path fits more than one.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares explicit instruction in online reading strategies with online reading without it, or compares one version of such instruction with another. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

  • Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]: two meta-analyses of strategy instruction on print reading. One, of studies with English second-language learners, found a medium-to-large effect on comprehension against control groups (g = 0.72, 20 articles) and similar effects on strategy use. The other, of 52 whole-classroom interventions delivered mostly by regular teachers, found a very small effect on standardised comprehension tests (d = .186), a larger one on researcher-made tests (d = .431) and a medium one on strategic ability (d = .786), with larger effects when researchers delivered the training and when it included explicit goal-setting. It supports teaching strategies explicitly and setting goals; it is print, not online, reading, and it warns that ordinary classroom delivery shrinks the effect. The claim's discussion adds that strategy instruction cannot make up for weak word recognition and that strategy scaffolding should fade for skilled readers; those two statements are the claim page's discussion, not its entries.
  • Metacognitive prompts improve learning [+S]: a meta-analysis of experiments in computer-based learning environments found prompts to plan, monitor and evaluate raised learning outcomes by a medium amount (g = 0.40) and self-regulation activity by more (g = 0.50), varying with feedback, task-specificity and adaptivity; a systematic review of 35 studies of online support for self-regulation judged prompting effective but computed no pooled effect, and reported one study in which lower-prior-knowledge learners benefited only after training to use the prompts. It supports building prompts into online reading practice. It does not isolate online reading of linked texts.
  • Higher compliance with MetaTutor's suggest-subgoal prompt is associated with larger proportional learning gains [+M], with review-notes compliance [+M] and revise-summary compliance [+M]: one regression study of 28 college students in MetaTutor, a computer environment that prompts self-regulation; students who followed these prompts more learned more. It is associational (students chose whether to comply), one study, and the partial η² values are not d-family effects. It is qualified by the same study: following the summarize, stay-on-subgoal, move-to-next-subgoal and open-diagram prompts showed no significant effect (no effect for four explicit prompts [~M]), nor did the stay-on-page and move-to-next-page monitoring prompts in the 16 students with gaze data (no effect for monitoring prompts [~M]). So "more prompts" is not the lesson; which move a prompt asks for matters, and this one study cannot say why.
  • Usability problems — disorientation, distraction, and cognitive overload — hinder demonstrating effectiveness of self-controlled hypermedia learning [~M] and Learner characteristics — prior knowledge, self-regulatory skills, cognitive styles, and attitudes towards learning — moderate hypermedia learning effectiveness [~M]: one narrative review (Scheiter & Gerjets 2007) of learner-controlled hypermedia, with no effect sizes. It names the problems the default design tries to manage (the link budget, the record, the goal) and the learners for whom they bite; it does not test any instruction.
  • Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]: a meta-analysis of learner control in educational technology (18 studies, g = 0.05) and one experiment with primary pupils in which learner-paced animation helped only on difficult questions. Its table gathers 11 distinct studies on handing learners control over pace, path or tasks. This is the main limit on the page: an open link environment does not help by itself, so the gains, if any, must come from what learners are taught to do with it.
  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S]: two reviews and one experiment on memory capacity (its table gathers 4 distinct studies), with a limit for novel material nearer three to five units. It is the reason to keep the number of open pages and pending decisions small for novices; it is not a study of reading online.
  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: 84 high-school girls learning a writing-revision skill did better with process goals than outcome goals, and a theory chapter synthesises related work. It supports phrasing the reading goal as steps; it is carried from writing.
  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S]: a meta-analysis (g = .55 across 69 effect sizes) and smaller studies, including eighth graders prompted to explain each line of a print text on the circulatory system. It supports asking learners for the reason behind navigation choices, as an untested extension; evidence is mostly immediate.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: mostly one-to-one tutoring studies (one with 8 learners per condition); a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding. It supports prompting in response to a learner's path and, in the strategy-training finding, teaching the strategy explicitly; classroom-scale contingency is not shown.
  • Concept mapping improves learning over most comparison conditions by a moderate average amount, but not over time-matched retrieval practice [+S]: two meta-analyses (constructing maps g = 0.72; studying supplied maps g = 0.43) and one experiment in which time-matched retrieval practice beat map construction. It supports the learner building a map of pages; it was not tested on navigation maps, and a short recall check may serve as well.

Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims (second-language and school readers on print, college students in a prompting environment, pupils with animations, high-school writers), so do not rank them by effect size. None measures online reading comprehension after strategy instruction, and none measures it at a delay.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does explicitly teaching reading strategies (modelled, practised and named) improve comprehension beyond reading practice alone, and how far does that hold in ordinary classrooms? Reading Strategy Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S] (print texts; the page carries it to online reading)

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims read for this rewrite that bear on the page less directly. Every claim the earlier page cited is used in the model above.

  • Lack of conceptual foundations and methodological shortcomings of many hypermedia studies contribute to ambiguous effectiveness findings [~M] — the same 2007 review; says why hypermedia studies are hard to read, not what to teach.
  • Studies on the effectiveness of hypermedia learning have produced ambiguous results [~M] — the same review's starting point; background to the hypermedia claims above.
  • More fixations on the learning content after an add-subgoal prompt are associated with higher learning gains, suggesting complying with that prompt may not be effective [~M] — the MetaTutor study again, seven students; complicates the subgoal prompt.
  • Explicit instruction of four reading comprehension strategies with short stories yields higher reading comprehension post-test scores than regular syllabus instruction in EFL preparatory students [+M] — 25 against 25 Turkish EFL students over 16 weeks, quasi-experimental, print short stories; one more instance of the print evidence.
  • Teacher modeling with worked examples helped EFL students internalize reading comprehension strategies [+W] — students' own comments from the same study's experimental group; no comparison.
  • Preservice science teachers exposed to explicit reading strategy instruction showed the highest diversity and frequency of cognitive strategy use while reading a science text [+M] and Metacognitive strategies and products of metacognitive processes were more diverse and frequent in reading strategy instruction groups than in the no-instruction group [+M] — one qualitative multiple-case study (29 preservice teachers, print text); strategy use, not comprehension.
  • A flexible hypertext course group averaged 76% on achievement tests against 59% for a direct-design group that studied different software, so the contrast confounds design with content [~M] — about course design, not reading instruction, and the two groups studied different software, so the difference cannot be attributed to the design.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually comprehension and integration of information from several linked pages on an unaided task like the one the learner will meet, judged on a product the learner makes from their reading. Elicit separately what the learner wants from reading online: to get an assignment done fast, to settle a practical question (a benefit entitlement, a medical instruction, a product choice), to follow an interest, or to avoid looking slow in front of others. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values speed may skip the goal and the record; one following an interest may value the drift this page treats as a problem.

For example, an adult in a community literacy class may want to find out from a council website whether they qualify for a reduction in a bill, while the designer's objective is synthesising information across three linked pages. One guided task on the council site serves both; a curated set of pages about a science topic serves only the designer. Record both, so that a good synthesis on practice pages is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparisons of taught online reading strategies against untaught online reading, with comparable learners and an unaided online comprehension task, show no advantage, or an advantage on strategy measures only (as whole-class print programmes partly did). If learners whose print comprehension is controlled show no extra difficulty online, the case for online-specific instruction, as opposed to ordinary strategy instruction, weakens. If goal-setting and a page record alone, without modelling, produce the same gains, the think-aloud can be cut. If link budgets or curated sets make no difference to novices, step 4 should go. If a recall check after reading serves integration as well as a map does, as time-matched retrieval practice beat map construction in one experiment, replace the map. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too few sessions" or "not enough modelling" after the fact.

Navigation behaviour, strategy use, comprehension of the pages read, integration across pages, transfer to new online tasks and use in the setting the learner values are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no number of sessions, no link budget and no hand-over criterion for online reading.

  • Evaluating Sources — online reading requires credibility judgment as well as comprehension
  • Clear Structure & Presentation — structured digital environments reduce avoidable navigation burden
  • Annotating — note capture and marking can stabilize understanding across tabs and links
  • Pre-reading Questioning — questions attempted before reading; the goal-setting step here borrows the move
  • Self-Regulated Learning — the general plan–monitor–evaluate relationship this page applies to linked texts
  • Note-taking and Graphic Organizers — how the running record and the page map are made
  • Guided Practice and Scaffolding and Fading — the practice phase and the withdrawal of prompts

Examples¶

  • Think-Alouds for Online Reading Strategies — expert navigation decisions are modeled explicitly
  • Reading Online Strategy: Evaluating Online Information — combines reading with credibility checks
  • Reading Online Strategy: Formulating Effective Search Queries — supports pre-reading goal and query construction
  • Guided webquests or hypertext tasks with note-taking and path-tracking prompts
  • Mapping Online Reading Behavior — learners record their path and reflect on each decision
