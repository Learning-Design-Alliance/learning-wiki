    Professional Development¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    Professional development applies the science of teacher learning to the design of experiences that change what educators know, do, and believe about instruction. Effective PD treats teachers as learners of a complex cognitive skill — much as Cognitive Apprenticeship treats students — combining modeling of expert practice, coached enactment in real classrooms, and iterative feedback. The problem it solves is well documented: one-shot workshops and one-off "sit and get" trainings rarely change classroom practice or student outcomes, while sustained, practice-embedded programs do Darling-Hammond et al.'s review of effective PD features [+S].

    Implications¶

    PD design is grounded in the same learning sciences as student instruction: teachers, like all learners, acquire complex skills through modeling, guided practice, feedback, and progressive independence. Reviews converge on a set of features that distinguish effective programs — sustained duration, active learning, collective participation, coherence with curriculum and policy, and a focus on concrete classroom practice Effective PD features review [+S]. Coaching with feedback is among the most powerful single components: instructional coaching produces larger effects on both teaching and student achievement than workshop-only formats Kraft, Blazar, & Hogan meta-analysis of coaching [+S], consistent with evidence that feedback is most effective at the task and process levels [+S]. Because teaching expertise is largely tacit, PD must make expert reasoning visible rather than merely transmit principles.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Sustained duration — typically 20+ hours of contact time spread across a semester or year, not a single session Effective PD features review [+S]
    • Opportunities for active practice: rehearsal, coaching, video analysis, or peer observation
    • Alignment with the curriculum, materials, and assessments teachers actually use
    • Collective participation — teams from the same school or department learning together
    • A feedback mechanism tied to enacted practice, not just stated intentions

    Constraints¶

    • One-shot workshops and lecture-format trainings show near-zero effects on practice or achievement Effective PD features review [-S]
    • Coaching effects shrink as dosage decreases and when coaches lack domain expertise Coaching meta-analysis [~M]
    • PD that ignores teachers' existing beliefs and prior knowledge produces compliance, not change; overly prescriptive programs can trigger reactance in experienced teachers [~M]
    • Effects attenuate without organizational support — scheduling, materials, and leadership follow-through [~M]
    • High-intensity coaching is expensive and difficult to scale; lower-digital-fidelity versions often lose effectiveness Coaching meta-analysis [~M]

    Grain Size¶

    Program — effective PD unfolds over months to years, embedded in teachers' ongoing work rather than delivered as isolated events.

    Target Goals¶

    • Change in classroom instructional practice, not just teacher knowledge or satisfaction
    • Improved student achievement as the downstream outcome Coaching meta-analysis [+S]
    • Development of adaptive expertise — teachers who can reason through novel situations rather than enact scripts
    • Collective capacity: shared norms, common language, and collaborative analysis of student work

    Target Learners¶

    • Practicing K–12 teachers, especially those adopting new curricula, technologies, or pedagogies
    • Novice teachers in induction and mentoring programs, who benefit most from coaching and observation
    • Instructional coaches, department heads, and teacher-leaders who mediate PD for others
    • Higher-education faculty transitioning to active or flipped classroom formats

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Situated Learning (Lave & Wenger) — teacher learning is most effective when embedded in authentic classroom practice and professional communities of practice, not decontextualized workshops
    • Cognitive Apprenticeship (Collins, Brown, & Newman) — expert teaching is tacit; PD works by modeling, coaching, and fading support as teachers gain independence
    • Social Learning Theory (Bandura) — observation of modeled practice, especially via video, builds self-efficacy and provides vicarious rehearsal
    • Self-Regulated Learning (Zimmerman) — goal-setting, self-observation, and reflective cycles turn PD into sustained professional self-improvement

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • Expertise Reversal Effect — highly scaffolded, scripted PD can impede experienced teachers who already have well-developed routines; support must be calibrated to prior expertise [~M]

    Claims¶

    Supporting¶

    • Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels. [+S] — coaching feedback on enacted practice outperforms generic praise or evaluation
    • Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M] — PD should progressively withdraw scaffolds so teachers own the practice
    • Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M] — coaching calibrated to the teacher's current struggles outperforms fixed scripts
    • Active learning improves exam performance. [+S] — by analogy, active PD formats outpassive transmission for teacher learners

    Contradicting¶

    • Expertise reversal effect. [~M] — excessive scaffolding and scripting can reduce the effectiveness of PD for experienced teachers

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    1. Diagnose starting points — assess teachers' current practice, beliefs, and student data to calibrate support (check-in, assessment)
    2. Model expert practice — demonstrate target strategies via live demonstration, video exemplars, or think-aloud of instructional decision-making (demonstration)
    3. Analyze together — deconstruct the modeled practice against student work and evidence, building shared language (class discussion)
    4. Rehearse in low-stakes settings — teachers practice routines with peers before enacting them with students (coaching)
    5. Enact with support — teachers implement in their own classrooms while a coach observes and provides feedback at the task and process levels
    6. Reflect and iterate — cycles of enactment, reflection, and revision, with support progressively faded as independence grows
    7. Sustain through community — ongoing collaborative inquiry, lesson study, and peer observation embed the practice in school culture

    Affordances¶

    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — PD enacts this principle for adult learners: modeling makes expert teaching visible, coaching supports enactment, and fading builds independent professional judgment
    • Active Learning — effective PD replaces passive transmission with rehearsal, video analysis, and collaborative problem-solving, mirroring what works for students
    • Assessment for Learning — student work and classroom evidence serve as formative data that drive PD content, rather than generic curricula delivered regardless of need

    Personalization¶

    Novice teachers: Provide high-frequency coaching, co-planning, and full modeling before independent enactment. Delay fading; novices need extended guided practice.

    Experienced teachers adopting a new practice: Compress modeling; focus on the points of friction between existing routines and the new practice. Avoid scripted materials that trigger the expertise reversal effect — offer principles plus exemplars instead.

    Teachers with low confidence or resistance: Use video of similar-status peers (not expert demonstrators) and build early wins with small, well-defined practices before tackling ambitious instruction.

    Heterogeneous departments: Differentiate coaching intensity — some teachers need in-class support while others benefit from peer observation and collaborative analysis. Use team structures so stronger practitioners extend reach.

    Remote or resource-constrained settings: Virtual coaching with video capture (e.g., Edthena) preserves the enactment–feedback cycle at lower cost, though dosage and coach quality remain critical [~M].

    Related Patterns¶

    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the core learning model PD applies to adult learners: modeling → coaching → fading of expert practice
    • Collaborative Inquiry — a common PD structure in which teacher teams investigate questions from their own classrooms using student data
    • Flipped Classroom — a frequent target of PD and itself a model for PD design: content study outside meetings frees session time for active practice

    Examples¶

    Reading Recovery (https://www.readingrecovery.org): Teachers receive a year of training behind a one-way mirror, observing expert lessons, teaching with live coaching, and discussing decisions afterward — a full apprenticeship cycle with fading support.

    MyTeachingPartner (https://curry.virginia.edu/myteachingpartner): Web-mediated coaching in which teachers upload classroom video and receive consultant feedback on teacher–student interactions; randomized trials show improved instructional quality and student outcomes.

    Japan's lesson study (jugyō kenkyū): Teacher teams collaboratively plan, observe, and refine a single research lesson over weeks — collective participation, practice focus, and sustained duration in one structure.

    NCSM / Illustrative Mathematics professional learning (https://illustrativemathematics.org): Curriculum-embedded PD in which teachers experience the lessons as learners, then analyze and rehearse facilitation — coherence with enacted materials.
