Role relational specificity: trust in schooling operates through asymmetric role relations among teachers, principals, and parents¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 7 claims (6 for, 1 mixed) · 3 studies (2 associational, 1 design), q2–q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Because school-based relations are asymmetric rather than symmetric, the normative basis of trust takes on role relational specificity. The paper examines "three role relations critical for sustained school change: teachers with their colleagues, with the school principal (and other administrators), and with parents." Each relation carries differentiated reciprocal obligations: colleagues need predictability and generalized reciprocity, teachers expect fairness and resources from principals, and teacher-parent trust depends on teachers initiating respectful bridges given parents' vulnerability. Both parties remain vulnerable to each other, unlike patron-client arrangements.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Recognition by each party of the other's role in children's education and of the asymmetries in knowledge and authority

Constraints¶

  • The framework deliberately excludes teacher-student relations, since in elementary schools student-teacher trust operates primarily through parent-teacher trust

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary school teachers, principals, and parents

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Sustained cooperative school improvement and structural change

Claims¶

  • Facilitative, inclusive principal leadership and effective principal supervision are associated with more positive trust relations [+W]
  • Relational trust varies substantially between schools, supporting its treatment as an organizational property [+W]
  • Small elementary schools (under 350 students) report more positive trust relations among teachers and between teachers and parents [+W]
  • High-trust schools are hypothesized to show greater teacher commitment, orientation to innovation, parent outreach, and collective responsibility [+W]
  • School size and student socioeconomic status conditioned trust levels differently in the two contexts [~W]
  • Teachers and students in Valparaíso and Illinois responded similarly and positively about trust among teachers, between teachers and principals, and between students and teachers [+W]
  • Over two decades, Chicago teachers reported increasing program coherence and higher trust in their principals [+W]
