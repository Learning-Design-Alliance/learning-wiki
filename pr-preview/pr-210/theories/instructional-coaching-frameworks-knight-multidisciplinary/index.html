Instructional coaching frameworks: Knight's four forms and the multidisciplinary skills-process-development framework¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The paper organizes coaching models in education, distinguishing coaching's "partnership-type relationship" from hierarchical mentorship. It presents Knight's (2007) four common forms — executive, coactive, Cognitive Coaching, and instructional coaching, the last helping teachers "incorporate research-based instructional practices" — and Kurz et al.'s (2017) multidisciplinary framework focusing coaching on skills, process, and development, where how teachers do something matters as much as what they do.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Coaching requires a collaborative coach-teacher relationship and a coach with practical teaching experience to build credibility

Constraints¶

  • Cognitive Coaching is a service-marked term, and the paper notes evidence of coaching impact is still a work in progress

Target Learners¶

  • K-12 teachers

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Improved instructional practice through sustained collaborative professional development

Claims¶

  • Coaching Meta Analysis Positive Effects Instruction Achievement [+M]
  • Coaching time focused on instructional delivery improves coached teachers' practices, while time focused on behavioral management is associated with a significant decrease in practice quality [+W]
  • Video-based analysis of teacher-student interactions mediates positive coaching outcomes, and challenging teachers supports reflection but is uncommon [+W]
  • Effective coaches require content expertise, classroom experience, technology proficiency, flexible scheduling, partnership orientation, and interpersonal skills [+W]
  • Teachers and coaches in the qualitative study perceived that coaching increased teacher knowledge and practice, with sustainable practices and positive student learning outcomes [+W]

Related Theories¶

  • Coaching as a partnership among principals, coaches, and teachers
  • Conceptual framework of active ingredients of instructional coaching comprising characteristics, relational contexts, and processes
  • Goal-directed coaching process staged as joint planning, action/practice, observation, individual reflection, feedback, and reflective discussion in a loop
  • Responsive literacy coaching as a co-learner model grounded in trust and inquiry

Examples¶

  • Design coaching programs as a systemic collective enterprise with shared vision, recruited and developed coaches, and continuous evaluation
