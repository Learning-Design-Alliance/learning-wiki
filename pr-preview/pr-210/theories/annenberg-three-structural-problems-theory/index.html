The Chicago Challenge's theory of action: improving student achievement by addressing three structural problems of schools — time, size, and isolation — through school-partner networks¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 associational, 1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The Chicago Annenberg Challenge's guiding framework holds that student achievement improves when schools restructure education, reconnect to their communities, and improve classroom practice. The report states the Challenge "encourages networks to create coherent, rigorous, and supportive teaching and learning environments by addressing three major structural problems of schools: (a) time, (b) size, and (c) isolation." Time concerns teachers' time to plan, reflect, and study; size concerns breaking oversized schools into smaller, more personal learning communities; isolation concerns disconnected working relationships among schools, communities, and educators. Networks of at least three schools plus an external partner are the primary organizational mechanism for this work.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Networks must include at least three schools and an external partner, addressing the problems of time, size, and isolation as means of improving student learning opportunities and outcomes.

Constraints¶

  • The report covers only the first 12 to 18 months of support and does not provide a sound basis for summative judgments about the Challenge's successes or failings.

Target Learners¶

  • Students in Chicago Public Schools elementary and high schools

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Improved student achievement through school restructuring, community connection, and improved classroom practice

Claims¶

  • Network Participation Moderate School Improvement [+M]
  • Networks Reduce School Community Isolation [+M]
  • External partners played the central role in forming and running implementation networks, with most principal interaction directed at partners rather than other schools [+W]
  • Network participation faced challenges of teacher time, resources, and fragmentation, with about 40 percent of principals reporting few useful resources [~W]
  • First-year (1995) Challenge grantmaking concentrated on networks with higher-education and community-based partners, which received most grants and dollars [+W]
