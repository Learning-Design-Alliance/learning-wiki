Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 causal r3, 1 quant-synthesis r? · q4 · i0 negligible

Teaching learners that abilities are malleable (a "growth mindset") has weak effects on academic achievement on average. The recorded evidence is one preregistered national trial, which found a statistically reliable but negligible GPA gain (standardized mean difference 0.11) for lower-achieving ninth-graders, larger in schools whose peer norms supported the message, and two meta-analyses that found weak overall effects, with some support for larger benefits among low-SES or academically at-risk students.

Evidence¶

Yeager et al. 2019¶

Yeager, D. S., Hanselman, P., Walton, G. M., Murray, J. S., Crosnoe, R., Muller, C., Tipton, E., Schneider, B., Hulleman, C. S., Hinojosa, C. P., Paunesku, D., Romero, C., Flint, K., Roberts, A., Trott, J., Iachan, R., Buontempo, J., Yang, S. M., Carvalho, C. M., Hahn, P. R., Gopalan, M., Mhatre, P., Ferguson, R., Duckworth, A. L., & Dweck, C. S. (2019). A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement. Nature, 573(7774), 364–369. doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y

q4 · pre-registered, individually randomized field experiment · i0 · negligible effect, standardized mean difference = 0.11 · n=6,320 (target subgroup); N=12,490 overall · causal · r3

A stratified random sample of 65 US public high schools (12,490 ninth-graders, individually randomized) received either a brief, self-administered online growth-mindset intervention or a control activity. Among the pre-registered subgroup of lower-achieving students (n=6,320, k=65 schools), the intervention raised core-course (math, science, English, social studies) GPA at the end of ninth grade by B=0.10 grade points (95% CI 0.04–0.16, t=3.51, P=0.001), a standardized mean difference effect size of 0.11. The same subgroup also showed reduced fixed-mindset beliefs (n=5,650, standardized effect 0.33). Effects on GPA were larger in schools whose peer norms already supported the growth-mindset message.

Sisk et al. 2018¶

Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses. Psychological Science, 29(4), 549–571. doi:10.1177/0956797617739704

q4 · meta-analysis (two, k=273 and k=43) · i? · no pooled effect size reported in the abstract · n=365,915 (correlational meta-analysis); n=57,155 (intervention meta-analysis) · quant-synthesis · r?

Two meta-analyses: the first (k=273 studies, N=365,915) examined the correlational strength of the mindset–achievement relationship and its moderators; the second (k=43 studies, N=57,155) examined the effect of mindset interventions on achievement and its moderators. The abstract reports overall effects as weak for both meta-analyses, with some support for the theory's prediction that students of low socioeconomic status or academically at-risk students benefit more from mindset interventions than the general population. Discussion¶

Small average effects, meaningful for at-risk students. The best-known national study of mindset interventions in U.S. high schools (Yeager et al., 2019, Nature) found an effect on GPA of about 0.1 grade points (standardized 0.11) in its preregistered subgroup of lower-achieving students, and larger effects in schools whose peer norms supported the growth-mindset message. This pattern — near-null averages masking heterogeneous effects — is the central interpretive challenge for this claim. A widely cited meta-analysis (Sisk et al., 2018, Psychological Science) reported weak overall correlations between mindset and achievement and small intervention effects, concluding that mindset is not a universal lever. Both studies are recorded as Evidence entries above: the trial's effect is negligible on this wiki's scale, and the Sisk et al. entry rests on the abstract, which prints no pooled effect size, so no magnitude for the meta-analyses is recorded here.

Delivery conditions matter. Effects appear strongest when interventions are (a) delivered as integrated, sequenced sessions rather than one-off assemblies, (b) embedded in academic content rather than generic motivation, and (c) accompanied by structural changes in teaching practice. A standalone "you can do anything" session without supporting instruction tends to produce no durable change. Mindset interventions also interact with classroom context: in classrooms where teachers hold fixed-mindset beliefs or use practices that signal ability is fixed (e.g., public ability ranking), student mindset gains are unlikely to translate into achievement. This contextual dependence parallels the heterogeneity seen in other brief social-psychological interventions such as belonging interventions.

Not a substitute for instruction. Mindset manipulations address beliefs about learning, not the quality of learning itself. They should be treated as a complement to — never a replacement for — sound instructional design such as cognitive load management and assessment for learning. Claims that mindset alone can close achievement gaps are not supported by the entries here.

Open questions. Durability beyond one academic year, effects outside Western schooling contexts, and the mechanisms linking mindset change to behavior change (persistence, strategy use, help-seeking) remain contested in the literature. The strategy-use pathway is plausibly mediated by self-regulated learning, but direct mediation evidence is thin.

Merged from “Growth Mindset Interventions Improve Achievement” (growth-mindset-interventions-improve-achievement): Effect size and heterogeneity. The overall effect of mindset interventions on achievement is small on average, and the literature is marked by substantial heterogeneity and an ongoing replication debate. Effects appear larger for at-risk or academically vulnerable students, for interventions delivered through structured multi-session curricula rather than single sessions, and when interventions are embedded in school contexts that reinforce the growth message. Short, standalone assemblies or one-off lessons often produce negligible achievement gains. The Evidence section now records one trial (d = 0.11 in its target subgroup) and two meta-analyses reported as weak overall, with no pooled effect size recorded for them.

Mechanism. Mindset interventions are theorized to work by changing how students interpret difficulty — from evidence of fixed inability to a signal that effort and strategy change are worthwhile. This places mindset work close to related motivational levers: see Belonging interventions improve outcomes and Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation, which target adjacent social-motivational pathways. The "growth" framing is most credible to students when it is paired with concrete strategy instruction; effort praise without strategy support can even backfire, since students who try hard and fail anyway may conclude ability really is fixed.

Boundary conditions. A growth message delivered in a classroom that still rewards only performance, ranks students publicly, or offers no strategy support is unlikely to change behavior. Mindset interventions pair most naturally with Assessment for learning improves achievement practices that make improvement visible and actionable. Open questions include how long effects persist, whether effects compound across repeated doses, and how much of the measured effect reflects expectancy or demand characteristics rather than genuine belief change.

Design implications. Practitioners should treat mindset interventions as a complement to, not a substitute for, instructional quality. The most defensible implementations (a) target students at risk of disengagement, (b) use multi-session curricula with spaced follow-ups, and (c) align grading, feedback, and classroom norms with the malleability message. Measuring belief change — not just achievement — helps distinguish genuine mechanism from placebo effects.

Merged from “Growth mindset interventions produce small positive effects on academic outcomes” (growth-mindset-interventions-small-effects): Small average effects, heterogeneous conditions. The claim is deliberately about magnitude: growth mindset interventions are not discredited, but their average effect on grades or test scores is small (the Sisk et al. entry here records no pooled effect size, so this page cannot confirm a range; the one trial recorded found 0.11 in its target subgroup), with wide heterogeneity across studies. Effects tend to be larger for at-risk or low-achieving students, in studies with higher design quality, and when interventions change actual teaching practices rather than delivering a one-off message.

Moderators matter more than the mean. A one-size-fits-all reading of the literature is misleading. The intervention appears to work by redirecting effort and persistence after setbacks, so it should benefit learners most when the learning environment actually rewards renewed effort — i.e., when tasks are challenging but attainable and feedback is actionable. In environments where effort cannot change outcomes, a mindset message alone is unlikely to help. This aligns with broader findings that assessment for learning improves achievement: mindset messages presuppose an environment in which effort is informative and rewarded.

Distal outcome, brief treatment. Most interventions are short (a few sessions or less) targeting a distal outcome (semester grades), which caps the plausible effect size. Designers should treat mindset interventions as cheap, low-risk complements to — not substitutes for — instructional quality, feedback, and cognitive load management.

Replication controversy. Large-scale preregistered national studies have found small effects (the one recorded here, d = 0.11, is negligible on this wiki's scale), sharpening the debate over publication bias in the original small-sample literature. The most defensible reading is that effects are real but rare and narrow: concentrated in struggling students in supportive environments, not general-purpose boosts.

Open questions. Long-term durability of effects, dose–response relationships, and whether effects compound when combined with belonging interventions remain actively debated. It is also unresolved whether mindset gains persist once the intervention context ends, or whether they require ongoing environmental support to maintain.
