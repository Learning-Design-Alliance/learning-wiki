Phonics instruction has a small but statistically confirmed effect for children with reading disabilities, while Orton-Gillingham structured-literacy programs show positive but non-significant effects¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis r2 · q3–q4 · i0–i1

Structured literacy is explicit, systematic, cumulative instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, and decoding. Two meta-analyses are recorded for students who struggle: among treatments for children and adolescents with reading disabilities, phonics instruction was the only one with a statistically confirmed effect, and that effect was small after adjustment for publication bias (g′ = 0.198); Orton-Gillingham interventions, a branded structured-literacy approach, showed positive but non-significant effects (ES = 0.22 and 0.14).

Evidence¶

Galuschka et al. 2014¶

Galuschka, K., Ise, E., Krick, K., & Schulte-Körne, G. (2014). Effectiveness of Treatment Approaches for Children and Adolescents with Reading Disabilities: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials. PLoS ONE, 9(2), e89900. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0089900

q4 · meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials · i0 · negligible effect, g'=0.198 (95% CI 0.039–0.357) · n=22 RCTs (49 comparisons) · quant-synthesis · r2

Systematic literature search (ERIC, PsycINFO, PubMed, Cochrane, plus unpublished-trial searches) identified 22 RCTs comparing treatments for children/adolescents below the 25th percentile in reading. Across seven treatment categories (phonics, phonemic awareness, reading fluency, reading comprehension, auditory training, medical treatment, coloured overlays/lenses), phonics instruction — 29 of the 49 comparisons — was "the only approach whose efficacy on reading and spelling performance ... is statistically confirmed," with a publication-bias-adjusted effect of g' = 0.198 (95% CI [0.039, 0.357]); the mean effect sizes of every other treatment category did not reach statistical significance. The authors conclude that "systematic instruction of letter-sound-correspondences and decoding strategies, and the application of these skills in reading and writing activities, is the most effective method for improving literacy skills of children and adolescents with reading disabilities."

Stevens et al. 2021¶

Stevens, E. A., Austin, C., Moore, C., Scammacca, N., Boucher, A. N., & Vaughn, S. (2021). Current State of the Evidence: Examining the Effects of Orton-Gillingham Reading Interventions for Students With or at Risk for Word-Level Reading Disabilities. Exceptional Children, 87(4), 397–417. doi:10.1177/0014402921993406

q3 · meta-analysis (mixed RCT/quasi-experimental) · i1 · small, non-significant effects, ES=0.22 (foundational skills, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.77) and 0.14 (vocabulary/comprehension) · n=16 studies in meta-analysis (24 identified) · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of 24 identified studies (16 with sufficient data for pooling; 15 studies of quasi-experimental or randomized design) of Orton-Gillingham, described in the paper as "a direct, explicit, multisensory, structured, sequential, diagnostic, and prescriptive approach to reading," for students with or at risk for word-level reading disabilities. The weighted mean effect on foundational-skill outcomes (phonological awareness, phonics, fluency, spelling) was ES = 0.22 (95% CI [−0.33, 0.77]), not statistically significant (p = .40); vocabulary/comprehension outcomes showed ES = 0.14, also non-significant (p = .59). The authors state this "yielded findings lacking support for the effectiveness of Orton-Gillingham interventions" comparable to a prior synthesis and What Works Clearinghouse reports, while noting the mean effect was positive and that more high-quality, adequately powered research is needed.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. The proposed mechanism is that explicit, sequenced decoding instruction builds automatic word recognition, which in turn frees attentional resources for comprehension — see Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.. Structured literacy's emphasis on small, cumulative steps also aligns with working-memory limits: instruction that chunks new grapheme–phoneme correspondences rather than presenting them all at once reduces load — see Chunking reduces working memory load.. Unstructured or incidental approaches, by contrast, can overwhelm struggling readers whose decoding is not yet automatic — see Cognitive overload degrades learning..

Boundary conditions. The claim is scoped to struggling readers. For students who acquire decoding incidentally or arrive already fluent, heavy explicit phonics instruction may add little and could displace time on vocabulary, syntax, and comprehension work — a plausible expertise-reversal-style moderator, consistent with the general pattern that highly scaffolded instruction loses its advantage as expertise grows — see Expertise reversal effect.. Effects should also be expected to be strongest on word-level outcomes (decoding, word reading, spelling) and weaker or more indirect on comprehension, which depends on language knowledge the intervention does not directly teach.

Design implications. Practitioners implementing structured literacy should treat it as one component of a comprehensive reading program: once decoding is secured, instructional time is better shifted toward vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and background knowledge. Cumulative review — revisiting previously taught correspondences rather than only adding new ones — is a defining feature of the approach and aligns with spacing and retrieval practice principles. Intervention delivery should be explicit and teacher-directed rather than discovery-oriented; the broader pattern favoring explicit guidance for novices — see Direct instruction. and Cognitive load theory. — is the theoretical backbone of the structured literacy movement.

Open questions. Optimal dosage and intensity, the durability of gains after intervention ends, and how much comprehension benefit follows once decoding is secured all require stronger evidence than this page currently carries. The evidence recorded above finds small effects: phonics instruction was the only approach with a confirmed effect for reading disabilities (g′ = 0.198), and Orton-Gillingham programs showed non-significant effects.
