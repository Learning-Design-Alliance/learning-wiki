Distributed Practice Improves Retention¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 causal r?, 1 quant-synthesis r? · q3–q4

Spacing the same learning episodes over time (distributed practice) produces stronger long-term retention than massing them together in a single session. The benefit is best established for delayed tests of verbal and declarative material, and the optimal gap between episodes scales with how long retention is measured.

Evidence¶

Cepeda et al. 2006¶

Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354

q4 · meta-analysis (quantitative synthesis of accuracy differences) · i? · no standardised effect size for spaced vs massed; 36.7% vs 47.3% correct · n=839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of verbal recall experiments (paired associates, list learning and similar tasks). For the spaced-versus-massed question it pooled 271 comparisons from 254 studies with 14,811 participants in total, and compared percentage correct, because too few studies reported effect sizes. With study time equal, spaced presentation beat massed presentation overall (47.3% vs 36.7% correct) and at every retention-interval band, including tests under a minute later (50.1% vs 41.2%); several individual bands between 10 minutes and 7 days were not statistically significant on their own because few studies fell into them. Separate lag analyses found that the interstudy interval giving the best retention increased as the retention interval increased.

Cepeda et al. 2008¶

Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. Psychological Science, 19(11), 1095–1102. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x

q3 · large-sample experiment (not pre-registered) · i? · no effect size reported in the abstract · n=more than 1,350 participants · causal · r?

More than 1,350 people were taught a set of facts, reviewed them after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and were tested after a further delay of up to one year. At any given test delay, lengthening the gap first improved and then gradually reduced final-test performance, so there is an optimal gap rather than "longer is always better". The optimal gap grew with the test delay, but as a share of that delay it fell from about 20–40% (1-week test) to about 5–10% (1-year test). Only the abstract was read for this entry.

Discussion¶

Mechanism. Two complementary accounts dominate. On a trace-strengthening or consolidation view, time between episodes allows memory traces to consolidate and partially decay, so re-encountering material at the point of near-forgetting strengthens it more than immediate repetition — an account consistent with Information Processing Theory. On a study-phase retrieval view, spaced episodes force learners to retrieve the earlier episode from long-term memory, and that retrieval itself strengthens the memory. Both accounts predict the same design implication: the gap should be long enough to make re-encoding effortful, but not so long that the material is fully forgotten. Spacing is thus a form of desirable difficulty, and its benefits are conceptually distinct from the working-memory mechanisms described in Cognitive Load Theory — see Cognitive load reduction improves learning.

Scope and moderators. The effect is most robust for verbal, declarative material (vocabulary, facts, definitions) and for delayed retention measures; the benefit on near-immediate tests is uneven rather than absent: Cepeda et al. (2006) found spaced study ahead even at retention intervals under one minute (50.1% vs 41.2% correct), while some short-interval bands showed small, non-significant differences (49.8% vs 48.9% at 1–10 minutes), so evaluations should still use delayed outcomes. The optimal spacing gap is not fixed: it grows with the retention interval, meaning a schedule optimal for a test next week is suboptimal for a test next semester. Spacing also interacts with retrieval practice — spaced retrieval is generally stronger than either spaced re-reading or massed retrieval — though a dedicated claim page does not yet exist in this wiki.

Boundary conditions. Learners systematically misjudge spacing: massed study feels more effective in the moment and is what learners prefer, while spaced study feels harder and its advantage is only visible on a delayed test. This metacognitive illusion means simply offering spacing options is insufficient — see Self-Regulated Learning. Designers should build spacing into scheduling defaults (spiraled homework, cumulative quizzes, revisit-and-review cycles) and explicitly teach learners why distributed study feels worse but works better. Curriculum pacing that front-loads instruction before a single assessment pushes learners toward cramming and works against spacing.
