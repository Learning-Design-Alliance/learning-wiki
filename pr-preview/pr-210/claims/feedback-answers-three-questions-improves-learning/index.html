Feedback that answers three questions (Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next?) improves learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 1 study · review r? · q2 · n=N/A (a synthesis, not a study of individual participants)

Feedback is most effective when it tells learners the goal, their current status relative to that goal, and the next actionable step. The three questions — goal, gap, next step — form a loop that connects instruction to learning: without a goal, performance information has no reference point; without gap information, learners cannot calibrate; without a next step, feedback stops at evaluation.

This is the paper's origin: Hattie and Timperley propose a model in which effective feedback answers three questions — "Where am I going?", "How am I doing?", and "Where to next?" — operating across four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self), and argue this reduces the discrepancy between current understanding and a learning goal. They synthesize prior empirical and theoretical work on feedback's effect on learning and achievement to build the model rather than reporting a new primary study or a pooled quantitative estimate specific to the three-question structure. Discussion¶

Why the loop closes. The framework's power lies in closing the loop between instruction and learning: without a clear goal ("Where am I going?"), performance information has no reference point; without gap information ("How am I doing?"), learners cannot calibrate; without a next step ("Where to next?"), feedback stops at evaluation rather than guiding improvement. Feedback that only rewards or grades, without answering the third question, is often described in the literature as the least effective form — which is why action-oriented feedback centers on the "where to next" step specifically.

Moderators to pin down. Three boundary conditions are commonly discussed: (a) feedback directing attention to the self rather than the task tends to be less effective; (b) detailed feedback may diminish or reverse in value for highly proficient learners — an expertise-reversal pattern analogous to Cognitive Load Theory and the expertise reversal effect — so feedback detail should fade as proficiency grows; and (c) feedback must arrive while learners can still act on it — timing and opportunity to act are moderators, not afterthoughts, linking the claim to assessment for learning practice generally. The framework also presupposes that learners monitor and act on the information, placing it inside self-regulated learning: the three questions map onto goal setting, self-monitoring, and strategy adjustment.

Status of the evidence. The three-question framing is a widely used organizing heuristic in the formative assessment literature, but no supporting studies have yet been pinned to this page, so the claim's strength cannot be rated. The related claim Assessment for learning improves achievement shares the goal–gap–next-step loop.

Open questions. Whether the three questions must be answered explicitly by the teacher or can be partially answered by learners through self-assessment; how the framework interacts with grading (grades appended to comments may wash out the benefit of the comments); whether "where to next" must be immediately actionable or can be a longer-term learning intention; and whether the three questions operate as a package or whether the third question carries most of the effect on its own.
