Project Topeka: an AI-equipped AES/AWE writing platform with prompts, feedback, and teacher supports¶

Element · All elements Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Project Topeka was an initiative using Turnitin's Revision Assistant, an AES/AWE platform, to improve middle school students' argumentative writing. Its "primary goal was to offer an AI tool featuring automated scoring and feedback" so students could write longer pieces, get rapid tailored feedback, revise, and learn argumentation. Students scored 1-4 in four dimensions (Support & Evidence, Claim & Focus, Organization, Language & Style) with dimension-aligned feedback; teachers accessed score data and received professional development, teaching materials, an AI-literacy guide, and a peer learning network.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Teachers were encouraged to have students complete a baseline draft and a revision draft with AI feedback; the platform was positioned for formative rather than summative assessment

Constraints¶

  • Teachers could see scores in the platform but could only see feedback by looking at students' screens; implementation varied, e.g., baseline only or revision only

Target Learners¶

  • Middle school language arts students
  • Teachers in schools where over half of students qualified for free or reduced-price lunch or were students of color

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Argumentative writing skills
  • Revision through multiple drafts
  • Response to automated feedback

Claims¶

  • Teachers at convenings said the three AI approaches are fluid rather than static, with movement across approaches as experience grows [+W]
  • Trust in AI varied by positioning: partners saw bias and assigned their own grades, while substitutes trusted AI scores as actual grades [+W]
  • The focus of teacher-student interactions differed by AI positioning: learning for partners, score-increasing for assistants, completion for substitutes [+W]
  • Partner-positioned teachers built students' AI literacy, while assistant and substitute teachers' transparency centered on gaming scores [+W]
  • Teachers who positioned AI as a partner remained instructional leaders, weaving the tool into rich instruction and using score data to decide what to re-teach [+W]
  • Among 24 Topeka teachers, half positioned the AI as a teaching partner, 29% as a grading assistant, and 21% as a substitute teacher [+W]
