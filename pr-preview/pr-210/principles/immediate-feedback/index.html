Immediate Feedback¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 8 claims (3 for, 5 mixed) · 15 studies (5 causal, 5 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 15 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page covers when corrective feedback arrives relative to a learner's attempt: during or straight after it, while the learner can still use it on the next attempt, or later, after a fuller performance or a delay. The relationship it proposes is conditional. For a learner practising a task where an error can be seen and corrected (a recall item, a procedural step, a short constructed response), corrective information delivered before the next attempt is expected to keep the error from being rehearsed and to change that next attempt. Whether delivering it at once, rather than at the end of the item set, the session or the task, raises delayed-retention or near-transfer is a separate question, and the wiki's evidence does not settle it: in the one direct comparison recorded here, immediate and delayed feedback were equally effective, and one second-hand report favours delayed feedback for vocabulary learned incidentally from reading.

So the page's working rule is that feedback presence and content are the better-supported levers, and timing is a design choice to be made for the task and tested locally, not a default to maximise. What the designer can rely on is narrower: correct errors, especially confident ones, before the learner repeats them; make sure an attempt comes first; and keep a chance to use the correction. The Formative Assessment principle holds the broader model of using a response to choose the next action, and timing is one parameter of that action. The Retrieval Practice principle holds the condition that retrieval benefits depend on initial success or corrective feedback; this page does not repeat it. The feedback element's Design Decisions carry the same reading of the timing evidence ("timing is not a strong lever in the evidence the wiki holds"), and this page is written to agree with it. No pattern page instantiates this principle; the strategies timely feedback and timely_feedback are recipes, not tested models.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The page's earlier guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Make the learner's action visible. Feedback needs a step, response, draft move or decision to respond to. Untested; from the earlier page.
  2. Let the attempt come first. Give feedback after the learner has tried, not instead of trying: a correction after a failed retrieval is where feedback has shown its largest benefit, and feedback after correct answers added little. Evidence: Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+M], laboratory word pairs and classroom quizzing; the timing of that feedback is not reported on the claim page.
  3. Correct errors before they are practised again, confident errors first. If the learner will answer the same or a similar item again before any feedback arrives, deliver the correction before that attempt. On multiple-choice practice, always give feedback, because chosen lures are reproduced later; immediate or end-of-set delivery both worked. Evidence: lure intrusions halved by feedback, immediate and delayed equally [~M]; the confident-errors-first ordering rests on the hypercorrection claim under Further evidence, not re-read here.
  4. Say what to change, not only whether it was right. A correctness signal without the correct answer or a next step can leave the misconception in place. Untested here; from the earlier page. The wiki has no claim comparing elaborated with answer-only feedback.
  5. Give an immediate chance to use it. Feedback matters most when another attempt follows. Untested; from the earlier page.
  6. For whole performances (writing, projects, discussion, complex skills), consider synthesised feedback after the performance rather than interrupting each step, and keep step-level correction for steps that would otherwise be repeated wrongly. Untested; from the earlier page. No claim here compares the two for complex-skill goals.
  7. Avoid correcting in front of peers by default where it may embarrass; instructors in one interview study held back oral correction to the end of class for this reason. Evidence: teachers' stated reasons [~W] (beliefs, not learner outcomes).
  8. Consider letting the learner ask for feedback after an attempt. In one motor-skill laboratory study, learners who decided after a trial whether to see their result retained the skill better than those who decided before it. Evidence: self-controlled knowledge of results decided after the trial [~M]; carry no further than simple motor tasks until tested elsewhere.

How far each neighbouring claim may be carried: the multiple-choice and retrieval claims are about verbal-association and fact learning, mostly in laboratories and classrooms of university students; the vocabulary-timing claim is one study reported in a review; the knowledge-of-results claim is one laboratory motor task. None tests timing for procedural skills in school mathematics, clinical simulation or writing.

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response on an attempt, the feedback given (its content, its timing relative to the attempt, who gave it, in front of whom), and the next response. "The feedback came too late" or "the learner was interrupted from productive struggle" are inferred states. Record the delay, what happened in between, and whether the learner had another attempt.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Repeats the same error on the next attempt despite immediate feedback Feedback not read or not understood; feedback said only "wrong"; the error reflects a missing concept, not a slip; no time to process before the next item Ask the learner to restate the correction before the next attempt; vary answer-only against explained feedback on matched items; note the gap between feedback and the next item.
Corrects at once with feedback, fails the same item a week later Correction held in short-term memory only; the learner copied the shown answer; later learning interfered Compare a delayed probe after immediate feedback with one after end-of-session feedback on matched items; record whether any retrieval came between.
Stops trying and waits for the hint Feedback arrives before the learner has attempted; dependence on step-level support; low confidence or fear of error Delay feedback until the learner commits to an answer or confidence rating, and see whether attempts return.
Performance on a whole task drops when step-level feedback is removed Capability was supported by the feedback, not held; integration demand of the whole task; the removed feedback carried task information Probe the steps alone and the whole task without feedback; compare with a version that gives synthesised feedback after the whole task.
Withdraws or reacts negatively to correction in class Public correction threatens face; the feedback was perceived as judgement; disagreement with the correction Offer the same correction privately or in writing, and ask the learner which they prefer; see whether participation changes.

These are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence between explanations; it does not identify a cause, and feedback during probing is itself an intervention.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki tests the page's original statement, that immediate feedback beats delayed feedback for learning, with its own evidence. The claims below bear on the timing question directly or on the conditions around it.

  • Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective [~M]: a review chapter's report (Karpicke 2017) of studies in which students restudied correct answers after multiple-choice tests. Feedback cut lure intrusions on a final short-answer test from 20% to roughly 10% in one study, and "both immediate and delayed feedback formats were equally effective". The only direct timing comparison here: it supports giving feedback, not giving it at once. Second-hand, no effect size; the delays compared, learners and horizon are not reported on the claim page. Not yet checked against its source.
  • Incidental vocabulary gains show retention problems, with losses over time moderated by feedback timing and topic interest [~W]: a review of incidental vocabulary acquisition through reading reports one study in which a short study–test lag with delayed feedback gave better word retention, and "delayed feedback is better than immediate feedback with respect to vocabulary retention". It limits the relationship: for L2 vocabulary learned from reading, immediacy was not better. One primary study, reported second-hand, no effect size, design and sample not described. Not yet checked.
  • Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice [+M]: a classroom meta-analysis in which quizzes with corrective feedback gave g = 0.537 against g = 0.374 without (a between-study comparison; an earlier meta-analysis found no such moderation), and a laboratory study of 258 participants learning Luganda–English word pairs where the correct answer after an incorrect response raised one-week retention by 494%, while feedback after correct responses made little difference. It supports correcting errors after an attempt; it does not test timing, which its Discussion lists as an open moderator. One of two entries checked, on an abstract.
  • Learners who could decide after a trial whether to receive knowledge of results were more accurate on a 24-h motor retention test than learners who decided before the trial and than their yoked counterparts [~M]: a laboratory experiment (assignment not described on the claim page) with university volunteers on a slider-positioning task; deciding after the trial whether to see the result gave less retention error (M = 10.04 and 12.45) than deciding before it (M = 29.18) and than yoked groups receiving the same schedule. It qualifies the relationship: feedback chosen in light of one's own just-finished attempt helped, and a fixed schedule did not. One study, one motor task; checked by the judge on full text. Its companion claim finds no benefit for deciding before the trial over a yoked group (no equivalence tested).
  • Instructors favored delayed over immediate oral corrective feedback, believing immediate correction intimidates students [~W]: a qualitative interview study of six EFL teachers; most corrected at the end of class rather than interrupting, and only one used immediate oral correction. It records a practical constraint (public correction and its affective cost), not a learning outcome. Not yet checked.

Taken together: feedback after an error, especially in multiple-choice and recall practice, has the strongest evidence here; the timing comparisons that exist find no advantage for immediacy, or an advantage for delay, each in one reported study; and learner choice and public setting may matter alongside timing. Learners, tasks and outcomes differ across these claims, so do not rank them by effect label. A nonsignificant difference between immediate and delayed feedback is not equivalence.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective fixes what the feedback is for and the horizon it is judged at: getting today's items right, remembering them next month, or performing a whole task unaided. Immediate correction can raise accuracy during practice without showing anything about the later horizon, so name the horizon before choosing a timing. Elicit separately what the learner wants from feedback: to know at once whether they are right, to work a problem through before being told, to avoid being corrected in front of others. Agreement and divergence are both observations.

For example, an adult learning Italian on a phone app may want each answer marked at once, while the designer's objective is recall of the vocabulary a week later. Immediate marking serves the learner's wish and keeps lures from being rehearsed; whether it serves the week-later objective better than a short review of errors at the end of the session is untested here, and the one vocabulary report favours the delay. Recording both lets the designer try end-of-session review on a subset of items and compare delayed recall, rather than assuming either.

What would revise this model?¶

An advantage for immediate over delayed feedback should be stated if controlled comparisons with comparable learners, tasks and aligned delayed outcomes reproduce one, for example in procedural skills or school mathematics, where none is recorded here. If more studies like the vocabulary report find delayed feedback better for retention, say so as a default for that task type. If immediate feedback is shown to reduce learners' attempts or later unaided performance, add that as a constraint with its evidence. Do not protect the model by relabelling every null as feedback that was too slow or too vague after the fact.

Accuracy during practice, delayed retention, transfer and the learner's wish for prompt answers are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal delay, a timing rule across domains, or that faster feedback is better.

Related Principles¶

  • Formative Assessment — immediate feedback is one of the most actionable forms of formative assessment.
  • Guided Practice — guided practice depends on feedback that arrives while practice is still underway.
  • Error Analysis — timely correction helps learners understand and revise errors before they harden.
  • Check-ins — quick check-ins can trigger immediate feedback and support adjustments.

Examples¶

  • Step-level tutoring feedback: A digital system flags an incorrect move and gives a hint before the learner proceeds.
  • Live coaching during practice: An instructor responds to a reading, writing, or math step while the learner is still working.
  • Immediate corrective feedback on retrieval: Learners answer a prompt, see the correct answer, and try again quickly.
  • Inline draft comments during conferences: Feedback is given during revision rather than after the writing task is fully over.
