Epistemic Cognition¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 8 claims (4 for, 4 mixed) · 18 studies (9 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 4 of 18 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

Epistemic cognition is reasoning about knowledge itself: what counts as evidence for a claim, how a claim is justified, how sure to be, and when to revise. As a design principle it says: when a learner has to judge claims rather than only recall them, teach the standards for judging that kind of claim explicitly, have the learner apply them to real claims and justify each judgment, and check the judgment on new claims later.

For whom and on what. The evidence recorded here concerns adolescent and adult learners (high-school government students, eleventh graders, first-year university students in a critical-thinking course, in psychology and in a library session) and one group of child learners in grades 2–6 who were observed, not taught. The knowledge targeted is a procedure (moves for checking an unfamiliar web source) or a principle (the ideals by which a discipline judges evidence, such as valid methods and replication), and in argumentation a complex-skill (writing or arguing a case with evidence). The starting response that calls for this principle is a judgment made on the wrong grounds: trusting a page because of its logo or domain, judging a study by whether it matches one's own experience, or offering an opinion where a reason is asked for.

What is expected to change, and when. Teaching these standards is associated with better judgments of the same kind of evidence, at an immediate posttest and, in one small randomised pilot, five weeks later (near-transfer to new sites or new study reports). Dialogic argumentation over several years was followed by better individual written arguments on new topics. No claim here reports a horizon beyond five weeks for evaluation skills, and none measures whether learners judge evidence better in their own lives.

The limit that matters most for design. The gains are specific to the standard taught. A semester of critiquing flawed psychology studies changed how students judged a study's methods and did not change how they judged a source's credibility. And a single library session, whether it taught a checklist or a lateral-reading framework, produced modest gains with no difference between the two. So the model predicts improvement in the kind of judgment that is taught and practised, with enough sessions; it does not predict a general rise in "critical thinking" or a large change from one session.

No claim in the wiki tests "epistemic cognition instruction" as a whole, or measures epistemic beliefs (how certain or simple a learner thinks knowledge is) as an outcome of teaching. The claims below test particular forms of it: source evaluation, disciplinary evidence critique and argumentation.

Sibling. Inquiry-based learning models learning by investigating a question, and its default design ends with a synthesis "with explicit standards of evidence". This page owns those standards as a target in their own right, in any domain, including where no investigation happens (judging a news site, a popular report of a study, an argument). Inside an inquiry, this page's model applies to the synthesis and evaluation steps; the inquiry page's guidance on how much to guide the investigation is not repeated here. Evaluating Sources is a narrower application (credibility of sources) that has not yet been converted to this format.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The page's earlier guidance, made concrete as a default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and no claim here tests the sequence as a whole.

  1. Anchor the work in real claims the learner must judge, not in discussion about knowledge in general: a web page, a news report of a study, a published argument, two competing explanations. Untested; from the earlier page ("abstract discussion about knowledge can stay detached from practice"). Every study below used real or realistic material: live websites, popular reports of psychological findings, flawed studies to critique.
  2. Name the kind of claim and its standard before teaching. Decide which judgment is the target: who is behind a source and what others say about it; whether a study measured what it claims and has been replicated; whether an argument's evidence supports its conclusion. Teach that standard explicitly. Evidence: the critique-and-redesign course changed only the disciplinary ideals it emphasised, not lay credibility judgments (Collaborative critique-and-redesign [~M], below).
  3. Model the expert move, then have learners do it on new cases. For web sources, show a reader leaving the page to search what independent sites say about it (lateral reading), rather than inspecting the page's own features. Dose in the studies: two 75-minute lessons (university), six 50-minute lessons (high school), eight lessons (eleventh grade). Evidence: Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M]. Plan more than one session: one-session instruction of either kind produced only modest gains (Checklist-based evaluation [~M]).
  4. Contrast stronger and weaker justifications. Put a well-supported and a poorly supported claim, source or study side by side and ask which is better warranted and why; in a discipline, have small groups critique a flawed study and redesign it. The critique-and-redesign course used three such tasks across a 12-week course. Partly supported by that single-group study; the contrast itself is untested, from the earlier page.
  5. Require a justification for every judgment: the claim, the evidence, and why that evidence warrants it, then a reply to a counterargument from a peer. Evidence: argumentation activities improved argument quality and attention to evidence (Argumentation Improves Reasoning [+M]); the Justification and Argumentation elements describe the activity.
  6. Ask for confidence, then show the evidence. With each judgment, have the learner rate how sure they are; after feedback, discuss the judgments made with high confidence that were wrong. Untested; from the earlier page ("certainty calibration is hard", "overconfident mistakes can become useful if followed by evidence-based correction"). The hypercorrection claim it cited concerns memory for corrected facts, not epistemic judgment (see Further evidence).
  7. Judge the design on new claims of the same kind, at a delay, scoring the reasons as well as the verdict, and scoring separately any kind of judgment the instruction did not teach. The randomised pilot tested five weeks after the lessons; a constructed-response task was used in the eleventh-grade study. Untested as a whole; from the studies' designs.

Constraints from the earlier page, still untested: without explicit standards, discussion defaults to opinion-sharing; learners may swing between overconfidence and blanket relativism ("it's all just opinion").

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision.

  • Which kind of evidence the learner must judge. Gains have been recorded only for the standard taught, so a design for web sources and a design for research studies are different designs. If unstated, ask: what will the learner have in front of them when they have to decide what to believe, and what decision follows?
  • Time available. The recorded gains come from two to eight lessons or a semester; a single session produced modest, method-independent gains. If unstated, ask: how many sessions, and is there a later point where the learner judges new material?
  • What the learner already knows about the topic and the domain. Children applied an accuracy criterion from their own biological knowledge; checking a source laterally requires knowing which outside sources are reliable. If unstated, ask: can the learner already tell a reliable reference source in this area from an unreliable one?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a judgment and the reason given for it: which source or explanation the learner trusts, what they say supports it, how sure they say they are, and what they check before deciding. "Has sophisticated epistemic beliefs", "is a critical thinker" or "understands objectivity" are inferred states. A correct verdict does not show a sound standard (it can be reached by agreeing with the learner's prior view), and a learner who can recite the standard may not apply it to a new case.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Trusts a page because it looks professional, has a logo or a familiar domain Has no other strategy; knows to check elsewhere but is short of time or search skill; does not know which outside sources are reliable Ask the learner to check one source with a search allowed and time given; if they still stay on the page, teach the move; if they search but consult unreliable sites, teach reference sources.
"It's all just opinion; nobody can really know" Blanket relativism as a belief about knowledge; a way of avoiding a contested or identity-laden topic; lack of the domain knowledge needed to weigh the evidence Give two claims on a low-stakes topic with clearly unequal evidence; if the learner now ranks them with reasons, the response was topic-specific, not a general belief.
Correct verdict on a study, justified by "it matches my experience" Personal coherence used as the standard; knows the disciplinary standard but defaults to the familiar one; the item did not require the standard Give a study whose finding conflicts with common experience but whose method is sound; record which reason the learner gives.
Confident and wrong Over-reliance on surface cues; a misconception in the domain; overconfidence that would also show on other tasks Ask for confidence ratings across several items; check whether errors cluster on one cue (design, authority) or one topic.
Applies disciplinary critique well, judges source credibility poorly (or the reverse) Only the taught standard was learned; the two require different knowledge; the assessment for one was harder Assess both on matched material at the same time; treat them as separate targets unless both rise together.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners. A probe can shift confidence among explanations; it does not identify a cause, and each probe is itself practice in judging evidence.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M]: three studies of teaching fact-checkers' strategies. A district-wide high-school field study (n = 499; 271 taught, 228 matched controls; teacher professional development, then six 50-minute lessons in a government course) found treatment students grew significantly more in judging the credibility of digital content; the abstract calls the design matched-control, the authors' project page cluster-randomised, and it is coded q2. A randomised pilot (four university course sections, n = 67; two 75-minute lessons) found treatment students significantly more likely to gain from a pretest six weeks before to a posttest five weeks after. A single-group study of 68 eleventh graders (eight lessons) improved on three of four constructed-response tasks, with no control. No effect sizes are reported. Not yet checked against its sources. The lateral-reading claim has been merged into Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M] (its old slug is now an alias), so the two are one claim resting on these same three studies, not independent support. It predicts better judgments of unfamiliar web sources after several lessons; it does not predict durability beyond five weeks, changed browsing or sharing outside class, or benefit for evaluating a primary source that has no outside coverage.
  • One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction in source evaluation did no better than the alternative methods it was compared with in two quasi-experiments [~M]: qualifies the relationship. A think-aloud study found historians and undergraduates who stayed on a site were misled by logos and domain names while fact checkers who left it judged better (descriptive; taught nothing). In a one-session library comparison with first-year business students, a CRAAP checklist and six question words did not differ on quizzes; end-of-semester papers from the question-word sections scored higher on a rubric (2.81 against 2.28 of 4), possibly because of prior library instruction. In one 110-minute session with first-term nursing students, accuracy in the checklist group rose from 41% to 59% five weeks later and did not differ from a networked framework at any time point; there was no untrained control. The claim page's own discussion says its title overstates this: the defensible reading is that one-session instruction does no better than its alternatives. Partly checked: 1 of 3 entries passes. It is marked ~ because it limits what one session of any method achieves.
  • Collaborative critique-and-redesign instruction shifts students from a value-free view of objectivity toward a procedural one [+M]: 120 first-year psychology undergraduates (24 groups of 5) at one university, a 12-week course with three small-group tasks critiquing and redesigning flawed studies, parallel written evaluations before and after. Appeal to valid methods rose from 2% to 38% of responses, to replication from 24% to 55%; naive appeal to objectivity fell from 41% to 17%, and reliance on personal experience from 69% to 52%. None of the lay-evaluation ideals (source expertise, outlet credibility) changed significantly. Single group, no control (withheld on ethical grounds), so maturation and other coursework are not ruled out. Not yet checked against its sources. It supports teaching disciplinary evidence standards and limits the relationship to the standards taught.
  • Argumentation Improves Reasoning [+M]: a meta-analysis of 46 studies (5,415 students) of online scientific argumentation reports g = 0.603 on argumentation skill and g = 0.596 on content learning, larger in K-12 than higher education (read from the abstract; comparators and study quality not checked). A multi-year quasi-experiment in which two cohorts of young adolescents argued about social issues in peer dialogues found better argument quality and more attention to evidence in individual essays on new topics than comparison groups given essay practice and whole-class discussion. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries passes. It predicts better justification with evidence after sustained argumentation; it does not say how much is needed or how long gains last.
  • Elementary students prefer and can evaluate mechanistic explanations using explanatory power and accuracy criteria [+W]: 53 students in grades 2–6, interviewed; across 318 evaluation occasions they accepted mechanistic explanations 71% of the time against 53% for non-mechanistic ones (p < .001), citing whether an explanation answered "how", and used their own biological knowledge to reject explanations that conflicted with it. Exploratory, no instruction or comparison group, one context. Not settled: the abstract could not confirm the entry. It shows young children already apply epistemic criteria when asked; it is evidence about a starting state, not about the effect of teaching, so it is marked W.

How far each may be carried: the source-evaluation studies are US high-school and university classes on the open web; the critique study is one university psychology course; the argumentation evidence is mostly science and social-issue argument with adolescents; the child study observed, it did not teach. None reports outcomes beyond five weeks for evaluation skill, none reports effect sizes for the evaluation studies, and two of the core claims rest on single-group designs. Keep the kind of evidence judged, the number of sessions, the comparator and the horizon when transporting these results.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner wants to be able to decide, and why, separately from the designer's objective. A learner may value settling a question that matters to them (is this treatment safe, is this news true, who is right in this argument) while the designer's objective is a transferable standard of judgment. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. A learner who disagrees with a conclusion the evidence supports is not thereby showing poor epistemic cognition; ask what reason they give. Choose the kind of claim, the representative material, whether outside searching is permitted, and the horizon, and score the reason as well as the verdict.

For example, a first-year nursing student may want to know whether a herbal remedy her family uses actually works, while the designer's objective is that she can judge the reliability of any health website by checking who is behind it and what independent sources say. A correct verdict on the remedy, reached because a lecturer said so, establishes neither. Let her investigate the remedy as the practice case, record her reasons and confidence, and check the designer's objective separately on unfamiliar health sites several weeks later.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if controlled comparisons of comparable learners find that several sessions of explicit standards and practice do not improve judgments of new claims of the taught kind, or that gains fade well before a few weeks. If instruction in one standard is found to improve untaught kinds of judgment as well, the specificity limit above should be relaxed rather than defended. If a single well-designed session proves as effective as several, the dose guidance should change. If learners' confidence ratings track their accuracy no better after instruction, the calibration step should be dropped or redesigned. Do not protect the model by calling every failure a lack of motivation or domain knowledge after the fact.

A shift in the reasons learners write, a better verdict on new material, durability over months, and better judgment in their own lives are separate claims. The evidence here reaches the first two, at horizons up to five weeks, for particular kinds of evidence. It establishes no general epistemic development, no optimal dose and no measure of epistemic beliefs as an outcome.

Related Principles¶

Examples¶

Illustrative¶

Justification — Learners are asked not only for an answer or claim, but for the evidence and reasoning that warrant it.

Source comparison routines — Students evaluate multiple sources making related claims and determine which is better supported and why.

Argument critique in discussion or writing — Learners compare strong and weak uses of evidence, confidence, and counterargument rather than only choosing a side.
