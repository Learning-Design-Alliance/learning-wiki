Explicit Instruction: Vocabulary¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 26 claims (22 for, 3 mixed, 1 against) · 34 studies (11 quant-synthesis, 10 causal, 9 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 5 of 34 report an effect size · 16 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

For a learner whose understanding of a text, an explanation or a task is limited by particular words they do not yet know (everyday words for a young or second-language learner, general academic words, or the technical terms of a subject or job), explicitly teaching a small, selected set of those words (meaning in plain language, examples and non-examples, word parts where they help) and then arranging repeated encounters and use of them across reading, listening, speaking and writing is expected to produce better knowledge of those words, and better comprehension of texts that use them, than leaving the words to be picked up from exposure alone. The capability is word knowledge (verbal-association for form and meaning, concept where the word names a disciplinary idea) and its use in comprehension and production. The expected change is on measures that use the taught words, at an immediate horizon; comprehension of untaught texts, standardised comprehension and delayed-retention are separate claims, and the evidence for them is much weaker.

Claims test the core of this relationship, not its dose. Two syntheses of school-age vocabulary interventions found that teaching word meanings improves comprehension of passages containing the taught words, and does little for standardised or generalised comprehension (Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]). A 1986 meta-analysis, read from its abstract, concluded that the most effective methods combined definitions with context, gave more than one or two exposures and asked for deeper processing (Pairing Contextual Encounters With Explicit Instruction Produces Stronger Vocabulary Outcomes Than Either Alone [+M]). Second-language syntheses show that exposure alone teaches only a small share of the words met (Incidental Vocabulary Exposure Limited [+M]). No claim here sets how many words to teach at once, how many encounters a word needs, how to space them, or when a word counts as learned; those parts of the design below are proposals.

The relationship is conditional on four things: which words matter for the learner's next texts and tasks (selection is the designer's first decision, and no claim settles it); the learner's current vocabulary and language proficiency (in second-language reading, a learner who knows too few of the surrounding words cannot use context to infer the rest); whether the learner can decode the written word at all; and the outcome and horizon that count. The population is a scope qualifier: most evidence below is from school-age first-language readers or from second-language learners at school or university, and carrying it to adults in work settings or to young children's oral vocabulary is an extrapolation.

How the converted siblings sit beside this page: Direct Instruction holds the general relationship between explicit explanation and modelling and less guided discovery; vocabulary is one domain of it, with its own evidence. Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax holds the decision about which wording to remove from a task and which essential terms to keep and teach; this page holds how to teach the words that are kept. Phonics holds decoding, which a learner needs before written vocabulary work can help. Scaffolding and Fading holds how glosses, word banks and sentence frames are withdrawn, and Spaced Learning and Retrieval Practice hold the scheduling and recall-practice models this page borrows for review.

Default design¶

Each step names its evidence status. The direction (teach selected words explicitly, then give many varied encounters and uses) rests on claims; the quantities are proposals to set locally and record.

  1. Select a small set of high-value words before the unit. Take the words the learner will meet in the next texts and tasks and must understand or use: words that recur, that carry the key ideas, or that the learner will need to say or write. Leave aside rare words a gloss can handle. A default dose of 5–8 new words per week of instruction, 3–4 per session, is an untested proposal (the earlier page asked for "a manageable set"); the reason to keep the set small is that the evidence for gains is on taught words, not on general comprehension (Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]).
  2. Introduce each word explicitly, in about 2–3 minutes per word. Say and show it, give a learner-friendly meaning, two or three examples in contexts like the coming text, one non-example, and, where the word has transparent parts (prefix, root, suffix), point them out. Teaching word meanings directly is supported for taught-word comprehension (above); teaching word parts has a small effect on school-age children's vocabulary (Morphological Instruction Improves Vocabulary [+M]). The minutes are an untested proposal.
  3. Put the words straight into a text or task that uses them. Read, hear or watch the text in the same or the next session, with the taught words recurring. Combining definitions with contextual encounters is the authors' conclusion in one meta-analysis (Pairing Contextual Encounters… [+M]); exposure without teaching yields a small share of words (Incidental Vocabulary Exposure Limited [+M]).
  4. Ask learners to do something with each word, not only recognise it. Explain why an example fits or does not, answer a question that needs the word, use it in a sentence about their own experience or the content, sort words by meaning. The Wright and Cervetti review found that instruction involving active processing typically did more than giving a definition or using a dictionary (Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]). One second-hand report links productive use in a task that needs the word to retention (Task-essentialness and productive use of new words… [+W]).
  5. Plan repeated encounters across at least two to three weeks. A default of at least six encounters per word in varied contexts (two in reading, two in talk, two in writing or a task) is an untested proposal. The number of encounters goes with incidental word learning in second-language studies, a correlation rather than a tested dose (Vocabulary Knowledge Grows Incrementally With Repeated Encounters In Varied Contexts [+M]).
  6. Review with spaced recall, 5 minutes at the start of later sessions. Bring each word back after about 1, 3, 7 and 14 days, asking learners to recall or use it before showing the meaning (an untested schedule). Supplemental computer-based spaced repetition gave much higher end-of-semester credit on taught items than class and homework alone, in one double-blind study of EFL cadets (Supplemental computer-based spaced repetition… [+M]).
  7. Respond to misuse as it appears. When a learner uses a word in the wrong sense, collocation or register, give a short contrast (the word in the right use beside theirs) and ask for a second attempt, rather than re-teaching the whole set. Contingent support is drawn from one-to-one tutoring in other domains (Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]); for words it is untested.
  8. Shift the source of meaning from teacher to learner across the unit. Early sessions: teacher gives the meaning and examples, glosses are on the page. Middle sessions: learners work out a meaning from context or word parts first, then check against the gloss or teacher. Late sessions: no glosses for the taught words; learners meet them in a new text and use them unprompted. Moving from explicit explanation to independent inference over time is from the earlier page; how to withdraw supports is modelled in Scaffolding and Fading. Untested for vocabulary.
  9. Move a word from "being taught" to "maintained" on use, not on a definition. A default criterion: the learner uses the word correctly, without prompting, in two different tasks (for example a spoken explanation and a written answer) in two different sessions. Then it joins a smaller maintenance review. Untested proposal; a definition test alone shows recognition, not use.
  10. Judge the design on the texts and tasks that matter. Check comprehension of a new text that uses the taught words, and use of the words in the learner's own speaking or writing, at the horizon the objective sets. Do not expect a general reading score to move from a small word set (Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension [~S]).

When a condition is not met: if the learner cannot decode the written word, teach the word orally and through pictures or demonstration first, and pair it with decoding work (Phonics); if the learner knows too few of the surrounding words for context to help, gloss more words in the text rather than expect inference (Correct word inference in reading requires knowing roughly 95–98% of surrounding words… [~W]); if the learner already knows the words, skip to use in harder texts.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which words the learner must understand or use, in which texts and tasks, and by when. A designer who knows the coming texts can pre-select and recycle; one who does not will teach lists that never recur. If unstated, ask: what will the learner read, hear, say or write in the next few weeks, and which words in it would stop them?
  • The learner's current vocabulary, language background and decoding. A first-language child, a second-language beginner, a fluent adult entering a new field and a learner who cannot yet read the word need different selection, glossing and modes. If unstated, ask: in which language does the learner read best, how many of the words in a typical coming text do they already know, and can they read the target words aloud?
  • Whether the outcome is recognising words, comprehending texts that use them, or using them productively. Recognition can be reached with a few explicit encounters; productive use needs speaking and writing tasks; general comprehension is barely moved by a small set. If unstated, ask: what should the learner be able to do with these words, and how will you check it?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response to a word under stated conditions: the item or text it appeared in, whether a gloss or picture was available, what the learner said, wrote or chose, and how long after teaching. "Knows the word", "has partial knowledge" and "cannot infer from context" are inferred states. Recognising a meaning in a multiple-choice item, recalling it, understanding it in a new text and using it correctly in one's own sentence are different capabilities.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Gives the definition, cannot use the word in a sentence or a new text Recognition without productive knowledge; the new text's other words or syntax are the barrier; the concept behind the word is not held Ask for a use in a short spoken explanation with a frame, then present the word in a new passage with the surrounding words glossed; see which, if either, changes the response.
Understands the word in the taught passage, not in a new text Knowledge tied to one context; a different sense of the word in the new text; too many unknown surrounding words Give a second new text with familiar surrounding words and the same sense; then one with a different sense.
Uses the word in the wrong collocation or register ("make a research") The meaning is held but its use is not; first-language transfer; the examples given showed only one pattern Ask the learner to choose between two uses, and to say which sounds right in their first language; give two more examples of the right pattern.
Knew the words at the end of the unit, much weaker two weeks later Too few encounters after teaching; no recall practice; the words did not recur in later texts Re-test with a new item at the delay and count the encounters and reviews each word actually had.
Cannot read the word aloud, though the meaning was taught A decoding gap; an irregular spelling; the word was taught only orally Present the word spoken and written separately, and ask for the meaning each time.
Avoids the taught words in speech or writing, using simpler ones Uncertainty about use; fear of error in front of others; the task did not need the word Give a task where the word is needed, privately or in pairs, and compare.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners as diagnostics. A probe is itself another encounter with the word, so record each one, and keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Vocabulary Instruction Improves Comprehension [+S]: a meta-analysis of 37 pre-K–12 vocabulary interventions found comprehension gains on researcher-built passage measures (d = 0.50) and little on standardised comprehension (d = 0.10); on custom measures students with reading difficulties gained much more (d = 1.23) than others (d = 0.39). A systematic review of 36 studies found that teaching word meanings almost always helped comprehension of texts containing the taught words, that active processing typically did more than a definition or a dictionary, and that there was very limited evidence for generalised comprehension and none for teaching one or two word-solving strategies. This is the core evidence for the page and also its main limit. School-age learners only; the meta-analysis is read without a rigour code (r?).
  • Pairing Contextual Encounters With Explicit Instruction Produces Stronger Vocabulary Outcomes Than Either Alone [+M]: one 1986 meta-analysis, read from its abstract, reporting d = 0.97 for comprehension of passages containing taught words and 0.30 for global comprehension, and concluding that methods combining definitional and contextual information, with more than one or two exposures and deeper processing, were most effective. The comparison of combined against single methods carries no effect size; no study here tests context-only, explicit-only and combined instruction against each other.
  • Incidental Vocabulary Exposure Limited [+M]: three syntheses. Students learned about 15% of the unknown words met in normal reading; in second-language studies the number of encounters correlated r = .34 with learning; and incidental learning from meaning-focused input had a large effect against controls (g = 1.14) while learners picked up only 9–18% of target words on immediate posttests. A large effect is not a large share: exposure teaches some words, too few to rely on for words a learner needs now. Spaced encounters did better than massed ones in the last synthesis. The claim page's Discussion still says it has no verified entries, which is stale.
  • Vocabulary Knowledge Grows Incrementally With Repeated Encounters In Varied Contexts [+M]: the same two incidental-learning syntheses (about 15% per reading; r = .34 between encounters and learning in second-language studies). It supports planning many encounters; it is correlational, about incidental reading, and does not isolate the variety of contexts, so the "varied" half of the title is not tested here.
  • Morphological Instruction Improves Vocabulary [+M]: a meta-analysis of 30 studies of school-age children found a small effect of morphological instruction on vocabulary (d = 0.34) and larger ones on decoding and morphological knowledge, with no reliable effect on reading comprehension or fluency; a review of 22 studies (preschool–grade 8) found particular benefit for less-able readers and when combined with other literacy instruction. It supports teaching word parts as one part of explicit instruction, not as a route to comprehension. The claim page's Discussion says vocabulary "is not separately estimated", which its own entry (d = 0.34) contradicts.
  • Supplemental computer-based spaced repetition activities nearly triple long-term vocabulary retention in EFL students compared with conventional instruction alone [+M]: one double-blind study with 22 cadets in which each lexical unit was assigned to the tutor or to class and homework only; items the tutor counted as learned got some credit on an end-of-semester test at 50.1% against 16.9%. It supports spaced review in addition to teaching; the observations are word-by-learner pairs, not independent learners, and no effect size is printed.
  • Intentional vocabulary learning yields higher, faster gains and better retention than incidental learning through reading [+W] and Combining intentional and incidental vocabulary learning produces greater gains and better retention than either mode alone [+W]: one review's second-hand reports of Peters et al. (2009) and of a study of 93 Chinese university ESL students; no effect sizes. They point the same way as the pairing claim. The pairing and combined claims may be one proposition (the pairing page flags a merge candidate).

The limiting claims.

  • Incidental vocabulary acquisition through comprehensible input is more powerful than intentional vocabulary learning [-W]: a narrative review (r1) argues the reverse of this page, that acquisition through comprehensible input outperforms intentional learning. Weak and second-hand; it is a reason to keep wide reading and listening in the design, not to drop explicit teaching, since the syntheses above show exposure alone teaches a small share of words.
  • Correct word inference in reading requires knowing roughly 95–98% of surrounding words, corresponding to about 3,000–5,000 word families [~W]: a review's second-hand report of coverage figures. It limits the hand-over to inference from context for learners with small vocabularies.
  • Proficiency moderates gloss effects: vocabulary benefits appear at Levels 2 and 3 but not Levels 1 and 4 [~M]: in one EFL study, gloss conditions differed in vocabulary recall only for learners at middle proficiency levels. One support suits some learners and not others.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (school-age first-language readers, second-language university students, military cadets), so do not rank them by effect label. None tests a number of words per session, a number of encounters, or a criterion for a word being learned, and none follows adults in work settings.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model, and others found while rewriting it. They bear on parts of the design but have not been read against the model above; each says how far it bears on vocabulary.

  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] — about explaining worked examples and texts (physics, mathematics, the circulatory system), not word learning; it bears on asking learners to explain why an example fits a word only by analogy
  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S] — about immediate-memory capacity; the earlier page used it to argue for small word sets, which it does not test
  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] — high-schoolers learning writing revision and a dart-throwing synthesis; it does not test vocabulary routines
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M] — one-to-one tutoring in long division and science; used above, labelled as carried, for responding to misuse
  • Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [~S] — faded worked examples and a scaffolding review; bears on withdrawing glosses only by analogy
  • Tasks with higher involvement load, involving search and evaluation, are conducive to better word retention, with marginal glosses efficient for incidental learning [+W] — a review's second-hand report of one study of 125 Chinese ESL freshmen; bears on asking learners to work with a word, not only read past it
  • Vocabulary instruction should focus on procedures of meaning negotiation as much as on declarative word meanings [+W] — a theoretical argument (q1), no data; bears on teaching how to ask about and negotiate a word's meaning in talk
  • All three L1 gloss conditions significantly improve immediate vocabulary recall over a no-gloss control in EFL reading [+M] — one study of 135 Taiwanese undergraduates; bears on first-language glosses for second-language learners
  • Morphological Instruction Improves Literacy [+M] — the same two syntheses as the vocabulary claim, on literacy outcomes
  • Students' mathematical vocabulary use shifted from lower to higher rubric levels after the intervention [+W] — one class of 40 grade 8 students, pre–post without a comparison group, in a mathematical-communication intervention; bears on disciplinary vocabulary only weakly
  • The concept model of vocabulary instruction produced larger gains in content-area vocabulary test performance than the definition/sentence-writing model for all six middle school students with learning disabilities [+W] — attached 2026-10-07 from Fore III et al. (2007), which proposed "Teach content-area vocabulary to students with learning disabilities through explicit concept-model instruction rather than definition-only methods"; tests this page's relationship.
  • The probability that students learn new words incidentally while reading is low—about 15 percent—motivating explicit vocabulary instruction [+W] — attached 2026-10-09 from Kamil et al. (2008), which proposed "Provide explicit vocabulary instruction in reading, language arts, and content-area classes".
  • Intensive academic vocabulary instruction across several days is supported by strong evidence from six WWC-standard studies [+W] — attached 2026-10-09 from Baker et al. (2014), which proposed "Select a small set of academic vocabulary words for in-depth instruction using explicit selection criteria"; tests this page's relationship.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective names the words and the capability: which terms the learner must understand in which texts, or use in which tasks, judged how and when. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the words: to follow a lecture or a briefing, to sound competent in a meeting, to pass an exam, to read to their child, to stop feeling lost in a new job. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values passing a vocabulary quiz may memorise definitions and never use the words; one who values speaking at work may want the everyday phrases around a term more than the term.

For example, an adult second-language learner in a work-readiness programme may want to describe their experience confidently in a job interview, while the designer's objective is the general academic words of a reading syllabus. Teaching the interview words (and the phrases they sit in), and checking them in a practice interview, serves the learner's goal; a reading quiz on academic words serves the designer's. Record both, so that a quiz score is not read as the capability the learner came for.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if well-controlled comparisons of explicit teaching plus contextual encounters against contextual encounters alone, with comparable time and learners, show no advantage on taught-word comprehension or use, or show that the advantage disappears at a delay while incidental learning holds. If studies with adults in work settings, or with young children's oral vocabulary, find different results from the school-age and second-language evidence, the scope should narrow. If explicit teaching of a small set keeps failing to move comprehension of untaught texts, keep that limit rather than claiming general reading gains. If a study finds a number of encounters, a spacing schedule or a set size that changes outcomes, replace the proposals above with it. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "too few encounters" after the fact.

Recognising a word, recalling its meaning, comprehending a text that uses it, using it in one's own speech or writing, and keeping it at a delay are separate claims. The present evidence establishes the first three for taught words over the short term in school-age and second-language learners; it establishes no dose, no schedule and no criterion for a word being learned.
