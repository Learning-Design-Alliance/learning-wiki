Multiple Methods of Assessment¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 16 claims (9 for, 6 mixed, 1 against) · 26 studies (6 causal, 6 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 theoretical, 2 qualitative, 2 design, 1 associational), q1–q4 · 4 of 26 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns how several kinds of evidence about one learning goal are planned, read together and turned into a decision. When a teacher or designer must judge whether a learner has reached a goal, a judgement drawn from two or more methods that call for the same capability in different ways (a written answer and a short oral explanation; a product and the learner's account of the decisions behind it; a test and an observed performance; the teacher's, the learner's own and a peer's reading of the same work against one set of criteria) is expected to be more accurate (assessment-accuracy) than a judgement drawn from one method, when the learner's result on that one method depends on something other than the goal: reading and writing load, a symbol system the learner does not use, test anxiety, an unfamiliar context, or a capability that only shows with help. Where learners also get a bounded choice between formats held to one standard, the expected further gain is in motivation (motivation-affect), not in achievement. Adding peer and self-assessment as methods can also raise later performance (general-achievement), because learners apply the criteria, not because the evidence becomes more accurate.

The relationship is conditional on four things: every method must sample the same goal (a poster and a written explanation of photosynthesis may test drawing and writing more than photosynthesis); the criteria must be common across methods and the people applying them calibrated, or the variation added is noise; the format must not itself be the goal (if the goal is writing an argued essay, an oral alternative removes the construct rather than a barrier); and a second method costs time for the learner and the marker, so it pays only where the first is likely to mislead for this learner or where the decision has stakes.

No claim in the wiki tests the relationship as a whole. None compares decisions made from several methods with decisions made from one, against an independent measure of what learners can do, and none compares a choice of format with an assigned format on achievement. The nearest claims argue or illustrate that one format can hide what a learner knows (a review's argument about verbal tests, a design study of family-grounded tasks with young children, a theoretical argument for assisted assessment), show that peer assessment raises performance in a meta-analysis, show that autonomy support raises motivation, and limit the relationship (untrained self-assessment is inaccurate; raters differ in severity; matching format to a "learning style" does not help). The design below is therefore a labelled default.

How the converted siblings sit beside this: Universal Design for Learning owns the planning audit that offers several ways to show an outcome, held to one standard, so that a barrier does not stop a learner; this page owns what is done with the evidence once several methods exist: whether they agree, what to do when they do not, and when a second method is worth its cost. Validity, Reliability and Bias in Classroom Assessment owns the measurement properties of any one score; this page uses them and adds the combination of scores. Assessment for Learning owns how evidence is used during instruction, with shared criteria and a revision step; Competency-Based Assessment owns the threshold judgement and assessor agreement for a "demonstrated / not yet" decision. Autonomy owns choice within structure. Summative Assessment and Standardized Test Fairness and Bias hold end-of-sequence judgement and large-scale cases. The Authentic Assessment pattern is one design that uses a performance plus feedback and revision as the unit's assessment; the Formative Assessment pattern is the reusable loop for reading a response and acting on it.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page's guidance (clear targets, comparable criteria, intentional evidence design, use of results, and its four examples), kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

  1. Name one goal per judgement, then decide which formats are part of it. Write the capability as the learner will show it (verb, content, conditions). Mark which response modes belong to the goal (writing for a written argument; speaking for an oral presentation) and which do not (writing for a science concept). Only modes that do not belong to the goal may vary. Untested; from the earlier page ("clear learning targets"; "some skills still require specific formats").
  2. Choose two methods per goal, rarely more than three, chosen to fail differently. Pair a method that depends on reading and writing with one that does not (a short oral explanation, a demonstration, a labelled drawing, an observed task), or a product with the learner's account of the decisions behind it. A single verbal test can underestimate what some learners know (Typical verbal achievement tests may underestimate students' subject-matter knowledge because of a mismatch between the test's symbolic form and students' symbolic encoding [+W], an argument in a review, not a tested result). The number of methods is an untested proposal; the earlier page warned against "assessment sprawl".
  3. Write one set of criteria for all methods, with an example of each level in each format. Learners see the criteria and examples before they start. Markers score one example in each format together before marking, and double-mark a sample of about one in five (an untested proportion). Self-assessing against a rubric improved work in two non-randomised studies (Self-assessing against a rubric improved the quality of student work in two non-randomised studies, one of which also found more stress and performance-avoidance [+M]); raters still differ in severity (Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis [~M], open-ended items in one test).
  4. Read the methods together, with a stated rule. If the methods agree, act on the result. If they disagree, do not average them: ask which demand differs between them (language, speed, context, help available) and give a short third check that removes that demand (2–5 minutes, one item). Record which method each decision rests on. The rule is an untested proposal; what to look for is in the observation table below.
  5. Add self- and peer assessment as methods only after learners have practised judging against the criteria. Show a model of someone assessing work against the criteria, have learners judge two or three examples and compare their judgement with the teacher's, then use their judgements. Untrained self-assessment is inaccurate and tends to overestimate (Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training [~M]); peer assessment raised performance by a small average amount in a meta-analysis (Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment [+M]). Treat learners' judgements as evidence about their calibration as well as about the work; do not use untrained peer grades in a final mark.
  6. Where learners choose a format, bound the choice. Offer two or three formats, each listed with what it must contain and the common criteria; check before release that each option demands the same capability at the same level, and that none is visibly easier or better supported. Bounded choice can raise motivation (Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning. [+M], a narrative review and a meta-analysis on rewards; not a test of assessment choice). Do not assign formats by a learner's "style" (Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-M], tested for instruction, carried to assessment format). From the earlier page ("uneven rigor", "learner agency in assessment").
  7. Feed every method's result into the next instructional step. After each round, name the one goal most learners have not shown in any method and plan the next lesson around it; do not collect evidence no one will use. Untested; from the earlier page ("use of results").
  8. Plan across the unit, not one task. A default arc, all quantities untested proposals:
  9. Early sessions (first third of the unit): two low-stakes methods per session, each 3–5 minutes: a written exit item and a spoken or drawn one ("explain to a partner", a sketch with labels). The teacher chooses the methods; criteria and examples are on display.
  10. Middle sessions: learners practise self-assessment on one piece of work a week against the criteria and compare it with the teacher's reading; one peer review with the criteria; one task where learners choose between two or three formats.
  11. Late sessions: the summative judgement rests on at least two methods for each goal: one common format where the format belongs to the goal, plus one chosen format or an observed performance; a learner whose methods disagree near the cut gets a third, short check before the decision.
  12. Moving on: a learner moves to the next goal once two different methods both show the goal at the criterion; one method at criterion and one below means a targeted third check, not a repeat of the same test.

When a condition is not met: if no marker time is available for a second method, use the second method only for learners whose first result is near a decision point or out of line with their other work; if the format is part of the goal, keep it for everyone and vary only support, time or context.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Which response modes belong to the goal. If the goal includes writing, speaking or performing in a particular way, that mode stays fixed and only other demands vary; if it does not, the format is free to change. If unstated, ask: if a learner showed this capability by speaking, drawing or doing instead of writing, would you accept it as the same capability?
  • What the result will decide, and how much rides on it. A check that steers tomorrow's lesson needs one quick method and a second only where it misleads; a grade, placement or certification near a cut needs at least two methods and calibrated markers. If unstated, ask: what decision will this result change, for whom, and can it be reversed?
  • Who can collect and read a second kind of evidence, and how much time they have. An oral check costs teacher time per learner; peer and self-assessment cost learner time and training; a portfolio costs marking time. If unstated, ask: who will look at the second piece of evidence, for how many learners, and how many minutes each?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response to a particular method under stated conditions: the task, the mode (written, spoken, drawn, performed), the time allowed, the help available, who scored it and against which criteria, and the score. "Understands the concept", "can do it but cannot write it" and "was helped" are inferred states. Two methods agreeing does not prove the inference; they may share a demand (both in English, both timed).

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim in the wiki compares judgements made from several methods with judgements made from one, or compares a choice of assessment format with an assigned one on learning. The claims below bear on parts of the relationship.

  • Typical verbal achievement tests may underestimate students' subject-matter knowledge because of a mismatch between the test's symbolic form and students' symbolic encoding [+W]: a 1986 literature review (Shavelson, Webb & Lehman) contends that tests in a predominantly verbal code may place irrelevant demands on test takers and underestimate some students' subject-matter knowledge. Coded q1, theoretical; the review offers it as a contention to be examined, not a tested result. Its closest indirect evidence, that people acquire different knowledge from a map than from navigation, is recorded in People acquire different knowledge from different media, the closest indirect evidence for symbolic encoding specificity [+W] (q2, reported second-hand in the same review). This is the reason to add a second method in another mode; it does not show that doing so changes decisions.
  • Assessment tasks built on family-identified funds of knowledge elicited computational thinking from K-2 children in a small design-based study that made no comparison with standard assessment [+W]: a design study with 13 K–2 children in a low-income district; tasks built from family practices (bus riding, a healthcare visit) and run as play-based clinical interviews showed sequencing, data reasoning and, for some children, abstraction. The claim page says there was no comparison with a standard assessment for the same children, so "misses" is an inference, and the approach was resource-intensive. Coded q2, design r2. It supports a context-grounded, interview-based method as a second source for young children; it does not show it is more accurate.
  • Static individual assessment misses developing functions; ZPD-based assessment reveals potential competencies [+W]: a theoretical argument (Seng 1997, after Vygotsky) that unaided testing shows only matured functions, and assisted performance shows developing ones. Coded q1. It is the reason to record what a learner does with help as a separate observation; it does not say how to combine it with an unaided score.
  • Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment [+M]: a meta-analysis of 54 control-group studies (141 effect sizes) across school and university: peer assessment improved performance against no assessment (g = 0.31) and against teacher assessment (g = 0.28), and did not differ significantly from self-assessment (g = 0.23); moderators tested did not change the effect. Coded q4 i1, rigour not established from the text read. It supports adding peer assessment as a method for its effect on performance; it says nothing about whether peer judgements are accurate enough to count in a grade.
  • Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning. [+M]: a narrative review of self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci 2000), a meta-analysis of 128 experiments on rewards (d = −0.4 for expected tangible rewards on intrinsic motivation), and a conceptual framework that names choice of presentation media as procedural autonomy support. The outcome is motivation, not achievement, and no entry tests a choice of assessment format. The claim's own Discussion says autonomy works best with structure, and premature autonomy can make novices anxious.
  • Limiting: Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training [~M]: in a randomised experiment, 80 Dutch pre-university students (about 15) who studied modelling examples of self-assessment judged their genetics problem-solving more accurately than those who did not (ηp² = .10); a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies, read as an abstract, found student and teacher marks agreed inconsistently, more closely in advanced courses and better-designed studies. Self-assessment adds a method only once learners have been taught to use the criteria.
  • Limiting: Raters differed significantly in severity/leniency in the MFRM analysis [~M]: three raters scoring open-ended items differed significantly in severity (one study, q3, associational). In one community-college lab class, six scorers rating six teams with an engineering rubric differed by a mean of 1.2 of 9 points, which the authors read as a sign the rubric may not be reliable [~W] is one class, with no reliability coefficient. Methods scored by judgement add rater variation; without calibration a second method can add noise as well as evidence.
  • Limiting: Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning [-M]: a systematic review and a randomised experiment with fifth graders found no learner-style × modality interaction for instruction. Carried here to assessment format: it is evidence against offering or assigning formats by "style". It is not evidence against offering an alternative mode to get round a barrier the goal does not include, which is a different claim.
  • Math anxiety has a small-to-moderate negative correlation with math achievement (r = −.28) in a meta-analysis of correlations from 223 studies [~M]: a meta-analysis of 223 studies found a correlation of r = −.28 between mathematics anxiety and achievement, across nearly every moderator. Correlational; it does not test whether a less pressured method gives a truer picture, only that one pressured method may confound anxiety with achievement.
  • Self-assessing against a rubric improved the quality of student work in two non-randomised studies, one of which also found more stress and performance-avoidance [+M]: middle-school writers (N = 162) and pre-service teachers (N = 218); the second study also found more accurate self-assessment with a rubric, and more stress. It supports common criteria that learners use, and warns of a cost.
  • Preschool children demonstrate behavioral knowledge of classification, one-to-one correspondence, and seriation during everyday free play with everyday materials [+W]: one observed play episode with two children, in which a teacher recognised different stages of seriation. It illustrates observation in play as a method for young children; it is not a comparison.

Learners, settings and outcomes differ across these claims (school and university peer assessment, pre-university self-assessment, children in play, reviews of testing), and only four print an effect size, each on a different outcome, so they cannot be ranked against each other.

[~M] — about support during tutoring (fourth and fifth graders, one-to-one, long division; a meta-analysis of dynamic assessment). It bears on this page only through dynamic assessment, where help given during the assessment is itself evidence; the old page cited it for "the next instructional move".

Lowered to W because the claim records no test.
  • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions. [~M] — two syntheses (a review and a theoretical synthesis) on self-monitoring against criteria; bears on self-assessment as one of the methods, not on combining methods. Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is a decision about a learner (what to teach next, a grade, a certificate) that matches what the learner can do with the goal, in the conditions that matter. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the assessment: to show what they know in a way that feels fair, to get the grade with least risk, to have a product they can use or show, to avoid speaking in front of others. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who values a safe grade may pick the format they think is easiest, which is evidence about the options' equality as well as about the learner; one who dreads speaking may do worse orally for reasons that are not the goal.

For example, an adult apprentice electrician may want a portfolio of real installations to show future employers, while the course objective is passing a written regulations exam. A photographed installation with a short spoken account of the regulations it follows serves the apprentice's goal and gives a second method for the regulations goal; the written exam stays where the qualification requires it. Record both, so that a strong portfolio is not read as exam readiness, or a weak exam as weak practice.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if studies comparing decisions from several methods with decisions from one, against an independent measure of capability, show no gain in accuracy, or show that the added rater variation outweighs the added evidence. If learners given a choice of format, held to one rubric, reach the goal no more often and are no more motivated than learners assigned one format, the choice step is a preference only. If calibrated peer judgements prove as accurate as teachers' for a kind of task, peer review can carry more of the second reading. If a single well-designed method with low language load predicts later performance as well as two methods, the second method is a cost to cut. Do not protect the model by calling every disagreement between methods "evidence of hidden knowledge"; sometimes the lower result is the accurate one.

Agreement between methods, accuracy of a decision, later achievement and motivation are separate outcomes. The present evidence establishes no number of methods, no rule for combining them and no evidence that a choice of format improves achievement.

Examples¶

  • Mixed evidence portfolios: Learners submit written work, reflections, performances, and artifacts to demonstrate growth.
  • Choice of demonstration format: Learners can show understanding through presentation, written analysis, or multimedia artifact using a common rubric.
  • Performance plus reflection: An authentic task is paired with a brief explanation of decisions and learning.
  • Peer, self, and instructor assessment combination: Different viewpoints are combined to improve the evidence picture.
  • Type 2 checklist assessment: tasks coded to each first-grade critical objective for teacher judgment of progress
  • Portfolio Assessment — a collection of several kinds of evidence judged over time.
  • Varying Levels of Choice in Assessments — bounded choice of format in practice.
  • Authentic Assessment — a performance with feedback and revision, often paired with a written account.
  • Family-interview-based assessment task design — building a context-grounded second method for young children.
  • Combine observational measures with other methods in formative evaluation of preschool-through-third-grade programs, keeping them free of high stakes.
