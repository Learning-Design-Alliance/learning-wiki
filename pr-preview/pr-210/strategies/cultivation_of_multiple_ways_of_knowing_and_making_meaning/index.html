Cultivation of Multiple Ways of Knowing and Making Meaning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 13 studies (8 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 3 of 13 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy designs learning so that knowledge can be built, expressed, and validated through multiple epistemic channels — narrative, demonstration, dialogue, embodied practice, visual representation, and cultural tradition — rather than through a single privileged mode. It draws on culturally responsive pedagogy and Constructivism: learners arrive with different funds of knowledge, and instruction treats those as assets to build on rather than deficits to overcome. Importantly, "multiple ways of knowing" refers to varied sources and modes of knowledge construction, not to the discredited notion that individuals have fixed learning styles that instruction should match [X].

Design Implications¶

Presenting concepts through multiple representations and inviting learners to connect content to their own cultural and experiential knowledge deepens understanding and supports transfer Multiple cases and representations across contexts support flexible knowledge transfer. [+M]. Connecting content to learners' backgrounds and communities also strengthens engagement and intergroup outcomes Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M] Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Source materials and examples drawn from more than one cultural, disciplinary, or representational tradition
  • Multiple channels for learners to demonstrate understanding — e.g., Choice Boards, oral, written, visual, and enacted formats
  • Classroom norms that make it safe to voice differing perspectives (Check-In, Class Discussion protocols)
  • Instructor knowledge of learners' communities and funds of knowledge, gathered rather than assumed

Constraints¶

  • Superficial "multicultural add-ons" (holidays, food, flags) without epistemic substance do not improve learning and can read as tokenism [-W]
  • Openly inviting personal perspectives without structure can expose learners to stereotype threat or discomfort in mixed-status classrooms [~M]
  • Treating "multiple ways of knowing" as license to abandon accuracy or evidence standards degrades conceptual learning [-M]
  • Matching instruction to self-reported "learning styles" does not improve outcomes and wastes design effort [X]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Curriculum-level: alternate case sources, authors, and knowledge traditions across a unit
  • Activity-level: offer parallel pathways (narrative, visual, manipulative, dialogic) into the same concept
  • Assessment-level: allow multiple demonstration formats against a common rubric
  • Community-level: invite elders, practitioners, or community experts as co-teachers of local knowledge

Target Learners¶

  • Learners from cultural or linguistic backgrounds underrepresented in standard curricula, who benefit when their funds of knowledge are treated as instructional assets [+M]
  • Multilingual learners, who profit from dual-coding and multiple representational formats Dual coding improves recall. [+M]
  • All learners in heterogeneous classrooms — exposure to diverse perspectives benefits majority-group students as well Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding robust across contexts, built from multiple representations Multiple cases and representations across contexts support flexible knowledge transfer. [+M]
  • Perspective-taking, cultural awareness, and empathy Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
  • Epistemic flexibility: recognizing that disciplines and cultures have different valid standards for warranting claims

Instructions¶

  1. Audit the unit: identify whose knowledge, examples, and modes of expression are represented, and where a single tradition dominates.
  2. Open with Activation — elicit learners' prior experiences and community knowledge relevant to the topic Activation improves learning. [+M].
  3. Present the core concept through at least two representational modes (e.g., Analogies, Case Studies, visual models, Act It Out) Dual coding improves recall. [+M].
  4. Structure Class Discussion or Argumentation so learners compare how different traditions or disciplines frame the same question.
  5. Offer Choice Boards or equivalent options for how learners demonstrate mastery, assessed against a shared rubric.
  6. Close with Articulation — learners name what their own perspective contributed and what they learned from others'.

Related Strategies¶

  • Culturally Responsive Teaching — the pedagogical framework this strategy most directly enacts
  • Universal Design for Learning — multiple means of representation and expression as accessibility infrastructure
  • Case-Based Learning — using varied cases as multiple entry points into a concept
  • Indigenous–non-Indigenous academic collaboration grounded in trust, collegiality and un-learning of Western epistemic authority

Related Elements¶

  • Choice Boards — operationalizes multiple demonstration formats
  • Analogies — bridges unfamiliar content to learners' existing knowledge domains
  • Case Studies — carries situated, culturally specific knowledge into the classroom
  • Act It Out — embodied and dramaturgical ways of knowing
  • Class Discussion — dialogic validation of diverse perspectives

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitive Flexibility Theory — multiple representations and cases as the core mechanism for ill-structured domains
  • Community-Based Learning — community knowledge as a legitimate instructional source
  • Discussion-Based Learning — structured exchange of differing ways of making meaning

Examples¶

  • Zaretta Hammond, Culturally Responsive Teaching and the Brain (2015) — a widely used practitioner framework for building learning partnerships on students' cultural funds of knowledge.
  • Facing History and Ourselves — humanities curriculum that juxtaposes primary sources, testimony, narrative, and visual media so students construct historical understanding through multiple modes of evidence.
  • Native Science curriculum approaches, e.g., the Oregon Natural Resources Education Program — pairs Indigenous ecological knowledge with Western scientific frameworks in environmental education.
  • CAST UDL Guidelines — concrete design guidance for multiple means of representation, action/expression, and engagement.
