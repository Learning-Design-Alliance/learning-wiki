Ten Frame Activities¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 review, 2 associational, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

A ten frame is a 2×5 grid on which counters are placed to represent numbers up to ten. Ten frame activities ask learners to build, read, compare, and combine quantities on the frame — for example, flashing a filled frame for two seconds and asking "how many did you see?", or showing 7 as 5-and-2 and asking what is needed to make 10. The frame's fixed structure anchors quantities to the benchmarks of 5 and 10, the organizational backbone of the base-ten system.

Design Implications¶

Ten frames exploit the visual system's capacity to perceive small quantities at a glance (subitizing), reducing the working-memory demand of counting Subitizing and structured quantity representations reduce cognitive load in early number work. [+M]. Because the frame partitions quantities into "full row" (5) and "extra" chunks, learners internalize decompositions (7 = 5 + 2) that later support strategies like making-ten in addition Structured manipulatives improve early arithmetic strategy development. [+W]. The fixed frame also prevents the common misconception that numeral order determines quantity arrangement.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Physical or digital frames with counters that fit one per cell (no overlapping or stacking)
  • A sequence that moves from building concrete quantities → recognizing flashed quantities → comparing → combining
  • Teacher questioning that asks how learners saw a quantity ("Did you see 5 and 2, or 6 and 1?"), not just how many
  • Eventual connection to numerals and equations so the representation does not remain an isolated ritual

Constraints¶

  • If learners count counters one-by-one indefinitely, the frame adds no value over loose counters; activities must press toward structure-based recognition [~M]
  • Over-reliance on a single representation can block abstraction; learners who always need the frame to compute have not developed the intended mental decompositions [-M]
  • Frames capped at ten do not directly support place-value reasoning beyond teen numbers without extension to double ten frames [~W]
  • Rapid flashing before learners have built quantities physically produces guessing rather than subitizing [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Quick-look routines: flash a filled frame for 2–3 seconds; learners state the quantity and how they saw it
  • Make-N games: roll a die, add counters to reach a target number, verbalize the decomposition
  • Double ten frames: extend to 11–20 to bridge toward place value and regrouping
  • Ten frame cards: replace standard dot cards in card games (war, go-fish variants) to keep the 5/10 structure visible during play
  • Digital versions: apps such as DreamBox Learning and Didax virtual manipulatives allow dynamic filling and clearing

Target Learners¶

  • Pre-K through Grade 2 learners developing cardinality and number relationships
  • Learners who count one-by-one and have not yet formed benchmark-based decompositions Structured quantity activities support strategy advancement in early arithmetic. [+W]
  • Older struggling learners filling gaps in foundational number sense; frames should be faded as automatic decompositions develop [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Subitizing and rapid quantity recognition
  • Part-whole decomposition of numbers within 10
  • Benchmarks of 5 and 10 as anchors for addition and subtraction strategies
  • Foundational place-value reasoning (via double ten frames)

Instructions¶

  1. Build: learners place counters on a blank frame to show a dictated number, filling the top row first to make the 5-structure salient.
  2. Read: show a pre-filled frame; learners name the quantity and explain how they saw it (e.g., "5 on top and 2 more").
  3. Flash: display a frame for 2–3 seconds, hide it, and ask for the quantity and the visual chunk used — press for structure, not counting.
  4. Compare and combine: show two frames and ask "how many altogether?" or "how many more to make 10?", connecting the visible decomposition to an equation.
  5. Fade: replace filled frames with empty frames and mental imagery ("picture 8 on a ten frame — what's missing?"), then drop the frame entirely.

Examples¶

  • Illustrative Mathematics K–2 curriculum embeds ten frame routines in its number sense warm-ups.
  • DreamBox Learning uses interactive ten frames with adaptive scaffolding that fades the frame as decompositions become automatic.
  • Didax Virtual Manipulatives — free browser-based single and double ten frames for whole-class demonstration.
  • Sternberg & colleagues' Number Talks routine — "quick look" ten frame cards used in published Number Talks materials (Parrish, 2010).
