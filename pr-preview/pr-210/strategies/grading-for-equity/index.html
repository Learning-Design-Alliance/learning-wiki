Grading For Equity¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Grading for equity is an approach to classroom assessment that treats grades as accurate, bias-resistant, and motivational communication of student learning. It replaces traditional practices — averaging, zero grades for missing work, behavior penalties, single-attempt deadlines — with practices such as standards- or mastery-based scoring, minimum-grade floors, late-work acceptance, reassessment opportunities, and rubrics that describe performance rather than compliance. The core claim is that conventional grading conflates achievement with behavior, timing, and circumstance, distorting both the information grades carry and the incentives they create.

Design Implications¶

Grades function as feedback and as a motivational signal; when they mix academic performance with behavior or punctuality, they lose informational value and can depress the motivation of the students already furthest behind Assessment practices that make learning criteria visible and support improvement improve achievement. [+S]. Equitable grading aligns with formative assessment principles: grades should describe current proficiency relative to clear criteria, not rank effort or penalize process Assessment practices that make learning criteria visible and support improvement improve achievement. [+M]. Because grading practices shape students' sense of fairness and belonging, opaque or punitive grading can undermine engagement, particularly for students from historically marginalized groups Interventions that strengthen students' sense of belonging improve academic outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear learning targets or standards against which performance is scored
  • Rubrics or scoring guides that describe quality levels in student-accessible language (Rubrics)
  • A system for tracking evidence of learning separately from behavior, effort, and work habits
  • Institutional willingness to explain and defend nontraditional grading to students, families, and colleagues

Constraints¶

  • Grades lose their gatekeeping function where institutions require rank-ordered, single-number summaries; hybrid implementations often reintroduce the distortions they were meant to remove [~M]
  • Reassessment and late-work policies can be gamed when students learn they can defer effort indefinitely; policies need limits (e.g., reassessment windows, caps on retakes) to preserve incentives [~M]
  • Minimum-grade floors (e.g., 50% instead of 0%) are frequently misread as grade inflation and can erode stakeholder trust without clear communication [-W]
  • Teachers' grading consistency is limited: the same work can receive different scores from different raters, and rubrics reduce but do not eliminate this variance [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Mastery/standards-based grading: report proficiency per standard rather than a single averaged grade (Competency-Based Assessment)
  • Minimum-grade floors: replace zeros with a floor (commonly 50%) so early failures remain recoverable arithmetically
  • Reassessment policies: allow retakes or revisions on summative tasks, with the most recent evidence replacing earlier scores
  • Separate conduct reporting: report behavior and work habits on a separate scale rather than folding them into the academic grade

Target Learners¶

  • Students who develop at different rates or face out-of-school demands (work, caregiving) that make fixed deadlines punitive Assessment practices that make learning criteria visible and support improvement improve achievement. [+M]
  • Students from marginalized groups, who are disproportionately affected by behavior-based grade penalties and zero policies Interventions that strengthen students' sense of belonging improve academic outcomes. [+M]
  • High-achieving students motivated by grade competition may perceive reduced differentiation; the approach works best when grades are decoupled from status [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Accurate communication of proficiency on defined learning standards
  • Long-term mastery orientation over point accumulation (Mastery Learning)
  • Student self-regulation: using feedback and reassessment to revise understanding Assessment practices that make learning criteria visible and support improvement improve achievement. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning standards and convert them into Rubrics that describe performance levels in observable terms.
  2. Audit current grading practices for non-academic factors (participation, punctuality, extra credit) and move them to a separate conduct or process report.
  3. Replace averaging with a proficiency model: score each standard on its most recent or most consistent evidence rather than the mean of all attempts.
  4. Adopt recovery mechanisms — late-work acceptance, reassessment windows, minimum-grade floors — with explicit limits students can articulate.
  5. Communicate the rationale to students and families, and involve students in tracking their own proficiency data (Assessment as a shared record rather than a verdict).

Related Strategies¶

  • Standards-based grading — the reporting system most equity-oriented grading reforms build on
  • Reassessment and revision policies — the mechanism that makes grades reflect current rather than historical performance
  • Separate conduct reporting — removes behavior from the academic grade without abandoning accountability

Examples¶

  • Feldman's Grading for Equity framework (Crescendo Education Group, https://crescendoedgroup.org) — a widely adopted professional-development model built on three pillars: accuracy, bias-resistance, and intrinsic motivation; piloted in districts including those in California and Washington, D.C.
  • Mastery Transcript Consortium (https://mastery.org) — a network of schools replacing single-letter transcripts with competency-based records of demonstrated learning.
  • CBE reporting in Maine's proficiency-based diploma policy — a statewide mandate (2018) requiring districts to certify proficiency on standards rather than seat time, illustrating both the promise and the implementation difficulty of systemic grading reform.
