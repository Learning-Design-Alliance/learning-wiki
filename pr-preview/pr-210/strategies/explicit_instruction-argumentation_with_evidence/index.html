Explicit Instruction: Argumentation with Evidence¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy directly teaches learners how to construct arguments and support claims with evidence, rather than leaving argumentation skill to develop implicitly through discussion. Instruction typically covers claim–evidence–reasoning structure (often via the Toulmin model), criteria for evaluating source quality, and the deliberate consideration of counterarguments. It is frequently applied in online and discussion-based settings, where unsupported opinion tends to dominate unless norms and skills are explicitly taught.

Design Implications¶

Argumentation quality improves when the skill is modeled and scaffolded rather than assumed; unguided peer discussion rarely produces evidence-based reasoning on its own [~M]. Teaching learners to generate and address counterarguments is the component most consistently linked to higher argument quality Nussbaum's counterargument work shows direct instruction in counterargument improves essays. [+M]. Prompting learners to explain why evidence supports a claim deepens conceptual understanding beyond mere citation of sources Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An explicit argument framework (e.g., Toulmin: claim, evidence, warrant, rebuttal) taught through worked models
  • Access to credible evidence sources and instruction in evaluating them
  • Structured practice with feedback on argument quality, not just participation Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+M]
  • Norms that require engaging opposing viewpoints, not only defending one's own position

Constraints¶

  • Argumentation skill develops slowly; single-lesson interventions show little durable gain [-M]
  • Learners with low prior topic knowledge struggle to evaluate evidence quality and may cite sources without understanding them [~M]
  • Requiring counterarguments can backfire when learners lack the background knowledge to represent opposing views accurately, producing straw-man rebuttals [-W]
  • In online forums, evidence requirements without moderation can devolve into link-dropping rather than reasoning [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Sentence starters and argument templates (claim–evidence–reasoning frames) for novices, fading to open argumentation
  • Structured controversy formats where positions are assigned, forcing engagement with a viewpoint one may not hold
  • Online variants: evidence-linked discussion posts, argument mapping tools, peer critique protocols

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students developing disciplinary reasoning skills
  • Adult learners in professional contexts where justification of decisions is required
  • Novices benefit most from explicit structure; advanced learners may find templates constraining [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Constructing evidence-based arguments in writing and discussion
  • Critical evaluation of sources and evidence quality
  • Perspective-taking through mandatory engagement with counterarguments
  • Conceptual understanding of contested or multi-causal topics Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Model an expert argument using an Argument framework, thinking aloud about evidence selection and warranting
  2. Directly teach the claim–evidence–reasoning structure and source-evaluation criteria
  3. Have learners critique flawed or incomplete arguments to sharpen discrimination
  4. Provide structured Practice constructing arguments, initially with templates and sentence starters
  5. Require explicit treatment of counterarguments and rebuttals
  6. Deliver feedback targeting the quality of evidence–claim links and the handling of opposing views

Related Strategies¶

  • Structured academic controversy — assigns positions so learners must argue both sides
  • Peer critique protocols — operationalize evidence standards in review of others' arguments

Examples¶

  • Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) frameworks in science education (e.g., the UC Berkeley Lawrence Hall of Science SEPD sequence) — students write scientific explanations using a fixed claim–evidence–reasoning scaffold before the scaffold is faded
  • Nussbaum's argumentation interventions — college students taught to generate counterarguments and rebuttals wrote measurably stronger argumentative essays
  • Online debate forums with evidence-linking requirements — discussion platforms where posts must cite linked sources, with moderators flagging unsupported claims
