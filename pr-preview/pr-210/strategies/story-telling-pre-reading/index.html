Story Telling (Pre-Reading)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The instructor prepares a short, personalized narrative related to the reading topic and tells it enthusiastically before learners encounter the assigned text. The story should be creative and engaging, ideally including suspense and humor. Sharing this narrative creates interest, activates relevant prior knowledge, and establishes a conceptual and emotional bridge into the reading material.

Design Implications¶

Pre-reading storytelling works as a form of Activation: it surfaces relevant background knowledge so that incoming text can be integrated with existing schemas rather than processed in isolation Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension and learning. [+M]. Because narrative structure is highly memorable and attention-capturing, a well-told story also functions as an advance organizer — a familiar framework onto which the text's content can be mapped Advance organizers improve learning and retention. [+M]. The story must connect meaningfully to the text's central concepts; a merely entertaining story with no conceptual link consumes time and working memory without aiding comprehension Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-crafted narrative whose central conflict, characters, or setting map onto the key concepts of the assigned reading
  • Effective oral delivery — pacing, suspense, and enthusiasm — since delivery quality drives engagement
  • A brief transition that makes the story-to-text connection explicit ("As you read, notice how the author deals with the same dilemma…")
  • Reasonable length: typically 2–5 minutes, so the story primes rather than replaces the reading

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends heavily on the instructor's storytelling skill and the story's relevance; a weak or off-topic story wastes time and can mislead learners about the text's focus [~W]
  • Learners who do not identify with the personal narrative may feel excluded; culturally unfamiliar references can raise rather than lower the barrier to comprehension [-W]
  • Overly elaborate or emotionally dominant stories can overshadow the text itself, leaving learners with a vivid but shallow memory of the story instead of the content [~W]
  • In second-language contexts, stories told above learners' proficiency level create listening load that defeats the priming purpose [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Personal anecdote — instructor's own experience, adding authenticity and relational warmth
  • Fictional or historical vignette — a suspenseful scenario learners must resolve by reading the text (a "cliffhanger" design)
  • Student stories — inviting learners to tell their own related narratives shifts the activity toward peer Activation
  • Multimedia retelling — recorded audio or illustrated versions support Audiobooks-style access for emerging readers

Target Learners¶

  • Emerging readers and second-language learners, for whom a concrete narrative context compensates for limited vocabulary and background knowledge Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension and learning. [+M]
  • Reluctant or disengaged readers, where interest-building is the primary barrier
  • Young learners, who respond strongly to narrative framing; older learners benefit when the story poses a genuine problem or dilemma rather than merely entertaining

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension: building a schema for the text before decoding it
  • Motivation and engagement: establishing purpose and anticipation for reading
  • Prior knowledge activation: connecting new text content to lived experience
  • Vocabulary pre-teaching in context: key terms introduced naturally within the story

Instructions¶

  1. Read the assigned text and identify its central concept, conflict, or theme.
  2. Draft a 2–5 minute personal or fictional narrative that embodies that concept, ending with suspense or an open question.
  3. Tell the story with deliberate pacing and enthusiasm; use Analogies within the story to link unfamiliar concepts to familiar ones.
  4. Make the bridge explicit: pose one or two questions connecting the story to what learners are about to read, functioning as an Advance Organizer.
  5. Transition directly into the reading task, referring back to the story during follow-up discussion to reinforce the connection.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the broader family of pre-reading techniques; storytelling is its narrative form
  • Anticipation Guides — a written complement that turns the story's dilemma into predictions learners verify while reading
  • Read-Alouds — shares the oral-narrative delivery mode but occurs during rather than before reading

Examples¶

  • Before reading an article on environmental conservation, an instructor tells a 3-minute personal story about witnessing pollution damage in a local creek, ending with "I didn't know then what I know now — and that's what this article explains."
  • In an ESL class preparing to read a news text about immigration, the instructor tells a suspenseful story about their own grandmother's journey, pre-teaching key vocabulary (border, permit, sponsor) in context.
  • A history teacher opens a unit on the Great Depression with a cliffhanger vignette about a family losing their farm, then asks students to read to find out what happened to families like theirs.
