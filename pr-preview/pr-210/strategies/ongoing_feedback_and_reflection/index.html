Ongoing Feedback and Reflection¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 8 claims (3 for, 2 mixed, 3 against) · 14 studies (9 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 9 of 14 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Ongoing feedback and reflection is a sustained arrangement rather than an event: feedback arrives at regular, predictable points, and each time it does, the learner is required to do something deliberate with it — record what it said, judge their own work against the same criteria first, decide what to change, and later check whether the change worked. Run over a term rather than a task, the pattern shifts responsibility: early cycles are dominated by the instructor's judgement, later ones by the learner's, with the instructor's role reducing to confirming or correcting a judgement the learner has already made.

Design Implications¶

The strategy's justification is Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick's argument that the point of feedback is not to correct the current work but to build the learner's capacity to evaluate their own — feedback that produces a better draft and no better self-assessment has spent the instructor's time to buy a one-off improvement. Structured reflection is the mechanism that converts one into the other, and its effectiveness depends heavily on the structure Reflective Practice Improves Outcomes When Structured [+M]. Unstructured reflection — "write about how the project went" — has a notably weaker and more inconsistent evidence base Reflection interventions in higher education have a medium positive average effect on learning that varies with duration, peer interaction and the reflective activity used [~M].

Self-assessment against explicit criteria is the specific form that works, because it gives the reflection something external to check against rather than leaving learners to introspect on their own competence Self Assessment Against Criteria Supports Self Regulated Learning [+M]. Without criteria, learners' self-judgements are poorly calibrated Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training [-M], and confident fluency is systematically mistaken for understanding Fluent Illusions Mislead Self Assessment [-M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A predictable rhythm — feedback at known points, so learners can plan around it rather than receive it as interruption
  • Explicit criteria the learner uses to judge their own work before seeing the instructor's judgement (Criteria Development)
  • Prompts that ask for specific evidence and a specific next action, not for feelings about the work (Metacognitive Reflection)
  • A persistent record across cycles — journal, portfolio, or log — so a learner can see whether the thing they committed to changed (Learning Journals)
  • Enough time in the course for several cycles; a single feedback-plus-reflection event is not this strategy
  • Goals the learner has set or accepted, since reflection without a target has nothing to measure against Goal setting improves performance [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Reflection required at fixed intervals without genuine content becomes a compliance genre: learners produce the expected register and learn nothing Reflection interventions in higher education have a medium positive average effect on learning that varies with duration, peer interaction and the reflective activity used [~M]
  • Grading reflections corrupts them — learners write what scores well rather than what is true, which is precisely the wrong direction for a self-assessment instrument [-M]
  • Learners lacking domain knowledge cannot judge their own work reliably, so early cycles must lean on instructor judgement Prior Knowledge Needed For Accurate Self Assessment [~M]
  • Feedback with no opportunity to act before the next feedback point breaks the loop and turns the reflection into commentary Feedback improves learning, with a medium average effect that varies widely by feedback type [-M]
  • Sustained cost to both parties; feedback fatigue on the instructor side and reflection fatigue on the learner's are the usual reasons the rhythm collapses mid-term [-M]
  • Written reflection disadvantages learners for whom writing is itself the barrier; the modality should not be the assessment [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Learning journal with fixed prompts — a short entry after each feedback point, structured by the same three questions each time (Learning Journals)
  • Portfolio with reflective commentary — learners select work and argue for what it demonstrates, which forces judgement against criteria (Portfolio Assessment)
  • Self-assess-then-compare — learner scores their work against the rubric, instructor scores independently, the two are compared and the gap discussed (Formative Self-Assessment)
  • Action-commitment logs — each cycle ends with one written commitment, and the next cycle opens by checking it
  • Conference-based — a short recurring one-to-one where the learner leads with their own assessment (Goal-Setting Conferences)
  • Fading structure — heavily scaffolded prompts early, reduced to open questions late, as calibration improves (Fading)

Target Learners¶

  • Learners on extended work — courses, projects, practica, apprenticeships — where there is time for several cycles
  • Learners in professional formation, where self-monitoring is itself a target competence
  • Learners with adequate domain grounding to make their self-judgement worth eliciting
  • Weaker fit for short courses, where the loop cannot run enough times to shift responsibility
  • Weaker fit for complete novices as a self-assessment device, though the feedback half still applies

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and adjusting one's own learning Self Assessment Improves Self Regulated Learning [+M]
  • Calibration — bringing confidence into line with actual performance
  • Improved performance on the work itself, via the feedback half of the pair Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance [+S]
  • Internalized criteria: knowing what good looks like without being told each time
  • Persistence, since visible cumulative progress is more motivating than isolated marks

Instructions¶

  1. Set the rhythm and publish it. Fix the feedback points at the start so both sides can plan; irregular feedback cannot anchor a reflection routine.
  2. Establish criteria before the first cycle. Develop or share the rubric, ideally with learners, so self-assessment has a referent (Criteria Development).
  3. Have learners self-assess first, every time. Before releasing your feedback, require their own judgement against the criteria — this is the step that builds calibration (Formative Self-Assessment).
  4. Release feedback and compare. Discuss where the two judgements differ rather than only what the work needs; the gap is the more informative object (Feedback).
  5. Require one written commitment. End each cycle with a single specific action for the next one, recorded where it can be found again (Learning Journals).
  6. Open the next cycle with last cycle's commitment. Check it explicitly. This is what stops reflection becoming a genre exercise.
  7. Keep the reflections ungraded. Assess the work; use the reflection as evidence for the conversation, not for the mark.
  8. Fade the scaffolding across the term. Move from supplied prompts to learner-generated questions as calibration improves (Fading).

Examples¶

Self-assess-then-compare in higher education: Students submit a rubric self-score with each assignment; the marker scores independently and returns both. Discussion focuses on criteria where the two disagreed, and disagreement shrinks measurably across a term.

Clinical practice logs: A trainee records a structured entry after each supervised session — what was attempted, what the supervisor observed, one thing to change — and the next session opens by checking the previous entry's commitment.

Writing portfolios: Learners assemble selected drafts with a commentary arguing what each demonstrates against the course criteria; the argument, not the selection, is the assessed reflection.

Fortnightly project check-ins: A fixed twenty-minute conversation in which the learner presents their own assessment of progress against their goals first, and the supervisor responds — inverting the usual order so the learner's judgement is the primary object.
