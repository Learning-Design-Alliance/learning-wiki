Peer Feedback Protocols¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q3 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer feedback protocols are structured routines — rubrics, sentence stems, staged review cycles, and role scripts — that govern how learners critique one another's work. The protocol converts the vague instruction "give feedback" into a repeatable procedure: examine the work against explicit criteria, identify specific strengths and gaps, suggest actionable revisions, and do so in language the receiver can act on.

Design Implications¶

Peer feedback works when it functions as formative assessment in the hands of learners, not as grading Formative assessment practices improve achievement. [+S]. The protocol is the load-bearing component: unstructured peer comments tend toward vague praise ("good job") or personal taste, while criterion-referenced protocols produce feedback that correlates with instructor feedback in quality and improves the quality of subsequent work Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when criteria and training are provided. [+S]. Giving feedback is often as valuable as receiving it — the reviewer must evaluate work against criteria, which deepens their own understanding of quality Peer learning benefits both giver and receiver. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit success criteria or a rubric the reviewer applies, ideally co-constructed or practiced with exemplars first
  • Training and rehearsal: learners need to see and critique sample feedback before reviewing real work
  • Sentence frames and role scripts that specify what to comment on (e.g., two strengths, one question, one suggestion)
  • Time for the receiver to act on feedback — a revision cycle, not just a read-through
  • Psychological safety norms, since peers evaluate work publicly
  • A staged protocol (e.g., describe → question → suggest) with sentence stems or prompts
  • Training and rehearsal in giving feedback before it counts — feedback quality improves with explicit instruction in critique
  • Time and structure for authors to act on the feedback, not just receive it

Constraints¶

  • Without training and criteria, peer feedback degrades into social politeness and unactionable praise [-M]
  • Receivers often discount peer feedback on high-stakes work, treating it as less credible than instructor feedback [~M] — pairing peer review with instructor spot-checks mitigates this
  • Reviewers cannot give useful feedback on content beyond their own expertise; protocols work best for structure, clarity, and criteria-visible features rather than deep domain accuracy [-M]
  • Anonymity reduces social bias but can strip context needed to interpret the work [~W]
  • Grading peers introduces status dynamics and reciprocity concerns ("you scratch my back") that distort comments [-M]; keep peer feedback ungraded or grade only the quality of feedback given
  • Feedback quality degrades when reviewers lack domain knowledge; novices may endorse flawed work or misidentify problems [~M] — pairing peer review with instructor moderation or exemplar calibration reduces this
  • Unstructured or purely social peer exchange produces negligible gains and can entrench errors [-M]
  • If feedback is graded or tied to social standing, reviewers inflate ratings and authors dismiss criticism [-M]; anonymity or low-stakes framing mitigates this
  • Protocols applied ritually, without follow-up revision time, become compliance theater with no learning benefit [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise-Question-Polish: lightweight stems for younger or novice reviewers
  • Calibrated Peer Review: reviewers first score an instructor-anchored exemplar; only calibrated reviews count, used in systems like Calibrated Peer Review and Peergrade
  • Gallery walk / silent critique: written sticky-note feedback on displayed work, reducing social pressure
  • Reciprocal pairs: stable partners alternate reviewer roles across drafts, building trust over time
  • Author-driven: the receiver poses specific questions ("Does my argument in paragraph 2 follow?"), focusing reviewer attention
  • Calibrated review: learners rate sample artifacts and compare their judgments to expert ratings before reviewing peers (used by Peergrade and Calibrated Peer Review)
  • Reciprocal pairs vs. rotating groups: stable pairs build trust; rotation widens exposure to varied work
  • Written vs. oral protocols: oral formats (critical friends tuning protocols) suit complex, in-progress work; written formats suit drafts and asynchronous settings
  • Anonymous vs. attributed: anonymity increases candor; attribution builds accountability and dialogue

Target Learners¶

  • Effective across K–12 through higher education, with strongest effects in secondary and tertiary writing contexts Peer assessment improves academic performance compared with no assessment or teacher-only assessment. [+S]
  • Developing writers benefit from seeing peers' approaches and receiving multiple readings of a draft Collaborative writing improves text quality. [+M]
  • Novice reviewers gain the most from the giving side; experts may find protocol steps redundant [~W]
  • Secondary and higher-education learners with enough metacognitive maturity to critique work against criteria [~M]
  • Less effective for very young learners without scaffolded sentence frames and teacher moderation [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Quality criteria internalization: learning what "good work" looks like by applying criteria to real cases
  • Revision and self-regulation: using external feedback to plan improvements Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, what next?" improves learning. [+S]
  • Evaluative judgment: building the capacity to assess one's own work independently
  • Quality improvement of drafts and products (writing, design, code, arguments)
  • Communication and argumentation skills through justified critique

Instructions¶

  1. Establish criteria: share or co-construct a rubric and study annotated exemplars at different quality levels (Assessment)
  2. Model the protocol: the instructor reviews a sample work aloud, demonstrating how to apply criteria and phrase actionable comments (Coaching)
  3. Pair or group reviewers and exchange drafts; reviewers apply the protocol stems (e.g., two strengths, one question, one concrete suggestion) within a time limit
  4. Receivers paraphrase the feedback back and select which comments to act on, making revision decisions explicit
  5. Revise: receivers produce a new draft, closing the loop so feedback demonstrably matters
  6. Debrief the feedback itself: which comments were most useful and why, building reviewers' skill for the next cycle

Related Strategies¶

  • Formative assessment — peer feedback is formative assessment distributed among learners
  • Collaborative learning — protocols structure the peer interaction that makes collaboration productive
  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the principle protocols operationalize: feedback must specify what to do next
  • Rubric-Based Self-Assessment — the same criteria applied to one's own work; peer review builds toward self-assessment
  • Critique Protocols — closely related routines (e.g., tuning protocols) for group examination of a single work

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu/) — web system where students first calibrate on instructor-scored exemplars, then review peers' writing in large STEM courses.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — anonymous distribution of student work with rubric-driven comment fields and feedback-quality ratings by receivers.
  • Two-stars-and-a-wish in primary writing workshops — reviewers name two strengths and one wish, keeping feedback criterion-anchored and age-appropriate.
  • Writing workshop protocols (e.g., National Writing Project response protocols) — author reads aloud, peers respond with "I noticed… I wondered…", author speaks last.
  • Critical Friends Groups tuning protocols (National School Reform Faculty) — staged presentation, clarifying questions, and warm/cool feedback on work in progress.
  • Two Stars and a Wish — primary-classroom protocol: two specific strengths plus one suggestion, constraining young reviewers to balanced, concrete comments.
  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://calibratedpeerreview.org) — learners calibrate against expert-scored samples before reviewing peers, addressing the novice-reviewer constraint.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio — platforms that anonymize submissions, distribute reviews, and flag low-quality feedback for instructor moderation.
