Repeat Key Points¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 8 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 8 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Repeating key points means deliberately restating the most important ideas of a lesson multiple times and through multiple representations — verbal restatement, visual display, worked example, student paraphrase — so that learners receive several spaced, varied exposures to each core concept. The variation matters as much as the repetition: each restatement should re-encode the idea in a different form or context rather than merely duplicating it.

Design Implications¶

Repetition increases the probability that a key idea is encoded, consolidated, and retrievable, but simple verbatim repetition yields diminishing returns; spaced and varied restatements produce stronger retention than massed, identical ones Spaced repetition improves long-term retention compared with massed repetition. [+S]. Combining verbal and visual representations engages dual channels and supports richer encoding than either alone Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. Emphasis and repetition also direct learner attention to what matters, but only when the emphasized content is genuinely central Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of emphasized content. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Careful lesson planning to identify the small set of key concepts worth repeating; repetition of everything dilutes the signal
  • Varied language and representations for each restatement — plain language, analogy, visual, example (Analogies, Chunking)
  • A structure that spaces the repetitions across the lesson or unit (opening preview, mid-lesson application, closing summary) rather than clustering them

Constraints¶

  • Verbatim, massed repetition is boring and can reduce attention and perceived relevance [~M] — learners disengage from content they have already heard once
  • Repetition without variation can create fluency illusions: learners mistake ease of recognition for understanding and stop studying prematurely [~M]
  • Effectiveness depends on learners noticing the repetition as a signal of importance; buried or unmarked restatements are often missed [~W]
  • Students with weak self-monitoring may not recognize when they have not processed a repeated point and fail to ask for another pass [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher-led restatement at planned points in a lesson (Direct instruction style)
  • Student-generated repetition: learners restate key points to a peer, which adds Self-Explanation benefits to the exposure
  • Distributed repetition across sessions (opening review of prior key points) rather than within a single lesson
  • Signaled repetition: explicit framing ("this is the third way to see this idea") to mark importance

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with speed-of-processing or working-memory challenges who need additional exposures to consolidate an idea Chunking reduces working-memory load and supports processing of complex content. [+M]
  • Novices, who lack prior knowledge structures to anchor a single hearing of new content
  • Less necessary for advanced learners, for whom repeated restatement of familiar content is redundant and can feel patronizing [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of core facts, definitions, and procedures Spaced repetition improves long-term retention compared with massed repetition. [+S]
  • Comprehension of key concepts through multiple representations
  • Support for learners who need processing time

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the 2–4 key points of the lesson; plan a distinct representation for each repetition (Chunking, Advance Organizers)
  2. Preview the key points at the start of the lesson in plain language (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  3. Revisit each key point mid-lesson in a different modality — visual, example, or demonstration — rather than restating verbatim
  4. Have students restate the key points to each other or in writing, converting repetition into retrieval practice (Check-Ins, Annotating)
  5. Close by summarizing the key points and flagging where each will recur in future lessons

Related Strategies¶

  • Chunking — grouping content so that what gets repeated is a coherent unit, not fragments
  • Clear Structure — a predictable lesson structure gives repetitions natural, recognizable slots
  • Spaced practice scheduling — the research basis for distributing repetitions over time
  • Switch to written notes when verbal communication with a hearing-impaired patron breaks down
  • STeLLA® teaching strategies for explaining, identifying, and engaging science concepts

Examples¶

  • During a science lesson, a teacher states the key idea, shows a diagram of it, works an example using it, and has students explain it to a partner — four varied exposures in one lesson. Resource: Speed of Processing micro-credential, Friday Institute at NC State.
  • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) — video explanations restate key steps verbally and on-screen, followed by practice items that repeat the same concept in varied problem forms.
  • Opening "warm-up" problems in published curricula such as Illustrative Mathematics (https://illustrativemathematics.org) deliberately re-expose prior key concepts in new contexts at the start of each lesson.
