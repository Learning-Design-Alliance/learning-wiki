Spark Engagement with Student-Generated Questions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 4 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Students formulate their own questions about a topic and pursue investigations to answer them, rather than responding only to teacher-posed prompts. The strategy converts curiosity into an instructional engine: question generation precedes and drives inquiry, giving students ownership over both the direction and the purpose of their learning.

Design Implications¶

Student question generation increases engagement and investment because learners pursue questions they find personally meaningful, and autonomy over learning goals supports intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. The quality of student questions is itself learnable — explicit instruction in question formulation (open vs. closed, testable vs. speculative) markedly improves the questions students produce [~M]. Question generation also primes productive cognitive conflict: a question students cannot yet answer creates a knowledge gap that inquiry then resolves Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A classroom norm that treats student questions as legitimate curriculum material, not interruptions
  • Explicit scaffolds for question formulation (e.g., the Question Formulation Technique: produce, improve, prioritize questions) — unstructured "ask anything" prompts yield low-quality questions [~M]
  • Curricular flexibility to follow promising questions, or a deliberate mapping of student questions onto required content
  • A mechanism for students to pursue answers: research time, data collection, or structured class discussion

Constraints¶

  • Unguided inquiry from student questions can overload novices, who lack the background knowledge to formulate productive or answerable questions [~S] — minimal-guidance findings suggest novices need question scaffolds, not free exploration
  • Student questions may diverge from mandated curriculum goals; forcing alignment can drain the autonomy that makes the strategy motivating Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [-M]
  • Time-intensive: supporting diverse student investigations requires more class time and teacher facilitation skill than teacher-directed instruction
  • Students accustomed to answer-oriented schooling may initially generate shallow or test-anticipating questions; the skill develops over repeated cycles

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full inquiry projects: student questions define multi-week investigations (project- or problem-based formats)
  • Lesson-level hooks: students generate questions at the start of a unit; the teacher uses them to structure subsequent instruction
  • Question Formulation Technique (QFT): a bounded 30–50 minute routine for producing, refining, and prioritizing questions without requiring full inquiry follow-through
  • Discussion seeding: student questions replace teacher questions as the agenda for class discussion or argumentation activities

Target Learners¶

  • Effective across K–12 and higher education; particularly valuable for students who have experienced disempowerment in teacher-directed settings, since valuing their questions signals epistemic authority and supports belonging Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • Students with some topical background knowledge — they generate more productive questions than complete novices [~M]
  • Reluctant learners, for whom personally chosen questions provide a reason to engage that teacher-assigned tasks may lack Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Engagement and motivation: building investment in learning through personally meaningful purposes
  • Inquiry and research skills: formulating, refining, and investigating answerable questions
  • Critical thinking and epistemic practices: evaluating what makes a question testable or productive
  • Deeper conceptual understanding when questions drive active learning investigations Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Prompt a topic or phenomenon — present an artifact, event, or problem connected to the unit, and activate what students already know (Activation).
  2. Generate questions — students produce as many questions as possible without evaluation, then improve them (convert closed to open, make them testable) and prioritize a shortlist.
  3. Plan investigation — map prioritized questions to available resources and methods; where questions exceed the unit's scope, name that explicitly and connect them to future learning.
  4. Investigate — students pursue answers through research, experiments, text study, or structured class discussion, with the teacher providing scaffolds and assessment checkpoints rather than answers.
  5. Report and reflect — students share findings, evaluate which questions were answered and which remain open, and generate new questions, making the cycle recursive.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — surfacing what students know sharpens the questions they can ask
  • Case-Based Learning — cases provide rich phenomena that provoke student questions
  • Think-Pair-Share — a low-stakes structure for generating and refining questions with peers

Examples¶

  • Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute) — a published routine in which students produce, improve, and prioritize questions on a "QFocus" prompt; used across K–12 and higher education to shift question-asking from teacher to student.
  • Driving questions in project-based learning — e.g., in Expeditionary Learning (EL Education) schools, students help shape the guiding questions that anchor multi-week expeditions, then investigate them through fieldwork and expert interviews.
  • Science classrooms — students generate testable questions about a local environmental issue (e.g., water quality in a nearby stream) and design investigations to answer them, connecting inquiry to authentic audiences.
