Learning Students Names¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Learning students' names is a foundational relational practice in which the instructor commits to knowing and using every learner's name — typically within the first weeks of a course. It is carried out through deliberate techniques (name tents, photos with rosters, name-learning games, repeated use during discussion) and signals that each student is seen as an individual rather than an anonymous attendee.

Design Implications¶

Using students' names lowers the social threshold for participation: students who expect to be called by name are more likely to speak, and instructors who can name students can call on them equitably rather than relying on volunteers Active learning improves exam performance, and cold-calling is a key mechanism. [+S]. Name use also contributes to belonging, which is itself linked to persistence and achievement Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]. The practice costs little but must be genuine — mispronouncing or avoiding names after promising to learn them can damage credibility.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A roster with photos (most LMSs supply these) reviewed before or during the first sessions
  • A low-stakes mechanism for name exposure: name tents or placards, introductions, or seating charts
  • Repeated retrieval practice by the instructor — names are learned the same way any content is, through spaced recall, not a single reading of the roster
  • Correct pronunciation, including asking students directly rather than guessing

Constraints¶

  • Very large classes (100+) make full name learning impractical; partial strategies (learning a subset, using name tents in discussion sections) partially preserve the effect but dilute the individual-recognition signal [-W]
  • Instructors with poor face-name memory may rely on crutches (seating position) that fail when students move, producing visible failure moments that undermine rapport [~W]
  • Online asynchronous courses remove the natural cue environment; name use must be engineered into discussion feedback and video messages or it will not happen [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Name tents/cards — visible names for the first weeks; standard in discussion-heavy formats
  • Photo-roster study — reviewing LMS photo rosters before class and quizzing oneself; effective for large lectures
  • Name-learning activities — first-day games where students introduce each other, giving the instructor multiple retrieval exposures
  • Cold-calling with names — pairing name use with equitable calling practices; students report higher engagement even when the experience is mildly stressful [+M]
  • Digital proxies — using names in discussion-board replies, personalized feedback, and video check-ins in online courses

Target Learners¶

  • Students in discussion-based or seminar courses, where participation depends on feeling known [+M]
  • First-generation and marginalized students, for whom instructor recognition disproportionately signals belonging Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]
  • Large-lecture students, who otherwise experience the course as anonymous; even partial name learning measurably improves course climate ratings [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective/climate goals: establishing a safe environment for intellectual risk-taking
  • Participation and engagement: enabling equitable cold-calling and class discussion
  • Formative assessment: making it possible to target check-ins and questions to specific students

Instructions¶

  1. Obtain photo rosters before the first session and study them; quiz yourself using spaced retrieval.
  2. Use name tents or introductions on day one (Check-In) and record pronunciation notes.
  3. Greet students by name at the door or at the start of each session during the first weeks.
  4. Use names when calling on students and when giving feedback, distributing turns equitably rather than taking volunteers only.
  5. Re-test yourself periodically; if a name slips, ask rather than avoid the student.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-In — routine individual check-ins give the instructor repeated name-retrieval opportunities
  • Cold-calling — name knowledge is the precondition for equitable, non-volunteer questioning
  • Community-building norms — name learning is usually the first concrete move in a broader climate strategy

Examples¶

  • Elon University's "Name Game" first-day practice — instructors open courses with structured introductions and commit to full-name use by week two; widely used in faculty development workshops on inclusive teaching.
  • Large-lecture photo-roster study — instructors of 200+ student courses at several research universities review LMS photo rosters before each class for the first month; documented in teaching-center guidance (e.g., University of Michigan CRLT).
  • Online discussion personalization — instructors address students by first name in every discussion reply and feedback comment, substituting for in-person recognition in asynchronous courses.
