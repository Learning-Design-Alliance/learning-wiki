Perspective-taking and Empathy¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 review), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Perspective-taking and empathy involve the instructor deliberately considering each student's viewpoint, circumstances, and challenges, and communicating in ways that signal genuine understanding and non-judgment. In practice this means asking about students' interests and backgrounds, listening as if one might be wrong, modeling perspective-taking aloud, and using solution-focused rather than blame-focused problem-solving when difficulties arise.

Design Implications¶

Teacher–student relationship quality is consistently associated with engagement, achievement, and reduced dropout, with the strongest effects for at-risk and disengaged learners Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with higher engagement and achievement. [+M]. Perspective-taking is the mechanism by which those relationships are built and repaired: it reduces attribution errors (interpreting a missed deadline as laziness rather than a caregiving obligation) and increases the accuracy of instructional decisions. Empathy must be paired with structure — warmth without clear expectations does not produce the same benefits Affective teacher–student relationships relate to engagement and achievement, but effects vary by student characteristics. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Regular, low-stakes opportunities to learn about individual students (interest inventories, check-ins, conferences)
  • Commitment to Active Listening and non-judgmental interaction
  • Explicit modeling of perspective-taking language ("I can see why you might read it that way…") so students acquire the practice themselves, connecting to Building Empathy

Constraints¶

  • Requires sustained time and attention; difficult to maintain under high stress, large class sizes, or limited resources [-W]
  • Perspective-taking can backfire when it becomes projection — assuming one knows the student's view without checking produces misattuned responses [-W]
  • Perceived as inauthentic or performative, empathy efforts can erode rather than build trust [-W]
  • Empathy alone, without accountability and clear structure, does not improve outcomes and may reduce challenge Affective teacher–student relationships relate to engagement and achievement, but effects vary by student characteristics. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Brief structured routines (greetings at the door, two-minute check-ins) versus deeper one-on-one conferences
  • Written channels (reflection journals, exit tickets) for students who do not disclose verbally
  • Whole-class perspective-taking activities (structured Class Discussion, role reversal) versus individual relationship-building

Target Learners¶

  • Students who feel marginalized or at risk of disengagement benefit most from strong teacher relationships Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with higher engagement and achievement. [+M]
  • Students with low academic self-confidence, for whom teacher support sustains persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Educators themselves, when perspective-taking is modeled as a professional practice rather than only directed at students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Socioemotional goals: building trust, belonging, and classroom climate
  • Communication goals: accurate interpretation of student behavior and needs
  • Metacognitive and relational goals: students internalizing perspective-taking as a peer and professional skill

Instructions¶

  1. Gather perspective data early: interest inventories, brief conferences, or Check-ins that surface students' goals and constraints.
  2. Practice Active Listening — paraphrase the student's position before responding, and ask clarifying questions rather than assuming intent.
  3. Reframe problem-solving as joint and solution-focused: name the obstacle from the student's point of view, then negotiate next steps without blame.
  4. Model perspective-taking publicly during Class Discussion ("Let me try to state the opposing view fairly first"), making the skill observable.
  5. Verify your inferences — check whether your read of a student's situation is accurate before acting on it.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Listening — the core conversational technique through which perspective-taking is enacted
  • Building Empathy — the broader principle this strategy operationalizes in teacher–student interactions
  • Pair increased academic demand with increased teacher support when raising instructional rigor
  • Use small shared-meal conversations among teachers, students, and a facilitator to surface student perspectives on assessment and strengthen relationships

Examples¶

  • Gehlbach et al.'s "birds of similar feathers" intervention — teachers completed a perspective-taking exercise emphasizing similarities with students, improving teacher–student relationships and achievement: https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000042
  • Restorative-practice check-ins — opening class with a brief round in which students share how they are arriving, followed by non-judgmental teacher responses.
  • Writing conferences — a teacher asks "Walk me through what happened when you got stuck" before offering feedback, surfacing the student's process rather than judging the product.
