Mystery Motivator¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

A Mystery Motivator is a behavioral reinforcement technique in which learners can earn a reward for meeting a specified criterion (e.g., completing work, staying on task), but the reward itself — or whether it will be delivered at all — is hidden until the criterion is met. The teacher seals a reward in an envelope, covers a hidden prize chart, or uses a spinner revealed only after the target behavior occurs. The unpredictability of the payoff is the mechanism: variable, uncertain reinforcement sustains responding better than predictable delivery.

Design Implications¶

Mystery Motivators apply the variable-ratio/variable-interval logic of reinforcement schedules to classroom incentives: uncertain reward delivery produces more persistent behavior than certain, predictable delivery [+S]. They are typically embedded in a larger contingency system — a stated criterion, a visible tracking mechanism (e.g., a grid the teacher marks), and a revealed reward — so learners always know what earns the payoff even when they don't know what the payoff is. Because the reward is extrinsic, design should minimize the risk of undermining intrinsic interest in the task itself [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clearly specified, observable target behavior or performance criterion, communicated in advance
  • A reward menu with genuinely appealing, varied options (survey learners; a "mystery" that is unwanted is simply absent reinforcement)
  • A visible tracking display so learners can monitor their own progress toward the criterion
  • A consistent, immediate reveal-and-delivery routine once the criterion is met
  • A plan for fading: shifting from daily to intermittent checks, then to natural reinforcers

Constraints¶

  • Rewards contingent only on participation rather than performance criteria can reduce intrinsic motivation for inherently interesting tasks [~M] — the criterion must require genuine effort or achievement, not mere compliance
  • Effectiveness degrades quickly if the revealed reward is low-value or if delivery is inconsistent; a broken contingency is worse than none, because it teaches that criteria don't matter [-M]
  • Novelty wears off; the same mystery loses potency within weeks without rotating reward menus or criteria [-W]
  • Poorly suited to complex, creative, or collaborative goals where a single observable criterion distorts what learners attend to [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual: one learner's chart with a hidden reward cell, used in check-in routines
  • Whole-class group contingency: the class earns the reveal only if a collective criterion is met (e.g., 90% on-task), leveraging peer influence
  • Random-criterion variant: the teacher pre-selects random "magic" squares or times; the reward is delivered only if behavior is on target at those unpredictable points
  • Academic variant: criterion tied to performance (e.g., math facts mastered) rather than conduct

Target Learners¶

  • Students with persistent off-task or disruptive behavior who have not responded to routine reinforcement [+M]
  • Young learners (K–8) and learners with ADHD or behavioral support plans, where immediate, salient consequences are most needed [+M]
  • Less appropriate for learners already intrinsically engaged, for whom added extrinsic incentives may crowd out interest [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral regulation: on-task behavior, work completion, transitions
  • Fluency-building goals where high response rates matter (math facts, reading practice)
  • Habit establishment that can later be maintained by fading to natural consequences

Instructions¶

  1. Define one observable target behavior and a specific, attainable criterion; state it explicitly (Clear Structure).
  2. Survey learners and build a reward menu; conceal the selection in an envelope, covered chart, or randomizer.
  3. Set up a visible tracking display the learner or class can see fill in (Check-In).
  4. At the designated time, evaluate the criterion; if met, reveal and deliver the reward immediately with specific labeled praise naming the behavior.
  5. Fade systematically: lengthen intervals, raise criteria, and shift toward self-monitoring and natural reinforcers.

Related Strategies¶

  • Token Economies — the broader point-system framework a Mystery Motivator often sits inside
  • Group Contingencies — the whole-class variant that Mystery Motivators frequently implement
  • Precision Requests — complementary behavioral strategy for managing noncompliance
  • Use a themed token-reward system (moon rocks) to motivate on-task behavior during tutoring

Examples¶

  • The Tough Kid Book (Rhode, Jenson & Reavis) — the original classroom package: a laminated Mystery Motivator chart with hidden ink-revealed reward squares, used with individual students or whole classes.
  • ClassDojo-based variant — a teacher hides a class reward behind a "mystery box" slide revealed only if the class meets a daily point criterion.
  • Math intervention (Theodore et al., 2004) — elementary students earned a mystery reward for meeting math-work completion criteria, improving academic engagement and accuracy.
