Emphasize Reasoning over Answers¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 14 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 4 of 14 report an effect size

Description¶

De-emphasizing correct answers and spending more time on reasoning shifts the instructional focus from the product of problem solving to the process. Teachers elicit and examine students' thinking — initial ideas, solution paths, and errors — before or instead of revealing the correct answer. Common enactments include presenting worked examples containing deliberate errors for students to diagnose, asking students to share approaches before confirming answers, and grading or giving feedback on the quality of justification rather than only on correctness.

Design Implications¶

Attending to reasoning converts answer-checking into Self-Explanation, which reliably improves conceptual understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. Diagnosing flawed worked examples (erroneous examples) builds conceptual knowledge by forcing learners to discriminate correct from incorrect reasoning Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M], and errors held with high confidence are especially productive learning moments when surfaced and corrected High-confidence errors improve retention. [+M]. Feedback should therefore target the task and process levels — the strategy chosen and why — rather than the person or the final answer Feedback most effective at task and process levels. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Tasks with multiple defensible solution paths or interpretable errors, not single-step recall items
  • A classroom culture where errors are treated as information; teachers must respond to wrong answers by probing the reasoning rather than immediately correcting
  • Assessment and grading practices that reward explanation and justification, or the strategy will be undermined by what students perceive as counting
  • Teacher facility with facilitating discussion and with Coaching moves that press on thinking ("Why did you choose that operation?")

Constraints¶

  • Undermined when high-stakes assessments reward speed and correct answers only — students rationally optimize for what is graded [-M]
  • Time-intensive: examining several reasoning paths takes longer than confirming one answer, creating pressure to revert to answer-checking under pacing constraints [-M]
  • Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who may lack the schema needed to evaluate reasoning at all and benefit more from direct modeling first [~M]
  • Can confuse novices if multiple student strategies are compared without teacher synthesis; unstructured sharing of flawed reasoning can propagate misconceptions [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Erroneous examples: present a fully worked but incorrect solution; students find and fix the error, then explain why it was wrong
  • Answer-delayed discussion: collect answers to a conceptual question, have students defend or revise them in Class Discussion before the teacher confirms
  • Reasoning-first norms: teacher opens with "Tell me the first thing you thought about," explicitly banning immediate answer-giving
  • Two approaches compared: display two student solutions side by side and ask which is more efficient or generalizable — a Comparing Cases move that supports abstraction Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge who can meaningfully evaluate solution steps Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M]
  • Students who experience mathematics as answer-production and have developed unproductive beliefs about speed and correctness; the strategy broadens participation beyond fast answerers
  • Novices with minimal prior knowledge need worked modeling before error analysis becomes productive [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding of why procedures work Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]
  • Metacognitive monitoring: evaluating one's own solution path for validity
  • Mathematical argumentation and justification
  • Process-oriented learning rather than outcome-chasing Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Select or design a task with a rich, diagnosable solution space; prepare one or more erroneous worked examples.
  2. Pose the problem and elicit initial thinking before confirming any answer — "I want to hear your approach, not the answer yet."
  3. Have students articulate, compare, and critique solution paths in Class Discussion or small groups, using Argumentation norms ("agree/disagree, and say why").
  4. Guide error analysis: locate the faulty step, explain why it fails, and repair it.
  5. Synthesize: name the key reasoning moves across approaches so novices leave with a coherent model, not just a collection of strategies.
  6. Give feedback on the reasoning — strategy choice, justification quality, error detection — not just correctness Feedback most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  7. Align grading rubrics so explanation and justification carry explicit weight.

Related Strategies¶

  • Emphasizing reasoning pairs naturally with error-based learning: diagnosing incorrect worked examples is its most structured enactment
  • Works alongside formative assessment practices, since eliciting reasoning is formative evidence gathering
  • Question students about why a correct answer was chosen, rather than why an error was made

Examples¶

  • Erroneous-example routine: a teacher projects a solved equation with a sign error and asks, "Find the step where this went wrong, and convince your neighbor." Students must locate and justify the error before any correct solution appears.
  • "My favorite no": a widely used middle-school routine (popularized by Levi & Teaching Channel) in which the teacher collects anonymous wrong answers, displays one, and has the class identify what is right about the reasoning before fixing the error.
  • Cognitively Guided Instruction — teachers elicit and build instruction around students' own solution strategies rather than demonstrating the canonical algorithm first.
  • Case-Based Learning — in professional education, discussion of reasoning and diagnosis is the point; the "answer" is often deliberately withheld or contested.
