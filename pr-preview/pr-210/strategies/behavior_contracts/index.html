Behavior Contracts¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

A behavior contract is a negotiated written agreement specifying which behavior the learner will perform, under what conditions, and what consequences (rewards or privileges) will follow. The learner typically participates in drafting the terms, and both parties sign. Contracts operationalize contingency management: they make expectations and reinforcement contingencies explicit, monitorable, and mutually owned rather than implicit and teacher-imposed.

Design Implications¶

Behavior contracts apply Behaviorism — specifically contingency management and reinforcement principles — to classroom conduct and work habits [+S]. Their distinctive contribution over teacher-administered reinforcement is procedural: negotiation gives learners a voice in setting goals and consequences, which increases perceived autonomy and commitment Autonomy support improves intrinsic motivation. [+M]. Contracts work best when target behaviors are specific, observable, and achievable within short time frames, and when the reward follows the behavior promptly.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A precisely defined, observable target behavior (e.g., "completes the first three problems of independent work," not "tries harder")
  • Negotiated terms: the learner helps choose the goal, the reward, and the monitoring method
  • A reliable, low-effort tracking system the learner can see (chart, checklist, app)
  • Consistent delivery of the agreed consequence, with a short delay between behavior and reward
  • A review point and a plan for fading the contract once the behavior is established

Constraints¶

  • Externally administered rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks the learner already finds interesting Autonomy support improves intrinsic motivation. [-M] — contracts are better suited to behaviors learners would not otherwise perform
  • Contracts negotiated under coercion or imposed without learner input function as surveillance and often provoke reactance [-M]
  • Effects frequently fade when the contract is withdrawn unless reinforcement is faded gradually and the behavior acquires natural consequences [-M]
  • Poorly calibrated goals (too hard, too vague) produce failure cycles that damage rather than build compliance and self-efficacy [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual contracts target one learner's specific behavior; most common in special education and behavior-intervention plans
  • Group or class contracts establish shared norms (e.g., discussion rules) with collective consequences
  • Home–school contracts coordinate parents and teachers on the same contingency system
  • Self-contracting shifts monitoring to the learner, bridging toward Self-Regulated Learning as the contract becomes a self-set goal with self-administered consequences

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with persistent off-task, disruptive, or avoidance behaviors who have not responded to general classroom management [+S]
  • Younger learners and those with limited self-regulation, for whom external contingencies scaffold self-control [~M]
  • Less appropriate for motivated learners performing well: added extrinsic incentives can crowd out existing intrinsic interest [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and executive-function goals: attendance, task initiation, homework completion, on-task time
  • Work-habit and procedural fluency goals rather than conceptual understanding
  • Self-regulation development when contracts are progressively self-managed and faded

Instructions¶

  1. Define the target behavior in observable, countable terms; baseline it for a few days.
  2. Negotiate the contract with the learner: goal, reward menu, tracking method, and duration (Check-Ins support the negotiation conversation).
  3. Set a goal the learner can meet on the first attempt — early success builds momentum.
  4. Track performance visibly and deliver the agreed consequence promptly and consistently.
  5. Review at the agreed checkpoint; raise the criterion gradually and fade tangible rewards toward natural consequences and self-monitoring.

Related Strategies¶

  • Token economies — a broader classroom contingency system of which a contract is an individualized instance
  • Check-Ins — the regular conversation that supports negotiation and review of contract terms
  • Achievable micro-goals — the goal-setting logic contracts should embed

Examples¶

  • A middle-school teacher and a student with chronic incomplete homework sign a two-week contract: homework turned in on time earns 10 minutes of free reading choice; the chart is reviewed every Friday and the reward fades in week three.
  • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) programs, widely used in PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions & Supports) schools (https://www.pbis.org), pair a daily point card with a brief adult check-in — effectively a lightweight, recurring behavior contract.
