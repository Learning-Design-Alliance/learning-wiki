Mini-Lessons on Features¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 13 studies (5 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 13 report an effect size

Description¶

A mini-lesson on features is a brief (5–15 minute), tightly focused instructional episode targeting a single, nameable craft feature of writing — e.g., how a strong title works, how sentence variety controls rhythm, how evidence is introduced and cited. Each lesson typically follows a consistent arc: name the feature, show it in a mentor text or Demonstration, then have students try it immediately in their own drafts. Mini-lessons are sequenced across a unit so that features accumulate into a repertoire rather than arriving as a one-time lecture.

Design Implications¶

Mini-lessons work because they isolate one skill at a time, keeping working-memory demands low and making the target feature salient against the complexity of whole-text writing Chunking reduces working-memory load. [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on the immediate application step: instruction on a feature followed by same-day drafting practice outperforms instruction alone, consistent with meta-analytic findings that explicit, strategy-focused writing instruction improves writing quality Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of writing instruction [+S]. Asking students to find or explain the feature in mentor texts adds a Self-Explanation step that deepens conceptual understanding of why the feature works, not just what it is Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A curated set of mentor texts (published or student-written) that display the target feature clearly
  • A predictable lesson arc: name → show → try, with time reserved for immediate drafting practice (Practice)
  • Ongoing student writing in progress, so each mini-lesson has a live place to land
  • A planned sequence of features aligned to the unit's genre and assessment criteria

Constraints¶

  • Fragmenting writing into isolated features can crowd out attention to ideas and meaning; students may produce technically polished but hollow prose if features are taught as ornaments rather than as serving purpose [~M]
  • Whole-class mini-lessons mismatch learners who have already mastered the feature or lack the prerequisite skill; the expertise-reversal effect means redundant instruction for proficient writers wastes time or even interferes [~M]
  • Time-consuming to prepare well: each lesson needs a genuine mentor example, not a contrived one
  • If mini-lessons are not revisited, one-shot exposure to a feature rarely sticks; features need spaced re-teaching and conferring follow-up Spaced repetition improves retention. [+M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class launch: teacher delivers the mini-lesson to all students before writing time (classic workshop model)
  • Needs-based small group: re-teach a feature only to students whose drafts show the gap, using draft-based data
  • Student-selected: students choose from a menu of feature mini-lessons (recorded videos or stations) based on their own revision goals
  • Mentor-text inquiry: instead of direct telling, students examine contrasting mentor examples and name the feature themselves — a comparing-cases variant that supports abstraction Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers in upper elementary through college who have drafts in progress and can apply a feature the same day
  • Particularly valuable for students who write at length but with limited craft range — the feature focus gives them a concrete next move
  • Less beneficial as whole-class instruction for advanced writers, who need individualized goals rather than uniform feature coverage [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Craft and style: sentence variety, imagery, word choice, titles, leads
  • Organization and structure: introductions, transitions, paragraphing
  • Genre conventions: citing evidence, integrating quotations, audience-appropriate tone
  • Revision behavior: giving students named features to hunt for in their own drafts builds revision strategies

Instructions¶

  1. Name the feature in student-friendly language and connect it to the unit's writing purpose (Direct Instruction).
  2. Show it working: display the feature in one or two mentor texts, annotating what the writer did and why (Demonstration); contrasting a strong and weak version sharpens discrimination.
  3. Debrief the why: ask students to explain the effect of the feature on the reader, prompting Self-Explanation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].
  4. Try it now: students apply the feature to their own draft for 3–5 minutes (Practice), while the teacher confers with individuals.
  5. Share and revisit: one or two students read their attempt aloud; the feature reappears in revision checklists and future conferences so instruction is spaced, not one-shot Spaced repetition improves retention. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Mentor Texts — the source material mini-lessons draw from; a mini-lesson is often the analysis of a mentor text made explicit
  • Writing Conferences — the individualized follow-on where a mini-lesson feature is re-taught one-on-one
  • Model Texts — full exemplars from which features are extracted for mini-lesson treatment

Examples¶

  • Units of Study in Opinion, Information, and Narrative Writing (Calkins et al., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project) — each session opens with a 10-minute mini-lesson on a named craft feature, followed by independent writing with conferring (Heinemann).
  • A mini-lesson on introductions: the teacher shows two published essay openings, annotates how each establishes stakes, students explain the difference, then revise their own opening paragraphs in the last five minutes of the lesson.
  • A mini-lesson on evidence integration: students compare a dropped quotation with one introduced and analyzed in a mentor paragraph, then revise one quotation in their own draft using the "quote sandwich" move.
