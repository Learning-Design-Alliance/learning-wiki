Peer Revision¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q3 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer revision (also called peer review or peer feedback) asks learners to exchange drafts of their work, critique one another against explicit criteria, and revise in response. It operates in two directions at once: reviewers deepen their understanding by analyzing others' work against quality criteria, while authors receive multiple perspectives on their drafts before final submission.

Design Implications¶

Peer revision works because evaluating someone else's work against criteria forces learners to internalize quality standards, and because feedback from peers arrives earlier and in greater volume than instructor feedback alone can provide Peer assessment can be as reliable as instructor assessment when criteria and training are well designed. [+M]. The strategy is a form of Collaborative Learning that also serves Assessment for Learning: the feedback loop is the learning mechanism, not an add-on to it. Structured protocols and reviewer training are essential — unstructured "I liked your essay" comments produce negligible gains Peer assessment improves when reviewers are trained and given explicit criteria. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria or a rubric that reviewers apply, so feedback targets quality dimensions rather than personal taste
  • Reviewer training or modeling — e.g., the instructor critiques a sample draft aloud before students review each other
  • Time and expectation that authors will actually revise; if revision is not required, feedback becomes decorative
  • Psychological safety: anonymity or small stable groups reduce defensive reactions

Constraints¶

  • Reviewer accuracy is imperfect: novices systematically over- or under-rate, and agreement with expert grades is moderate at best Peer assessment reliability improves with rubrics and training but never reaches perfect agreement with experts. [~M] — use peer feedback formatively, not as the sole grading basis
  • Feedback quality drops when reviewers lack domain knowledge needed to diagnose problems, not just spot them
  • Power dynamics and friendship effects inflate ratings when reviews are non-anonymous
  • Low-stakes social contexts can produce uniformly positive, uninformative feedback unless the rubric demands specific, actionable comments

Implementation Variability¶

  • Calibrated peer review: reviewers first grade an instructor-anchored sample and receive accuracy feedback before reviewing peers (as in the Calibrated Peer Review platform)
  • Reciprocal rotation: every student both gives and receives reviews, doubling the criterion-analysis benefit
  • Staged review: outline → draft → final, so feedback arrives when it can still change the work
  • Anonymous vs. attributed: anonymous reviews tend to be more critical; attributed reviews can build Community of Inquiry norms but risk social softening

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and higher-education students producing extended written or designed work Collaborative writing and peer feedback improve text quality in secondary and postsecondary settings. [+M]
  • Developing reviewers benefit most when criteria are concrete; experts-in-training may find peer feedback less informative than expert feedback [~M]
  • Requires sufficient maturity for constructive critique; younger learners need heavily scaffolded sentence stems and narrow criteria

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: revision, audience awareness, and criterion internalization Collaborative writing and peer feedback improve text quality. [+M]
  • Evaluative judgment: learning to recognize quality, not just produce it
  • Self-regulated learning: applying to one's own work the standards used on others' work

Instructions¶

  1. Publish the criteria or rubric and walk through an instructor-annotated sample so students share a definition of quality (Advance Organizers can frame the criteria).
  2. Have students produce a complete draft — peer review fails on outlines or half-drafts.
  3. Assign 2–3 reviews per student, ideally anonymous, with a structured form (strengths, specific problems, actionable suggestions).
  4. Model a think-aloud critique of a sample draft before the first round (Coaching during the first review cycle raises feedback quality).
  5. Require a revision memo: authors state what feedback they acted on, what they rejected, and why — this converts feedback receipt into Articulation.
  6. Close the loop: spot-check revisions and occasionally grade the quality of the review given, signaling that critiquing is itself assessed work.

Related Strategies¶

  • Instructor Feedback Loops — peer review supplements rather than replaces expert feedback; combine for high-stakes work
  • Writing Conferences — one-to-one instructor conferencing targets what peers cannot diagnose
  • Collaborative Writing — co-authoring shares the revision burden but differs from critique-and-revise

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — discipline-agnostic web system in which students calibrate against instructor-scored samples before reviewing peers; widely used in undergraduate science writing.
  • SWoRD Peer Review (Cho & Schunn) — scaffolded web-based reciprocal peer review for writing-intensive courses; assigns multiple blind reviewers and aggregates reliability-weighted feedback.
  • ELI Review (https://elireview.com) — a commercial peer feedback platform built on staged draft–review–revision cycles with instructor-designed rubrics.
  • Writing-across-the-curriculum programs (e.g., Michigan State's peer writing consultant model) embed trained peer review in disciplinary courses.
