Word Of The Week¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 0 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Word Of The Week is a classroom routine in which a single academically useful word is selected each week and given sustained, multimodal treatment: the teacher introduces its meaning, pronunciation, and morphology; students generate examples, non-examples, and related words; and the word is deliberately recycled in discussion, writing, and display throughout the week. The goal is not exposure to many words but depth of processing for a few high-utility words, on the principle that rich, repeated encounters build the flexible word knowledge that supports comprehension.

Design Implications¶

Vocabulary research consistently shows that word knowledge develops through multiple, varied encounters with a word in meaningful contexts, not through single definitions Stahl & Fairbanks found vocabulary instruction produces reliable comprehension gains when words are taught deeply rather than merely defined. [+S]. A weekly cycle structures those encounters: initial explicit explanation, elaborative processing (examples, connections, morphology), and spaced retrieval across days. Selecting words from Beck, McKeown, and Kucan's "Tier Two" category — high-frequency academic words used across domains (e.g., analyze, reluctant, significant) — maximizes return on instructional time.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A principled selection process: words must appear widely in texts and talk students will encounter (Tier Two), not be rare or domain-specific
  • An explicit, student-friendly explanation on day one, ideally with an Analogy or concrete context
  • Multiple planned encounters across the week — at least 4–10 varied exposures, including student production, not just teacher repetition
  • A visible anchor (word wall, posted card) so the word stays in the environment
  • A culminating use: students must use the word in speaking or writing, not merely recognize it (Application)

Constraints¶

  • One word per week covers too few words to close large vocabulary gaps alone; students need thousands of words, so this routine must supplement, not replace, wide reading and morphological instruction [-M]
  • Rote copying of dictionary definitions produces weak, inflexible knowledge; definitional-only instruction yields far smaller gains than contextual and elaborative instruction [-S]
  • If the word is introduced but never revisited, the routine collapses into a single exposure, which rarely produces durable learning [-S]
  • Poorly chosen words (too rare, too easy, or domain-bound) waste the weekly slot; the routine's value depends almost entirely on selection quality [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Language classrooms: the target word may be a foreign-language item, with the same cycle of explanation, spaced practice, and production
  • Student-selected words: older students nominate candidates from their own reading, building engagement and autonomy
  • Word of the Day variants: higher turnover with lighter treatment; trades depth for coverage
  • Digital variants: platforms like Flocabulary and Vocabulary.com deliver weekly words with video, spaced quizzing, and adaptive review

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and middle-grade students still building academic vocabulary; explicit instruction of high-utility words benefits them most [+S]
  • English learners, for whom structured, repeated, contextualized encounters with academic words are especially valuable [~S]
  • Struggling readers, who encounter fewer words incidentally through reading and therefore depend more on direct instruction [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Receptive and expressive vocabulary: knowing meaning, pronunciation, and appropriate use
  • Word consciousness: attention to morphology, connotation, and nuance
  • Comprehension support: freeing reading resources for meaning-making Automatic word recognition and word knowledge free cognitive resources for comprehension. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Select a Tier Two word that students will encounter in upcoming texts and discussions; avoid words that are too rare or already known.
  2. Introduce (Monday): pronounce the word, give a student-friendly explanation, and anchor it in a familiar context or Analogy.
  3. Elaborate (Tuesday–Wednesday): students generate examples, non-examples, synonyms, and related forms; connect to known words through shared morphology.
  4. Use and revisit (throughout the week): require the word in discussion and writing; award recognition when students use it spontaneously; keep it displayed.
  5. Retrieve and review (Friday and beyond): a quick activity requiring recall and production, then schedule the word into later weeks — spaced retrieval is what converts exposure into durable knowledge Dunlosky et al. rate distributed practice and practice testing among the most effective learning techniques. [+S]

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — linking the new word to known concepts and experiences is the elaboration step that makes it stick
  • Morphological Analysis — teaching roots and affixes multiplies the payoff of each weekly word

Examples¶

  • Bringing Words to Life — Beck, McKeown, and Kucan's instructional framework supplies the Tier Two selection logic and the "friendly definition" introduction routine that most Word Of The Week implementations follow.
  • Flocabulary — weekly vocabulary videos with a word of the week feature, followed by activities and spaced review quizzes.
  • Elementary word wall routine — a common classroom practice: the word is posted Monday, students earn tally marks for spontaneous use, and Friday's exit ticket requires one original sentence.
