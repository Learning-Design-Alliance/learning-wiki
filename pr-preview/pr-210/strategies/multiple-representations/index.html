Multiple Representations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (5 causal, 3 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 10 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Multiple representations present the same concept or relation in two or more forms — for example, a graph alongside its equation, a physical model alongside a verbal description, or a diagram alongside symbolic notation. The goal is for learners to map between forms, so each representation constrains and enriches interpretation of the others. This is the instructional application of dual coding: verbal and visual codes provide independent retrieval routes Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M].

Design Implications¶

Multiple representations support abstraction and transfer because identifying the invariant structure across differing surface forms is exactly what abstraction requires Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]. But representations are not automatically better in quantity: each added form imposes coordination costs in working memory, so representations must be explicitly linked (color coding, dynamic linking, prompts to translate between forms) or they become competing sources of load Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Sequencing matters — introduce representations one at a time and build mappings incrementally rather than presenting all forms simultaneously.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Representations that are genuinely complementary (each revealing something the others obscure), not redundant duplicates
  • Explicit linking devices: shared labels, color correspondence, or tasks that require translation between forms
  • Learner activities that ask "what does this show that the other does not?" — mapping is the learning event, not side-by-side display

Constraints¶

  • Unlinked, simultaneous representations increase extraneous cognitive load, especially for novices [~S] — learners split attention between forms without building connections
  • Effectiveness reverses with expertise: advanced learners often benefit more from a single preferred representation, and added forms become redundant Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [~M]
  • If representations are superficially similar, learners may map surface features instead of deep structure, producing misconceptions [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static multiple representations — textbook-style pairing of graph, equation, and table with linking annotations
  • Dynamic/interactive linking — tools such as Desmos or GeoGebra where manipulating one representation updates the others in real time, making correspondence perceptually salient
  • Contrasting cases — sequences of representations differing on one dimension at a time, used to isolate the target structure
  • Concrete-to-symbolic progression — physical manipulatives or Analogies bridging everyday knowledge to formal notation

Target Learners¶

  • Novices in symbolically dense domains (mathematics, chemistry, physics) who must learn to interpret formal notations Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  • Learners with weak prior knowledge who benefit from concrete anchors before formal forms
  • Less beneficial for experts, for whom additional representations are often redundant Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder experts. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: grasping what a notation means, not just how to manipulate it
  • Abstraction and generalization: extracting invariant structure across varied forms
  • Flexible problem-solving: selecting and translating among representations appropriate to a task

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the target concept and select 2–3 representations that each reveal a distinct aspect of it.
  2. Introduce representations sequentially, not simultaneously, to manage Cognitive Load Management.
  3. Build explicit mappings — color correspondence, shared labels, or translation tasks ("sketch the graph this equation describes").
  4. Use Comparing Cases or contrasting representations to highlight what stays invariant across forms.
  5. Require learners to generate translations between representations themselves; producing the mapping drives deeper processing than reading it.
  6. Fade representational support as expertise develops, converging on the most efficient form for the domain.

Related Strategies¶

  • Use Analogies — an analogy is a representation drawn from prior knowledge; mapping it to the target concept is a translation exercise
  • Use Worked Examples — worked examples can embed multiple representations of the same solution step

Examples¶

  • Desmos (desmos.com) — dynamically links equations, graphs, and tables; dragging a slider updates all representations simultaneously, making correspondence perceptual rather than inferred.
  • CPM Educational Program (cpm.org) — mathematics curriculum that systematically presents concepts through tables, graphs, equations, and situational (word) contexts with explicit translation tasks.
  • Chemistry particulate drawings — instruction that pairs symbolic equations (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O) with molecular-level diagrams and macroscopic observations; research on multiple representations in chemistry shows translation tasks diagnose and correct misconceptions.
