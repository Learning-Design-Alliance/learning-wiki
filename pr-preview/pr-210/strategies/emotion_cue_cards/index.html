Emotion Cue Cards¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Emotion cue cards are portable visual or auditory prompts designed to remind students of strategies for managing emotions, such as using a fidget, taking deep breaths, or requesting a mindfulness break. Students carry them on a keyring, post them at a workstation, or access them digitally, selecting strategies that work best for them. Consistent use aims to move self-regulation responses from deliberate effort toward automatic, habitual execution.

Design Implications¶

Cue cards function as environmental scaffolds for emotion regulation: they offload the recall of coping strategies to an external artifact at precisely the moment working memory and executive control are compromised by emotional arousal [~M]. Their effectiveness depends on prior instruction — the card must cue a strategy the student has already rehearsed, not introduce a new one. Self-selection of strategies supports ownership and use Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Cards (physical or digital) with a small number of clearly illustrated or narrated strategies
  • Prior explicit teaching and rehearsal of each strategy during calm moments (Practice, Coaching)
  • Consistent reinforcement from educators and caregivers across settings
  • A low-stigma way to access and use the cards (keyring, desk strip, app)

Constraints¶

  • In the grip of strong emotional arousal, executive function is impaired and even cued strategies may go unused [-M] — cards work best as early-intervention prompts at the first signs of dysregulation, not mid-meltdown remedies
  • Effectiveness drops for students with severe emotional or behavioral challenges who need adult-mediated support rather than self-directed cues [-W]
  • Cards become wallpaper if not periodically revisited, rehearsed, and updated [-W]
  • Over-reliance can prevent students from internalizing strategies; prompts should fade as regulation becomes habitual [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Physical formats: laminated keyring cards, desk strips, lanyard tags, "calm corner" posters
  • Digital formats: apps and repositories offering audio, visuals, and animations (e.g., GoNoodle's calm channels, the Zones of Regulation digital tools)
  • Blank or partially completed cards that students customize with their own strategies and drawings
  • Emotion-first vs. strategy-first organization: cards sorted by feeling ("when I feel frustrated…") vs. by action ("breathing break")

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and middle school students developing foundational self-regulation
  • Students with ADHD, autism, or emotional/behavioral disabilities, for whom external prompts are a common accommodation (Accommodations)
  • Early learners who benefit from visual rather than text-based prompts

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: recognizing emotional states and executing coping responses
  • Emotional awareness: building vocabulary and discrimination among emotional states (Check-In)
  • Habit formation: integrating coping strategies into long-term memory through repeated, cued practice

Instructions¶

  1. Teach a small set of regulation strategies explicitly during calm, low-stress times (Provide Guidance).
  2. Rehearse each strategy through role-play and practice (Act It Out, Practice).
  3. Co-construct the cards with the student, selecting the 3–5 strategies they find most workable.
  4. Establish when and how to use the cards, and model a first use (Coaching).
  5. Review use in brief check-ins, retire strategies that don't work, and fade prompts as independence grows (Check-In, Enhance Retention and Transfer).

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — builds the emotion vocabulary the cards presuppose
  • Check-Ins — regular emotional state monitoring that pairs naturally with card use
  • Achievable Micro-Goals — framing each card strategy as a small, attainable action

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — strategies must be rehearsed before a card can cue them
  • Coaching — adult prompting that scaffolds early card use
  • Accommodations — cue cards often function as a formal accommodation in IEPs/504 plans
  • Check-In — the monitoring routine that tells students when to consult their cards

Examples¶

  • A student keeps a keyring of laminated cards — deep breaths, squeeze a fidget, ask for a break — and uses them when anxious during tests; the teacher reviews card use in a weekly check-in.
  • A digital product provides a repository of prompt cards with audio, visuals, and animations that students access when emotions are heightened.
  • Zones of Regulation — a widely used curriculum in which students sort strategies by emotional "zone," often rendered as portable cue cards or desk strips.
  • GoNoodle — short guided movement and mindfulness videos that function as digital, animated cue cards for regulation breaks.
