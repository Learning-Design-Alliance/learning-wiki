Don’t Jump Into "Fix It" Mode¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 12 studies (5 review, 2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 1 of 12 report an effect size

Description¶

When a student brings a problem — academic, social, or emotional — the instinctive adult response is to propose a solution. This strategy deliberately suspends that instinct: first listen fully and understand what is wrong, then ask what kind of help (if any) the student wants before discussing possible solutions together. Students often need to be heard rather than repaired, and premature fixing short-circuits both understanding and the student's own problem-solving.

Design Implications¶

Premature solutions undermine the autonomy that fuels intrinsic motivation; supporting students' own reasoning about a problem sustains engagement and ownership Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. Teachers who ask questions and acknowledge perspectives rather than issuing directives promote more engagement and self-regulation than those who steer immediately to answers Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]. The strategy treats the conversation itself as a learning opportunity: the student practices diagnosing, weighing options, and requesting support — skills that transfer beyond the immediate problem.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Genuinely open-ended listening before any evaluative or advisory response (Active Listening)
  • Questions that return ownership to the student ("What have you tried?" "What would help most?")
  • Willingness to accept that the student may want no intervention at all
  • A follow-up structure for co-constructing solutions once the problem is understood (Coaching)

Constraints¶

  • In genuine safety or crisis situations (harm, abuse, acute distress), deferring to the student's preferences is inappropriate [-S] — the adult must act regardless of what the student asks for
  • Time-pressured contexts (large classes, brief encounters) push adults back toward quick fixes; without protected time the strategy degrades into token listening [-M]
  • Students with very low prior problem-solving experience may find open questions frustrating rather than empowering [~M] — they may need more directive modeling before ownership is productive
  • Repeatedly asking "what do you think you could do?" without ever offering expertise can read as abdication and erode trust [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Counseling contexts: full non-directive listening, with solutions raised only at the student's initiative
  • Classroom contexts: abbreviated version — one clarifying exchange before jointly deciding on next steps
  • Mentoring/advising: listen first, then offer a menu of options the student selects from, rather than a single recommendation
  • Parenting: name the feeling, ask whether the child wants ideas or just company with the problem

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents and young adults, for whom autonomy support is especially consequential for motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Students with low academic self-efficacy, who benefit from conversations that treat them as capable problem-solvers rather than problems to be solved Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • K-12 educators, counselors, mentors, and parents as the primary implementers

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: diagnosing one's own difficulties and selecting strategies Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Problem-solving: generating and weighing options before acting
  • Relational trust and help-seeking: learning that asking for support does not mean surrendering control

Instructions¶

  1. Stop and listen. Give full attention; do not interrupt with advice, reassurance, or correction (Active Listening).
  2. Check understanding. Reflect back what you heard and ask clarifying questions until the student confirms you understand the problem as they see it.
  3. Ask before advising. "Do you want ideas, or do you just want me to know?" — let the student set the agenda.
  4. Co-construct options. If help is wanted, brainstorm together rather than prescribing (Collaborative Decision-Making); the student should leave owning the plan.
  5. Follow up. Check in later on how the chosen approach went (Check-Ins), reinforcing that the student's judgment is taken seriously.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Listening — the core skill that makes the pause before fixing possible
  • Actively Listen to What Students Say — classroom variant of the same listening discipline

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the facilitative stance that replaces directive problem-solving
  • Collaborative Decision-Making — how solutions get chosen when the student retains ownership
  • Class Discussion — group-scale version: surface the problem before proposing remedies

Examples¶

  • A student says they're failing a course. Instead of proposing a tutoring schedule, the advisor asks what has been hardest, what the student has already tried, and what support would actually be used — then helps arrange only that.
  • Motivational interviewing in school counseling: the counselor elicits the student's own reasons for change rather than arguing for a solution, a stance with consistent evidence in adolescent behavior-change contexts.
  • A teacher responding to "this project is impossible" by asking the student to walk through where it broke down, then jointly deciding whether to adjust scope, provide a model, or extend the deadline — rather than immediately reassigning the task.
