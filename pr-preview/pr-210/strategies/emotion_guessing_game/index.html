Emotion Guessing Game¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

An emotion guessing game involves an instructor or student making an emotion face, and the others guess the emotion being expressed. After guessing, the group discusses situations that might evoke that emotion, connecting facial configurations to causes, labels, and social consequences. The game builds emotional vocabulary and nonverbal decoding skill through active-learning rather than direct explanation.

Design Implications¶

Emotion guessing games work because they pair perception of facial expressions with retrieval and elaboration of emotion labels and situational causes — a generative activity that strengthens the link between expression, label, and context Durlak et al.'s meta-analysis shows social-emotional learning activities improve both social-emotional skills and academic outcomes. [+S]. The discussion phase is where most of the learning happens: naming an expression without discussing when and why it occurs leaves learners with shallow, context-free associations. Accuracy of the model matters — exaggerated or stereotyped faces can teach oversimplified categories, so facilitators should introduce mixed, subtle, and culturally varied expressions over time Emotion recognition accuracy varies by culture and display intensity, so single-cue training generalizes poorly. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Minimal materials (e.g., a paper or blanket to cover the face between rounds); a facilitator to guide turns and keep interactions respectful
  • A supportive climate where learners can opt out of performing; some learners will not want to make faces in front of peers
  • A follow-up discussion structure that asks "when have you felt this?" and "what would you do?" rather than stopping at the label

Constraints¶

  • Learners with social anxiety or autism spectrum profiles may find live performance aversive; picture-based or video-based variants avoid the performance demand [-M]
  • Static, exaggerated facial expressions overstate how readable emotions are in real life, which are dynamic, blended, and context-dependent [~M]
  • Guessing can devolve into ridicule of the "actor" without explicit norms; facilitation quality is a precondition, not an add-on
  • Emotion labels are culturally situated — a face read as "anger" in one culture may be read differently in another — so games should not present one culture's decoding rules as universal [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Picture cards or video clips replace live acting for learners uncomfortable performing or for remote settings
  • Role-play extension: after guessing, learners act it out a scenario that would evoke the emotion, adding perspective-taking
  • Written variant: learners write a situation matching a shown expression, useful for older learners and assessment
  • Escalating subtlety: begin with basic emotions (happy, sad, angry, afraid), progress to complex ones (frustrated, embarrassed, relieved, skeptical)

Target Learners¶

  • Early childhood and elementary learners building foundational emotional vocabulary SEL programs show the strongest effects when social-emotional skills are explicitly taught and practiced. [+S]
  • Learners who struggle with nonverbal cue decoding, including some autistic learners (using the picture/video variants)
  • Adult learners in counseling, healthcare, negotiation, or language-learning contexts where reading affect matters professionally [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: expanding and refining emotion vocabulary beyond "happy/sad/mad"
  • Nonverbal communication: decoding facial expressions and linking them to causes
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' internal states from external cues Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes when learners practice perspective-taking. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Model the format: the facilitator demonstrates one round, making an exaggerated face and narrating the guessing process (Demonstration)
  2. Play a round: a volunteer makes an emotion face (or shows a picture card); peers guess the emotion, with the facilitator accepting multiple plausible labels and refining them
  3. Discuss the context: the group generates situations that evoke the emotion and what a person might need in that moment (Class Discussion)
  4. Practice in context: learners apply the emotion in a short role-play or write a matching scenario (Practice)
  5. Check in: close with learners naming their own current emotion, transferring the vocabulary to self-awareness (Check-In)

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a check-in variant that uses the same format to open a session with emotional self-labeling
  • Acting-Role-Play — extends guessing into enacting full scenarios with the target emotion

Related Elements¶

  • Demonstration — the facilitator's modeled round establishes the format and quality of expression
  • Act It Out — learners enact emotion-eliciting situations, deepening the expression–cause link
  • Practice — repeated rounds with varied emotions build fluency in recognition
  • Check-In — transfers decoded vocabulary to self-awareness routines

Examples¶

  • Second Life Skills lesson (elementary): morning circle opens with three rounds of the guessing game using subtle expressions, then students write one sentence about a time they felt the guessed emotion.
  • Medical/nursing education: standardized-patient debriefs use expression-recognition rounds to train clinicians in reading patient affect before history-taking.
  • ESL classrooms: emotion faces paired with target vocabulary ("anxious," "relieved," "annoyed") give learners embodied anchors for low-frequency emotion words.
