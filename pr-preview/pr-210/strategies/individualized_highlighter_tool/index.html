Individualized "Highlighter Tool"¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (1 mixed, 1 against, 1 unmarked) · 8 studies (4 review, 2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 1 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Students use an individualized "highlighter tool" (e.g., a Google Doc add-on such as Highlight Tool) to create custom highlighters in different colors. They label each color with a category (e.g., "Argument #1" in red, "Counterclaim" in blue) and use them to mark relevant text. The highlights can then be exported by color into a table, where students summarize each highlighted category in their own words.

Design Implications¶

The strategy converts passive marking into a generative activity: the labeled color scheme forces students to commit to a categorical structure before reading, and the export-and-summarize step requires them to transform — not merely select — the text. Highlighting alone is among the weaker study techniques Learner-generated highlighting supports learning better than reading alone, but unstructured highlighting adds little over careful reading. Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A digital highlighter tool that supports multiple labeled colors and export by color (e.g., the Highlight Tool add-on for Google Docs)
  • A document worth analyzing structurally — argumentative essays, primary sources, research articles, lab reports
  • A pre-taught category scheme (or a guided process for students to devise one) and a summary table template
  • Teacher modeling of the first pass, so students see what counts as evidence for each category

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured or indiscriminate highlighting produces an illusion of fluency without durable learning Highlighting as commonly practiced adds little over reading alone. [-S]
  • If the category scheme is imposed without explanation, students highlight to match labels mechanically rather than analyzing [-M]
  • Overloaded schemes (more than ~4–5 categories) fragment attention and add extraneous load Chunking information reduces working memory load, but too many concurrent categories increase it. [-M]
  • Tool friction (clunky add-ons, export failures) consumes time that should go to analysis; test the workflow before class

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher-provided vs. student-generated category schemes — student-generated schemes demand more but build deeper structural understanding [~M]
  • Whole-class shared color conventions vs. individual schemes; shared conventions enable comparative discussion of what different students marked
  • Apply to one's own writing during revision (highlight claims, evidence, analysis in a draft) as well as to readings
  • Pair the exported table with a Concept Map or discussion to push beyond categorization toward relational reasoning

Target Learners¶

  • Middle school through college students working with complex, multi-part texts
  • Developing readers who benefit from an explicit, visible structure for analytical reading [+M]
  • Less beneficial for expert readers, for whom the scaffold may be redundant and slow Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Text analysis: identifying arguments, evidence, rhetorical moves, or disciplinary structures
  • Categorization and organization of information Learner-generated highlighting supports learning better than reading alone. [+M]
  • Summarization and synthesis — the export table is a stepping stone to writing
  • Metacognitive monitoring: color choices make students' interpretive decisions visible and discussable

Instructions¶

  1. Assign the text and preview its purpose (Assigned Readings).
  2. Introduce or co-construct the category scheme; model one highlighting pass on a short excerpt, thinking aloud about borderline decisions.
  3. Students create and label their custom highlighters, then mark the document.
  4. Students export highlights by color into a table and write a 1–3 sentence summary per category (Application of Knowledge).
  5. Debrief: compare highlighting choices across students to surface interpretive differences and refine the category scheme.

Related Strategies¶

  • Annotating — the broader family of text-marking practices; the labeled color scheme is a structured subset
  • Concept Mapping — a follow-on step that turns exported categories into relational structure
  • Dual Coding — color categories add a visual-verbal code that can support recall

Examples¶

  • Google Docs + Highlight Tool add-on (https://workspace.google.com/marketplace/app/highlight_tool/2078802590): students build custom labeled highlighters, then export highlights by color to a Doc table for summarizing.
  • AP English argument analysis: students highlight claim, evidence, and commentary in an op-ed in three colors, export to a table, then draft a paragraph using only their exported evidence.
  • History primary-source reading: colors for "author's position," "supporting evidence," "bias/loaded language," with summaries feeding into a sourced-based short-answer response.
