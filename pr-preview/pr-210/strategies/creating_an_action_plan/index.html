Creating An Action Plan¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 13 studies (7 review, 3 theoretical, 2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q1–q4 · 1 of 13 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Creating an action plan asks learners to translate a goal into a concrete, sequenced set of actions — specifying what they will do, when and where they will do it, what resources they need, and how they will know they are on track. The plan functions as an implementation intention ("if situation X, I will do Y") that links the goal to specific cues and behaviors, and as a self-monitoring tool that makes progress visible against a standard.

Design Implications¶

Action planning operationalizes the forethought and performance phases of Self-Regulated Learning: it converts vague intentions into proximal, cue-linked commitments that learners can actually execute and monitor Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]. Plans built around process goals — what the learner will do — outperform outcome-only goals for novices, who cannot yet map outcomes back to effective actions Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A goal that is specific and challenging but attainable; vague or easy goals do not reliably raise performance Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S]
  • Sub-goals small enough to be completed within days or weeks, so progress feeds self-efficacy Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • A monitoring mechanism — a log, checklist, or scheduled Check-In — that surfaces progress and slippage Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Time to revise the plan when monitoring reveals it is not working; a plan treated as fixed becomes a liability

Constraints¶

  • Plans imposed by the instructor rather than authored by the learner lose the motivational benefits of ownership Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • Outcome-only plans ("get an A") give novices no actionable steps and can depress performance relative to process plans Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [-S]
  • Overly ambitious plans produce repeated failure experiences that erode efficacy and persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [-M]
  • Planning without follow-through — plans never revisited or checked — yields little benefit; the monitoring loop, not the document, drives the effect Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured templates (goal → steps → timeline → evidence of completion) suit novices; open formats suit learners with planning experience
  • If-then format (implementation intentions) attaches each step to a concrete situational cue — strongest evidence base for follow-through
  • Public vs. private plans: sharing with a peer or group adds accountability but can shift focus to appearance; private plans with instructor check-ins are a lower-risk default
  • Digital vs. paper: planning tools (Trello, Notion, a course LMS tracker) matter less than the scheduled review cycle built around them

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and struggling learners, who benefit most from explicit process steps rather than outcome targets Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+S]
  • Learners with weak self-regulation, for whom external monitoring structure builds the habit Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Learners with low confidence, for whom achievable proximal sub-goals build efficacy through mastery experiences Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less necessary for expert self-regulators, who already plan adaptively and may find templates constraining

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own learning process
  • Sustained multi-session projects (research, portfolios, capstones) where progress must be managed over time
  • Habit formation and study routines (spaced review schedules, practice regimens)
  • Metacognitive awareness: learning what one's own realistic capacity and failure points are

Instructions¶

  1. Set the goal. Learners state a specific, challenging but attainable goal; help them reframe outcome goals as process goals Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S]
  2. Break it down. Decompose the goal into sub-tasks of manageable size, sequencing them (Chunking)
  3. Attach cues. For each step, specify when, where, and how it will be done — an if-then commitment rather than a vague intention
  4. Define evidence. For each step, state what completion looks like, so progress is checkable rather than felt
  5. Schedule monitoring. Build in regular Check-In points or a progress log where learners compare actual against planned progress Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  6. Review and revise. At each check-in, treat slippage as information: adjust the plan, the sub-goal size, or the cues — not just the effort

Examples¶

  • Morisano et al. (2010) — Struggling university students who completed a structured goal-setting and planning intervention (elaborating personal goals, specifying means, and reflecting on conflicts) improved GPA relative to controls.
  • Implementation intentions in coursework — Students write "I will draft my essay outline at 7 pm Tuesday in the library" rather than "I will work on my essay"; the cue-specific format substantially increases follow-through on academic intentions.
  • Khan Academy — Mastery-learning dashboards let learners set course goals and see progress against them, pairing the plan with built-in monitoring.
  • Project-based courses — Capstone or research courses that require a written project plan with milestones and scheduled advisor check-ins enact this strategy with instructor-supported monitoring.
