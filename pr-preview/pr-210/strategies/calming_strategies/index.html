Calming Strategies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (3 review, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 0 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Calming strategies are concrete, repeatable techniques — deep breathing, seeking a quiet space, squeezing a fidget, taking a cool drink, brief mindfulness — that learners use to down-regulate anger, anxiety, and other intense emotions. They are explicitly taught and rehearsed during calm periods, so that the response is available and fluent when emotional arousal is high. Effectiveness varies by individual and by the intensity of the emotion, so learners build a repertoire rather than a single technique.

Design Implications¶

Calming strategies are a form of emotion-regulation skill training: they work best when taught as explicit procedures, modeled by adults, and practiced repeatedly under low-arousal conditions before being needed under high-arousal conditions [~M]. Because high emotional arousal narrows working memory and impairs deliberate reasoning, strategies must be overlearned to the point of automaticity — a one-off lesson in "take three deep breaths" rarely changes behavior [~M]. Self-monitoring of emotional state is a prerequisite: learners must notice rising arousal before they can deploy a strategy Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A curated repertoire of strategies matched to the learner's age, preferences, and setting
  • Time to teach, model, and rehearse strategies during calm, low-stakes periods
  • Consistent support and reinforcement from educators and caregivers across settings
  • Environmental supports (a calm-down space, accessible fidgets, visual cue charts) that make strategies easy to execute in the moment

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness drops sharply when the learner is already at peak arousal; strategies must be initiated early in the escalation curve [~M]
  • A single strategy rarely works for every child or every situation — mismatched strategies can escalate distress [~W]
  • Without regular rehearsal and reinforcement, use decays; strategies taught once and never revisited show little durable effect [~M]
  • Adults who model dysregulation undermine instruction; children learn regulation largely through co-regulation with calm adults [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class universal teaching (as in SEL curricula) versus individualized plans for learners with intensive needs
  • Physical strategies (movement, sensory tools), cognitive strategies (counting, self-talk), and environmental strategies (leaving the situation) can be combined into a personal "toolkit"
  • Visual supports and scripted steps for younger learners; self-selected and self-monitored toolkits for older learners

Target Learners¶

  • Young children (preschool–early elementary) acquiring initial emotion-regulation skills, who benefit most from concrete, rehearsed strategies [~M]
  • Learners with frequent anger outbursts, anxiety, or identified self-regulation difficulties
  • All learners in universal SEL programs — broad meta-analytic evidence shows social-emotional instruction improves both behavior and academic outcomes [+S]
  • Less effective without adult co-regulation support for learners with significant trauma histories, who may need relational safety before self-regulation techniques can take hold [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional regulation: reducing the intensity and duration of anger and distress
  • Self-awareness: recognizing early physiological signs of escalation Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Self-efficacy: building the belief that one can manage one's own emotional states Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Prosocial behavior: reducing aggressive responses in conflict situations

Instructions¶

  1. Identify escalation signs with the learner — build awareness of personal physiological cues (hot face, clenched fists, racing thoughts) before teaching responses.
  2. Introduce a small set of strategies through Direct Instruction — name each strategy, when to use it, and its steps explicitly.
  3. Model each strategy yourself, narrating your own regulation ("I'm feeling frustrated, so I'm going to take a slow breath") — children adopt strategies they have seen adults use [~M].
  4. Rehearse during calm periods through Practice — role-play, games, and brief daily routines so strategies become automatic.
  5. Support in-the-moment use through Coaching — prompt and cue the strategy during early escalation, then fade prompts as the learner initiates independently.
  6. Review and personalize — after incidents, reflect with the learner on which strategies worked and update their personal toolkit.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-ins — brief emotional state check-ins build the self-monitoring foundation that tells learners when to deploy a calming strategy
  • Achievable micro-goals — framing regulation as small, attainable steps ("try one breath") supports early success and self-efficacy

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — rehearsal during calm periods is what makes strategies available under arousal
  • Coaching — in-the-moment prompting and fading of adult support
  • Cognitive Strategies — the self-talk and attention-shifting techniques within a calming repertoire
  • Modeling — adults demonstrating regulation aloud makes strategies learnable

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — modeling and coaching applied to internal emotional states rather than visible tasks
  • Direct Instruction — explicit teaching of strategy steps before independent use

Examples¶

  • Incredible Years (Webster-Stratton) — teaches young children the "Turtle Technique" (withdraw into shell, calm down, think of solutions) through puppet modeling, rehearsal, and reinforcement across home and classroom settings.
  • PATHS Curriculum — universal SEL curriculum in which children learn the "Calming-Down Steps" (stop, name the feeling, take deep breaths, count) via daily lessons and a "calm-down" classroom routine.
  • Calm-down corners — a designated classroom space with visual strategy charts, sensory tools, and a timer, letting learners self-select and execute strategies with minimal adult direction.
  • Mindfulness breaks — brief guided breathing or body-scan practices embedded in the school day; meta-analytic evidence shows small but reliable benefits for attention and emotional problems in youth [~M].
