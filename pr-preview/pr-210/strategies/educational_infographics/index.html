Educational Infographics¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 9 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Educational infographics are visual aids that simplify complex information into easily digestible formats using a combination of charts, icons, and text. They summarize topics, visualize statistics, explain processes, and present timelines in a single coherent artifact. Effective infographics use color and layout strategically — not decoratively — to highlight relationships and patterns within the data.

Design Implications¶

Infographics exploit the capacity of the visual channel to carry information in parallel with verbal processing, consistent with dual coding of verbal and pictorial information Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. Their value depends on disciplined design: text and graphics must be integrated and mutually explanatory, not merely adjacent, and extraneous decoration should be stripped because it competes for limited working memory Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Color and emphasis direct attention, so highlighting should mark what is actually important rather than what looks appealing Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear communicative goal: one infographic should answer one question, not catalog everything known about a topic
  • Careful selection of visuals and concise, plain-language text (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  • Visual hierarchy that guides the eye through the intended reading order (Clear Structure & Presentation)
  • Integration into a lesson with accompanying activity — an infographic studied in isolation rarely produces durable learning

Constraints¶

  • Overly dense infographics overload working memory and depress comprehension; adding more visual elements often reduces learning rather than enhancing it [~S]
  • Decorative but irrelevant graphics (seductive details) divert attention from core content and harm retention [-S]
  • Effectiveness drops for learners with high prior knowledge, for whom heavily pre-structured visuals can be redundant [~M]
  • Data visualizations can mislead when axes are truncated or proportions distorted; learners may absorb the distortion as fact [-M]
  • High-quality production requires time and design skill; poor execution undermines credibility of the content itself

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static posters or handouts for reference and revision
  • Annotated infographics where learners add labels or commentary (Annotating)
  • Learner-authored infographics as an assessment or synthesis task, forcing selection and organization of content
  • Interactive or animated infographics in digital environments, which can reveal process steps sequentially

Target Learners¶

  • Novices encountering an unfamiliar topic, who benefit from pre-structured visual overviews Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]
  • Learners with limited working memory capacity or reading difficulty, for whom visual chunking reduces load Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less beneficial for experts, who may find the simplification imprecise or unnecessary [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual overview: building an initial mental model of a topic's structure
  • Data literacy: interpreting statistics, trends, and comparisons
  • Retention of key facts and relationships through dual coding
  • Synthesis and communication (when learners design their own infographics)

Instructions¶

  1. Define the single message or question the infographic must answer; discard everything else.
  2. Draft the core visual (chart, timeline, process diagram) before writing any text.
  3. Add minimal, integrated labels and captions; place text next to the graphic it explains (Clear Structure & Presentation).
  4. Use color and emphasis only to mark relationships and key findings Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M].
  5. Pair the infographic with an activity — prediction, Self-Explanation, or learner annotation — rather than passive viewing Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].
  6. Optionally, have learners create their own infographic to demonstrate synthesis (Demonstration in reverse: learners produce the model).

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — an infographic can serve as a visual advance organizer presented before instruction
  • Case Studies — infographics can compress case data for initial analysis
  • Annotating — learner annotation turns a static infographic into an active processing task

Examples¶

  • Information is Beautiful — David McCandless's data-journalism infographics; widely used in media literacy and statistics teaching to discuss both insight and visual distortion.
  • Venngage and Canva — template-based design platforms commonly used for learner-authored infographics in classroom and corporate training settings.
  • Our World in Data — research-backed interactive charts and infographics on global development, used in secondary and university curricula.
  • Science classrooms using infographics of the water cycle or photosynthesis as pre-reading organizers, followed by Self-Explanation prompts.
