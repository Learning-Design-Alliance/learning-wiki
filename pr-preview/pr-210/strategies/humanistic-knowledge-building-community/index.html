Humanistic Knowledge Building Community¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 unmarked) · 2 studies (2 qualitative), q1 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

A four-phase collaborative knowledge-building design (Hod & Ben-Zvi, 2018) applied here to a topic — national identity — where students hold personal stakes in the content, not just academic interest. Students use an online knowledge-building platform (Knowledge Forum) to collect, merge, and iterate on ideas, moving through phases of steadily increasing collective cognitive responsibility: from low (structured, teacher-directed assignments) to high (student-determined content and direction). The design deliberately curates materials that present the topic as contested and multiple — pluralistic, sometimes contradictory legal, philosophical, and journalistic sources — rather than converging on one authoritative account, and pairs this with humanistic classroom practices (trust, active listening, psychological safety) intended to let students bring personal experience into academic analysis.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An online collaborative knowledge-building platform where students can post, merge, and iteratively revise notes over months (not a single-session discussion tool)
  • Curated source materials that present the topic through genuinely conflicting definitions or framings, not a single settled account
  • A teacher able to sustain a humanistic classroom climate (active listening, psychological safety) over an extended, multi-month unit

Constraints¶

  • Demonstrated over roughly four months (23 sessions); unclear whether the collective-cognitive-responsibility progression works on a compressed timeline
  • The source study's topic (Jewish national identity in Israel) is culturally and politically specific — the four-phase structure is general, but the curated-contradiction materials must be freshly assembled for any other identity or contested-topic domain

Implementation Variability¶

  • The degree of collective cognitive responsibility ceded to students in Phases 3–4 can be tuned; the source design moved from teacher-selected texts to fully student-directed inquiry, but a more conservative implementation could cap responsibility at Phase 2's intermediate level

Target Learners¶

  • Students in a domain where the standard curriculum's authoritative account conflicts with, or excludes, some students' lived identity or experience
  • Link to sub-claim: Restorying supports Black girls' identification of and resistance to dominant narratives — a related mechanism (contested-narrative materials enabling identity work) demonstrated in a different domain

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Building capacity to hold and reason across multiple, conflicting definitions of a contested category, rather than defaulting to a single inherited (essentialist) account
  • Link to sub-claim: Participant examples support identity reconstruction through academic content

Instructions¶

  1. Orientation (~8 sessions) — Introduce knowledge-building norms and the online platform; have students analyze a curated set of texts (e.g., news articles) that surface tension or contradiction in the topic at low collective cognitive responsibility (structured, detailed assignments).
  2. Rise-Above (~7 sessions) — Have students merge their initial notes into a smaller number of "rise-above" notes that abstract cross-cutting themes; introduce more challenging primary source material (e.g., a contested legal case); use Reciprocal Teaching so students present, critique, and discuss the harder texts themselves; responsibility moves to intermediate.
  3. Presentation (~4 sessions) — Students present their knowledge-building work to an external audience (administrators, researchers, community members), raising the stakes and collective cognitive responsibility further.
  4. Knowledge Building (~4 sessions) — Students pursue self-chosen lines of inquiry with access to curated resources but no fixed assignment; responsibility is now fully student-held. Close with an individual reflective writing task connecting the unit's content to the student's own stance.

Examples¶

  • A 23-session, four-month unit in a 9th-grade Israeli classroom in which students examined competing legal, religious, and secular definitions of Jewish identity, with one focal student (Joshua) using a note on the Oswald Rufeisen Supreme Court case to argue for his own status as a secular Jew.
