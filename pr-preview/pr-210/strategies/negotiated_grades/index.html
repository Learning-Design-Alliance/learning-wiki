Negotiated Grades¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Negotiated grades involve students and instructors jointly determining assessment criteria, the evidence that will demonstrate learning, and ultimately the grade itself, through structured dialogue. Rather than assessment being delivered to students as a verdict, negotiation makes students participants in setting standards and interpreting their own performance against them. The practice typically combines co-constructed criteria, self-assessment with evidence, and a conferencing step where the final grade is agreed rather than assigned.

Design Implications¶

Negotiated grades build on the finding that assessment improves learning when students understand and act on criteria rather than merely receive scores feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels [+S]. Giving students genuine voice in assessment satisfies autonomy needs that support intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S], and the negotiation process itself functions as a self-assessment and self-regulation exercise. The strategy only works when quality criteria are externally anchored — negotiation about how well work meets standards, not about the standards themselves.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Transparent, stable criteria (rubrics or exemplars) agreed before work begins, so negotiation concerns evidence and judgment, not moving goalposts
  • Structured conferencing time (individual check-ins or written grade proposals with justification)
  • Student evidence-gathering: portfolios, reflective self-assessments, or work samples mapped to criteria
  • Instructor willingness to revise a grade when the student's evidence-based case is sound — a negotiation that never changes anything is theater and erodes trust

Constraints¶

  • Without clear external criteria, negotiation drifts into grade inflation and impression management [-W] — students with strong negotiation skills or cultural capital gain advantages unrelated to learning
  • High-stakes or externally mandated grading contexts (standardized transcripts, competitive admissions) leave little legitimate room for negotiation [~M]
  • Students unfamiliar with self-assessment initially propose grades poorly calibrated to quality; calibration improves only with practice and exemplar comparison [-W]
  • Adds significant instructor time per student; scales poorly in large-enrollment courses without structured tools [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Criteria negotiation only: students help define rubric language but grades are assigned conventionally — lowest risk, lowest autonomy benefit
  • Grade proposal with evidence: students propose a grade with a written justification mapped to criteria; instructor confirms, adjusts, or counters
  • Full conferencing model: one-on-one meetings where grade and next-step goals are agreed jointly, common in portfolio-based writing programs
  • Contract grading: grade bands negotiated at the start of a course tied to completed work (e.g., the "contract grading" tradition from postsecondary writing instruction)

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescent and adult learners with enough metacognitive development to evaluate their own work against criteria [~M]
  • Students whose motivation is undermined by controlling, judgment-heavy assessment environments Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • At-risk or low-efficacy students who benefit from assessment framed as dialogue rather than verdict Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Young children and complete novices lack the evaluative frameworks to negotiate meaningfully; they need exemplars and heavy scaffolding first [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulated learning: evaluating one's own work against standards
  • Deep conceptual goals where quality is multidimensional and judgment-laden (writing, projects, portfolios)
  • Metacognitive goal-setting and monitoring, not speed or recall objectives

Instructions¶

  1. Co-construct or clearly present quality criteria and exemplars before work begins, consistent with Assessment for Learning.
  2. Have students complete the work and prepare a self-assessment: their proposed grade plus evidence mapped to each criterion.
  3. Hold a structured conference (check-in) where student and instructor compare judgments, resolve discrepancies by returning to the evidence and criteria, and agree on the grade.
  4. Close the conference by jointly setting one or two improvement goals, connecting the grade conversation to next learning steps rather than ending at the number.
  5. Record the agreed grade and goals so subsequent assessment cycles can reference prior agreements.

Related Strategies¶

  • Contract grading — a pre-negotiated variant where grade bands are agreed at course start
  • Portfolio assessment — the evidence base that makes grade negotiation concrete
  • Establish evaluative criteria as a creative project evolves through student-faculty feedback conferences

Examples¶

  • Iowa Writers' Workshop-style contract grading — graduate creative writing courses where students negotiate semester goals and grade expectations with faculty at the outset, then assess against them in end-of-term conferences.
  • Portfolio-based secondary programs (e.g., New York Performance Standards Consortium schools) — students defend portfolios before panels including teachers, with the resulting judgment, not a test score, determining standing (https://www.performanceassessment.org).
  • University self-assessment conferences — writing-intensive courses where students submit a final portfolio with a cover letter arguing for a grade against the course rubric, which the instructor treats as the opening of negotiation.
