Share Teacher's Written Work¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 unmarked) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Instructors share their own written work — current drafts, polished pieces, or writing from earlier in their education — with learners and invite feedback and criticism. The strategy models expert work-in-progress, normalizes revision, and positions feedback as a reciprocal practice rather than a one-way judgment. It is a form of Demonstration applied to the writing process itself, with the instructor deliberately occupying the learner's role.

Design Implications¶

Sharing teacher work makes the usually invisible processes of expert writing — drafting, receiving editorial critique, revising — observable, which supports modeling of both craft and disposition [~M]. Its distinctive value is affective and cultural: learners see that criticism is not an affront but a routine part of improvement, which can increase their willingness to seek and use feedback on their own work Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+M]. The strategy works best when the instructor genuinely acts on the feedback received, closing the loop visibly.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Authentic instructor work at an appropriate level of incompleteness — a real draft with real weaknesses, not a flawless exemplar
  • Structured feedback protocols so critique is specific and task-focused (Provide Feedback)
  • A classroom culture of openness established before the exchange, so learners believe criticism is safe to give
  • Visible follow-through: the instructor revises in response to learner comments, demonstrating that feedback has consequences

Constraints¶

  • Learners often hesitate to criticize an authority figure; without explicit permission and structure, feedback stays superficial or insincere [-M]
  • If the shared work is too polished, learners conclude that good writers don't need revision — the opposite of the intended model [-M]
  • Power asymmetry can turn the exercise into performance; learners may flatter rather than analyze, especially in cultures or classrooms where deference to instructors is strong [~M]
  • Instructor vulnerability without facilitation skill can shift focus from the writing to the teacher's feelings, reducing instructional value [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Share a current draft with editor feedback attached and ask learners to advise on revisions
  • Share a developmental arc — high school, college, and graduate essays — for learners to analyze improvement over time
  • Live revision: the instructor rewrites a passage in response to learner suggestions during class
  • Anonymous or written channels (online forums, index cards) lower the social cost of criticizing the teacher

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and higher-education students developing as writers, who benefit from seeing expert work as revisable rather than innate [~M]
  • Learners with low confidence or feedback anxiety; observing the instructor receive criticism can reduce threat and support persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less effective for learners who lack enough craft knowledge to give substantive critique — pair with Annotating or feedback rubrics first

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Dispositional: normalizing revision, feedback-seeking, and a growth orientation toward one's own work
  • Critical analysis: evaluating a real text against audience and purpose
  • Metacognitive modeling: seeing how an experienced writer monitors and responds to reader insight

Instructions¶

  1. Select authentic work with genuine revision needs — a current draft, or earlier writing showing growth over time
  2. Frame the exchange explicitly: state that you want criticism and that specific, task-focused comments are most useful Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+M]
  3. Model receiving feedback non-defensively as it arrives — a form of Demonstration of disposition
  4. Have learners give structured critique (Provide Feedback), optionally via Whole-Class Sharing or written channels
  5. Revise visibly in response, narrating which suggestions you take and why — prompting learners to Self-Explanation about the reasoning behind each revision [+M]

Related Strategies¶

  • Modeling Writing and Revising — the broader practice of making expert writing processes visible
  • Peer Review — learners apply the same critique protocols to each other's work after practicing on the teacher's
  • Teacher as Learner — the general disposition of the instructor participating as a co-learner

Examples¶

  • A journalism instructor shares a draft feature article along with the editor's markup, and the class advises on how to address each edit; the instructor then revises live using their advice
  • A composition teacher shares high school, college, and graduate-school essays in sequence, and students analyze what improved across the arc and what each revision gained
  • An online writing course uses a discussion forum where the instructor posts a draft weekly and responds to threaded student critiques before submitting the final piece
