Snap It¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal), q2–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

A game where children make a train of connecting cubes, snap the train into two parts, hide one part behind their back, and show the remaining cubes. The other children work out the full number combination — inferring the hidden quantity from the visible part and the known whole. The game converts part–part–whole relationships into a concrete, social, and self-checking activity.

Design Implications¶

Snap It builds early number composition knowledge by making the "missing addend" structure physically manipulable, which reduces the working-memory demands of mental arithmetic for young children Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Because the whole is known and one part is hidden, children practice the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction rather than isolated addition facts, supporting flexible fact retrieval over rote counting strategies [~M]. The game format provides immediate, self-evident feedback — the hidden cubes can be revealed to verify answers — which keeps learners at the task and process level of correction Feedback most effective at task and process levels. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Connecting cubes (or similar countable, snappable manipulatives), one train per child
  • A known range of totals matched to children's current fluency (e.g., totals to 5, then 10)
  • A group or partner structure so one child hides and others infer
  • Norms for revealing and verifying so feedback is built into play

Constraints¶

  • Limited to addition/subtraction combinations within a small number range; it does not by itself build multi-digit or formal symbolic arithmetic
  • If totals exceed what children can subitize or count reliably, the inference task collapses into guessing [-M]
  • Children who have not consolidated counting and cardinality may only copy peers' answers rather than reason about the hidden part [-W]
  • Repeated play with the same total can encourage memorized responses without part–whole reasoning; totals and hidden quantities should vary

Implementation Variability¶

  • Vary the whole (5, 8, 10) or the hidden part to adjust difficulty; totals of 10 are especially valuable for later place-value work
  • Reverse the roles: show the hidden part and ask for the whole, or give the missing part verbally and ask children to build it
  • Progress from physical cubes to drawings and then to symbolic notation (5 + __ = 8), following a concrete–representational–abstract progression
  • Introduce a brief Think-Aloud variant where the inferring child explains "I saw 5, and 5 and 3 make 8, so 3 are hidden"

Target Learners¶

  • Primary school children (roughly ages 5–7) consolidating number combinations within 10 [~M]
  • Children transitioning from counting-all strategies to derived-fact and recall strategies; the hidden-part structure prompts decomposition rather than counting [~M]
  • Less suitable for children still developing reliable counting and cardinality, who need Practice with visible quantities first [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Part–part–whole structure of number (missing-addend and complementary subtraction)
  • Addition fact fluency within small number ranges
  • Early algebraic reasoning: reasoning about an unknown quantity from a known whole

Instructions¶

  1. Each child builds a train of connecting cubes with a agreed total (e.g., 8), counting aloud to verify.
  2. The child snaps the train into two parts and hides one part behind their back, showing the remaining part to the group.
  3. The group works out how many cubes are hidden and states the full combination ("5 and 3 make 8").
  4. The hider reveals the hidden cubes so the group can verify — built-in feedback at the task level.
  5. Rotate roles and vary the total; as fluency grows, ask children to state the matching subtraction fact (8 − 5 = 3) to link the operations.

Related Strategies¶

  • Ten Frames — another structural representation of combinations within 10; Snap It adds a hidden-information inference step
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — begin by having children build and count the whole train before any snapping
  • Math Talks — the verbal justification step ("how did you know?") extends Snap It into discourse-based practice

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — repeated, varied snapping rounds constitute distributed practice on a small fact set
  • Feedback — revealing the hidden cubes provides immediate, concrete verification
  • Manipulatives — the cubes are the concrete substrate; they should be faded toward drawings and symbols as fluency develops

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI for Math) — Snap It is a missing-addend problem type enacted physically, consistent with CGI's use of children's informal strategies
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the think-aloud variant has children articulate their inference strategy, making reasoning visible to peers

Examples¶

  • A child has 8 cubes, snaps the train, puts 3 behind their back, and shows 5. The group says three are missing and that 5 and 3 make 8; the hider reveals the cubes to confirm.
  • A kindergarten class plays with totals to 5 for two weeks, then moves to totals of 10; the teacher records children's stated combinations on an anchor chart organized by whole, making the family of combinations for each total visible.
  • A first-grade teacher follows the game with children drawing the visible part and writing 5 + __ = 8, connecting the physical game to symbolic notation.
