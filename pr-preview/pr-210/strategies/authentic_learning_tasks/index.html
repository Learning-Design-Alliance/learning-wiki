Authentic Learning Tasks¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 review, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Authentic learning tasks ask learners to engage with problems that resemble those encountered in real professional, civic, or everyday contexts — ill-structured, situated in a meaningful scenario, and directed toward a genuine purpose or audience. Rather than practicing decontextualized exercises, learners perform the discipline itself: investigating, designing, evaluating, or arguing as a practitioner would. The task, not just the content, carries the authenticity.

Design Implications¶

Authentic tasks support transfer because knowledge encoded in the context of use is more accessible when similar conditions recur Anchored instruction improves problem-solving transfer when problems are situated in realistic scenarios. [+M]. They also strengthen motivation by giving work a visible purpose and audience Autonomy and meaningful purpose support intrinsic motivation. [+M]. Authenticity is a design dimension, not a binary: tasks can be authentic in surface features (realistic artifacts) or in underlying thinking (genuine disciplinary reasoning), and the latter matters more for learning Shaffer & Resnick's "thick authenticity" argument. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A realistic problem scenario with genuine stakes, constraints, and an identifiable audience or purpose (Authentic Audiences & Purposes)
  • Access to the tools, resources, and information sources practitioners would actually use
  • Sustained time and space for investigation, iteration, and Coaching during the task
  • Assessment criteria that evaluate the quality of disciplinary performance, not just the correctness of an answer (Assessment)

Constraints¶

  • High task complexity can overwhelm novices; without Cognitive Load Management or scaffolding, learners flounder in unguided search [-S]
  • Authentic tasks consume substantial instructional time relative to the number of distinct objectives covered [-M]
  • Surface realism without disciplinary depth ("authentic-flavored" tasks) adds production cost without improving learning [~M]
  • Ill-structured tasks can leave learners uncertain about what counts as success; without clear criteria and exemplars, performance and motivation both suffer [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Case-based tasks: learners analyze and decide within a documented real case (Case-Based Learning)
  • Simulated performance: realistic role, tools, and constraints without real-world stakes (e.g., clinical simulations)
  • Real-world projects: genuine clients, data, or community partners (Community-Based Learning)
  • Anchored problem solving: realistic video or narrative scenarios designed to surface disciplinary questions, as in the Jasper Woodbury series CTGV anchored instruction. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Learners who already have foundational knowledge and are ready to apply it in variable, messy conditions Case-based learning improves exam performance when learners have sufficient prior structure. [+M]
  • Learners whose motivation depends on seeing the relevance of what they are learning Self-efficacy and perceived relevance predict persistence. [+M]
  • Novices need more structure within the authentic task (worked models, part-task practice) before full complexity is productive [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Application and transfer: using knowledge under realistic, variable conditions (Application of Knowledge)
  • Problem-solving and decision-making in ill-structured domains
  • Professional identity and practices: reasoning, communicating, and collaborating as a practitioner
  • Integration of knowledge across topics, which fragmented instruction rarely achieves [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the real-world performance the learning should enable, and the context, audience, and constraints that surround it (Authentic Audiences & Purposes).
  2. Design a task that requires that performance, with an ill-structured problem, realistic resources, and a deliverable for a real or simulated audience.
  3. Provide an entry model — a demonstration, exemplar, or Case Studies — so learners see what quality looks like before attempting the task.
  4. Structure the work in phases with Coaching, checkpoints, and opportunities for Articulation of reasoning.
  5. Assess with criteria drawn from real-world standards, ideally involving Assessment by audiences beyond the instructor.
  6. Debrief to help learners abstract general principles from the situated experience, so transfer is not confined to the original context [~M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-Based Learning — a compressed authentic task built around a documented real situation
  • Productive Failure — an authentic problem can serve as the initial exploration before instruction
  • Teach the five steps of scientific investigation through personally motivating video analysis and modeling projects

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Anchored Instruction — realistic narrative scenarios anchor problem solving
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — authentic tasks are the context for modeling, coaching, and fading
  • 4C/ID — whole authentic learning tasks form the backbone of the task class sequence

Examples¶

  • Jasper Woodbury Problem Solving series (Vanderbilt Cognition and Technology Group) — video-anchored adventures in which students solve complex mathematical planning problems CTGV anchored instruction.
  • Problem-Based Learning in medical education (e.g., McMaster University's original MD curriculum) — patient cases as authentic tasks requiring diagnosis, reasoning, and self-directed inquiry.
  • Project-based science curricula such as WISE (Web-Based Inquiry Science Environment) — learners investigate authentic scientific questions with real data and tools.
