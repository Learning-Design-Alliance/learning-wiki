Improved Air Quality¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Improved air quality is a physical-environment strategy that addresses ventilation rate, filtration, and pollutant source control in classrooms and other learning spaces. Poor indoor air quality is a major contributor to absenteeism, particularly for students with asthma, and is associated with "sick building syndrome" symptoms that depress attendance and performance for all occupants. Because bacteria, viruses, and allergens that contribute to childhood illness accumulate in poorly ventilated schools, air quality management functions as a precondition for learning rather than an instructional technique itself.

Design Implications¶

Classroom ventilation and air quality operate upstream of instruction: elevated CO₂ and pollutant levels measurably degrade attention, reaction speed, and academic performance, while improved ventilation reduces illness-related absence [~M]. The effect is strongest where baseline conditions are poor — many schools operate below recommended ventilation rates, so modest interventions (increased outdoor air supply, upgraded filtration) yield disproportionate gains. Air quality improvements are best treated as infrastructure-level Cognitive Load Management: they remove physiological interference with working memory and attention rather than adding instructional support.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Assessment of current conditions (CO₂ monitoring, humidity, allergen and pollutant screening)
  • Adequate outdoor air supply or mechanical ventilation meeting standards such as ASHRAE 62.1
  • Filtration (e.g., MERV-13 or HEPA) and source control for allergens, mold, and chemical pollutants
  • Maintenance budget and schedule — filters and ventilation systems degrade without upkeep

Constraints¶

  • Upkeep cost is the primary failure mode: systems installed without maintenance budgets revert to poor performance within months [-W]
  • Benefits are invisible and delayed (reduced absenteeism over months), making them politically vulnerable to budget cuts compared to visible instructional spending [-W]
  • In older buildings, increasing outdoor air supply can raise heating/cooling costs substantially, creating a trade-off that districts often resolve against ventilation [~M]
  • Portable air cleaners help but only in enclosed rooms; open-door or open-window strategies in noisy or polluted environments can introduce noise or outdoor pollutant problems [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Mechanical ventilation upgrades (HVAC replacement or rebalancing) — highest cost, most durable
  • Portable HEPA filtration units — low cost, immediate, requires filter replacement discipline
  • Behavioral and scheduling measures — CO₂-informed window opening, classroom rotation to reduce occupancy density
  • Asthma management programs combining air quality with school-nurse interventions for affected students

Target Learners¶

  • Students with asthma or allergies, for whom poor air quality is a leading driver of absence [-M] (i.e., they are harmed most by poor conditions and benefit most from improvement)
  • All students and teachers in buildings with inadequate ventilation; effects on performance are general, not limited to sensitive subgroups [~M]
  • Younger children, who breathe more air per body weight and are more susceptible to airborne pollutants [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Not a learning goal in itself — the strategy serves all instructional goals by increasing attendance and preserving attentional capacity
  • Health-related learning goals: schools can pair air quality improvements with student monitoring projects (measuring CO₂, graphing trends) that build data literacy

Instructions¶

  1. Assess baseline conditions. Place low-cost CO₂ sensors in classrooms; sustained readings above ~1,000 ppm indicate under-ventilation.
  2. Increase outdoor air supply or filtration. Rebalance HVAC systems, upgrade filters, or deploy portable HEPA units sized to room volume.
  3. Control sources. Remediate mold and moisture, enforce idling restrictions near air intakes, schedule cleaning with low-emission products.
  4. Maintain and monitor. Establish a filter-replacement and inspection schedule; re-check CO₂ seasonally.
  5. Integrate with health supports. Coordinate with school health staff so students with asthma benefit from both improved air and individualized management plans.

Related Strategies¶

  • Acoustics and Noise Management — the other major physical-environment factor; both operate by removing physiological interference with attention
  • Community-Based Learning — school building conditions can anchor authentic science investigations of local environmental health

Examples¶

  • Harvard Healthy Buildings program (https://healthybuildings.hsph.harvard.edu) — research and practical guidance on ventilation, filtration, and cognitive performance in buildings, including school-specific recommendations.
  • US EPA "Indoor Air Quality Tools for Schools" (https://www.epa.gov/iaq-schools) — a free action kit used by thousands of US districts to assess and improve classroom air quality, including asthma management components.
  • CO₂-informed ventilation in Danish school studies — Wargocki and colleagues' intervention studies in actual classrooms, where improving ventilation from ~2 to ~8 L/s per person improved task performance and reduced health symptoms.
