Specific Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 6 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q3 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Specific feedback directs learner attention to the exact location and nature of a strength or error — a particular claim, a piece of evidence, a transition, a sentence-level convention — rather than delivering global judgments ("good work," "unclear"). In writing instruction it addresses both the what (content: evidence, claims, analysis) and the how (organization, rhetorical strategy, style, voice, conventions). Teachers who are less comfortable with mechanics can focus feedback on content without losing effectiveness.

Design Implications¶

Feedback improves learning when it answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, and where to next?" — questions that only location-specific, actionable comments can answer Feedback is most effective when it provides cues on how to improve rather than praise or grades alone. [+S]. Vague or evaluative comments (praise, grades, generic encouragement) produce little improvement because learners cannot act on them [-S]. Specific feedback works by closing the gap between current and target performance, so it must be paired with opportunities to revise Formative assessment and feedback improve achievement when learners act on the information. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Close reading of student work, with comments anchored to specific text (margin comments, inline highlights, or timestamped voice notes)
  • A clear success model the feedback points toward (Exemplars or a rubric)
  • Time or structure for students to act on the feedback — revision, resubmission, or application to the next task (Assess Performance)
  • Feedback focused on the task, not the person; comments on the self ("you're a good writer") do not improve performance [-S]

Constraints¶

  • Time-intensive if unbounded; effectiveness drops when teachers spread comments across every error instead of prioritizing one or two focus areas [~M]
  • Feedback on work students will not revise is largely wasted effort [-M]
  • Overly dense commenting can overwhelm novice writers and trigger defensive disengagement rather than revision [~M]
  • Digital annotation tools increase comment volume but not necessarily comment quality or student uptake Checklist-style evaluation is ineffective in online feedback contexts. [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Focused feedback: limit comments to one or two dimensions per draft (e.g., evidence use on draft one, sentence craft on draft two)
  • Voice or video commentary: tools like Kaizena or Screencastify deliver location-specific feedback faster than typing and convey tone better
  • Student-requested feedback: learners specify what they want feedback on, increasing ownership and uptake
  • Peer feedback with protocols: structured sentence stems and rubrics let students deliver specific feedback to each other, which also builds evaluative judgment
  • Whole-class feedback: common patterns are addressed to the entire group with anonymized examples, reducing individual marking load

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers in K-12 and higher education, especially those revising drafts Feedback with revision opportunities improves writing quality. [+M]
  • Struggling writers benefit most from concrete, location-specific cues; global comments leave them without a next action
  • Advanced writers need less line-level correction and more strategic feedback on argument and audience [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: content, organization, evidence use, and mechanics
  • Self-regulated revision: building students' capacity to find and fix their own errors over time
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing the criteria behind the feedback

Instructions¶

  1. Establish criteria before drafting with a rubric or Exemplars so feedback has a shared reference point.
  2. Read closely and anchor each comment to specific text — quote the phrase, name the move, and state the effect on the reader.
  3. Prioritize: limit comments to the one or two issues with the highest leverage for that learner at that stage.
  4. Phrase comments as next-step cues ("Add a sentence here connecting this evidence to your claim") rather than verdicts.
  5. Require students to act on the feedback through revision or application to the next task (Assess Performance); grade the revision, not the first draft.
  6. Gradually shift responsibility: move from teacher comments toward student self-checks and Peer Feedback as evaluative judgment develops.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the broader principle that feedback must specify what to do next; specific feedback is its writing-specific application
  • Peer Feedback — students delivering location-specific comments to one another builds evaluative judgment
  • Formative Assessment — the assessment-for-learning cycle that specific feedback serves
  • Provide sentence evaluation criteria (clarity, intended audience) and model revision against them

Examples¶

  • Writing conferences (Atwell's In the Middle workshop model): brief one-to-one conferences where the teacher names one specific strength and one specific next step, which the student applies immediately.
  • Kaizena (https://www.kaizena.com): Google Docs add-on for attaching voice comments and reusable mini-lessons to specific highlighted passages.
  • NoRedInk (https://www.noredink.com): delivers immediate, item-specific feedback on grammar and mechanics errors within practice exercises.
  • Turnitin Feedback Studio: inline margin comments and voice comments anchored to exact sentences in submitted drafts.
