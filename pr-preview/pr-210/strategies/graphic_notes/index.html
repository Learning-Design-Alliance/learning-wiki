Graphic Notes¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 2 mixed) · 11 studies (5 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 11 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Graphic notes combine verbal lecture content with visual structures — diagrams, mind maps, timelines, charts, sketches, and spatial layout — so that learners record and organize spoken information graphically rather than as linear text. The instructor models or provides the visual scaffolding during presentation, and learners revisit the notes afterward as a study artifact.

Design Implications¶

Pairing spoken information with visual organization recruits both verbal and visuospatial processing channels, improving encoding and retrieval Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. The visual structure does double duty: it reduces the working-memory burden of transcribing continuous speech by giving learners pre-organized slots to fill Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M], and it makes the discourse structure of the lesson visible so learners can follow the argument as it unfolds.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Visual aids such as diagrams, graphic organizers, timelines, or sketching conventions introduced before or during the presentation (Advance Organizers)
  • Instructor modeling of how to translate speech into visual form — learners rarely invent effective graphic conventions unaided
  • A structure that mirrors the lesson's logic (sequence → timeline; hierarchy → tree; comparison → matrix), consistent with Clear Structure & Presentation
  • Time for learners to revisit, complete, or elaborate the notes afterward

Constraints¶

  • Poorly designed visuals can add extraneous load: decorative images, cluttered layouts, or graphics unrelated to the spoken content depress learning rather than help it Media combinations affect recall and retention. [~M]
  • Note-taking while listening splits attention; learners with weak transcription or handwriting fluency may capture neither the visual structure nor the content, so instructor-provided partial notes (skeletal organizers) outperform blank-page graphic notes for these learners [~M]
  • Visual formats are not universally accessible; learners with visual impairments need equivalent auditory or tactile organization cues
  • Copying an instructor's organizer verbatim produces shallower learning than generatively completing or constructing one Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-provided organizers: skeletal handouts or slides learners complete during the lecture — lowest load, best for novices
  • Modeled graphic notes: instructor builds the visual live (whiteboard, document camera, tablet sketching) while narrating decisions
  • Learner-generated formats: mind maps, Cornell-style layouts, sketchnotes, timelines — higher generative value, requires prior instruction
  • Post-hoc graphic reorganization: learners convert linear notes into a visual map after the lecture as a consolidation activity

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with auditory processing challenges, who benefit from a persistent visual trace of spoken content [+W]
  • Novices facing dense, unfamiliar content, for whom a pre-structured organizer reduces transcription load Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Learners with strong visuospatial preferences or drawing fluency, who gain most from generative sketchnoting [~W]
  • Less beneficial without adaptation for learners with visual impairments or limited graph-literacy

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension and retention of lecture-based expository content Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]
  • Relational knowledge: seeing hierarchies, sequences, and comparisons among concepts Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [~M]
  • Study-skill development: building a reusable personal note-taking system

Instructions¶

  1. Choose a graphic format that matches the lesson's structure (timeline for processes, matrix for comparisons, concept map for hierarchies).
  2. Preview the organizer with learners before the presentation so they know what to listen for (Advance Organizers).
  3. Model the translation from speech to visual form while presenting, narrating choices ("this goes under the second branch because…").
  4. Provide a partially completed organizer for learners who cannot yet generate structure themselves; fade the scaffolding over successive lessons.
  5. Prompt learners to elaborate on their notes afterward — adding examples, self-explanations, or questions in the margins (Annotating) Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].
  6. Review notes as a retrieval activity in a later session rather than rereading them passively.

Related Strategies¶

  • Annotating — graphic notes are a structured form of annotation applied to live oral content
  • Chunking — the organizer's structure pre-chunks continuous speech into manageable units
  • Advance Organizers — the preview frame that makes graphic notes usable in real time

Related Elements¶

  • Advance Organizers — the skeletal structure learners fill during presentation
  • Articulation — learners verbalizing connections represented in their notes deepens processing
  • Application — using notes to solve problems verifies that the visual organization supports use, not just storage

Examples¶

  • Sketchnoting in secondary classrooms — students use icons, spatial layout, and connectors to record humanities lectures; programs such as Verbal to Visual (https://www.verbaltovisual.com) provide curricula for teaching the technique.
  • Cornell Notes with graphic column — widely adopted AVID (https://www.avid.org) note-taking system pairs a cue/question column with a notes column, adding visual hierarchy to linear notes.
  • Mind-mapping in science lectures — students construct concept maps during units on ecosystems or cell processes, then compare maps in pairs to surface misconceptions.
  • Auditory Processing micro-credential, Friday Institute at NC State — students are taught to create mind maps, charts, or timelines when taking notes to support auditory processing challenges.
