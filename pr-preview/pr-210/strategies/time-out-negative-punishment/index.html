Time-Out (Negative Punishment)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Time-out operates on the principle of negative punishment: removing a pleasant stimulus to decrease a behavior. When a child displays an undesirable behavior, the child is removed from the desirable activity at hand and placed somewhere less desirable for a brief period, then returned to ordinary activity. Time-out is one instance of a broader category of negative consequences called exclusion — removing a learner from one setting and placing them in another.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • The activity the child is removed from must actually be desirable to the child — removing a child from an undesirable activity will backfire, since being removed becomes rewarding rather than punishing
  • A timer or other clear duration signal, so the child knows how long the time-out lasts

Constraints¶

  • Time-out is a negative consequence, not automatically an effective punisher — under behavioral-science convention, something only counts as a punisher if it demonstrably reduces the frequency of the target behavior; if records don't show the target behavior decreasing, the technique is not functioning as a punisher and should be reassessed rather than simply repeated
  • Caregiver attention during time-out can itself reinforce the very misbehavior the time-out is meant to reduce, since attention-seeking is a common reason learners persist in mistakes or misbehavior

Target Learners¶

  • Young children (the "one minute per year of age" duration guideline is aimed at early-childhood contexts)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reducing a specific undesirable behavior (e.g., hitting, throwing objects) without relying on verbal reprimand, overcorrection, or response cost

Instructions¶

  1. Warn the child once, naming the specific behavior and consequence, before applying time-out for a first instance.
  2. When the behavior recurs, remove the child calmly from the desirable activity and place them in a designated, less desirable location.
  3. Set the duration to roughly one minute per year of the child's age (e.g., five minutes for a five-year-old), using a visible timer.
  4. Remain calm while directing the child to time-out; ignore the child during the time-out itself, since attention may reinforce the misbehavior.
  5. When time-out ends, return the child to the activity with a brief hug or kind word rather than a lecture or continued discussion of the incident.

Related Strategies¶

  • Token Economies — token loss is a milder negative-punishment technique (removing an earned token rather than access to an entire activity) that can be used alongside or instead of time-out

Examples¶

  • Two children are playing with building blocks; one throws blocks at the other. After a warning, a repeat instance leads to the child being removed from the room for a duration matched to their age; on return, they resume play without the incident being revisited.
