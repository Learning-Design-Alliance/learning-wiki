Affinity Mapping¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Affinity mapping (also called affinity diagramming or the KJ method) asks learners to respond to a broad, generative question — "What were the impacts of the Great Depression?" — by writing one idea per note and posting all notes publicly. The group then clusters related notes into categories, labels the categories, and discusses how the clusters relate. Variants include silent generation and sorting before discussion, and a second round of re-clustering into new categories to reveal alternative structures.

Design Implications¶

Affinity mapping converts divergent idea generation into convergent organization, forcing learners to discriminate similarities and differences among ideas and to construct category structure themselves rather than receiving it Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure. [+M]. The physical manipulation and discussion of ideas also raises engagement relative to listening Active learning improves exam performance over lecture alone. [+S], and explaining why ideas belong together prompts self-explanation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A broad question likely to yield many distinct ideas (not one with a single correct answer)
  • One idea per note (physical sticky notes, whiteboard, or digital equivalent such as Miro or Jamboard)
  • A visible shared surface and enough space for all notes to be seen simultaneously
  • Clear phases: generate → cluster → label → relate, with time limits for each
  • A follow-on task (e.g., an analytical essay) that uses the resulting structure

Constraints¶

  • Group brainstorming can produce fewer and lower-quality ideas than individuals working alone when vocal turn-taking dominates Productivity loss in interacting groups reduces idea generation. [-S] — silent note generation before discussion mitigates this
  • Clustering is cognitively demanding; learners with weak domain knowledge may create surface-level or idiosyncratic categories [~M]
  • Large note volumes overwhelm working memory and the sorting process Chunking reduces working memory load. [~M] — cap notes or pre-filter before clustering
  • Time-consuming; without facilitation, dominant voices control placement and quieter learners disengage

Implementation Variability¶

  • Silent-first (KJ method): individuals sort silently before any discussion, reducing anchoring on early vocal contributions
  • Re-clustering: after labeling, groups re-sort the same notes under different criteria (e.g., by cause vs. effect), exposing multiple valid structures
  • Digital: tools like Miro, Mural, or Padlet allow asynchronous generation and simultaneous sorting in remote or hybrid settings
  • Individual pre-work: learners arrive with 3–5 notes already written, shifting class time entirely to clustering and discussion

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who can both generate and meaningfully categorize ideas [~M]
  • Novices benefit from the generation phase but need instructor scaffolding or exemplar categories during clustering
  • Less suitable for learners with minimal background knowledge, who lack the schema to see genuine affinities [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Divergent generation: producing many ideas before evaluation
  • Conceptual organization: identifying patterns, relationships, and category structure
  • Preparation for analytic writing: the map becomes an outline for a position or analysis paper
  • Collaborative meaning-making: negotiating shared categories surfaces and resolves differing interpretations

Instructions¶

  1. Pose a broad generative question and give 5–10 minutes for silent individual generation, one idea per note (Activation)
  2. Have all learners post notes to a shared surface without grouping or commentary
  3. In small groups, silently sort notes into clusters, then discuss and label each cluster (Collaboration)
  4. Facilitate whole-class discussion of the category labels, contested placements, and relationships among clusters (Class Discussion)
  5. Optionally re-cluster under a different criterion to show alternative valid structures
  6. Assign a follow-on writing or analysis task that uses the map as its organizing structure

Related Strategies¶

  • Brainstorming — the divergent phase affinity mapping formalizes; affinity mapping adds the convergent sorting step
  • Concept Mapping — similar structural outcome, but learner-supplied relational links rather than spatial clustering
  • Think-Pair-Share — shares the silent-individual-then-social sequence
  • Frame-aided writing procedure for authors of historical explanations

Examples¶

  • Design education: the KJ method (Kawakita Jiro, 1960s) is standard practice in UX research synthesis — teams cluster hundreds of field observation notes into affinity notes before defining design requirements (see Beyer & Holtzblatt, Contextual Design)
  • History classroom: after silent generation on "impacts of the Great Depression," students cluster notes into economic, political, and social categories, then re-cluster by short-term vs. long-term effects before drafting an essay
  • Digital: Miro's built-in Affinity Diagram template (https://miro.com/templates/affinity-diagram/) supports remote clustering with voting and color-coded grouping
