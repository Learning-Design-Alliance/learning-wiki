Skip Counting with Movement¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Make up a movement that comes in 2, 3, or more parts. Whisper the first parts, and call out the final move loudly. Example: Windmills. Whisper "1" and touch your right hand to your left foot. Whisper "2" and touch your left hand to your right foot. Call out "3" and do a jumping jack! Continue counting like this up to 30, calling out the multiples of 3 and whispering the numbers in between.

The strategy pairs verbal counting with gross-motor actions, using the loud/soft contrast to make the multiples perceptually salient within the count sequence. It is a form of embodied learning: the mathematical structure (every third number) is encoded in the body as well as the voice Embodied tasks integrated with the content to be learned support retention better than movement unrelated to content. [+M].

Design Implications¶

Movement-based counting exploits two supports for memory: rhythmic grouping, which chunks a long sequence into a repeating unit Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S], and enactment, which adds a motor trace to the verbal one Gestures and physical actions integrated with learning tasks improve recall compared with seated instruction. [+M]. The whisper/call-out contrast is the critical design feature — it isolates the multiples as the loud, accented events in the sequence, so students hear and feel the pattern rather than merely reciting it.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Open space where students can move freely
  • A movement with a clear number of parts (2, 3, 5) that maps onto the target multiple
  • A Demonstration of the full movement-count cycle before students join in
  • Repeated sessions over days or weeks, since the goal is automaticity of the count sequence Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • May not be suitable for students with physical limitations; offer seated or hand-only variants rather than excluding students
  • Can be difficult for students who struggle with skip counting — if the movement outruns the counting, working memory is overloaded and neither is learned well; slow the tempo or reduce the range before increasing it
  • Movement that is engaging but unrelated to the count can consume attention without adding learning; the action must be synchronized with and structured by the number pattern Movement integrated with content supports learning; non-integrated movement shows weaker or null effects. [~M]
  • As a whole-class chant, it can mask individual errors — students can ride along without producing the multiples themselves

Implementation Variability¶

  • Vary the multiple (2s, 5s, 10s) and the movement (jumping jacks, shoulder taps, claps, windmills) to keep the routine fresh across the year
  • Have students invent their own movement-count patterns; designing a movement with exactly n parts requires reasoning about the structure of the multiple
  • Transition to a silent variant (mouth the whispers, call the multiples) and then to a no-movement variant to check that the count sequence stands on its own
  • Use it as a transition or brain-break routine for short, frequent exposure rather than one long session

Target Learners¶

  • K–4 students building fluency with count-bys as a foundation for multiplication; adaptable to other grades for harder sequences
  • Students who benefit from multimodal input — the motor and auditory channels reinforce each other Gestures and physical actions integrated with learning tasks improve recall compared with seated instruction. [+M]
  • Less effective for students who have already automatized the count sequence, for whom the movement adds load without adding information [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Fluency and automaticity with skip-count sequences (procedural fluency)
  • Pattern recognition: hearing and feeling that multiples are equidistant in the number sequence
  • Foundational schema for multiplication as equal groups Practice toward retrieval, not conceptual derivation

Instructions¶

  1. Choose the multiple and a movement with that many parts; keep the final part the biggest and most distinctive (e.g., a jumping jack).
  2. Demonstration: perform one full cycle slowly while narrating — "whisper, whisper, LOUD" — so students see how the movement maps onto the count.
  3. Practice the movement alone without numbers until it is rhythmic, then add the count.
  4. Count together up to the target (e.g., 30 for 3s), whispering the in-between numbers and calling the multiples.
  5. Provide guidance during the count: slow the tempo when errors appear, and highlight the called-out numbers ("every time we jump, our number jumps by 3").
  6. Fade support: drop the whispers, then the movement, and check that students can produce the sequence unaided.

Related Strategies¶

  • Counting Circles — oral count-around-the-circle routine targeting the same fluency goal without movement
  • Choral Counting — public recording of count-bys that makes the pattern visible on the board, complementing the embodied version

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — the routine is repeated, low-stakes practice toward automaticity
  • Demonstration — the teacher models the movement-count mapping before students join
  • Chunking — the movement groups the sequence into a repeating unit, reducing memory load

Tools¶

  • Number charts or a hundred board posted nearby so students can visually track the called-out multiples
  • Music with a steady beat to stabilize the rhythm of the movement cycle

Examples¶

  • Skip counting by 5s: students tap their shoulders four times whispering each time, then do a jumping jack and call out the multiple of 5.
  • Skip counting by 10s with a two-part movement: whisper "1" with a knee bend, call "10" with a full jump — a deliberately short cycle for students new to the routine.
  • A grade 2 class invents movements for counting by 3s during morning meeting; each week a new student team teaches its pattern, then the class compares which multiples appeared in each pattern.
