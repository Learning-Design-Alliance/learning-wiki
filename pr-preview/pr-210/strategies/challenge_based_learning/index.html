Challenge Based Learning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 against) · 10 studies (5 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 causal), q1–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Challenge Based Learning (CBL) is a collaborative, inquiry-driven framework in which learners move from a broad "big idea" (e.g., sustainability, health) to a learner-defined challenge, investigate it through research and fieldwork, then design and implement an evidence-based solution with a real audience. Developed by Apple (2008) with educators, it structures this work through three phases — Engage, Investigate, and Act — each with guiding questions, activities, and resources.

Design Implications¶

CBL situates learning in authentic problems, which raises perceived task value and sustains engagement Learners invest more effort when they perceive a task as relevant and valuable. [+M]. Because learners work on a whole, ill-structured problem rather than isolated subskills, CBL builds transferable whole-task competence, but only when learners have enough prior knowledge to avoid being overwhelmed by unguided search Whole-task practice improves transfer of complex skills. [+M]. The framework's scaffolds (guiding questions, structured activities, checkpoints) are what distinguish it from unguided discovery.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuine, open-ended challenge connected to a "big idea" that learners can act on locally
  • Collaborative team structures with defined roles and shared artifacts (Collaboration)
  • Access to real-world resources, data, experts, or community partners
  • Sustained time blocks; CBL typically spans weeks, not single sessions
  • Facilitator skill in guiding without solving — questioning, checkpointing, and Coaching rather than lecturing

Constraints¶

  • Weakly scaffolded versions collapse into unguided discovery, which is ineffective for novices Minimal-guidance instruction underperforms scaffolded instruction for novice learners. [-S]
  • Deep content coverage can suffer; CBL must be deliberately aligned to curriculum standards or key concepts get skipped [~M]
  • Group work without structure and individual accountability allows free-riding and uneven learning [~M]
  • Assessment is difficult to standardize; success depends on authentic rubrics and process evidence, not exams alone

Implementation Variability¶

  • Scale: full-semester challenges vs. short 2–3 week mini-challenges
  • Locus of the challenge: teacher-nominated big idea with learner-defined challenge questions (typical entry point) vs. fully learner-generated challenges
  • Domain: science (environmental audits), civics (community campaigns), media/technology (app or prototype design)
  • Hybrid forms: CBL phases interleaved with direct instruction on needed concepts, blending Explicit Teaching with inquiry

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge who can conduct guided investigation without drowning in search Whole-task practice improves transfer of complex skills. [~M]
  • Adolescents and adults; younger learners need heavily simplified challenge structures and more facilitator scaffolding
  • Learners disengaged by abstract instruction — authentic purpose and audience increase persistence Learners invest more effort when they perceive a task as relevant and valuable. [+M]
  • Less suitable as a first exposure for complete novices, who need foundational Procedural Information before open investigation

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Whole-task problem solving: framing, investigating, and acting on ill-structured problems Whole-task practice improves transfer of complex skills. [+M]
  • Collaboration and communication: negotiating roles, producing shared artifacts
  • Research and information literacy: gathering, evaluating, and synthesizing evidence
  • Agency and self-efficacy: completing a real solution builds confidence that transfers to future challenges Self-efficacy predicts persistence in academic tasks. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Engage — present a big idea, have teams generate essential questions and commit to a specific, actionable challenge (Challenge Identification)
  2. Investigate — teams plan and conduct research: background reading, expert interviews, data collection, field observation; facilitator provides just-in-time instruction on needed concepts
  3. Act — design, implement, and document an evidence-based solution with an authentic audience (Act, Application of Knowledge)
  4. Assess and reflect — evaluate solution quality and process using rubrics; deliver feedback on strategy and collaboration, not just the product Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and self-regulation processes. [+S]
  5. Publish and debrief — share results publicly and reflect on what was learned and what would be done differently

Related Strategies¶

  • Project-Based Learning — CBL is a structured variant with an explicit Engage–Investigate–Act arc and a required real-world action
  • Problem-Based Learning — similar inquiry structure but typically anchored in a case rather than a learner-defined challenge
  • Case-Based Learning — provides the investigation material CBL teams often need
  • Service Learning — overlaps in the Act phase when solutions serve a community partner

Examples¶

  • Challenge Based Learning (Digital Promise / Challenge Institute) — the official framework site with implementation guides and example challenges from K-12 and higher education
  • Apple's original CBL pilots (2008–2009) — documented classrooms in six countries where students tackled challenges such as energy conservation and community health, producing measurable behavior-change campaigns
  • University engineering capstones — teams identify a local infrastructure or sustainability challenge, investigate with municipal data, and prototype solutions presented to city stakeholders
