Steelmanning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Steelmanning asks learners to articulate the most compelling, charitable version of an opposing position — stronger than its typical advocates state it — before responding to it. It is carried out by having learners research, reconstruct, and often argue for the view they reject, then compare that reconstruction against their critique. It inverts the more common "straw man" pattern of engaging with weakened versions of opposing claims.

Design Implications¶

Steelmanning leverages the finding that argumentation improves reasoning and conceptual understanding when learners must genuinely engage with counterpositions rather than dismiss them Argumentation improves reasoning and conceptual understanding. [+M]. It also exploits the "consider the opposite" debiasing effect: prompting learners to generate reasons supporting an alternative hypothesis reduces overconfidence and one-sided reasoning Considering the opposite reduces biased reasoning and overconfidence. [+S]. Because it requires holding two competing frameworks in mind, it works best when the opposing view is presented as a coherent case rather than a list of isolated claims.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuinely contestable issue with at least two defensible positions
  • Access to the best sources for the opposing view, not weak popular versions
  • An explicit product: a written or oral reconstruction of the opposing argument that its advocates would endorse
  • A norm of intellectual charity established before the activity (Community of Inquiry norms help)

Constraints¶

  • Fails when learners lack enough background knowledge to reconstruct the opposing view accurately — they produce caricatures and call them steelmen [-M]
  • Can backfire with emotionally charged identity-relevant topics if learners experience the exercise as forced belief change rather than understanding; framing as "explain, don't endorse" mitigates this [~M]
  • Time-intensive; the reconstruction phase must be assessed or learners skip to critique [-W]
  • Less useful for non-contestable content where there is no substantive opposing position

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written steelman essay before a Debate, so students argue the side they did not choose
  • Two-column analysis in Case Studies: strongest case for each stakeholder, then adjudication
  • Peer steelmanning: each student restates a classmate's argument until the classmate agrees it is accurate before responding
  • Steelman-first critique in writing instruction: the strongest counterargument must appear before the rebuttal

Target Learners¶

  • Advanced secondary and post-secondary learners with sufficient domain knowledge to reconstruct arguments accurately
  • Learners prone to one-sided reasoning or confirmation bias; the "consider the opposite" prompt is most effective for those who initially hold a confident position Considering the opposite reduces biased reasoning and overconfidence. [+S]
  • Less appropriate for novices who cannot yet distinguish strong from weak versions of a position [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Argumentation quality: anticipating and accurately representing counterarguments
  • Conceptual change: exposing learners to coherent rival explanations creates the conditions for Cognitive Disequilibrium Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]
  • Perspective-taking and epistemic humility in discussion-based and ethics-oriented courses

Instructions¶

  1. Select a contestable question and assign learners a position they currently disagree with (or the opposite of their stated view).
  2. Have learners gather the strongest sources from within the opposing camp, not critiques of it.
  3. Require a written or oral reconstruction of the opposing argument, checked for accuracy by someone who holds that view (Argument Construction).
  4. Only after the reconstruction is validated, have learners build their critique or synthesis (Argumentation).
  5. Debrief: what did the steelman change about the learner's original position, if anything?

Related Strategies¶

  • Debate — steelmanning is the preparation discipline that separates high-quality debate from slogan exchange
  • Case Studies — multi-stakeholder cases provide natural material for steelman reconstruction
  • Peer Review — restating an author's argument before critiquing it is steelmanning applied to feedback

Examples¶

  • Structured Academic Controversy (Johnson & Johnson, University of Minnesota): students argue one side, switch sides and argue the other from assigned materials, then drop roles and synthesize — the side-switch is a formal steelman.
  • AP Seminar and AP Research (College Board): scoring rubrics explicitly require "the strongest counterargument" to be presented and refuted, rewarding steelmanning in assessed writing.
  • Philosophy and ethics courses using position-reconstruction assignments: students must restate Kant's or a critic's position in its best form before offering objections.
