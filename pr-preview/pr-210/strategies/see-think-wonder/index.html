See, Think, Wonder¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal), q3–q4 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

See, Think, Wonder is a thinking routine developed by Harvard Project Zero's Visible Thinking project. Learners observe a stimulating image, artifact, or primary source, then respond to three prompts in sequence: What do you see? (pure observation), What do you think about it? (interpretation), and What does it make you wonder? (question generation). The routine is typically carried out with a three-column organizer, individual writing time, and then peer discussion and whole-class sharing.

Design Implications¶

The routine makes thinking visible and separates observation from interpretation, which trains learners to distinguish evidence from inference. Because every response is grounded in what is directly observable, every learner can succeed from the first contribution, lowering the stakes for participation and normalizing tentative or partially formed ideas active-learning-improves-exam-performance [+M]. The "wonder" step activates curiosity and generates authentic questions that can drive subsequent inquiry activation-improves-learning [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A carefully selected, engaging image, artifact, or primary source appropriate to the learners and content
  • A three-column organizer (See / Think / Wonder) or equivalent recording format
  • Protected time for individual brainstorming before sharing, so responses are not anchored by peers
  • A structure for sharing: individual response, peer discussion, then whole-class synthesis
  • A rich, ambiguous-enough artifact that rewards close looking (a photograph, artwork, graph, primary source, specimen, or code snippet)
  • Time for unhurried observation before interpretation — the "see" step must not be rushed
  • A mechanism for capturing responses (board, shared document, sticky notes) so the group's thinking is visible and reusable
  • A follow-on lesson that actually addresses the questions generated, or learners learn that wondering is decorative
  • A rich, ambiguous, or evocative stimulus (image, primary source, artwork, data display, specimen) selected to reward close looking
  • A three-column organizer or shared board with See / Think / Wonder headings
  • Individual thinking time before sharing, so responses are not anchored by peers
  • A facilitator who presses for evidence ("What in the image makes you say that?") when learners move from seeing to thinking

Constraints¶

  • Poorly chosen images produce shallow engagement; the stimulus must be rich enough to sustain multiple interpretations [-M]
  • Some students stall at surface-level observation ("I see a red shape") and need prompting or sentence stems to move to interpretation and questioning [-W]
  • The routine opens inquiry but does not by itself build deep knowledge; without follow-up investigation, wonderings remain unresolved [-M]
  • Overuse as a generic warm-up can make the three prompts feel formulaic, reducing genuine curiosity [-W]
  • With sparse or overly familiar artifacts, "see" collapses into "think" and the observation discipline is lost [-W]
  • Whole-group versions can be dominated by a few confident speakers; think-pair-share or written variants mitigate this [-W]
  • If the instructor does not connect the routine to subsequent content, it functions as an isolated activity with little durable learning benefit [~M]
  • Less effective when the learning goal is procedural fluency rather than observation, interpretation, or inquiry
  • Weakly chosen stimuli (obvious, unambiguous, or low-detail images) collapse the routine into surface description with nothing to wonder about [-M]
  • Without facilitator pressure for evidence, learners conflate seeing and thinking, and the See column fills with inferences [-M]
  • As a standalone one-off activity it produces little durable learning; it must connect to a subsequent inquiry or content sequence [~M]
  • Whole-class sharing dominated by a few voices undermines the "everyone starts from success" design; use individual writing or pair-share structures first

Implementation Variability¶

  • Object-based: use physical artifacts, specimens, or lab setups instead of images
  • Text-based: apply the three prompts to a primary source document or data display
  • Digital: use collaborative boards (e.g., Padlet, Jamboard) so learners see peers' wonderings in real time
  • Personalized: let students choose which of their three entries to share with a peer; adjust image complexity to skill level
  • Extended: return to the "wonder" column at the end of a unit to evaluate which questions the learning has answered
  • Zoom In — reveal an image in successive enlargements, running the routine at each stage to model how evidence changes interpretation
  • Written/silent variant — learners record all three columns independently before discussion, increasing participation equity
  • Chalk Talk variant — responses posted on paper and built on silently by peers, then discussed
  • Discipline-specific framing — in science, "see" becomes observations vs. inferences; in history, evidence vs. speculation; in mathematics, what the representation shows vs. what it implies
  • Zoom In variant: reveal an image in successive enlargements, running the routine at each stage to model how evidence changes interpretation
  • Ten-Two variant: ten minutes of observation, two minutes of recording, repeated — for dense stimuli
  • Silent or written-first variant: all responses posted on sticky notes or a shared digital board (e.g., Padlet) before discussion, equalizing participation
  • Disciplinary variants: in science, Wonder becomes hypothesis generation; in history, it becomes sourcing questions about a primary source; in mathematics, it can open a Three-Act Task with a perplexing image or video

Target Learners¶

  • All levels from elementary through higher education; the low-floor structure makes it especially valuable for reluctant or anxious participants
  • Multilingual learners and novices, because observation-based responses do not depend on prior vocabulary or background knowledge activation-improves-learning [+M]
  • Advanced learners benefit when images are complex and ambiguous enough to support competing interpretations [~M]
  • Multilingual learners and younger students — the routine's simple, repeated sentence stems lower the language barrier to participation
  • Learners prone to jumping to conclusions; the enforced see-before-think sequence slows and disciplines inference
  • Reluctant participants, since descriptive responses carry no risk of being "wrong"
  • All levels from elementary through higher education and museum settings; the stimulus, not the routine, carries the difficulty adjustment
  • Less productive for learners who already have deep familiarity with the stimulus, for whom the See step is redundant [~W]

  • Critical thinking: distinguishing observation from inference
  • Curiosity and question-asking: generating authentic inquiry questions
  • Activation of prior knowledge before new content activation-improves-learning [+M]

  • Observation skills: attending carefully to evidence before interpreting it
  • Inferential reasoning: justifying interpretations with reference to what was observed
  • Curiosity and question-posing: generating genuine inquiry questions that drive subsequent learning
  • Metacognition: making one's own interpretive moves visible and discussable
  • Observation and evidence discipline: separating what is present from what is inferred
  • Question generation and curiosity: building the disposition to inquire before being told
  • Interpretation and justification: linking claims to visual or textual evidence

Instructions¶

  1. Gain attention — display the image or artifact without explanation or context (Gain Attention); silence and observation time first
  2. See — learners record only what they objectively observe, no interpretation yet (Individual Response)
  3. Think — learners record interpretations, using the stem "I think… because…" to force evidence-based reasoning
  4. Wonder — learners generate questions the image raises for them
  5. Share — peer discussion of chosen entries, then whole-class sharing that surfaces cognitive conflict between differing interpretations
  6. Bridge — connect the class's wonderings to the upcoming content or inquiry investigation

Examples¶

  • Science: students observe a microscopic image of an unfamiliar cell, record observations, propose what it might be, and generate questions that launch a unit on cell structure.
  • History: students observe a primary source photograph from a historical event; competing "think" interpretations expose gaps between evidence and inference, and "wonder" questions frame document analysis (Case Studies).
  • Project Zero / Visible Thinking (pz.harvard.edu) — the routine is one of the core Visible Thinking routines, documented with classroom video and implementation guides.
  • Project Zero's Visible Thinking — the originating initiative; See_Think_Wonder is one of its core routines, with documented classroom use across K–12.
  • Library of Congress primary sources — history teachers run See_Think_Wonder on photographs and documents before teaching a unit, using student "wonders" to frame inquiry.
  • Science labs — teachers project an anomalous demonstration or data graph and run the routine before explanation, letting students' predictions create readiness for the underlying concept.
  • Project Zero / Visible Thinking (pz.harvard.edu) — the source repository, with the routine documented for arts, museum, and classroom use.
  • History class, primary source: students view an uncaptioned Lewis Hine child-labor photograph; See yields concrete details, Think yields inferences about conditions, Wonder yields sourcing questions ("Who took this? Why?") that launch a document analysis sequence.
  • Science class: students observe a time-lapse of germinating seeds; Wonder questions become student-generated hypotheses tested in the subsequent lab.
  • Mathematics, Three-Act Tasks (Dan Meyer, dy/dan) — the Act 1 image or video functions as the stimulus; "What do you wonder?" generates the question the lesson then answers.
