Communicate Quickly and Easily Through Mobile Apps¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), q4 · 2 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Mobile apps such as Remind, ClassDojo, and Seesaw give teachers a fast, asynchronous channel for communicating with parents and students about assignments, progress, and school events. By lowering the effort cost of contact — no phone calls, notes home, or scheduled meetings — they increase the frequency and timeliness of home–school information flow, which supports Check-Ins and ongoing Feedback beyond the classroom day.

Design Implications¶

Frequent, low-cost communication increases parental awareness of student work and expectations, a precondition for parental involvement that actually affects achievement [~M]. The channel itself does not improve learning; effects come from what the communication carries — timely, specific information about tasks and progress rather than generic announcements Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. Two-way messaging matters: apps that let parents respond and ask questions build relationship and trust, not just information transfer.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Smartphone or device with internet access for teachers and (ideally) families; app installation and account setup
  • School/district policy compliance for privacy, records, and appropriate teacher–student contact
  • A sustainable teacher routine — batched, scheduled messages — so communication does not become an unmanaged workload
  • Translation support for multilingual families (Remind and ClassDojo both offer built-in translation)

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on families having device access, data plans, and digital comfort; app-based channels can exclude the families who most need outreach [-M]
  • One-way broadcast use ("announcement mode") produces awareness at best and rarely changes parent behavior or student outcomes [~W]
  • Notification fatigue: high message volume trains parents to ignore alerts, eroding the channel's value [-W]
  • Text-based updates can miss nuance and are a poor substitute for face-to-face conferences on sensitive issues; some families prefer in-person contact [-W]
  • Blurred boundaries — parents expecting replies at all hours — create teacher burnout risk without clear norms [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Broadcast updates: weekly class summaries of assignments and deadlines (lowest effort, lowest impact)
  • Individual progress messages: short, specific notes about a student's work or improvement, tied to Assessment evidence
  • Two-way conferencing-lite: scheduled windows when parents can message and expect replies
  • Student-facing use: reminders and Check-Ins sent directly to older students to support self-management

Target Learners¶

  • All grade levels; strongest documented effects in early childhood and middle school, where parental oversight still directly shapes homework completion and attendance [~M]
  • Working parents and families with limited time for in-person school contact
  • Multilingual families, when translation features are enabled
  • Less effective for families without reliable device or data access — pair with SMS fallback or paper communication [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improved assignment completion and homework submission through timely reminders
  • Stronger home–school partnership supporting attendance and engagement
  • Student self-regulation: external reminders that gradually transfer responsibility to the learner

Instructions¶

  1. Choose a platform that meets district privacy requirements and offers translation (e.g., Remind, ClassDojo, Seesaw).
  2. Set communication norms at the start of term: what will be sent, how often, and expected response times.
  3. Send brief, specific progress messages tied to concrete work — task-level and process-level information, not grades alone Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  4. Invite two-way use: prompt parents to ask questions and share context about the child.
  5. Provide non-app alternatives (SMS, paper, phone) for families who cannot or prefer not to use the app.
  6. Periodically review message open rates and family feedback; prune volume to prevent fatigue.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-ins — app messaging is a low-cost vehicle for routine check-ins with students and families
  • Action-oriented feedback — progress messages work when they name the next action, not just the outcome
  • Escalating multi-channel contact immediately upon missed assessment, starting with SMS
  • Pair personalized messages with an effort to call attention to them

Related Elements¶

  • Check-in — the recurring touchpoint the app operationalizes
  • Feedback — the content that makes messages educationally meaningful
  • Assessment — progress data that grounds individual messages

Tools¶

  • Remind — two-way messaging with translation; the canonical example of this strategy
  • ClassDojo — class feeds, portfolios, and parent messaging with translation
  • Seesaw — student work portfolios shared with families, making communication artifact-based

Assessment Evidence¶

  • Track message reach and response rates by family, not just messages sent, to detect exclusion.
  • Monitor attendance, homework completion, and course performance before and after adoption.
  • Survey families annually on usefulness, language access, and preferred channels.

Impact¶

Large-scale text-messaging programs show high parent engagement and meaningful effects on student outcomes in early childhood (e.g., READY4K's literacy text program for preschool parents) and on middle schoolers' homework completion and attendance [~M]. Effects are strongest when messages include specific, actionable tips tied to classroom content rather than generic encouragement [~M].
