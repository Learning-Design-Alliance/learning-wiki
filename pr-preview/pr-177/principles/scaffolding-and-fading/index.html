    Scaffolding and Fading¶

    Principle · All principles Evidence · 11 claims (6 for, 5 mixed) · 16 studies (6 causal, 3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 associational, 2 theoretical, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 2 of 16 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

    Conditional relationship¶

    A learner who cannot yet complete a task alone, but can complete it with help, may develop more independent performance when help is contingent and then withdrawn. Contingent means the tutor or system raises support after a failed attempt and lowers it after a success. The expected change is in later unassisted performance on the target task (immediate-performance, delayed-retention, near-transfer), not in assisted performance during the lesson. The learner's task-specific starting response bounds the relationship (novice for this task, not for the domain), as do the knowledge type (procedure and complex-skill in the studies below), the setting (most evidence is one-to-one tutoring or lab, little is classroom), how support ends, and the horizon. Withdrawing support too early or too late is a hypothesis about failure, not an established threshold. The cognitive apprenticeship pattern is a reusable policy built on this relationship; its diagnostic branches are untested proposals.

    Observation, state and explanation¶

    What can be observed is a response under stated conditions: a step attempted, the help requested or given (record its level), whether the learner applied, copied or ignored that help, and what they did next without it. "Within reach with support" and "has internalised the skill" are inferred states. The same response fits several states. Assisted success shows what the learner can do with this help; it does not show what they can do once it is gone. Copying a hint and applying it can produce the same answer.

    Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
    Succeeds with a hint, fails the next comparable step without one The hint supplied the reasoning; the learner has the reasoning but cannot yet start unaided; the next step demands something the hint did not cover Give a matched step with no hint and ask for the first move and why; then give a minimal prompt (a cue, not the step) and record which level is enough.
    Uses less help over successive problems Growing competence; problems getting easier; reluctance to ask (cost, embarrassment, time) Hold problem difficulty constant and check unassisted accuracy and reasoning; ask privately why help was not requested.
    Answer is correct after support, but the learner cannot explain it Copied the support (uptake as copying, not application); has a procedure without its rationale; language or expression barrier Ask for an explanation in an accessible mode, and pose a changed case that the copied answer would not solve.
    Performance drops sharply when support is removed Support withdrawn before the step could be done unaided; withdrawal was abrupt and unannounced; the unaided task differs from the supported one Check understanding before withdrawing (ask the learner to do or explain the next step); compare a gradual and an abrupt removal on matched tasks if feasible.
    Asks for or follows support the learner no longer needs Support has become redundant (expertise reversal); habit or anxiety; the support is required by the task's own conditions Offer the task with support optional and record whether it is used and whether accuracy changes.

    These rows are proposals for interpreting a response; none has been tested with learners as a diagnostic. A probe is itself practice and can change the state it measures, so record each one. Keep the explanations that remain open rather than choosing the one whose name fits.

    Evidence and qualifications¶

    • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]. Fourth and fifth graders learning long division one-to-one under fully contingent support solved more problems than those under moderate, high, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test. The design is coded as a quasi-experiment with 8 learners per condition. In a within-subjects study of 11 tutor–student pairs (grade 8, the circulatory system), more interactive tutoring showed similar immediate learning but better transfer. A meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching. Sources: 0 of 3 entries pass and the rest could not be confirmed from abstracts. This does not predict a classroom-scale effect, and it does not show that contingent support beats explicit strategy instruction.
    • Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [+M]. Three experiments (20 ninth graders; 54 and 45 college students) compared a gradual move from complete examples to incomplete examples to independent problems with static example–problem arrangements. Results favoured fading at least for near transfer. Removing the last worked steps first did better than removing the first steps first, and learning-phase errors played a mediating role. Not settled: the abstracts could not confirm the entries. It establishes no fading rate and no far-transfer effect.
    • Backward fading and principle prompts supported transfer in probability learning [+W]. University students (78, randomised, 2×2) learned probability principles. Backward fading did better than example–problem pairs on study-defined near- and far-transfer tasks, and so did principle-identification prompts; no interaction was detected. The claim page reports reading the full paper for these contrasts, but the entry carries no codes and the judge has not yet checked it. It gives no learner-specific threshold for when to fade and no delayed outcome.
    • Scaffolding improves learning [~M]. A meta-analysis of 144 experimental studies of computer-based scaffolding in problem-centred STEM curricula reports ĝ = 0.46 on cognitive outcomes. The effect did not differ by whether or how scaffolding was faded. It supports the "support" half of this relationship and qualifies the "withdrawal" half. A pooled program-level effect forecasts no individual learner's response. Not yet checked against its sources.
    • Students' application of teacher support predicts accurate answers in small-group work [~M]. This was observational (7 Dutch grade-7 social studies teachers, 28 students, 35 lessons). Students who applied support, rather than ignoring or copying it, gave more accurate later answers (odds ratio 1.29). How contingent the support was did not predict whether it was applied. In the qualitative cases, all 7 instances of applied contingent support followed a teacher checking understanding before withdrawing, and in 5 of 7 ignored instances the teacher withdrew without checking. That account is qualitative and associational; it does not show that checking before fading causes uptake. Not settled: the abstract could not confirm the entry.
    • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]. A review of cognitive-load studies: guidance that helps novices can be redundant for learners who already hold the schema, and can depress their performance. It is the reason for withdrawal, but it does not say when an individual has crossed over. Not settled: the abstract could not confirm the entry.

    Before transporting these observations, record learners, setting (one-to-one, small group, computer-based), the support actually given and how it ended, the comparator, outcome alignment and horizon. Most contingent-support evidence comes from one-to-one tutoring on well-structured tasks, and these studies do not rank one another; their comparisons differ.

    Objective and learner-valued goal¶

    The designer's objective is usually independent performance on a representative task, at a stated horizon and with stated aids. The learner may value something else: finishing tonight's assignment, not looking stuck in front of peers, or doing the real task (a working program, a passed placement) rather than its exercise version. Ask what they want and why, and record it apart from the objective. A learner who values finishing quickly may take every hint offered. A learner who values independence may refuse help they still need. In both cases help-seeking reflects the goal as well as the learner's competence.

    For example, a student writing a lab report may want a good grade on this report, while the designer wants the student to plan and justify an analysis unaided next term. A sentence-starter template serves the first aim and may hide whether the second has been met. Agree which aim the support is for, and check the second with an unassisted report later, not with the templated one.

    What would revise this model?¶

    The expectation should weaken if contingent support fails to beat fixed or no support on unassisted, aligned outcomes for comparable learners, beyond one-to-one tutoring. It should also weaken if the gain is matched by explicit strategy instruction at lower cost, as the dynamic-assessment synthesis hints. If faded and unfaded scaffolds keep producing the same outcomes, as in the computer-based scaffolding meta-analysis, the withdrawal half of the relationship is unsupported for that setting and should be stated as a design preference only. If checking understanding before withdrawal does not change uptake when tested experimentally, drop that diagnostic branch. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "faded too early" or "faded too late" after the fact. A threshold for withdrawal must be stated before the outcome is seen.

    Assisted performance, unassisted immediate performance, delayed retention, transfer and use in the learner's own setting are separate claims. The evidence above establishes no optimal fading rate, no individual readiness criterion and no classroom-scale effect of contingency.

    Related Principles¶

    • Worked Examples — often the first stage in a scaffolded sequence.
    • Chunking — breaking the task into units that can be individually scaffolded.
    • Modeling — a type of scaffold where expert performance is demonstrated.
    • Scaffolding — the broader principle of temporary support during learning
    • Guided Practice — often provides the instructional context in which fading happens
    • Procedural Learning — many procedural tasks depend on fading from modeled or prompted performance to independence
    • Observation/Shadowing — observation often provides the first exposure to expert practice before coached participation

    Examples¶

    • Khan Academy Hints — Provides on-demand, progressive hints that scaffold a problem-solving task; the learner is encouraged to use fewer hints as they progress.
    • Codecademy Completion Problems — Initially provides full code examples, then transitions to "fill-in-the-blank" code, and finally to independent coding challenges.
    • Faded Worked Examples — A sequence where the first problem is fully worked, the second is half-worked, and the third is a full problem to solve.
