    Speed Chatting (Pre-Reading)¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Speed chatting is a pre-reading activity where learners form two rows facing each other and ask simple questions related to the reading topic. Each pair has limited time (e.g., 60–90 seconds) to discuss a question before one row rotates, creating new pairs; the process repeats several times. The rapid rotation keeps energy high, surfaces diverse prior knowledge, and gives every learner multiple low-stakes speaking opportunities before encountering the text.

    Design Implications¶

    Speed chatting works as a Activation activity: retrieving and articulating what learners already know prepares them to connect new text information to existing schemas Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+S]. The oral, social format also functions as Active Learning — learners generate and exchange ideas rather than passively receiving them Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. Because each exchange is short and structured, the activity lowers the social cost of speaking compared with whole-class discussion, which can support participation from learners who rarely contribute publicly Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+W].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A clear space for learners to form two facing rows (or an equivalent online breakout structure)
    • 3–6 pre-prepared, open but accessible questions directly related to the reading topic
    • A visible timer and a clear rotation signal so transitions stay fast
    • A brief debrief or bridge into the text so the activated knowledge is connected to the reading task

    Constraints¶

    • Time pressure and constant rotation can disadvantage anxious learners, second-language learners processing under load, or those who need longer to formulate ideas [~M] — offering think-time before rotating or written prompts mitigates this
    • Shallow questions produce shallow activation; if prompts are answerable with "I don't know," the activity generates talk without retrieval [~M]
    • Without a debrief connecting the conversation to the text, gains fade before reading begins [~W]
    • Noise and rapid movement can be unmanageable in very large or very small rooms; a seated inner/outer circle variant works better in constrained spaces

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Silent start: give 30 seconds of individual think-time or quick jotting before each rotation to improve answer quality
    • Question ladder: escalate from personal/opinion questions to text-predictive questions across rotations
    • Online adaptation: use timed breakout pairs with auto-rotation (e.g., Zoom breakout rooms or Gatheround)
    • Vocabulary preview: embed 2–3 key terms from the text into the questions so learners encounter them orally first
    • Inner/outer circles: a seated variant for classrooms without open floor space

    Target Learners¶

    • Language learners building oral fluency and topical vocabulary before reading Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]
    • Learners with relevant life experience the text will draw on — the activity surfaces it for retrieval [~S]
    • Less suitable as-is for learners with high social anxiety or slow processing speed; provide think-time or written alternatives [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Prior knowledge activation: retrieving and articulating what learners already know about a topic Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+S]
    • Prediction and hypothesis-setting: forming expectations about text content that reading will confirm or revise
    • Oral fluency and academic vocabulary rehearsal in a low-stakes format

    Instructions¶

    1. Prepare 3–6 short, open questions tied to the reading's core concepts; sequence them from accessible to text-predictive.
    2. Have learners form two facing rows; assign each row a role (asker/answerer) or have both partners discuss each prompt.
    3. Start a 60–90 second timer; learners discuss the question with their partner (Peer Discussion).
    4. Signal rotation: one row shifts one position so every learner meets a new partner; pose the next question.
    5. Repeat until all questions are exhausted (typically 4–6 rotations).
    6. Debrief briefly as a whole group (Class Discussion), collecting a few ideas per question, then bridge directly into the reading with a purpose-setting prompt.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Activating Prior Knowledge — the broader family of pre-reading activities; speed chatting is a fast, social variant
    • Think-Pair-Share — same pair-talk structure without rotation; a calmer alternative for anxious groups
    • Anticipation Guides — a written pre-reading prediction activity that pairs well with or substitutes for oral chatting

    Related Elements¶

    • Activation — the cognitive function this strategy serves
    • Peer Discussion — the core interaction mechanism
    • Check-In — a lower-intensity social warm-up that can precede speed chatting
    • Advance Organizers — a structure the debrief can feed into before reading

    Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

    • Discussion-Based Learning — speed chatting as a warm-up that seeds later full-class discussion
    • Experiential Learning Cycle — the rotations function as concrete experience preceding reflective reading

    Examples¶

    • Before reading an article about climate change, learners rotate through prompts such as "What are the main causes of climate change?" and "How does climate change affect daily life where you live?", then read to check their predictions.
    • In an ESL reading course, an instructor embeds target vocabulary ("sustainable," "emissions") into speed-chat questions so learners rehearse pronunciation and meaning orally before meeting the terms in the text.
    • Facing History and Ourselves (https://www.facinghistory.org) uses structured pre-reading conversation protocols, including rapid pair exchanges, to activate learners' prior beliefs before engaging with primary-source texts.
