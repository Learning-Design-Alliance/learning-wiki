    Note-Taking/Note-Making¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Note-taking is the process of recording key points during a lesson or from a text; note-making is the generative follow-up in which learners transform those records into their own words, questions, summaries, and connections. The distinction matters because the two activities engage different cognitive processes: taking captures information, while making requires learners to restructure and elaborate it. Structured formats such as the Cornell method build note-making in as a dedicated stage (cue column and summary), rather than leaving it to chance.

    Design Implications¶

    Note-taking supports learning through two mechanisms: external storage for later review and encoding that occurs during generation itself [~M]. The encoding benefit depends on learners actively transforming material rather than transcribing it verbatim — verbatim transcription yields weaker learning than generative summarizing [~S]. Note-making activities are a form of Active Learning: the learner must select, organize, and integrate ideas rather than passively receive them.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Source material (lecture, text, video) with a discernible structure worth capturing
    • Explicit instruction in note-taking technique — students do not intuitively know how to select main ideas [~M]
    • A dedicated note-making stage: cues, questions, summaries, or personal connections, as in the Cornell cue and summary columns
    • Opportunities to use the notes (review, discussion, drafting), since notes that are never revisited contribute mainly their encoding benefit

    Constraints¶

    • Heavy transcription demands during fast, dense lectures consume working memory and reduce encoding [-M] — the more verbatim the notes, the weaker the learning benefit
    • Providing full notes or slides can reduce note-taking engagement; learners given instructor notes often review instead of encoding [~M]
    • Learners with weak prior knowledge struggle to distinguish main ideas from detail, producing poor notes regardless of format [-M]
    • Reviewing notes is only effective when it involves active reconstruction; passive rereading of notes yields limited benefit [-S]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Cornell format — page divided into cues, notes, and summary; forces a note-making pass after the lesson
    • Guided notes — instructor provides a skeleton with blanks; reduces transcription load for novices [~M]
    • Sketchnoting / visual notes — combines words and images, leveraging dual coding
    • Collaborative notes — shared documents where groups co-construct and compare records
    • Post-hoc note-making — students annotate or restructure raw notes a day later as a spaced retrieval and elaboration activity

    Target Learners¶

    • Secondary and post-secondary students encountering content-dense material
    • Novices benefit from guided note structures that offload selection decisions [~M]; as expertise grows, external structure should fade Expertise reverses the benefit of high-support formats. [~M]
    • Students with slow handwriting or processing-speed challenges benefit from guided notes or instructor-provided partial notes [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Factual and conceptual retention from lectures and texts
    • Comprehension monitoring — the note-making stage surfaces gaps and confusions
    • Study-skill development: selecting, organizing, and integrating information as a transferable skill

    Instructions¶

    1. Prepare learners with an Advance Organizer so they know what structure to listen for.
    2. During the lesson, students take notes — recording key points in their own words where possible; teach selection explicitly rather than assuming it.
    3. After the lesson, students make notes: write cue questions, a summary, and personal connections (Cornell cue/summary columns, or a structured prompt).
    4. Have students use the notes within days — Practice via retrieval, discussion, or drafting — rather than rereading passively.
    5. Model the process with a Think-Aloud showing how an expert decides what to record and how to condense it.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Annotating — note-making applied directly to a text rather than a separate page
    • Summarization — the core generative act within note-making
    • Retrieval Practice — converting notes into cue questions turns review into retrieval

    Examples¶

    • Cornell note-taking system (Pauk & Owens, How to Study in College) — the canonical two-column format with a mandatory summary stage; widely adopted in AVID college-readiness programs (https://www.avid.org).
    • Guided lecture notes in introductory biology — instructor-provided skeletons with blanks and embedded concept questions, letting novices attend to explanation rather than transcription.
    • Sketchnoting in history classes — students combine spatial layout, icons, and text to restructure a lecture's argument visually.
