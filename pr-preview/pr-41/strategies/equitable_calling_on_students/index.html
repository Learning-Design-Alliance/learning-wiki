    Equitable Calling on Students¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Equitable calling on students is the deliberate practice of distributing response opportunities across all learners during discussion and questioning, rather than allowing a handful of eager volunteers to dominate. It is carried out through techniques such as random selection (name cards, popsicle sticks, digital pickers), tracking who has been called on, and using wait time so that every learner — not just the fastest hand-raisers — is expected to formulate an answer. The underlying premise is that learners who are never called on disengage, while those who are systematically overlooked internalize lowered expectations.

    Design Implications¶

    Equitable response distribution converts questioning from a volunteer-driven activity into a universal participation expectation, which raises engagement and keeps learners cognitively active throughout a discussion Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture-only formats. [+S]. Teacher expectations are a powerful driver of achievement: learners who are given fewer opportunities to respond receive less practice, less feedback, and implicitly lower expectations, which compounds over time Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]. Structured calling-on practices work best when paired with adequate wait time — extending pauses after questions from under one second to three or more seconds increases the length and quality of student responses and broadens participation [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A tracking mechanism (clipboard roster, name cards, or a digital randomizer) so the instructor can monitor who has and has not responded
    • Established norms that being called on is expected and safe, communicated early in the course
    • Sufficient wait time after each question so cold-called learners can think rather than be caught out
    • Questions calibrated so every learner can attempt a response — scaffolds, sentence stems, or think time for harder items

    Constraints¶

    • Cold-calling without psychological safety increases student anxiety and can reduce willingness to participate voluntarily [-M] — anxious learners may comply behaviorally while disengaging cognitively
    • In large classes, tracking individual responses is labor-intensive and can consume instructional time
    • Learners with severe social anxiety may need opt-out or preparation mechanisms (e.g., advance notice of questions, written responses first); uniform cold-calling ignores this variability [-W]
    • If questions are recall-only, equitable calling distributes low-level participation without deepening thinking [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Random calling (name cards, wheel apps) removes instructor bias but can feel arbitrary; announcing the random system in advance preserves fairness perceptions
    • Warm calling — previewing a question with a learner ("Maya, I'll ask you about this in a minute") before cold-calling publicly
    • Think-pair-share before calling — learners rehearse an answer with a partner first, lowering the stakes of public response
    • Hand-raising with norms — calling on non-raised hands occasionally while still honoring raised hands, rather than banning hand-raising entirely
    • Equity sticks with passes — learners may pass a limited number of times per term, preserving autonomy while maintaining the expectation

    Target Learners¶

    • Reluctant or quiet learners who would otherwise never volunteer, and who benefit from structured response opportunities Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
    • Learners from groups historically subject to low teacher expectations, for whom equitable response distribution signals high expectations [+M]
    • High achievers benefit less from being called on more — they already dominate response opportunities; the strategy redistributes rather than adds

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Engagement and participation norms: establishing that every learner's thinking is expected and valued
    • Formative assessment: equitable calling gives the instructor a representative sample of class understanding rather than a volunteer-biased one Assessment for learning depends on eliciting evidence of all learners' thinking. [+M]
    • Oral reasoning and academic discourse: regular structured opportunities to articulate thinking aloud

    Instructions¶

    1. Establish the norm explicitly: explain that everyone will be called on, that think time is provided, and that partial answers are welcome.
    2. Pose the question to the whole class, then wait 3–5 seconds before selecting a responder — wait time improves response quality for everyone [+M].
    3. Select a responder using your tracking system (random cards, roster, or deliberate rotation toward under-called learners).
    4. If the learner struggles, scaffold rather than rescue: offer a hint, narrow the question, or allow a partner consult — then return to the learner for the answer.
    5. Record who responded; over successive sessions, deliberately rotate toward learners with fewer opportunities.
    6. Follow responses with feedback that treats every answer as data for discussion, not a verdict on the learner.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Wait Time — the questioning companion: equitable calling without wait time becomes equitable pressure
    • Think-Pair-Share — lowers the stakes of public response before cold-calling
    • Class Discussion Norms — the safety conditions that make cold-calling productive rather than punitive

    Examples¶

    • Teach Like a Champion "Cold Call" technique (Lemov, 2010) — a widely adopted K-12 protocol: predictable, positive-tone cold calling with warm-up questions, used across Uncommon Schools network classrooms.
    • Equity sticks in elementary classrooms — popsicle sticks with each student's name drawn randomly; the stick is set aside until all names have been drawn, guaranteeing full rotation.
    • Random name pickers in large lectures — tools such as the "Random Student Generator" in learning-management systems or wheel apps let instructors of 100+ student courses maintain equitable distribution without manual tracking.
