    Word Study¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Word study replaces rote spelling memorization with structured investigation of how words work. Students examine sets of words with shared features — sounds, spelling patterns, meanings, or morphological elements — and compare and contrast them to discover spelling generalizations themselves. Instruction is differentiated by developmental spelling stage, typically assessed with a spelling inventory, so each group works on patterns at the edge of what they already control.

    Design Implications¶

    Word study operationalizes Active Learning for orthography: learners induce the rule from sorted exemplars rather than receiving it as a stated fact to memorize. Its core activity — sorting words into categories and contrasting minimal pairs (e.g., hop/hope, hard c/soft c) — is a direct application of case comparison, which reliably improves discrimination and transfer Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+S]. Because pattern knowledge generalizes to untaught words, word study builds the automatic word recognition that frees working memory for comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A developmental spelling inventory (e.g., the Words Their Way Elementary Spelling Inventory) to place students in stages — emergent, letter name–alphabetic, within-word pattern, syllables and affixes, derivational relations
    • Carefully selected and sequenced word sets that make the target pattern salient and contrast it with a known pattern
    • Hands-on materials: word cards for sorting, word study notebooks, and word walls for accumulating discovered patterns
    • Teacher skill in facilitating discovery — asking "What do you notice?" rather than stating the rule — while knowing when to supply the generalization explicitly

    Constraints¶

    • Pure discovery is not sufficient for all learners: some students, especially those with weak phonological awareness or dyslexia, need the pattern named and explained explicitly rather than induced [~M] — word study works best as guided discovery, not unguided exploration
    • Requires a substantial shift from traditional Friday-test drill; teachers accustomed to memorization-based spelling may implement sorts as new forms of drill, losing the comparison and generalization work [-W]
    • Differentiated grouping multiplies the teacher's preparation and facilitation load; poorly managed groups default to unstructured play with word cards [-W]
    • Assessment must change accordingly: testing only the studied list rewards memorization and masks whether pattern knowledge transferred to new words [-M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Open sorts (students devise their own categories) vs. closed sorts (teacher-defined categories) — open sorts suit consolidation; closed sorts suit initial pattern introduction
    • Speed sorts, blind sorts, and writing sorts — successive variations that shift from sound-based to visual to memory-based sorting as the pattern consolidates
    • Word hunts — students search authentic reading and writing for words fitting the pattern, connecting word study to real text
    • Digital adaptations — drag-and-drop sorting tools and interactive word walls replicate the physical sort in online settings

    Target Learners¶

    • Elementary and middle school students progressing through developmental spelling stages
    • Struggling spellers and readers who need pattern-level understanding rather than more list memorization; for these learners, pairing sorts with explicit articulation of the generalization is important [~M]
    • English learners, who benefit from sorts that contrast the orthographic patterns of English with those of their first language [+W]
    • Less appropriate as-is for students still at the emergent stage, who need direct phonological and alphabetic instruction before pattern comparison is meaningful [-M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Orthographic pattern knowledge: spelling generalizations that transfer to untaught words
    • Automatic word recognition supporting reading fluency and comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S]
    • Vocabulary and morphology: meaning-based sorts (prefixes, suffixes, Latin/Greek roots) at upper stages
    • Analytical habits: comparing, categorizing, and hypothesizing about linguistic evidence

    Instructions¶

    1. Assess and group. Administer a spelling inventory; group students by developmental stage rather than grade level.
    2. Select and sequence words. Choose 15–25 words that exemplify the target pattern and contrast it with a previously mastered pattern, keeping set size within working memory limits Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S].
    3. Model the sort. Demonstrate categorizing a few words aloud, naming the features being noticed — a brief Cognitive Apprenticeship-style model before students sort independently.
    4. Sort and discuss. Students sort word cards, then explain their categories to a partner; require verbalization of the discovered generalization, not just correct placement.
    5. Provide feedback and reflect. Confirm or correct sorts, prompt students to state the rule in their own words, and record it in the word study notebook.
    6. Extend and transfer. Assign a word hunt in authentic text and a writing sort with new, untaught words to assess whether the pattern transferred.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Word Sorting — the core activity of word study; sorting by sound, pattern, and meaning features drives the comparison work
    • Word Hunts — transfer activity connecting the studied pattern to authentic reading and writing
    • Dictation and Writing Sorts — assessment variants that test pattern application rather than list recall

    Examples¶

    • Words Their Way (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — the most widely implemented word study program; developmental stages, spelling inventories, and hundreds of ready-made sort sets (pearson.com)
    • Hard c / soft c sort — students sort cat, cot, cup against cent, city, cycle, discover the role of the following letter, then hunt for both patterns in a shared reading text
    • Within-word pattern sorts for long vowels — contrasting rain, play, trade to induce the generalization that long vowels have multiple spellings, then applying it to spell new words in writing
    • Derivational relations sorts in middle school — grouping inspect, spectator, respect to connect the root spect ("look") across spelling and meaning, building morphological vocabulary knowledge
