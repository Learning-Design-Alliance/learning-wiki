    Expecting Excellence in PBL¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Expecting excellence in PBL means setting ambitious, public standards for project quality and communicating — through task design, feedback, and revision structures — that every student can meet them. It actively counters the deficit belief that project-based learning only works for already-privileged or high-achieving students, pairing high expectations with the scaffolding and knowledge of students' current realities needed to reach them.

    Design Implications¶

    Teacher expectations function as self-fulfilling prophecies: expectations shape instructional decisions, which shape student outcomes [~M] — the effect is real but smaller than popularly claimed, and it operates most powerfully on students already at risk of being underestimated (Jussim & Harber, 2005). In PBL, where tasks are open-ended and success depends on sustained effort over weeks, low expectations manifest as reduced scaffolding, narrowed task options, and lowered quality bars — all of which depress achievement. The remedy is not expectation-raising alone but expectation-plus-support: challenging goals combined with contingent scaffolding produce better outcomes than either challenging goals alone or softened goals Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Publicly stated quality criteria (rubrics, exemplars of strong work) that define "excellence" concretely rather than aspirationally
    • Scaffolding calibrated to students' current realities — prior knowledge, language demands, and available out-of-school support
    • Structured revision cycles so students can close the gap between first drafts and the standard
    • Feedback that targets task and process rather than the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]

    Constraints¶

    • High expectations without scaffolding produce failure and disengagement, particularly for students with weak prior knowledge — the expectation effect does not substitute for instructional support [-M]
    • Expectation effects are fragile: brief one-shot interventions (e.g., single "high expectations" messages) show weak and inconsistent durable effects [-W]
    • Teachers' implicit biases can persist under the language of "high standards," leading to harsher evaluation of some groups' work rather than better support [~M]
    • If excellence is defined only through culturally narrow formats (e.g., polished presentation style), the standard itself becomes a barrier rather than a ladder [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Whole-school cultures of excellence (e.g., EL Education's "Deeper Learning" crew structures) versus single-classroom implementation
    • Public critique protocols (gallery walks, tuned peer critique) versus private teacher-student revision conferences
    • Differentiated scaffolding within a common high standard — same destination, varied routes

    Target Learners¶

    • Students historically underserved by traditional instruction, who are most vulnerable to lowered expectations and reduced scaffolding [~M]
    • Students whose self-efficacy is low; experiencing success on genuinely challenging work builds persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
    • All students benefit, but the strategy is most consequential where adults' expectations have historically been lowest

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Quality of product and process: producing work that meets authentic professional or disciplinary standards
    • Persistence and revision: sustaining effort through critique-and-improve cycles
    • Academic identity: students coming to see themselves as capable of rigorous work Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]

    Instructions¶

    1. Define excellence concretely: publish rubrics and show annotated exemplars of strong prior work so the standard is visible, not implied.
    2. Launch projects with challenging, authentic goals; specific difficult goals outperform vague or easy ones Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S].
    3. Diagnose students' current realities — prior knowledge, language demands, constraints outside school — before assigning support.
    4. Scaffold toward the standard, fading support as competence grows Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]; use Coaching during work time rather than front-loading all instruction.
    5. Build in structured critique and revision: use Class Discussion protocols (e.g., gallery critique) so peer expectations reinforce teacher expectations.
    6. Give process-focused feedback and require revision before final assessment Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
    7. Celebrate finished work publicly with an authentic audience Authentic Audiences & Purposes, making the culture of excellence visible to the community.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Scaffolding in PBL — the support side of the expectation-plus-support pairing
    • Peer Critique Protocols — structures that distribute high standards across the classroom
    • Growth Mindset Framing — complements expectations by shaping students' beliefs about their own capacity

    Related Elements¶

    • Coaching — the in-process support that makes high standards reachable
    • Collaboration — team structures let students hold each other to shared quality norms
    • Assessment — rubric-based assessment operationalizes "excellence" as criteria rather than comparison

    Tools¶

    • EL Education models (elseducation.org) — published student exemplars and critique protocols from high-expectation PBL schools
    • High Tech High (hightechhigh.org) — project exemplars and public exhibitions of student work

    Examples¶

    • EL Education (Expeditionary Learning) schools publish "models of excellence" (https://elschools.org/models-of-excellence) — real student work held to professional standards — and use crew-based critique so every student revises toward public quality criteria.
    • High Tech High (San Diego) requires all students to exhibit polished project work to public audiences, refusing tracked or watered-down versions of projects for students entering with weak academic records.
    • A teacher launching a documentary project shows a strong prior student film with annotations explaining why it works, sets a rubric requiring multiple revision cycles, and schedules conferencing checkpoints — the same bar for all, with varied scaffolding.
