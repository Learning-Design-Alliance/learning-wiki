    Conditional relationship¶

    This page owns the expert consultation: a learner brings a specific question, attempt or piece of work to someone with more expertise in the domain (an instructor, practitioner, mentor or more knowledgeable peer), and the expert responds to it. For a learner who is novice or intermediate on the task (they can attempt it, but their attempt stalls, misses what matters, or they cannot say what a good result would look like), a consultation in which the expert first has the learner explain their attempt, then responds contingently (a question, then a hint, then modelling the decision aloud, explanation last), and in which the learner applies what they heard to a revision or a new attempt soon afterwards, is expected to produce better unaided performance on that task (immediate-performance, near-transfer) than either no access to the expert or an expert who simply supplies the answer. Across a run of consultations the expert does less and the learner more. The knowledge type is usually a principle or complex-skill where expert judgement is not visible in the finished product (a diagnosis, a design critique, a revision decision, a debugging choice); the settings range from one-to-one tutoring to classroom office hours and workplace-clinical mentoring.

    The relationship is conditional on what the expert does, not on their expertise alone: the nearest evidence suggests the learning comes from what the learner is prompted to construct, and one meta-regression found no tutor-background category (content experts included) that predicted learning in problem-based courses. It is also conditional on the learner's starting response (an advanced learner gains less from explanation and more from critique), on whether the advice is applied before it is forgotten, and on whether the learner can ask at all without social cost. No claim in the wiki tests access to an expert, as such, against no access, or compares ways of running a consultation. The nearest evidence is from one-to-one tutoring (contingent against fixed support; prompting against explaining), from prompted self-explanation, from fading support, and from process against outcome goals, all carried here to a setting they did not test.

    How the converted siblings sit beside this: Scaffolding and Fading holds the general model of raising support after a failure and lowering it after a success, and its withdrawal; this page applies it to one kind of encounter and does not repeat it. The Cognitive Apprenticeship pattern is the full modelling–coaching–scaffolding–fading–articulation–exploration sequence, of which a consultation is usually the coaching or articulation step. Guided Practice holds the practice phase after a model. Communities of Practice holds sustained participation alongside experienced members over a term or more; a single consultation, or a short series of them, is the narrower object here.

    Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

    The earlier page's guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; its evidence status is stated beside it.

    1. Bring real work, prepared. Before each consultation the learner writes a short question card: the task, what they tried, where it stalled, and what they think the answer might be. Unguided "ask me anything" sessions are replaced by consultations around an artefact, case or attempt. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement of "a concrete learner question, artifact, case, or task".
    2. Learner explains first. The expert opens by asking the learner to talk through their attempt and their reasoning at the point where it stalled, before saying anything about it (about the first third of the time). Carried from prompted self-explanation [+S] (learners studying examples or solving problems, not consultations) and from tutoring where prompting alone matched explaining-and-feedback (tutoring claim [~M], eighth graders, 11 tutor–student pairs).
    3. Respond on a ladder, smallest help first. A question that points at the decision; then a hint; then the expert thinking aloud through the same decision on a parallel case, naming the cues they look at and the criteria they apply; a full explanation only if those fail. Evidence: contingent support [+M] beat fixed or no support in one-to-one tutoring of children on long division, and interactive tutoring (questions and hints) gave better transfer than explaining; untested for experts consulted by older learners.
    4. Point at process and criteria, not only the verdict. Name the strategy or criterion the learner's work needs ("what would you check before choosing this?"), not just whether it is right. Carried from process goals [+M] (high-school writing revision; once the skill was established, outcome goals helped), which is about goal-setting, not expert talk.
    5. Close with one next action. End each consultation with the learner stating, in their own words, the one or two changes they will make and why. Untested proposal.
    6. Apply within a week. The learner revises or attempts a comparable task within about a week (sooner for a single-session visit), and brings the revision to the next consultation or submits it for brief written comment. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("expert advice that is not applied quickly is easily forgotten").
    7. Hand responsibility back across the series. A default arc of three to five consultations over a term, 15–20 minutes each per learner or small group (untested quantities): early, the expert models and explains on parts of the learner's work for up to half the time; middle, the learner presents attempt and diagnosis first and the expert asks and hints; late, the learner proposes the critique of their own work and the expert confirms, corrects or adds one point. Move a learner to the next stage when, on two consecutive consultations, they name the criterion the expert would have applied before the expert does, and their revision deals with the previous points without further help. The shape follows fading support [~S] (faded worked examples, near transfer); the thresholds are untested proposals.
    8. Make it safe to be uncertain. Ask for questions in writing or in small groups as well as aloud, accept partial and wrong ideas without comment on the person, and let low-confidence learners bring a question with a peer. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement of psychological safety.

    When a condition is not met: if the expert has no time to see each learner, collect question cards in advance and spend the session on the three or four commonest stalls, thinking aloud through each; if no expert is available live, record the expert thinking aloud through two or three representative pieces of work and pair it with a written response to each submitted question card.

    Fitting the design to a situation¶

    The three facts that most change the decision:

    • What the learner can already do and say about the task. A learner who cannot yet attempt the task needs a model before a consultation is useful; one who can already diagnose their own errors needs critique, not explanation. If unstated, ask: can the learner attempt one representative task now, and say where it went wrong?
    • How much of the expert's time each learner gets, and in what form. The evidence for contingent help is one-to-one; a guest visit, a drop-in office hour and a weekly mentor meeting allow very different designs. If unstated, ask: how many learners per expert, how many minutes each, how often, and live or written?
    • What the expert is expected to do. A practitioner with no teaching experience tends to tell; the design may need to brief them to ask first and to think aloud. If unstated, ask: who is the expert, have they taught or mentored before, and will someone brief them?

    Observation, state and explanation¶

    What can be observed in a consultation is what the learner brought, what they said when asked to explain it, which level of help was needed (question, hint, model, explanation), what they said they would change, and what the next revision or attempt shows. "Understands the expert's reasoning", "is dependent on the expert" and "is afraid to ask" are inferred states. Agreement during the conversation is not evidence of understanding; the next unaided attempt is.

    Evidence and qualifications¶

    No claim in the wiki tests consulting an expert against not doing so, or compares ways of running a consultation. The claims below test neighbouring parts of the relationship.

    • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fourth and fifth graders learning long division one-to-one solved more problems under fully contingent support than under fixed, partly contingent or no support, at an immediate and a one-month test (8 per condition, q3, no effect size printed); in tutoring of eighth graders, interactive tutoring (questions and hints) gave similar immediate learning but better transfer than explaining. A meta-analysis of dynamic assessment ranked explicit strategy training above scaffolding and scaffolding above coaching, which qualifies a consultation built only on prompting. The claim's Discussion notes that most evidence is one-to-one and that contingency is scarce in whole-class teaching.
    • Human tutoring has a medium effect over no tutoring, and in one small study students learned as well when tutors only prompted them as when tutors also explained and gave feedback [~M]: a review reports human tutoring at d = 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems at d = 0.76 against no tutoring (abstract only); in one study of eighth graders (11 pairs), tutors told to withhold explanations and feedback and only prompt produced learning as good as ordinary tutoring. The tutors were inexperienced college students, not domain experts. It supports "learner explains first" and qualifies the value of the expert's own explanations.
    • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S]: a meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes from 64 reports found that prompting learners to self-explain while studying or solving problems improved learning by a moderate average amount (g = .55), across task types, subjects and levels; a mathematics meta-analysis found gains in classroom settings and at a delay much less established. It is evidence for prompts, not for expert consultations; the page carries it to the expert's opening question.
    • Fading support promotes the transfer of responsibility from instructor to learner. [~S]: across three experiments (ninth graders and college students), fading worked examples into incomplete examples and then problems favoured near transfer over static example–problem arrangements; a review names fading as one of the three defining features of scaffolding. It is marked ~ because it is a condition on expert help (help that never fades), not support for expert help as such. It does not establish a fading rate, or that fading prevents long-term dependence.
    • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M]: high-school students learning writing revision with process goals outperformed those with outcome goals, and shifting to outcome goals once the skill was established was beneficial (sample size not stated on the claim page), with a theoretical chapter synthesising similar findings. It concerns goals the learner sets, carried here to what the expert directs attention to.
    • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies; the reason to shift advanced learners from explanation to critique. It gives no point at which a learner crosses over.

    The limiting claims. In a re-analysis of PBL tutor-background data, neither a meta-regression nor subgroup comparisons found that tutor expertise predicts student learning [~M] and its companion, In a re-analysis of PBL tutor-background data, neither a meta-regression nor subgroup comparisons found that tutor expertise predicts student learning [~M], re-analyse one problem-based-learning data set: content-expert tutors did not produce more learning than other tutor backgrounds. A non-significant difference does not show equivalence, and PBL tutors facilitate groups rather than answer individual questions, but it is the nearest test of whether domain expertise by itself matters, and it does not show that it does. In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems [~M] qualifies in the other direction: in secondary writing, more expert peers gave more helpful feedback but were not more accurate at finding problems, and with a well-specified rubric peer ratings were as valid in lower- as in higher-performing schools. Its own Discussion says the evidence does not support its title.

    Learners, tasks and settings differ across these claims (children with a tutor, eighth graders, high-school writers, PBL courses), so do not rank them by effect label. None measures a learner's later unaided performance after consulting a domain expert against a comparison.

    Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

    Claims bearing on expert access that the earlier page did not cite, listed so they are not lost. Each says what it is and how far it bears on this page.

    • Mentor modelling and a school-wide behaviour program's strategies transferred to the mentee's classroom management practices [+W] — one preservice teacher's field experience, qualitative; modelled strategies appeared in the mentee's teaching. Supports modelling within a mentoring relationship; one case.
    • Teachers without mentors reached the impact level more often than mentored teachers, raising the question that mentoring may inhibit development [~W] — 39 first-year and re-entry teachers, associational, volunteer groups; a question the authors raise, not a finding about mentoring's effect.
    • Tutoring center visits usually showed no significant difference in individual course performance, and sometimes tutored students performed worse [~M] and High-use tutoring center visitors had significantly higher cumulative GPA than non-visitors, with the main difference between >10-visit and no-visit groups [~W] — one observational study of a college drop-in centre; self-selection makes both readings uncertain. Bears on office-hours designs only by analogy.
    • Cognitive apprenticeship instruction produced no significant differences from traditional instruction on post-treatment problem-solving, final, and standardized mathematics exams in community college technical mathematics [~M] — one quasi-experiment, two teachers, 64 students enrolled; bears on the larger sequence an expert consultation sits inside, not on the consultation.
    • Passing individual microphones discouraged some students from asking questions during telelectures [~W] — students' reports from one 1972 telelecture course; bears on the format of asking.

    Objective and learner-valued goal¶

    The designer's objective is usually better unaided performance on a representative task, with the learner able to apply the criteria an expert would apply, at a stated horizon. Elicit separately what the learner wants from the expert: a verdict on today's work, reassurance, a shortcut, contact with someone in the profession they hope to join, or to understand how experts think. Agreement and divergence are both observations. A learner who wants a verdict will treat questions as delay; one who wants contact with the profession may value the conversation more than the advice.

    For example, a nursing student may want to hear from an experienced nurse what the job is really like, while the designer's objective for the consultation is prioritising patient observations. A consultation that answers only the objective may leave the learner's reason for coming unmet, and one that answers only the learner's question teaches no criterion. Record both, so that a well-liked visit is not read as a learning gain, and a useful critique is not read as a failure because the learner found it uncomfortable.

    What would revise this model?¶

    The expectation should weaken if studies comparing consultations with domain experts against comparable time on practice with worked feedback show no advantage in later unaided performance, or show that a gain at the end of a consultation disappears at a delay. If prompting-only consultations match those with expert explanation across settings, as one tutoring study suggests, the model should say "an expert who prompts" rather than "an expert who explains". If content expertise continues not to predict learning when tutors' behaviour is held constant, what matters is the consulting skill, and the page should say so in its title. If structured peer consultation with a rubric matches expert consultation for some goals, the expert's time should be reserved for the goals where it does not. Do not protect the model by calling every failure "the wrong expert" or "not enough follow-through" after the fact.

    The present evidence establishes no length or frequency of consultation, no number of consultations in a series, and no criterion for withdrawing the expert.

    Related Principles¶

    • Mentoring/Coaching — a broader relationship structure in which expert guidance is sustained over time rather than episodic
    • Communities of Practice — extends expert access into sustained participation in a practice community
    • Guided Practice — turns expert explanation into coached performance on real tasks
    • Explaining Their Thinking — experts are most useful when they elicit learner reasoning rather than replacing it
    • Purposeful Reflection — follow-up reflection helps consolidate what was learned from the interaction and what remains uncertain
    • Scaffolding and Fading — the general model of contingent support and its withdrawal, which a consultation applies

    Examples¶

    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — learners observe expert reasoning, attempt similar work, and receive coaching with gradually fading support
    • Leveraging Instructor Office Hours — scheduled access to disciplinary experts for question-driven clarification, feedback, and advice
    • Coaching — one-to-one or small-group expert guidance during performance
    • Demonstration — experts make tacit processes visible before learners attempt the task themselves
    • Clinical rounds, design critiques, code reviews, and studio conferences are common real-world forms of this principle when experts respond directly to learner work
    • Think-Aloud — the expert voices the cues and criteria behind a decision on a parallel case
