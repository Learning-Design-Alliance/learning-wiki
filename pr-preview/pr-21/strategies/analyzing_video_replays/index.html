    Analyzing Video Replays¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Video replay analysis involves reviewing recordings of one's own or others' performance — pausing, replaying key segments, annotating, and discussing them — to make performance visible in a way that real-time experience cannot. Because video separates the act of performing from the act of evaluating, it frees working memory for reflection and allows learners to examine their own behavior with something approaching an observer's perspective.

    Design Implications¶

    Video replay works because it converts fleeting performance into a stable, reviewable object that can be examined repeatedly and from multiple perspectives [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on structured analysis: unguided viewing produces little change, while prompted self-explanation and comparison against criteria drives metacognitive insight Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. Feedback grounded in video evidence is most useful when it targets the task and the learner's strategies rather than the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Recording equipment and playback or annotation tools (slow motion, clipping, side-by-side comparison)
    • Clear performance criteria or a rubric so learners know what to look for
    • Facilitation that prompts analysis — questions, annotation tasks, or structured discussion — rather than passive re-watching
    • A follow-on opportunity to apply insights (Practice, Coaching)

    Constraints¶

    • Unguided or repeated viewing without prompts wastes time and can entrench existing self-perceptions rather than change them [~M]
    • Self-confrontation with one's own recorded performance can trigger defensiveness or anxiety, especially for novices with low self-efficacy; psychological safety and a developmental framing are prerequisites
    • Video shows what happened but not why; without the performer's verbalized reasoning (Think-Aloud or stimulated recall interviews), observers misattribute causes
    • Effectiveness drops when the replayed performance is too complex for the learner's current expertise — experts benefit from full-game review while novices need short, single-focus clips Guidance that benefits novices can be redundant or counterproductive for experts. [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Self-confrontation: learners review their own performance, often with stimulated recall ("what were you thinking at this moment?")
    • Expert-model comparison: learners compare their replay against an expert Demonstration, making gaps concrete
    • Peer analysis: pairs or teams analyze each other's recordings, distributing the interpretive load and normalizing critique
    • Micro-analysis: short clips of single decisions or moves, annotated frame by frame, for high-precision skill work

    Target Learners¶

    • Professional trainees whose performance is observable and recordable: teachers, surgeons, nurses, counselors, athletes
    • Intermediate learners who have enough baseline skill to recognize what they are seeing; complete novices often cannot interpret expert or own performance without heavy scaffolding [~M]
    • Learners with inflated or deflated self-assessments — video evidence calibrates self-monitoring against reality Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Metacognition: accurate self-assessment of one's own performance and decision-making
    • Procedural refinement: identifying specific, correctable deviations from a target performance
    • Professional vision: learning to notice the features of a performance that experts notice
    • Reflective practice: building a habit of evidence-based self-evaluation

    Instructions¶

    1. Record the performance under realistic conditions, with consent and a clear developmental purpose.
    2. Before viewing, have learners predict what the recording will show — predictions create the discrepancy that drives reflection.
    3. Guide a structured viewing: select 2–3 key segments rather than the full recording; pause and prompt with criterion-referenced questions.
    4. Ask learners to verbalize their reasoning at chosen moments (stimulated recall), making hidden decisions explicit.
    5. Compare against criteria, an expert Demonstration, or a previous recording of the same learner to show change over time.
    6. Deliver Feedback anchored to specific moments in the video, at the task and process level.
    7. Set one concrete adjustment and schedule the next recording to close the loop through Application.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Video-stimulated recall — a variant in which the replay prompts reconstruction of the performer's thinking rather than evaluation of technique
    • Peer coaching cycles — replay analysis as the evidence base for coaching conversations
    • Portfolio-based reflection — annotated video clips as reflective artifacts accumulated over a course

    Related Elements¶

    • Demonstration — expert recordings serve as comparison models for the learner's own replay
    • Coaching — facilitator guidance determines whether analysis is productive
    • Feedback — video gives feedback concrete, shared referents
    • Class Discussion — group analysis of a shared clip builds professional vision collectively
    • Application — insights must transfer to the next live performance

    Examples¶

    • Teacher education: Programs such as those described in the TeachingWorks practice-based teacher education work use recordings of novices' teaching, analyzed against named "high-leverage practices," to develop professional vision.
    • Surgical training: Procedure videos and recorded simulated operations are reviewed step by step; self-review of recorded laparoscopic performance is used to identify technical errors and improve operative efficiency.
    • Athletic coaching: Tools like Hudl and Dartfish support clip tagging, slow-motion breakdown, and side-by-side comparison of athlete and elite performances.
    • Counselor and clinical training: Recorded mock counseling sessions reviewed in supervision, with the trainee first self-assessing before receiving supervisor feedback.
