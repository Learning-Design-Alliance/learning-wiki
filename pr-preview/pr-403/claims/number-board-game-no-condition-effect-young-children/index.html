Evidence¶

James-Brabham 2024¶

James-Brabham, E., Jay, T., & Sella, F. (2024). No evidence that playing a linear number board game improves numerical skills beyond teaching as usual: a randomized controlled trial in four- to five-year-old primary school children. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000857

q4 · i? · causal · r3

Pre-registered ANCOVA on post-test scores with pre-test and condition as predictors in an RCT of 249 children; the article reports "The effect of the condition was not statistically significant" for all primary outcomes, with no printed effect size for the condition effect.

"A pre-registered ANCOVA was run for each of the primary outcome measures with post-test scores as the dependent variable and pre -test scores and conditions as predictors"

Related Claims¶

  • Children preferred playing a number game over a letter game at pre-test, and this preference did not vary by condition — related
  • Children improved their numerical and letter-sound knowledge between pre-test and post-test regardless of condition — related
  • Number instruction outperformed both alphabet instruction conditions on number identification gains — reports the opposite
  • The game sessions showed high attendance, engagement, and enjoyment, with no differences among conditions — related
  • The authors attribute the null intervention effect to children's prior formal mathematics teaching and note the study is a failed conceptual replication of earlier linear board-game studies — related
  • Initial letter-sound knowledge and phonological awareness were not uniquely related to growth in each other — related
  • In the Nelson, Benner, & Gonzalez (2005) RCT, SSL produced statistically significant positive effects on all five alphabetics outcomes — related
  • In a Chicago quasi-experiment, Earobics®'s overall effect was not significant, but the CTOPP Sound Matching subtest showed a significant positive effect — related
