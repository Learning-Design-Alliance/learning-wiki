  • AI integration in academic settings has outpaced regulatory frameworks, leaving ethical practices, assessment, and institutional responses inconsistent — a broader claim this one bears on
  • In interviews with 25 engineering students at one Swedish university, students described GenAI as acceptable when it stimulates reflection and directly copying its outputs as cheating, and one used its fallibility as a prompt to double-check — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Institutions have expanded student access to generative AI tools faster than they have developed policies, guidance, and shared learning goals for their use — related
  • Teachers raise big-picture concerns about GenAI including surveillance, over-reliance, reduced teacher collaboration, job replacement, and training needs — related
  • Student perceptions of AI use in academic work are neutral and range from viewing AI as valid learning assistance to viewing it as cheating, depending on task type, transparency, and assessment design — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • University students' engagement with AI tools tends to cluster at two extremes: avoidance and uncritical use — related
  • A gap exists between students' ethical awareness and their GenAI practices, with students who frequently engage in behaviours they view as unethical and rationalisations normalising such use — related
  • Undergraduate students hold mixed views on whether using GenAI for coursework constitutes plagiarism, ranging from not misconduct to serious misconduct — related
  • Whether student GenAI use constitutes academic misconduct depends fundamentally on the prior question of the university's purpose — related
  • University administrations endorse contradictory positions on GenAI, encouraging faculty integration while treating AI-generated work as misconduct — related
