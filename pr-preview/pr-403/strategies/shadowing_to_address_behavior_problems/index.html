Shadowing to Address Behavior Problems¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (5 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 0 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Shadowing assigns a trained adult (aide, counselor, or behavior technician) to stay within arm's reach of a student exhibiting harmful behaviors (e.g., biting, aggression, elopement) to interrupt the behavior chain before an incident occurs. The shadow provides constant direct supervision without singling the student out publicly, models and prompts alternative strategies at the moment of need, and collects observational data on antecedents and triggers. The goal is to keep the classroom safe while breaking the reinforcement cycle sustaining the behavior and teaching replacement skills in context.

Design Implications¶

Shadowing works as an intensive, temporary scaffold: it substitutes adult regulation for the student's self-regulation while replacement behaviors are taught, then fades as competence grows. Its effectiveness depends on pairing prevention with explicit teaching of alternatives — supervision alone suppresses behavior without building skills [~M]. Because adult attention can itself reinforce problem behavior, the shadow must attend most richly to positive and replacement behavior, not to incidents [~S]. Framing matters for the relationship: interventions delivered in a warm, empathic way preserve the student–adult bond, while punitive framing damages it and worsens downstream behavior Brief, empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions by changing how teachers interpret and respond to misbehavior. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A trained adult with capacity to be present continuously during risk periods; administrative and parental consent and support
  • Clear, pre-agreed protocols for what the shadow does at each stage (prevention, prompting, de-escalation, crisis)
  • A functional understanding of the behavior — what triggers and reinforces it — gathered through observation before the shadow begins
  • A plan to teach replacement behaviors and to fading the shadow's presence as the student succeeds
  • A functional hypothesis about what triggers and maintains the behavior (antecedents, function, reinforcers), typically from a brief Functional Behavior Assessment
  • A pre-taught replacement behavior the adult can prompt (requesting a break, asking for a turn, a signal card)
  • Consistent adults who can commit sustained one-to-one attention for the shadowing period
  • A reinforcement plan so replacement behaviors pay off better than the problem behavior
  • A documented fading plan (reducing proximity, prompts, and monitoring over days or weeks)

Constraints¶

  • Labor-intensive and costly; dedicated one-to-one staffing is often unsustainable and can create dependency if not faded [-M]
  • Suppresses symptoms without addressing root causes (skill deficits, trauma, academic frustration, unmet needs) if used as containment only [-M]
  • Can stigmatize the student if peers perceive the shadow as surveillance; low-key presence and consistent staffing mitigate this [~M]
  • Adult proximity and attention can inadvertently reinforce the target behavior if attention follows incidents rather than prosocial behavior [-S]
  • Sustained one-to-one adult attention can itself become a reinforcer, increasing the behavior's frequency when shadowing is withdrawn [-M] — fading must begin early and be systematic
  • Constant close proximity can feel intrusive or stigmatizing to the child and peers, undermining autonomy and peer relationships [~M]
  • If implemented reactively (only hovering after incidents) rather than antecedently, it becomes surveillance and teaches nothing [-M]
  • It is labor-intensive and not scalable; it is a short-term bridge, not a standing intervention [-W]
  • Without a replacement behavior to prompt, shadowing only suppresses behavior temporarily and does not build skills [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full shadowing (arm's reach, all day) for dangerous behaviors, fading to proximity supervision, then periodic check-ins
  • Time-limited shadowing during identified high-risk segments (transitions, recess, unstructured time) rather than the whole day
  • Peer-mediated variants in which structured peer support partially substitutes for adult proximity in less severe cases
  • Integration into a school-wide framework such as Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports, where shadowing is the Tier 3 intensive tier rather than a stand-alone fix
  • Full shadowing — arm's-reach proximity across settings, used for dangerous behaviors (biting, aggression)
  • Setting-specific shadowing — proximity only during identified high-risk routines (transitions, unstructured play)
  • Faded shadowing — planned reduction from arm's reach → across the room → periodic check-ins, with the adult moving to a Check-In role
  • Peer-mediated variants — training a peer buddy to prompt replacement behaviors as the adult recedes

Target Learners¶

  • Students in early childhood and elementary settings with frequent, high-intensity harmful behaviors who have not responded to class-wide supports
  • Students whose behavior is predictable and antecedent-driven, where proactive interruption is feasible [~M]
  • Less appropriate for behavior driven primarily by acute distress or crisis, where therapeutic rather than supervisory responses are needed [-M]
  • Preschool and early-elementary children with emerging language who cannot yet self-monitor Contingent, responsive adult scaffolding improves learning and behavior for young children. [+M]
  • Learners with disabilities (autism, developmental delays, emotional/behavioral disorders) whose behavior poses a safety risk to self or others
  • Least appropriate for adolescents and learners with intact self-regulation, for whom constant proximity is intrusive and unnecessary [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral self-regulation: internalizing replacement strategies the shadow initially prompts and models
  • Social skill acquisition: turn-taking, requesting help, tolerating frustration in authentic settings
  • Classroom safety and access to learning for the whole class, protecting instructional time for peers
  • Replacement behavior acquisition: prompting and reinforcing a functional communicative alternative in the exact context where the problem behavior occurs
  • Self-regulation development: gradually transferring monitoring and prompting responsibility from adult to child Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility from adult to learner. [+M]
  • Generalization: because coaching happens in natural routines, replacement behaviors are practiced where they must actually be used

Instructions¶

  1. Observe and define. Collect baseline data on frequency, antecedents, and consequences of the target behavior; define it in observable terms.
  2. Brief the team. Secure administrator and parent agreement; write the intervention protocol, including what the shadow does before, during, and after risk moments.
  3. Assign a consistent adult. Use the same shadow across days to build trust and consistency; a stable relationship is itself protective [~M].
  4. Shadow proactively, not punitively. Stay within arm's reach during risk periods, pre-empting triggers and prompting replacement behaviors; deliver attention and praise contingent on positive behavior, not incidents.
  5. Teach in the moment. Use modeling and guided practice of the replacement behavior immediately before and during the situations where the problem behavior occurs.
  6. Check in and review data. Hold brief daily check-ins with the student and weekly team reviews of incident data to adjust the plan.
  7. Fade deliberately. Systematically increase distance and reduce prompting as the student succeeds, transferring monitoring to the student (assess performance before each fade step).

Related Strategies¶

  • Positive greetings at the door — a low-intensity relationship-building strategy that reduces misbehavior before one-to-one support is needed
  • Check-in/check-out — a Tier 2 mentoring structure that shadowing can fade into
  • Behavior-specific praise — the primary tool the shadow uses to reinforce replacement behavior
  • Planned Ignoring — often paired: minimize attention for the problem behavior while shadowing reinforces the replacement
  • Precorrection — the antecedent prompting strategy shadowing delivers in real time
  • School counselors should observe adolescent peer relations and identify high-risk girls early for prevention and intervention

Examples¶

  • A kindergarten student who bites during unstructured transitions is shadowed by a paraprofessional for three weeks; the shadow pre-teaches a "ask for a break" card, prompts it at each transition, and fades to proximity by week five as incident data drops.
  • A behavior technician shadowing a student with elopement risk as part of an individualized PBIS Tier 3 plan, collecting ABC (antecedent–behavior–consequence) data that later drives a function-based intervention.
  • Preschool biting plan — A teacher shadows a 4-year-old during free play, prompts "Can you tell Sam you want a turn?" when the child reaches for a toy, and reinforces the verbal request with immediate access; proximity is faded to across-the-room monitoring within three weeks.
  • Pyramid Model (Pyramid Equity Project) — Tier 3 individualized support within the Pyramid Model for Promoting Social Emotional Competence uses one-to-one adult proximity with embedded coaching of replacement skills in early childhood settings.
  • Autism support classroom — A paraprofessional shadows a student during transitions, prompting a break card before escape-maintained elopement occurs, then fades to a pre-arranged check-in at the classroom door.
