Teach genAI operations, prompt engineering, failure modes, and supervised overreliance before graded genAI-assisted writing¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 5 studies (2 qualitative, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2 · 0 of 5 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's pedagogical approach sequences comprehensive genAI instruction before students use genAI on graded work: "coverage of how genAI systems are developed and operate, prompt engineering, avoidance of genAI failure modes, and the hands-on experience of overly relying on genAI in the writing process." A required in-class assignment in which students rely on genAI as much as possible serves as an instructional tool demonstrating strengths and weaknesses, followed by demonstrations and exercises across research and writing sub-skills.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Several hours of take-home reading and in-class instruction spanning two to three weeks of the course
  • A low-stakes full-genAI assignment used as an instructional tool before graded genAI-assisted work

Constraints¶

  • Studied in a 7-week graduate elective at Carnegie Mellon University with 27 students; the article notes existing research on training genAI literacy in a college classroom is sparse

Target Learners¶

  • graduate students in professional programs
  • non-native and native English speaking students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • genAI competency for research and writing
  • effective and critical collaboration with genAI writing assistants

Claims¶

  • Reviewed literature reports risks of overreliance, AI errors in complex tasks, and students' uncritical acceptance of AI-generated outputs [+W]
  • Zero-shot LLM errors follow four recurring patterns: over-interpretation, failure to detect relevant information, hallucination, and failure to generate a response [+W]
  • Student reflections identify speed and scaffolding as the dominant theme, with verification burden, overreliance worries, and prompt precision also prevalent [+W]
  • Excessive GenAI reliance risks undermining foundational ICT knowledge, supporting supervised in-person assessment of foundational content [+W]
  • Teacher educators show shallow understanding of GenAI tools and gaps in aligning AI use with pedagogical goals [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Introduce prompt-engineering training before students use Gen AI for structured self-study techniques
  • Collaborative Human-AI Revision Design embedding prompt engineering across drafting, feedback, and rewriting phases
  • Train teachers in prompt engineering, evaluating AI-generated responses, ethical and privacy considerations, and integrating AI outputs into pedagogical decision-making
