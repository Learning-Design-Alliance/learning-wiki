Home Surveys¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 review), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Home surveys are short, recurring questionnaires sent to students' families (or to students themselves at home) to collect ongoing information about learning conditions, well-being, family priorities, and the effectiveness of instruction. Unlike one-time intake forms, they establish a feedback loop: the teacher gathers data, adjusts practice, and families see their input reflected in classroom decisions, which builds trust and sustained engagement.

Design Implications¶

Home surveys operationalize family engagement as a two-way data flow rather than one-way communication, and consistent responsiveness to survey input is what converts data collection into relationship-building [~M]. Surveys work best when they are short, low-stakes, and visibly acted upon; when families perceive that responses disappear into a void, participation decays rapidly [-M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A consistent, low-effort response channel (paper, SMS, or a tool such as Google Forms or TalkingPoints for multilingual families)
  • Translated or plain-language versions matched to families' home languages
  • A visible "you said / we did" follow-through mechanism so families see their input shaping practice
  • A manageable analysis routine — a handful of fixed-choice items plus one open-response question is typically sustainable

Constraints¶

  • Response rates skew toward already-engaged families, so survey data systematically underrepresents the hardest-to-reach households [-M] — pair surveys with calls, conferences, or community touchpoints to avoid acting on a biased sample
  • Surveys cannot substitute for relational trust; in low-trust contexts they read as surveillance or paperwork and may depress engagement [-W]
  • Over-surveying causes fatigue and declining response quality; weekly cadence suits early relationship-building, while monthly is more sustainable long-term [~W]
  • Asking about sensitive topics (income, housing, mental health) without clear purpose and privacy assurances damages trust [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pulse checks (3–5 items, weekly) focused on well-being and workload
  • Instructional feedback surveys (biweekly) asking what is helping or confusing the student
  • Family knowledge inventories (start of term, then updated) capturing cultural funds of knowledge, interests, and expertise families can contribute to instruction
  • Student-facing versions that mirror family surveys, allowing triangulation

Target Learners¶

  • Students whose home learning conditions differ substantially from school assumptions — surveys surface barriers (device access, quiet study space, caregiving duties) that would otherwise remain invisible [~M]
  • Multilingual families, when surveys are translated and offered in accessible formats, since they are often excluded by phone- and conference-based communication norms [+W]
  • Students with low self-efficacy [+W], whose families can report early signs of disengagement before they appear in performance data

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and environmental: understanding conditions that support or impede learning at home
  • Instructional calibration: adjusting pacing, difficulty, and support based on family-observed struggle or boredom
  • Relationship and community-building: establishing families as partners rather than recipients of information

Instructions¶

  1. Define the decision the survey informs. Every item should map to something the teacher is prepared to change; drop items that are merely interesting.
  2. Draft 3–7 items, mostly fixed-choice, plus one open question ("What's one thing that would make next week easier?"). Keep reading level low and translate into home languages.
  3. Send on a predictable schedule (e.g., Friday afternoon) through the channel families actually use, with a 2–3 minute completion target.
  4. Analyze quickly and act visibly. Triage responses within a week; follow up individually on red flags, and share aggregate changes with families ("Several of you asked for…").
  5. Close the loop with students. Feed what you learned into Check-Ins and instructional adjustments, so students experience the home–school connection rather than two separate systems.
  6. Audit reach each term. Compare respondents against the class roster; use calls or in-person touchpoints for non-responding families rather than assuming silence means satisfaction.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-ins — the in-class counterpart; surveys extend the same monitoring function into the home context
  • Activating background knowledge — family knowledge inventories supply the cultural and experiential material that activation depends on
  • Action-oriented feedback — the same principle applies to family input: data is only useful when it drives a visible change
  • Use a family engagement survey within a data inquiry cycle to gather family feedback and drive changes to policies and practices
  • Collect early objective and subjective program feedback

Examples¶

  • TalkingPoints (https://www.talkingpts.org) — two-way translated messaging used by schools to run recurring family pulse surveys in families' home languages without requiring smartphones or English proficiency.
  • Flipped-classroom implementations — teachers of flipped courses (see Flipped Classroom) use short weekly home surveys to verify video access and pre-class confusion points, adjusting the next day's in-class activities accordingly.
  • A middle-school advisory program sending a monthly 4-item survey ("How is homework going? What does your child talk about from school?") and reporting back a one-paragraph "you said / we did" summary in the school newsletter.
