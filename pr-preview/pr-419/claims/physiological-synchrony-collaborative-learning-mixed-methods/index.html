Evidence¶

Carolyn Rosé & Sanna Järvelä & Sten Ludvigsen 2020¶

Carolyn Rosé & Sanna Järvelä & Sten Ludvigsen. (2020). Experiencing CSCL: from motivation to the embodied experience and beyond. International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11412-020-09319-1

q2 · i? · associational · r?

Editorial report of the Schneider, Dich and Radu mixed methods study on physiological synchrony and collaborative learning. The editors stress that careful attention is needed for operationalizing constructs measured with physiological data.

"Schneiner and colleagues ' data come from 42 pairs of participants (N = 84) who worked on programming a robot to solve a variety of mazes. Their findings show partly contradictory and partly complementary findings as compared to prior studies."

Carolyn Rosé & Sanna Järvelä & Sten Ludvigsen 2020 (2)¶

Carolyn Rosé & Sanna Järvelä & Sten Ludvigsen. (2020). Experiencing CSCL: from motivation to the embodied experience and beyond. International Journal of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11412-020-09319-1

q2 · i? · qualitative · r?

Editorial report of the study's qualitative component, which contrasted a low performing and a high performing group to show how physiological data may distinguish productive from unproductive collaboration episodes. No effect sizes are printed in the editorial.

"the article provides a qualitative analysis highlighting important differences between a low performing and a high performing group, which illustrates ways in which it might be possible to pinpoint productive and unproductive collaboration episodes through physiological data."

Related Claims¶

  • CSCL needs to promote the inter-animation of complementary quantitative and qualitative perspectives rather than converge on a single ideal method. — related
  • Physiological synchrony among collaborating students related only to cognitive regulation, while motivational regulation changes related to achievement — related
  • Real collaborative groups show higher physiological synchrony than randomly matched false groups on %REC, %DET, and ADL — related
  • Session-level physiological synchrony occurred in the two groups that struggled with the collaborative exam, while the two groups that found the task easy showed no synchrony. — related
  • Neither physiological arousal nor physiological synchrony predicts equality of participation in monitoring interactions — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Increased physiological arousal and higher physiological synchrony relate to more monitoring with negative valence — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Higher equality of participation in monitoring interactions and more monitoring with positive valence predict better collaborative task performance — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Higher physiological synchrony relates to less monitoring with positive valence — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • During a collaborative exam, every group member contributed to joint monitoring, and students monitored cognition far more often than behaviour or motivation and emotions. — a narrower finding that bears on this claim
  • Physiological synchrony during collaborative interaction shows initial positive correlations with collaboration quality, task performance, and learning gains — related
  • Emotional synchrony frequency differs slightly across regulatory activities but the metric does not discriminate SSRL, cognitive, and task-execution interactions — related
