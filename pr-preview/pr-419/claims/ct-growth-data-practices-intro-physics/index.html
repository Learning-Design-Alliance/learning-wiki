Evidence¶

Sean Savage 2026¶

Sean Savage, Anand Shanker, Grace Michlitsch, and N. Sanjay Rebello. (2026). Using LLMs to Detect Growth in Computational Thinking in Introductory Physics. arXiv preprint. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06200 doi:10.48550/arxiv.2608.06200

q2 · i3 · causal · r1

Paired t-test on the human-graded sample of N=50 students comparing pre- and post-instruction written responses in a calculus-based engineering physics course. The article reports "large-magnitude growth in Data Practices" with d = 1.03, and moderate growth in CPP and STP; all p-values fell below the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.01.

"Students demonstrated highly significant, large-magnitude growth in Data Practices (p <0.001,d= 1.03), as well as moder- ate growth in Computational Problem-Solving (p <0.001, d= 0.52) and Systems Thinking (p <0.001,d= 0.45)."

Related Claims¶

  • Growth is compartmentalized by question: on the code-tracing prompt students grew in procedural code tracing while physics correctness improved only slightly — related
  • A multimodal LLM achieves moderate-to-substantial agreement with human consensus on well-defined CT practices but lower agreement on multi-component constructs like Physics Correctness and Systems Thinking — related
  • Systems Thinking Practices is the hardest CT construct to code reliably for both human raters and the LLM, requiring more explicitly operationalized rubrics — related
  • Applied to the full dataset of 936 students, the LLM reproduced the human-observed growth trends, detecting significant growth on Q25 and Q26 but none on Q27 — related
  • Modeling and Simulation Practices showed no measurable growth, attributed to a ceiling effect from students' high prior knowledge — related
