Leverage naturally occurring knowledge differences in the classroom to elicit socio-cognitive conflict through collaborative argumentation tasks¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 design), q1–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article proposes using collaborative peer tasks between children with contrasting mental models as an instructional strategy for conceptual change. It states its study "provides laboratory-based empirical evidence for alternative, more-efficient ways to foster these processes, by leveraging knowledge differences naturally found in the classroom to elicit socio- cognitive conflict presumably elaborated through collaborative argumentation". The recipe is concrete: assess children's mental models individually, pair a less knowledgeable child with a more knowledgeable peer on a joint consensus task such as a shared drawing, keep knowledge and role asymmetries implicit, and require a single joint answer before drawing each element.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dyad members must hold different conceptual models of the target content, leveraged from natural classroom knowledge disparity
  • The task must require agreement on one joint answer, with asymmetries kept implicit

Constraints¶

  • The evidence is laboratory-based, with sessions outside the classroom in a controlled room; the authors call for classroom-based research to test transfer
  • The article notes future research should address the slight role asymmetry of giving instructions only to the more knowledgeable member

Target Learners¶

  • second-grade children (around 7 years old) with diverse mental models of the target science content

Target Learning Goals¶

  • conceptual change in science domains with entrenched misconceptions, such as observational astronomy and the shape of the Earth

Affordances¶

  • Framework Theory Conceptual Change Earth Models

Claims¶

  • Responses adopted by less-knowledgeable children led predominantly to positive knowledge level shifts, indicating adoption of mostly correct information [+W]
  • The knowledge-level gap between members of dyads of children narrows after minutes of peer interaction [+W]
  • Knowledge level shift differs significantly across peer-interaction and control groups, with less-knowledgeable children differing from both controls and more-knowledgeable peers [+W]
  • Less-knowledgeable dyad members significantly increase their knowledge of the Earth after interaction with a more knowledgeable peer, while more-knowledgeable members and self-revision controls show no change [+W]
  • After interaction, dyad members' knowledge representations become more similar because less-knowledgeable children adopt their partners' responses, not the reverse [+W]
  • Perturbation type determines the interaction types needed: conflicting evidence elicits argumentation [~W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Foster and, if necessary, enforce socio-cognitive conflicts that lead to productive solutions as a strategy to optimize learning outcomes
