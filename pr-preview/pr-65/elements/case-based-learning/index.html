    Case-Based Learning¶

    Element · All elements

    Description¶

    Case-based learning places learners in the role of analyst or decision-maker confronting a realistic scenario — a patient presentation, a business dilemma, a legal dispute — and asks them to apply theoretical concepts to interpret it and choose a course of action. The case functions as an authentic problem context in which abstract principles must be retrieved, adapted, and defended rather than merely recalled.

    Design Implications¶

    Case-based learning improves exam performance and supports transfer by forcing learners to apply concepts in context rather than rehearse them in the abstract Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the case being genuinely ill-structured enough to require reasoning, and on discussion or feedback that surfaces and challenges learners' analyses rather than confirming them. Well-designed cases create productive Cognitive Disequilibrium: learners encounter gaps between their intuitions and what the case demands, motivating conceptual change Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Authentic, realistic cases with sufficient detail and ambiguity to require judgment, not just recall
    • Clear alignment between the concepts to be learned and the features of the case
    • Structured analysis prompts or facilitation (Class Discussion, Socratic questioning) that pushes learners beyond surface features
    • A debrief or expert comparison so learners can evaluate their reasoning against expert analysis

    Constraints¶

    • Cases with too little scaffolding overwhelm novices, who lack the schemas to distinguish relevant from irrelevant detail Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices hinders experts. [~M] — novices need more structure, experts more open-ended cases
    • Poorly matched cases (surface realism without conceptual relevance) lead learners to fixate on irrelevant details
    • Time-intensive: deep analysis of few cases trades breadth of coverage for depth [~W]
    • Group discussion without individual accountability can allow free-riding Cooperative learning requires group rewards with individual accountability. [-M]

    Target Learners¶

    • Advanced novices and intermediate learners who have foundational concepts and need to learn when and how to apply them Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]
    • Professional students in business, medicine, law, and social sciences, where judgment under uncertainty is the target competence
    • Complete novices benefit less without prior instruction or heavy scaffolding Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices hinders experts. [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Application: bridging theory and practice in meaningful ways
    • Decision-making and diagnostic reasoning under uncertainty
    • Transfer: recognizing when abstract principles apply across varied surface contexts Cognitive flexibility theory supports learning from multiple cases. [+W]

    Affordances¶

    • Situated Learning — cases embed knowledge in the authentic contexts and practices of a discipline, so learning is tied to the situations of use rather than to decontextualized abstractions
    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — case analysis followed by expert debrief makes disciplinary thinking visible; learners practice articulation and reasoning that experts model during debrief
    • Active Learning — learners must generate analyses, decisions, and justifications rather than receive explanations, producing the retrieval and elaboration that drive retention
    • Cognitive Flexibility — multiple cases illustrating the same concept from different angles prepare learners to apply it flexibly across varied situations

    Related Elements¶

    • Case Study — the written artifact that carries the scenario; case quality determines analysis quality
    • Problem-Based Learning — a closely related pattern in which the case is an open problem driving self-directed inquiry rather than guided analysis
    • Decision-Making — the core cognitive activity cases elicit; cases give it authentic stakes
    • Class Discussion — the typical vehicle for comparing analyses and surfacing alternative interpretations
    • Anchored Instruction — a related approach in which instruction is anchored to a rich problem context

    Patterns That Use This Element¶

    • Case-Based Learning — the full instructional sequence built around case analysis and debrief
    • Case-Based Learning (Harvard Method) — cold-calling and discussion-intensive facilitation of business school cases
    • Problem-Based Learning — case as the trigger for self-directed problem solving
    • Cognitive Apprenticeship — cases as the context for coaching and articulation

    Examples¶

    Harvard Business School case method — Students prepare a real business dilemma, then defend positions in discussion-facilitated classes; the instructor withholds the "answer" to keep analysis central.

    National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science — Published peer-reviewed science cases (e.g., on genetics, epidemiology) with teaching notes, used in undergraduate STEM courses.

    Problem-based medical curricula (e.g., Maastricht University) — Patient cases drive small-group inquiry, integrating biomedical knowledge with clinical reasoning.
