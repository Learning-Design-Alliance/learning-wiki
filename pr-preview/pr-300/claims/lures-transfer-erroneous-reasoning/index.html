Evidence¶

Marsh 2007¶

Marsh, E. J., Roediger, H. L., III, Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2007). The memorial consequences of multiple-choice testing. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 14(2), 194-199. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03194051

q2 · i? · review · r2

In the Bloom-level experiment, selecting a lure (e.g., gravitation) on an application question about acclimation increased later use of that lure on a conceptually similar but superficially dissimilar transfer question about animals thickening fur in winter; no effect size is printed.

"selecting a lure (e.g., gravitation) as the answer to a multiple-choice question such as “Allowing new fish to adjust slowly to tank water temperature is an example of what biological phenomenon?” increased students’ likelihood of later using that lure to answer a transfer question"

Discussion¶

Related Claims¶

  • Taking initial multiple-choice tests without feedback can lead students to later produce the incorrect lure answers they selected, even when an overall retrieval practice benefit occurs — related
  • Multiple-choice testing increases the later production of multiple-choice lures as answers on cued recall tests, even under warning against guessing — related
  • Positive and negative testing effects occur for questions tapping higher levels of Bloom's taxonomy, and question level does not change the memorial consequences of testing — related
  • Cued-recall testing can impair later memory for competitive, related but non-practiced information (retrieval-induced forgetting) — related
