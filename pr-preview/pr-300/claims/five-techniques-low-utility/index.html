Dunlosky 2013¶

Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

The monograph's overall assessment section lists the five low-utility techniques and states they "were rated as low utility for numerous reasons," including limited conditions of benefit and short-lived effects for the keyword mnemonic.

"Five techniques received a low utility assessment: summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, and rereading. These techniques were rated as low utility for numerous reasons."

  • A review chapter (Karpicke 2017) reports, second-hand, experiments in which retrieval practice produced more learning than concept mapping (Karpicke & Blunt 2011) and than imagery strategies such as the keyword mnemonic (Karpicke & Smith 2012) — related
  • Quizzing outperforms no/filler activity, fewer-question testing, restudying, and other elaborative strategies, with the advantage shrinking as the control condition's reexposure increases. — related
  • Practice testing and distributed practice receive high utility assessments because their benefits generalize widely across learners, tasks, and educational contexts — related
  • Rereading is less effective than retrieval practice — related
  • Training in text structure improves college students' summaries, but summarizing has not been shown here to improve recall or writing more than rereading or other active reading — related
  • Explicit transfer instructions, in which trainees discuss how trained strategies could apply to a new task, produced reliable transfer of imagery and sentence-generation training to unpracticed text learning in older adults — related
  • Comprehension-test expectancy tended to be more effective than delayed-keywords listing and rereading, though interventions did not substantially differ overall — related
