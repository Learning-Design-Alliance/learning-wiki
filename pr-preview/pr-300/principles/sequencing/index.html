Sequencing¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 41 claims (19 for, 21 mixed, 1 against) · 35 studies (12 causal, 10 theoretical, 7 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 design, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 3 of 35 report an effect size · 37 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns the general question of order: which of a target's ideas, sub-skills, examples and tasks a learner meets first, and what follows from that learner's response. For a learner who cannot yet perform or explain a target whose parts depend on each other (a novice on that target, not necessarily in the wider domain), meeting the parts in an order that puts what is needed to understand a step before that step, and moves from what the learner can currently process toward the full complexity of the target, is expected to produce better performance on tasks that draw on the whole (immediate-performance, near-transfer, conceptual-understanding) than the same content in an order chosen for convenience (the textbook's order, the order the topics were written, all detail at one level). The order is a hypothesis about this learner's state, so it is revised from what the learner does: a learner who already has the earlier steps skips them, and one who stalls goes back to the step the stall points to.

The relationship is conditional on four things:

  • The learner's starting knowledge of this target. An order built to protect novices from overload (parts before the whole, an example before a problem, one category at a time) can be redundant for a learner who already holds the structure.
  • The kind of goal. Procedural fluency, conceptual understanding and transfer, and telling confusable categories apart are each served by a different order in the evidence below; there is no order that is best for all three.
  • How much the parts interact. Where the parts of a task can only be understood together, the whole may be too much for a novice at first; where they are loosely linked, the order matters less.
  • The horizon. Several order effects show at one test and not at another, so an order chosen on how practice looks may not be the one that shows at the delayed or transfer test.

No claim in the wiki tests "order by dependency and readiness" as a general rule against another ordering principle. One claim recorded here reports the same gap from a systematic review: an IES practice-guide panel found that none of the 23 studies behind its recommendation to teach early mathematics along a developmental progression compared progression-guided instruction with instruction not guided by one, so the recommendation rests on the panel's expertise (no direct test of a developmental progression [~M]). What the wiki holds are tests of particular order contrasts: a hierarchical organisation (general ideas above detail) against a single-level one; isolated parts before the whole for absolute novices; a short primer on a system's parts before the main lesson against none; a worked example before a problem against a problem before an example; problem solving before instruction against instruction before problem solving; and interleaved against blocked examples of categories. Each holds for some learners, goals and outcomes and not others, and two of them point in opposite directions for different goals. The default design below keeps the earlier page's guidance as labelled proposals, organised around those tested contrasts.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page: Holistic Learning holds the stance of giving a usable whole early and keeping parts attached to it; Elaboration Theory is the reusable sequence for content (an epitome elaborated level by level) and 4C/ID the reusable sequence for whole tasks in a complex-skill; Knowledge Organization holds which organisation the learner should end up holding; Clear Structure holds making the order and organisation visible (signals, previews, outlines); Chunking holds how large one unit is and where its boundaries fall; Worked Examples holds what comes before the first attempt; Mastery Learning holds the check-and-correct gate before moving on; Scaffolding and Fading holds how support is withdrawn. This page holds the decision that joins them: which order decision a design faces, and what the learner's response and the goal say about it. Use the pattern pages for a step-by-step sequence.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The earlier page asked for a rationale for the order (dependency, complexity, readiness) and for transitions that connect one stage to the next, and gave two examples (a statistics unit that reaches inference only after learners can reason about variation; a carpentry course that teaches tool setup and sub-skills, then a whole cabinet). Kept here as a concrete default; each step's evidence status is beside it.

  1. Map the dependencies before choosing an order. List what the learner must be able to do at the end, then for each item ask "what must they already know or do to understand this?" and draw the result as a short chain or tree (a learning hierarchy). Order the unit so nothing is taught before what it rests on. Untested proposal; from the earlier page ("order instruction around dependency and readiness rather than convenience"). A worked example of such a map, with no learner outcome: an onto-semiotic analysis of the definite integral found that reading a textbook's formal definition requires relating functions, intervals, partitions and sample points and the role of each (prior knowledge the formal definition demands [+W]), and broke an intuitive presentation into five configurations, from constant velocity to the integral as a limit of sums (five configurations [+W]), which is an order of partial meanings a unit can follow. The same analysis flags where an intuitive route generalises from too few cases (abusive generalisation [~W]), so a map should mark where an informal stage stops being enough.
  2. Check the learner's starting point against the map, then cut. Before the first session of a unit, give three to five items that sample the prerequisites and the first steps (5–10 minutes; an untested proposal). Skip the steps a learner already performs unaided; go back to the prerequisite the errors point to. Guidance that helps novices can be redundant for learners who already hold the structure (expertise reversal [~M]).
  3. Open with the few general ideas, then hang detail from them. Give the two to five most general ideas of the unit first, and attach each later detail to one of them rather than teaching all details at one level. One small set of physics experiments found a hierarchical organisation of the same knowledge beat a single-level one on complex recall and problem solving (hierarchical organisation [+M]); it did not help low-ability students (ability moderates it [~M]). The step-by-step version is Elaboration Theory.
  4. Before a complex lesson, name its parts and terms. Where the main lesson is complex or interactive (a simulation, a demonstration, a procedure with a new tool), give a short primer naming the parts and what each does, kept to a few minutes. One randomised experiment found a pretraining video on a tool's parts improved knowledge and real-world transfer after a VR lesson, against no pretraining (pretraining [+M]); it did not compare the primer with the same content given later.
  5. For absolute novices on a task whose parts interact heavily, practise the critical parts first, then join them. Isolate the two or three sub-skills that carry most of the difficulty, practise each until it runs without stalling, then put them together in the whole task within the same week. One experiment supports parts first for novices on high-interactivity material (part-task practice [~M]); that whole-task practice improves transfer is an argument, not a tested result (whole-task argument [~W]). Do not leave the parts unjoined; the earlier page's carpentry example ends in a full build.
  6. Choose the order of example and problem by goal. For a procedure with novice learners and an immediate test, show a worked example before the first problem: in one experiment with secondary-school novices, sequences that began with an example beat sequences that began with a problem on test performance and reported effort (example first [+M]); with identical example–problem pairs, the early order advantage was not detected at the final test (identical pairs [~M]). For introducing a stated rule, a rule, then chosen examples, then incomplete examples for the learner to finish, is the order 1960s programmed-instruction work reported as efficient, second-hand and with no data recorded here (rule–example–incomplete example [+W]). For a concept or principle goal where conceptual understanding or transfer counts, a short problem-solving attempt before instruction is an option for learners above the early primary grades, provided the instruction afterwards builds on their attempts (step 7).
  7. If you put a problem first, keep it short and teach from the attempts. Give a problem learners can attempt in several ways but not yet solve canonically (one session segment of 15–30 minutes; an untested proposal), collect their solutions, then teach the canonical method by comparing it with them. A meta-analysis found problem solving before instruction beat instruction first on conceptual knowledge and transfer with no difference on procedural knowledge, and the advantage reversed for second to fifth graders and domain-general skills (problem first [~S]).
  8. Block new categories first, then mix them. When learners must tell confusable categories or problem types apart, introduce each one in a short blocked run (three or four items each; an untested proposal) until each is recognisable, then mix them so every item requires deciding which type it is. A meta-analysis found interleaved examples beat blocked ones for classifying visual and mathematical categories, with no benefit for expository texts and a reversal for word categories (interleaving [~M]). Expect lower accuracy during mixed practice, and say so to learners.
  9. Where learners hold a misconception, state it and then the correct idea. In text that addresses a known misbelief, put the misbelief first and then the fact that refutes it, not the reverse. One experiment found this order beat traditional text on free recall and the inverted order on free and cued recall (refutational order [+M]).
  10. Make each transition explicit. At each stage change, spend one to two minutes saying what the learner can now do, what comes next and how it rests on what they have (an untested proposal; from the earlier page's "transitions that connect one stage to the next", and see Clear Structure).
  11. Move on by unaided success, not by the calendar. A learner moves to the next stage when they perform the current one unaided on two consecutive items or tasks (an untested proposal); otherwise give one corrective round on the step the error points to and check again. Include at least one harder variant in the check (larger numbers, more parts, a changed form): in one case study, a student with learning difficulties who coordinated a count correctly with easy numbers lost track with harder ones (easy to hard numbers [~W]), and still needed prompts three episodes later (prompt-dependent [~W]), so success with prompts or easy cases is not yet the unaided success the gate asks for. The gate is modelled in Mastery Learning.
  12. Plan the arc across sessions. For a unit of four to eight sessions (an untested proposal): session 1, the prerequisite check, the general ideas and a primer for the first complex lesson (about 20–30% of the session on the map, the rest on the first step); early sessions, one new step per session, example first for procedures, blocked runs for new categories, the teacher supplying the examples; middle sessions, the steps joined into small whole tasks, problems before instruction where conceptual goals warrant it, mixed practice of the categories met so far, and learners supplying their own examples and cases; late sessions, whole tasks of the form the target assessment uses, mixed items only, help on request, and a cumulative check of earlier stages. Reorder when the checks say so: drop stages the class already performs, and return to a stage when a later one stalls on it.

When a condition is not met: if no one can check prerequisites before the unit, build the check into the first activity (the first three items sample the prerequisites, with feedback that routes a learner back); if the goal and the horizon are not stated, use example first and instruction first, which are the safer orders for a novice on a procedure, and record that the order was not chosen for transfer.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What the learner can already do on this target. A learner who already performs the early steps needs a shorter path and fewer protective orders; one who cannot start needs the prerequisites first. If unstated, ask: can the learner do one item from the first stage and one from a middle stage now, unaided?
  • The kind of goal and the test that will count. Fluent procedure, conceptual understanding and transfer, and discrimination between confusable categories each favour a different order. If unstated, ask: at the end, what must the learner do, on what kind of item, and when (end of session, a week later, on the job)?
  • How tightly the parts depend on each other. A chain where step 3 is meaningless without step 2 forces an order; a set of loosely related topics leaves the order open and makes the transitions matter more. If unstated, ask: can any part be understood or done without the others?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a response at a stage under stated conditions: which stage, which item, what help was available, whether the learner succeeded unaided, which earlier step their error relies on, and how long it took. "Missing a prerequisite", "overloaded" and "ready for the next stage" are inferred states. A stall at stage 4 does not show which earlier stage is missing; it shows that stage 4 did not go through under these conditions.

Stalls at a new stage after succeeding at the earlier ones A prerequisite was not in the map; the new stage combines too many parts at once; the transition did not connect it to the earlier stage Give one item from each earlier stage that the new stage rests on, unaided; then a version of the new stage with one part already done for them.
Succeeds on blocked items, fails when categories are mixed Has learned each procedure but not when it applies; the categories were mixed before each was recognisable; the mixed items differ in surface features too Ask the learner to name the type of each mixed item before solving it; compare naming accuracy with solving accuracy.
After a problem-first attempt, produces nothing and disengages The problem was too far beyond them; they read failure as a judgement on themselves; they had no ways in to try Look at what they wrote or tried; ask what they thought the problem was asking; offer the same problem with one more given and see whether attempts appear.
Follows each step in order but cannot say how the stages connect The order was followed without its rationale; the general ideas were shown but never used; the test has never asked for connections Ask the learner to place a new detail under one of the general ideas and say why; ask what stage 2 is for.

Evidence and qualifications¶

No claim tests the general rule that ordering by dependency and readiness beats a convenience order. The claims below test particular order contrasts; the learners, materials and outcomes differ, so do not rank them by effect label.

  • A hierarchical internal knowledge organization facilitates performance on complex recall and problem-solving tasks more than a single-level organization of the same knowledge [+M]: 36 volunteers from an introductory college physics course, blocked by ability and randomly assigned to a hierarchical organisation or a single-level one presented once or twice; the hierarchical group scored higher on composite complex-task scores (F tests printed, no effect size). It bears on presenting general ideas above detail. It is one report in one domain, and its sibling claim says the benefit did not appear for low-ability students (Ability moderates the hierarchical-organization advantage [~M]).
  • Part-task practice reduces cognitive load for absolute novices during initial skill acquisition. [~M]: one experiment (sample size not recorded) found that for complex tasks with high element interactivity, novices performed better when first given isolated parts before the integrated task. No effect size is recorded. It supports parts first for absolute novices on highly interacting material, and qualifies any whole-first rule for them.
  • Pretraining learners on a system's key parts and terms before the main lesson improved knowledge and real-world transfer in one VR experiment, and is reported second-hand to improve learning in multimedia studies [+M]: 93 participants randomly assigned to a pretraining video on a micropipette's parts or none, before the same immersive VR lesson; the pretraining group scored higher on a knowledge test and made fewer errors on a real-world transfer task, with lower reported load. No standardised effect size; neither source compares pretraining with the same content given later in the lesson.
  • Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load and improve learning outcomes [+M]: 96 Dutch secondary-school novices in electrical-circuit troubleshooting; the two conditions that began with a worked example beat the two that began with a problem on test performance (ηp² = .23) and invested mental effort (ηp² = .20); problem–example pairs did not beat problems only, so starting with the example was what mattered. Immediate test only. The companion study with identical pairs (Identical example–problem pairs showed an early order advantage but no detected final-test difference [~M], 16 per condition, abstract only) qualifies it: the order advantage during learning was not detected at the final test. Not detecting a difference does not show the orders are equivalent.
  • Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]: the limiting claim. Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction followed by problem solving on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), with no difference on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03); the advantage was larger with designs that followed productive-failure principles and reversed for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. Two randomised studies in mathematics found the same pattern. It means the order that protects novices (instruction or example first) is not the best order for every goal: for conceptual goals in older learners the evidence favours the reverse. The claim's own Discussion says the tension with the example-first literature has not been settled by a head-to-head comparison.
  • Interleaving category examples improves inductive category learning for visual and mathematical materials, but not for expository texts or word categories [~M]: a multilevel meta-analysis of 59 studies found interleaved presentation of category examples beat blocked presentation on classification (g = 0.42), largest for paintings, smaller for mathematics, not significant for expository texts and reversed for word categories (g = −0.39). It did not measure retention of practised problems or transfer. The blocked-then-mixed order in the default design comes from the claim's Discussion, not from its evidence.
  • In one experiment, refutational text (misbelief stated first, then fact) enhanced free recall relative to traditional text and free and cued recall relative to inverted refutational text, but its reported 14-day recognition advantage was over inverted refutational text only [+M]: one experiment with science facts; the order within a text changed recall. No effect size; the 14-day advantage is only over the inverted order.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a narrative review of cognitive-load studies and one three-experiment primary study (electrical trainees). It is the reason to skip stages a learner already performs; it gives no point at which to switch.
  • No reviewed study directly compared developmental-progression-guided math instruction with instruction not guided by a progression [~M]: the IES early-mathematics practice guide's panel found none of the 23 studies behind its first recommendation made that comparison. It is the nearest thing in the wiki to a test of this page's relationship, and it reports that the test was not found in that evidence base; it does not show that a progression is no better.
  • Weaker order contrasts, each one study or a second-hand report, no effect size recorded: concrete to abstract (concreteness fading) [+W] beat staying generic or concrete for undergraduates learning group properties, reported second-hand from McNeil and Fyfe (2012); rule, example, then incomplete example [+W] is reported as efficient from 1960s programmed instruction, with no data; and in one study of 155 sixth graders solving word problems, a free attempt followed by a test form carrying a seven-step plan produced a gain for girls (t = 3.618) while the reverse order was followed by a lower score for girls, with a significant time-by-group interaction (p = .006) and no effect size (free exploration then plan [~W], interaction [~W]). That study varied the order of test forms, not of teaching, and is coded causal · r1.
  • Language order, all second-hand and q1–q2: a classroom-teacher guide reports Postovsky's finding that beginners who focused on listening outscored those required to develop speaking and listening together (listening first [+W]); a 1997 conference paper reports Sticht's position that reading cannot exceed listening (Sticht's law [+W]) and a positive correlation, with no coefficient printed, between preschool oral language and later reading (oral language predicts reading [+W]), says its author could not find research supporting transfer from literacy back to oral skills (reverse transfer unsupported [~W]), and describes Japanese schools as teaching English reading and grammar before the oral-aural stage (inverted order [~W]). They argue for oral before written in a first foreign language; none tests that order with learners.
  • Analyses of an order, with no learner outcome: the PATHS emotional-literacy curriculum combines spiral and topical sequencing (PATHS macro order [+W]), orders topics general to detailed and by simplifying conditions (PATHS topical order [+W]), and treats basic emotions as prerequisites for complex ones (PATHS basic to complex [+W]); an onto-semiotic analysis of the definite integral maps the prior knowledge its formal definition demands (formal definition [+W]) and the partial meanings of an intuitive route (five configurations [+W], abusive generalisation [~W]), and reports second-hand that students can carry out integration procedures with limited understanding of the concepts (procedural strong, conceptual limited [~W]), which is the reason to check conceptual items and not only procedural ones before moving on.
  • Learning trajectories inside a stage: one case study of a student with learning difficulties (easy to hard numbers [~W], prompt-dependent [~W]) shows success with easy numbers that did not hold with harder ones. It bears on the move-on rule (step 11), not on the order of stages.
  • Teacher preparation, from one review of agricultural-education studies with outcomes in reported teaching efficacy: several kinds of observing other teachers correlated positively with efficacy, observing a first-year teacher explaining 11% of the variance (vicarious experiences [+W]); early field experiences were not related to efficacy and extra courses during student teaching went with lower efficacy (mastery experiences not first [~W]); and efficacy rose on campus, fell to its lowest at mid-practicum and rebounded by the end (mid-point dip [~W]). These are correlations and trajectories; no study recorded here assigned an order of observing and practising.

Taken together: order effects are real in several particular contrasts, they depend on expertise, age, material and outcome, and two of them (example first, problem first) favour opposite orders for different goals. None establishes that a whole unit or course ordered by dependency beats another order, the one systematic review recorded here that looked for that comparison in early mathematics did not find it, and none measures retention of an order effect over weeks. The weaker evidence added on 2026-10-15 (concrete to abstract, listening before speaking and reading, observing before teaching) points the same way as the dependency rule, but each is a second-hand report, a correlation or a description.

The evidence by question¶

  • Should learners attempt a problem before the idea is taught, and what must the teaching afterwards do? Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]
  • Should examples or problems of confusable categories be met in blocks or interleaved, and for which materials? Interleaving category examples improves inductive category learning for visual and mathematical materials, but not for expository texts or word categories [~M]
  • Should component knowledge come before the whole task? Pretraining learners on a system's key parts and terms before the main lesson improved knowledge and real-world transfer in one VR experiment, and is reported second-hand to improve learning in multimedia studies [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cites that bear on it only indirectly. Every other claim the earlier page cited, or that was found or attached later, is now weighed in the model above (reread 2026-10-15).

  • Chunking reduces working memory load by grouping information into fewer, more meaningful units. [+S] — cited by the earlier page; about grouping material into units, not about order. It bears on this page only as the reason a stage should not hold more new elements than a novice can process; Chunking owns it.
  • Personal connection to data supports critical data-literacy stance-taking [~M] — attached with the folded Data Storytelling pattern (forage, remix, DIY, in the deprecated block below); it is about choosing a dataset learners have a personal stake in, not about the order of the three phases, so it bears on topic choice rather than on sequencing.
  • Teams receiving part-task congruent representational tools (causal then simulation) outperformed teams with partly matched or mismatched tools on a complex business-economics learning task [+W] — attached 2026-10-19 from B. Slof et al. (2012), which proposed "Introduce qualitative representations before quantitative ones and let learners construct representations congruent with the tasks to be carried out".

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually unaided performance on the whole target, of a stated kind, at a stated horizon; the order is a means to it. Ask separately what the learner wants from the unit: to do the thing they came for as soon as possible, to pass the test, to not feel lost. A learner who wants the real task now may experience a long run of prerequisites as a delay, and drop out before the part they came for; one who fears failure may be put off by a problem-first opening. Both are observations about the order, not only about the learner.

For example, an adult learning spreadsheets for a job may want to build this month's budget report, while the designer's map puts cell references, then formulas, then functions, then the report. Bring a simplified version of the report forward as the first whole task, and teach each prerequisite at the point the report needs it; record both whether the learner can build the report and whether they can do the prerequisite steps on a new sheet, so success on the one is not read as the other.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if units ordered by a dependency map, compared with the same content in a convenience order with comparable learners and aligned unaided outcomes, show no advantage at the end of the unit or at a delay. If problem-first orders turn out to help procedural outcomes too, or example-first orders help conceptual transfer as much, the goal-dependent choice in the default design should collapse into one order. If the blocked-then-mixed order loses to mixing from the start for novices, step 8 should change. If prerequisite checks fail to predict who stalls at later stages, the move-on rule is a scheduling device, not a diagnostic. Do not protect the model by calling every failure a missing prerequisite after the fact.

Performance during practice, unaided performance at the end of the unit, delayed retention, transfer and use in the setting the learner values are separate claims. The present evidence establishes no stage length, no number of stages and no move-on criterion.

Examples¶

  • A statistics unit starts with interpreting distributions, then moves to sampling, and only later introduces inference once learners can reason about variation.
  • A carpentry course teaches tool setup and key subskills before asking learners to complete a full cabinet build, then integrates the pieces into a whole-task performance.
  • Simple-to-complex sequencing, Spiral curriculum and Part-task practice are elements that carry an order; Learning hierarchy task analysis is the method for mapping dependencies; 4C/ID sequences whole tasks.
  • Use sequences of problem-situations to progressively contextualize and develop mathematical comprehension of calculus concepts
  • Develop complex simulations incrementally, testing each stage before adding the next feature
  • Prospective mathematics and social-studies teachers used Gapminder and a Hans Rosling TED talk as the remix exemplar, then authored performances on topics like global CO2 emissions and national wealth/health measures.
  • Concentrate beginning foreign language instruction on listening and speaking
  • A lesson opening with a short review of the prior day's content, an overview of the new topic, direct instruction, an explicit comparison ("this is like what we just did, except..."), and a closing application task
  • Teach one-step equations in a hierarchical sequence: Type 1 without special features first, then Type 1 with special features, then Type 2 in the same order
