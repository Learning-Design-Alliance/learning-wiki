Culturally and Historically Responsive Literacy¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 5 studies (4 review, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 5 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Culturally and Historically Responsive Literacy (CHRL), developed by Gholdy Muhammad, is an equity framework that structures literacy instruction around four interdependent learning goals: identity (making sense of who one is), skills (proficiencies in reading, writing, and speaking), intellect (knowledge built through literacy practices), and criticality (the ability to read power, equity, and oppression in the world). Lessons and texts are selected and designed to be responsive to students' racial, cultural, and historical communities rather than treating those communities as peripheral. The framework draws on the literacy practices of 19th-century Black literary societies, which pursued all four goals simultaneously [Muhammad, 2020].

Design Implications¶

CHRL extends culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 1995) by making the four goals an explicit planning template: every lesson plan names objectives in each of the four dimensions, not skills alone. The framework is additive — it does not replace skill instruction but embeds it within meaningful cultural and historical contexts.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Texts, primary sources, and curricular materials that represent students' cultures and histories accurately and substantively, not decoratively
  • Educator knowledge of students' communities and of the histories those texts carry; this is a professional learning demand, not a one-off lesson tweak
  • Lesson plans that name objectives in all four dimensions (identity, skills, intellect, criticality) rather than skills alone
  • Texts and materials that reflect the histories, voices, and perspectives of students' communities and of marginalized groups broadly
  • Lesson plans that name goals for identity, skills, intellect, and criticality — not skills alone
  • Tasks that invite students to connect content to their own lives and to real social questions (Authentic Audiences & Purposes)
  • Teacher knowledge of the histories and cultural contexts surrounding the texts used

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on depth of integration; superficial "heroes and holidays" add-ons without substantive connection to the curriculum do not produce the engagement or identity benefits the framework targets [~M]
  • Requires sustained educator learning and institutional support; in schools lacking resources or leadership commitment, implementation often collapses into text substitution alone [-W]
  • Standards and accountability regimes that assess skills only can crowd out the identity, intellect, and criticality goals [~M]
  • Reducing HRL to "diverse book lists" without the criticality and identity pursuits produces surface-level engagement and no measurable skill gains [-W]
  • Teachers unfamiliar with the historical contexts of texts may present tokenized or inaccurate representations, undermining the identity pursuit [-W]
  • In accountability contexts that assess skills only, the intellectual and criticality goals are often dropped, collapsing the framework into standard instruction [~M]
  • Requires sustained professional learning; one-off adoption without curriculum redesign rarely changes instruction [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full-framework lesson planning (all four goals per lesson) versus unit-level integration across a text set
  • Adaptation across grade levels: picture books and read-alouds in K–5; primary-source inquiry and critical media analysis in secondary and higher education
  • Subject-area extensions beyond literacy (science, mathematics) using the same four-goal planning template
  • Text sets: pairing canonical texts with counter-narratives from the same period to teach criticality alongside comprehension
  • Writing for real audiences: students produce advocacy writing, poetry, or journalism addressed to actual communities
  • Interdisciplinary units: history, science, and literacy integrated around a single equity question
  • Digital extensions: students create podcasts or multimedia responses, connecting to Building Empathy through perspective-taking tasks

Target Learners¶

  • Youth of color and other learners whose histories and cultures have been marginalized in standards, texts, and school practices — the framework was designed for and with these students [Muhammad, 2020]
  • All learners benefit from criticality and identity work, but engagement gains are strongest when texts connect to students' lived experience Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
  • Learners whose sense of academic belonging is fragile; culturally responsive contexts support self-efficacy and persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Students from historically marginalized communities whose identities are underrepresented in standard curricula [~M]
  • Adolescents developing civic and racial-ethnic identity, for whom criticality tasks are developmentally salient [+W]
  • All learners benefit from the skills pursuit, which remains central — HRL adds goals rather than replacing explicit skill teaching

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Identity development: learners articulate who they are and their place in their communities
  • Literacy skill development: reading, writing, speaking, and listening proficiencies embedded in meaningful contexts
  • Intellectual development: building knowledge through literacy practices
  • Criticality: analyzing power, inequity, and oppression, and acting against them
  • Disciplinary literacy skills: reading, writing, speaking, and listening proficiency
  • Conceptual and historical knowledge: understanding how ideas and events connect across time
  • Critical consciousness: analyzing power, injustice, and agency in texts and the world

Instructions¶

  1. Select a central text or text set that reflects students' cultures and histories substantively (Assigned Readings)
  2. Activate students' funds of knowledge before reading (Activating Prior Knowledge)
  3. Frame the text with historical and cultural context, using a Case Study or primary sources where possible
  4. Facilitate Class Discussion that moves through all four goals — identity questions ("Who am I in this story?"), skill work, intellectual questions, and criticality questions ("Whose power does this serve?")
  5. Design an Application task in which students produce writing or action directed at an authentic audience and purpose

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — surfacing students' cultural funds of knowledge is the entry point for responsive instruction
  • Community-Based Learning — connects literacy tasks to students' actual communities and histories
  • Use culturally responsive practices to make preschool learning relevant for children from all backgrounds
  • Acting-Role-Play — embodying historical perspectives supports the intellect and criticality pursuits
  • Action-Oriented Feedback — feedback that names progress toward agency, not just accuracy
  • Use cultural data sets from students' everyday worlds as a gateway to literary interpretation
  • Engage learners consciously, reflectively, and critically in using texts to understand their world
  • Pair an action-based silent reading method with instruction in literary appreciation and extensive reading

Examples¶

  • Hill Pedagogy / Black literary societies model — Muhammad's own curricular examples use 19th-century Black literary society reading lists and questions as models for contemporary units that pair canonical skills work with identity and criticality goals (Muhammad, 2020)
  • Culturally sustaining text sets in EL Education curricula — published K–8 modules built around texts representing diverse communities, with discussion and writing tasks addressing identity and criticality (https://eleducation.org)
  • Rethinking Schools publications (Rethinking Columbus, A People's History for the Classroom) — teacher-written lessons pairing literacy skills with critical analysis of historical narratives (https://www.rethinkingschools.org)
  • Hill Pedagogy / Black literary society curricula — Muhammad's historical analysis of 19th-century Black literary societies, whose members pursued reading, writing, oratory, and social action together; the model for the four pursuits (Muhammad, 2020).
  • Culturally sustaining ELA units — districts adopting HRL through Scholastic's Cultivating Genius study guides and Muhammad's later Unearthing Joy (2023) plan templates, which structure lesson planning around the four pursuits.
  • Secondary English units on counter-narratives — students read a canonical text alongside a response text by an author of the affected community, then write their own counter-narrative for a school or community audience.
