Entry 435 annotates Williams's 1915 Psychological Clinic article, an "Application of the Stanford revision to selected children in four California cities," reporting the four printed classification percentages. A separate Williams study of 215 delinquent boys also found 32% feeble-minded (#431).

"Application of the Stanford revision to selected children in four California cities. 32% were feeble-minded, 20% borderline, 22% dull normal, and 26% normal or superior."

Related Claims¶

  • In Wallin's St. Louis psycho-educational clinic, of 430 referred cases, 25.9% of 320 boys and 40% of 110 girls were found feeble-minded, with almost twice as many backward and retarded as feeble-minded. — related
  • Terman and Knollin indicate the Stanford revision is more accurate than the Vineland revision in estimating dullness, borderlinity and feeble-mindedness. — related
  • Application of the Stanford Revision to 1000 non-selected children found the intelligence quotient a valid expression of intelligence, practically no sex differences, and social status not a disturbing influence on mental-age estimates. — related
  • Woolley found 25.5% of 18-year-old industrial workers feeble-minded on the point scale and advised changing the definition of feeble-mindedness so tested deficiency better matched common-sense opinion. — related
  • The Binet-Simon scale is a valuable diagnostic tool for classifying defective children when used by a trained examiner but dangerous in untrained hands — related
  • Delinquent boys at the Texas State Juvenile Training School averaged constructive merit of 51.3, equal to that of normal 9.5-year-olds — related
  • State charity dependent cases contained a much larger percentage of feeble-minded children than the county home cases, 19.5% versus 5.7% by the conservative estimate — related
  • Among 82 state charity cases of dependent children, the most conservative estimate put 19.5% feeble-minded and only about 37% testing up to normal — related
  • The year scale and the point scale yield disparate results and differ in diagnostic accuracy for delinquent populations, per Haines's studies — related
  • Distributions of normal and feeble-minded groups overlap, with a borderzone between them, per Kohs's examination of 335 inmates — related
