Evidence¶

Andrew C. Butler and Henry L. Roediger III 2008¶

Andrew C. Butler and Henry L. Roediger III. (2008). Feedback enhances the positive effects and reduces the negative effects of multiple-choice testing. Memory & Cognition, 36(3), 604-616. https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604

q3 · i? · causal · r2

Confidence-accuracy correspondence analysis in the 72-participant experiment: delayed-feedback (M = .56) and immediate-feedback (M = .49) proportions correct matched mean confidence estimates (55 and 46), while no-test (M = .14) and no-feedback (M = .31) conditions showed overconfidence. No effect size is printed for this contrast.

"The overall mean proportions correct for the delayed-feedback (M(( .56) and immediate-feedback (M(( .49) conditions were almost identical to the mean confidence estimate (means of 55 an d 46, respect ively)."

Andrew C. Butler and Henry L. Roediger III 2008 (2)¶

Andrew C. Butler and Henry L. Roediger III. (2008). Feedback enhances the positive effects and reduces the negative effects of multiple-choice testing. Memory & Cognition, 36(3), 604-616. https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604

q3 · i2 · causal · r2

Confidence ratings on forced-report intrusions: prior lure exposure raised intrusion confidence (overall M = 39) relative to the no-test condition (M = 26), d = .56, suggesting exposure increased belief in the veracity of misinformation.

"subjects assigned greater confidence to intru sions produced in these three conditions (overall M 39) than to those in the no-test condition ( M 26) [t(71) 4.11, SEM 2.98, d .56, prep .99]"
