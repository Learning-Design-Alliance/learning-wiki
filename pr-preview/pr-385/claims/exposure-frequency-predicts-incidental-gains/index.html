Evidence¶

Zou 2019¶

Zou, F., & Yan, X. (2019). Incidental English Vocabulary Acquisition Through Reading: A Review in the Last Two Decades. English Language Teaching, 12(12). https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v12n12p39

q2 · i? · review · r2

The review reports, citing Tekmen and Daloğlu (2006), a study of intermediate, upper-intermediate and advanced students using the Vocabulary Levels Test as pre-test and two post-tests of 30 of 60 words; more frequent words were gained by more learners. No effect size is printed.

"The results showed that if a word appeared in the text more frequently, more learners were a ble to gain it."

q2 · i? · review · r?

The review reports, citing Godfroid (2018), an eye-tracking study confirming repetition contributes to gains in form recognition, meaning recall and meaning recognition, with exposure most influencing form recognition. No effect size is printed.

"Godfroid (2018) in his study confirmed that repetition of target words contribute to the gai n of all three types of vocabulary knowledge: form recognition, meaning recall and meaning recognition."

Uchihara et al. 2019¶

Uchihara, T., Webb, S., & Yanagisawa, A. (2019). The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies. Language Learning, 69(3), 559–599. doi:10.1111/lang.12343

q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies, open data (abstract only) · i2 · medium effect, r=.34 · n=26 studies (45 effect sizes, N=1,918) · quant-synthesis · r?

This meta-analysis synthesised 45 correlations from 26 second-language studies (1,918 learners) between the number of encounters with a word and incidental learning of it. The mean relationship was medium (r = .34), and its size varied with learner variables (age, vocabulary knowledge), treatment variables (spaced learning, visual support, engagement, range in number of encounters) and method (nonword use, forewarning of a comprehension test, vocabulary test format). It supports frequency as a predictor, but it pooled only frequency–learning correlations and did not rank frequency against other predictors, so it does not test "strongest"; the abstract also does not say that all studies involved reading. Read from the abstract only; the design is correlational.

"Results showed that there was a medium effect ( r = .34) of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning."

Discussion¶

Uchihara et al. (2019) support the direction of this claim for second-language learners, with a medium mean correlation (r = .34) between encounters and incidental learning across 26 studies, but do not test the claim's "strongest predictor": the meta-analysis pooled frequency–learning correlations only. The title overstates what either entry on this page shows.
