Inquiry-based Learning¶

Principle · All principles
Evidence · 61 claims (34 for, 20 mixed, 7 against) · 68 studies (19 causal, 15 quant-synthesis, 10 review, 9 theoretical, 6 qualitative, 6 design, 3 associational), q1–q4 · 16 of 68 report an effect size · 48 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

Inquiry-based learning organizes learning around a question that learners investigate: they gather or examine evidence, test ideas and explain what they found. This page owns the general relationship. Read it in two layers.

Guided inquiry, in school science, is associated with better achievement than the comparison instruction, and guidance is what carries it. In school science, two syntheses recorded here report (one states its studies were pre-k-12; the claim page does not report the other's learners or settings) that inquiry-based teaching improved achievement and conceptual understanding, and that teacher-led inquiry showed larger effects than student-led inquiry. Most of the underlying studies are quasi-experimental, and the claim pages do not report the comparator, learners' prior knowledge or the horizon for each study.

Unguided inquiry is the condition under which the relationship fails. For a learner who is a novice on the target relation (for example, how to design a fair test), leaving essential content or procedures to be discovered without support learns less than explicit instruction, while discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations does better than other instruction (guided-discovery). Investigating before instruction (problem-first) is a narrower configuration with support for conceptual knowledge and transfer only when instruction follows, no procedural advantage, and a reversal for young children.

So the expected change is in conceptual understanding and evidence-based explanation (conceptual-understanding, near-transfer), conditional on: the learner's task-specific knowledge of the target relation and of the inquiry moves themselves; how much guidance is given and when (prompts, feedback, modelling, teacher-led explanation); whether a consolidation or synthesis phase connects findings to the canonical idea; the outcome chosen (conceptual, procedural, inquiry skill, far transfer); and the horizon. No claim in the wiki reports a delayed-retention outcome for inquiry, and none tests inquiry against another method outside science or with adults beyond one pre-service-teacher study; for undergraduate research courses, one-to-one mentored research, remote inquiry and gamified scenarios the wiki holds only descriptive case studies and learner ratings. The guidance itself should change with expertise: supports that help novices can become redundant for learners who already know the relation and the moves (expertise reversal, recorded outside inquiry).

Siblings. Problem-based learning is one inquiry form, centred on an authentic problem; its conditional model (guidance for novices leads, problem-first is a narrower configuration, the curriculum-level average is small and variable) sits inside this one and should be read for problem-centred designs. Scaffolding and fading models how support is raised, lowered and withdrawn; inside inquiry it supplies the policy for the guidance this page says is needed, though its evidence comes mostly from one-to-one tutoring rather than inquiry tasks. The inquiry-based learning pattern and the 5E learning cycle are reusable designs built on this relationship; neither has yet been converted to this format, and their sequences are proposals.

Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

The page's earlier guidance, kept here as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it, and no claim here tests the sequence as a whole.

  1. Name the target before the question. Decide whether the inquiry is meant to teach a content relation (why moths change colour), an inquiry practice (designing a fair test, judging evidence) or both, and how each will be assessed. Untested; from the earlier page's learning objectives.
  2. Pose a meaningful, investigable question that needs the target idea to answer, not recall in disguise, and give learners access to the evidence they will examine (data, phenomena, a simulation, sources, cases). Untested; from the earlier page's requirements.
  3. Check what each learner can already do, and teach what is missing. Probe the key relation and the key inquiry move (for example, ask the learner to judge whether a given comparison is fair). Where it is absent, explain or model it before or alongside the investigation. Evidence: unassisted discovery learned less than explicit instruction, and children who learned the control-of-variables procedure directly did as well on a later transfer task as the few who discovered it (Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S], below). Treat this as diagnosis, not as an "Engage" activity expected to raise learning by itself: advance organizers helped learning in a 1980 meta-analysis, but activating prior topic knowledge did not improve primary pupils' text comprehension in one randomised experiment, and two amounts of activation did not differ in another (Activating prior knowledge [~M]; none of these is an inquiry study).
  4. Guide the investigation: staged prompts, structured procedures, feedback on moves and teacher-led explanation phases, keeping the question with the learners. Evidence: teacher-led inquiry showed larger effects than student-led, and enhanced discovery beat other instruction (below). Asking learners to explain what each result shows is one form of guidance with its own evidence: self-explanation prompts improved learning by a moderate average amount (g = .55 across 69 effect sizes), mostly on immediate tests and outside inquiry (Self-explanation prompts [+M]). Make help contingent on what the learner just did (more support after a failed move, less after a success): in one-to-one tutoring, fully contingent support beat fixed, high or no support at immediate and one-month tests, though in one dynamic-assessment synthesis explicit strategy training did better still (Contingent scaffolding [+M]; untested in classroom inquiry). Which forms of guidance matter most inside inquiry is not settled in the wiki.
  5. Require a synthesis with explicit standards of evidence: learners explain, argue or present a conclusion and say what in the data supports it, then revise. Partly supported: in the 37-study meta-analysis, studies emphasizing epistemic activities, or procedural, epistemic and social activities together, showed the highest effects. Structuring the synthesis as argument (claim, evidence, rebuttal) has support of its own: across 46 studies of online science argumentation, both argument skill and content learning rose by a medium amount (g ≈ 0.6), more in K-12 than in higher education (Argumentation improves reasoning [+M]; read from the abstract). The standards themselves are untested here; from the earlier page.
  6. Connect findings to the canonical idea. If learners investigate before instruction, follow the investigation with instruction that builds on what they produced. Evidence: the problem-first advantage on conceptual knowledge is recorded only when instruction follows (Productive Failure [~M], below).
  7. Withdraw guidance as learners show they can make the moves unaided, checking on an unassisted task before removing each support. From Scaffolding and Fading; untested within inquiry here, and one large scaffolding synthesis found no difference by whether scaffolds were faded. The reason to withdraw is expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can become redundant, or depress performance, once learners have the schemas (Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as expertise increases [~M], reviews of worked-example research, not inquiry). Across a unit, a workable progression is heavy guidance and modelled moves in the first investigations, prompts only in the middle ones, and an unprompted investigation of a new question at the end; this dose is a proposal.
  8. Judge the design on separate outcomes: conceptual understanding, near and far transfer, procedural fluency and inquiry skill, at a stated horizon, not on fact recall alone and not on engagement. Check during the investigation, not only at the end: formative assessment raised K-12 achievement by a small amount in two meta-analyses (d ≈ .20–.29), more where students self-assessed (Assessment for learning [+M]; not inquiry-specific). Evidence: one study of guided simulation inquiry found a near-transfer gain and no far transfer (below).

Constraints from the earlier page, still untested: inquiry usually takes more time than explanation; open tasks can overtax learners without background knowledge or inquiry routines; inquiry can become opinion-sharing unless evidence standards are explicit; and making a task open-ended does not by itself give learners meaningful agency.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: can the learners already make the key inquiry move and do they know the target relation?, how old are they?, is the outcome conceptual understanding, inquiry skill or a procedure?, and will instruction come before the investigation or after it?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is what a learner does with a question under stated conditions: the question they pursue, the comparison or source they choose, the data they record, the conclusion they draw and the reason they give, the prompts and help they receive, and what they produce. "Can inquire", "owns the question" and "understands the concept" are inferred states. Activity, talk and enthusiasm are observations of participation; none measures whether the target relation was learned. A correct conclusion reached by following a worksheet does not show that the learner could reach it without the worksheet. In a group, a shared report does not show what each member can do.

Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
Designs a comparison that changes several things at once Missing the control-of-variables procedure; holds the idea but is overloaded by the apparatus or the setting; does not see the question as needing a comparison Ask the learner to judge a ready-made fair and unfair comparison (evaluation, not production); if they judge correctly, reduce the apparatus demand rather than reteaching the idea.
Records data accurately, but the conclusion restates a prior belief No working standard for what counts as evidence; a misconception resistant to one investigation; the data did not discriminate between the ideas Ask what result would have changed their mind; check whether the data, as collected, could distinguish their belief from the canonical one.
Completes every guided step correctly, cannot explain or apply the idea to a new case The prompts carried the reasoning (compliance, not inquiry); the idea is held but the learner lacks the language to explain it; the new case differs in more than surface features Pose a changed-context question without prompts, allow an accessible response mode, and score near and far transfer separately.
Busy and engaged, little use of the target idea in the conclusion Floundering search without the needed knowledge; consolidation not yet given; the question as posed does not require the target idea Ask the learner to name the idea their conclusion rests on; check whether a correct answer to the question as written needs it; record whether any instruction connected the findings to the canonical idea.
Group report strong, individual explanation weak Work carried by one or two members; learning distributed but not individually consolidated; the individual probe is harder than the group task Ask each learner for an individual explanation under matched conditions, and record who did what during the investigation.

These rows are proposals, untested with learners. One design study gives a partial observation for the second row: when Year 8 students' data contradicted their hypothesis and they were prompted to re-observe, most generated a plausible explanation of the discrepancy rather than dismissing the result, though the explanations were not judged against a scientific criterion (Scripted personally meaningful inquiry [~W]). In an undergraduate design inquiry, many students still could not identify engineering tradeoffs, and the designers judged the budget constraint meant to surface them too complicated (Students found tradeoffs difficult [-W], an instructor's report): a constraint built to make reasoning visible can add load of its own. A probe can shift confidence among the explanations; it does not identify a cause on its own. Each probe is itself a learning exposure and should be recorded as one.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Inquiry Based Teaching Improves Science Achievement [+M]: a meta-analysis of 37 experimental and quasi-experimental studies of inquiry-based science teaching (1996–2006) reports an overall mean effect on student learning of g = .50; a mixed-methods synthesis of 138 studies (1984–2002) of pre-k-12 science reports a positive trend favouring inquiry, especially instruction emphasizing active thinking and drawing conclusions from data, over more passive instruction for conceptual understanding, with no pooled effect size. The claim page frames the comparator as transmission-style instruction, but the entries do not state comparators, learners' prior knowledge or horizons by study. Not settled: the abstracts could not confirm the entries, and one judge failure was reviewed and dismissed. It does not predict the effect for one class, for unguided inquiry, outside science, or at a delayed horizon.
  • Teacher Guided Inquiry Outperforms Student Led [~M]: in the same 37-study meta-analysis, studies with teacher-led activities had mean effects about .40 larger than those with student-led conditions. It rests on the same two syntheses as the claim above and is listed there as a merge candidate, so it is not independent support. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries passes. It is marked ~ because it limits the relationship to guided forms; the claim page reads it as a claim about novices, but neither entry breaks results down by prior knowledge.
  • Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]: a meta-analysis of 164 studies found explicit instruction outperformed unassisted discovery across 580 comparisons (d = 0.38 in favour of explicit instruction), while enhanced or assisted discovery (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) outperformed other instruction across 360 comparisons (d = 0.30). An experiment with 112 third- and fourth-grade child learners, novice at the control-of-variables strategy, found many more mastered it under direct instruction than discovery, and those taught directly did as well on a later transfer task as the few who discovered it; that experiment compares direct instruction with discovery and does not test guided inquiry itself. A narrative review argues the same direction. Partly checked: 1 of 3 entries passes; one judge failure was reviewed and dismissed. This is the boundary of the principle: it predicts poor learning when inquiry means unsupported discovery of essential content, not that guided inquiry fails. The abstract does not break results down by prior knowledge.
  • PAIR-C scaffolding shows mixed evidence for deep understanding and reduced misconceptions in emergent-phenomena instruction [~W]: one quasi-experiment (adult pre-service teachers, n = 50, no random individual assignment) in which both groups learned natural selection with the same agent-based simulations; adding prompts, annotations and feedback around one causal framework produced a significant near-transfer gain, trends that did not reach p < .05 on deep understanding and misconceptions, and no far transfer in either group, at an immediate posttest only. Not yet checked against its sources. It shows that added guidance inside simulation inquiry can help near transfer and that far transfer did not follow; it does not test inquiry against another method.
  • Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~M]: a three-level meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons) set problem solving followed by instruction against the same instruction first; problem-first favoured conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), not procedural knowledge (g = −0.03), with larger effects when learners generated multiple solutions, worked in groups and received instruction building on their solutions, and a reversal for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. Partly checked: 1 of 2 entries passes. Carry it only to designs where investigation precedes instruction and instruction follows; it is reported most reliably in well-structured domains (mathematics, physics) and does not support investigation with no subsequent instruction.

How far each may be carried: the two inquiry syntheses are school science and mostly quasi-experimental; the discovery meta-analysis spans domains but is read from its abstract; the PAIR-C study is one domain, one adult population and one immediate test. Retain learner age and starting knowledge, the exact comparator (transmission, explicit instruction, unassisted discovery, instruction-first), the amount and timing of guidance, time on task, outcome type and horizon when transporting these results. A pooled effect over unlike implementations is not a forecast for one class or one learner, and a nonsignificant difference does not establish equivalence.

The evidence by question¶

Each question below links a general claim that gathers the studies behind it into one table, one row per distinct study, with each study's design and effect as recorded and no averaged verdict (eval/synthesis/inquiry.json, scripts/synthesize_claim.py). The count is of distinct studies across the claim and the narrower claims that bear on it.

  • Does inquiry-based teaching raise science achievement over the usual instruction? Inquiry Based Teaching Improves Science Achievement [+S]: 8 distinct studies.
  • Does adding guidance to inquiry improve what learners learn, and does the kind of guidance matter? Guidance during inquiry learning improves post-inquiry learning outcomes (d = 0.50) [~M]: 4 distinct studies.
  • Should novices be left to discover the target content without support? Unassisted discovery produces less learning than explicit instruction, while discovery enhanced with guidance outperforms other instruction [~S]: 5 distinct studies.
  • For whom, in which domain and on which measure does the cost of unassisted discovery (and the benefit of enhanced discovery) change? Age moderates discovery-learning effects: the explicit-instruction advantage over unassisted discovery is larger for adolescents than adults, and the enhanced-discovery benefit larger for adults than children [~M]: 1 distinct studies.
  • Should support be contingent on the learner's last response rather than fixed? Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support [+M]: 31 distinct studies, mostly tutoring and feedback, few inside inquiry.
  • When should guidance be reduced as learners gain knowledge? Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases [~M]: 7 distinct studies, none an inquiry study.
  • Does arguing from evidence improve reasoning and content learning? Argumentation improves reasoning [+M]: 7 distinct studies.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

The heading is kept because tools look for it; new links land here unread. Re-read against the model on 2026-10-19. Of the 27 claims listed here then, 18 are now used above (two were duplicate links to claims the model already cited). What remains is relevant but does not change the model, for the reason given beside each.

  • A training-design argument, not tested by any study recorded here, holds that practising whole complex tasks improves transfer of complex skills [+M] — checked by the judge: all 1 entries pass (abstract). A design argument with no experiment recorded; it does not test inquiry, and its own advice to lower load early for novices is already the model's.
  • Students Difficulties Solar Movement Observable Changes [+M] — a narrative review of secondary students' difficulties with the Sun's apparent motion; it informs an astronomy design's starting point (see the shadow-to-sundial design under Examples), not the general relationship.
  • A six-step C/I cycle administered in a sophomore engineering physics class and a graduate physics class guided a group to select a conveyor-belt power-calibration method via a co-constructed decision metric [+W] — a case report of one class's decision; it describes a structured cycle working and measures no learning.
  • Self-regulated learning models treat self-monitoring as a core process through which learners notice gaps and adjust strategy [~M] — theory syntheses, not tests; consistent with step 8's self-assessment note, and no inquiry evidence.
  • Students rated the image decoding, poster sharing, and synthesis lecture components of a digital-images inquiry highest on a five-point feedback scale [+W] — learner ratings (about 25 students) that favour the sharing and synthesis phases; consistent with steps 5–6, but ratings are not learning.
  • Undergraduate learners rated CyberAGENTS positively, with scenario authenticity highest and challenge difficulty alignment lowest [+W] — ratings of a gamified cybersecurity scenario by 24 students; at most a reminder that difficulty was the weakest-rated feature.
  • Teachers' reported weekly use of the 5E instructional model for lesson planning rose from 13 percent to 42 percent over the Initiative [+W] — teacher uptake of the 5E model, not an effect on learners.
  • Active learning improves exam performance. [+S] — undergraduate STEM active learning against lecture (0.47 SD); it does not separate inquiry from other active methods, so it neither tests nor bounds this page's relationship.
  • The bilingual cultural map functioned as a boundary object supporting divergent interpretations across students, museum educators, and end-users without requiring semantic consensus [+W] — (not about this page) a cross-sector design artifact, not inquiry learning.
  • Dewey argues the scientific attitude is achieved by pupils taking a hand in the making of knowledge through inquiry [+W] — attached 2026-10-24 from J. Dewey (1910), which proposed "Teach science for its method-value — cultivating the scientific attitude through pupil inquiry — rather than primarily for informational subject-matter".

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

Ask what the learner wants from the investigation, and why, separately from the designer's objective. A question chosen as authentic by the designer may not be one the learner cares about, and a learner who cares about the answer may not care about the method the designer wants practised. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations; engagement during the investigation does not establish that the learner values the target capability, and choosing one's own question does not by itself establish ownership. Choose a representative instrument, criterion, permitted resources and horizon for each outcome that matters: conceptual understanding, transfer, inquiry skill and the quality of the final explanation are different claims.

For example, a secondary student testing water from a local creek may want to know whether it is safe to swim in, while the designer's objective is that the student can design a fair comparison and justify a conclusion from data. A correct answer about the creek, reached by following the teacher's sampling plan, establishes neither. Record both aims, let the student's question drive the investigation, and check the designer's objective separately with an unprompted fair-test task on a new question.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if comparable learners receiving guided inquiry fail to outperform the comparison instruction on aligned conceptual measures in randomized or well-matched comparisons with stated comparators, or if the advantage disappears at a delayed horizon. If guided inquiry and explicit instruction of matched time produce the same conceptual and transfer outcomes, the case for inquiry rests on other aims (inquiry skill, the learner's valued goals) and should say so. If the teacher-led advantage turns out to be an artefact of study quality or outcome alignment rather than guidance, revise the guidance claim. If unguided inquiry is found to work for learners with stated prior knowledge, record that boundary rather than generalizing it. Do not protect the model by relabelling every failure as poor facilitation or insufficient authenticity after the fact.

An engaging investigation, an immediate conceptual gain, near transfer, far transfer, delayed retention and the learner's valued use are separate claims. The evidence recorded here establishes no optimal amount or form of guidance, no readiness threshold for open inquiry, and no effect of inquiry outside school science beyond one adult simulation study.

Claims¶

  • The relation between frequency of inquiry activities and science achievement is curvilinear, with the highest gains where inquiry combines with direct teacher-based instruction [~W]
  • Guided inquiry was positively associated with science achievement in all 16 PISA 2015 regions where it was applied [+W]
  • Program-based studies show mixed results: a large inquiry-curriculum study found higher achievement, while two carefully designed comparison studies found no differences [~M]
  • Instructional approaches that include inquiry learning are more effective than direct instruction for acquiring conceptual knowledge, per the review's synthesis of meta-analyses [+W]
  • Open inquiry enhances students' science literacy and related skills such as environmental literacy, argumentation, autonomy and reflection [~W]
  • Student-led inquiry-based investigation is identified as the least effective instructional element in PISA 2015 analyses [-W]
  • Unguided inquiry students overestimate their hypothesis quality in the transfer task more than video modeling students [-W]

  • Video modeling examples improve hypothesis and argumentation quality in an inquiry training task compared to unguided inquiry [+W]

Related Principles¶

  • Problem-Based Learning — inquiry and problem-based designs overlap when questions are organized around authentic problems.
  • Evaluating Sources — inquiry requires judging the quality and relevance of evidence.
  • Explaining Their Thinking — inquiry culminates in explanation, justification, and revision.
  • Guided Practice — inquiry often works better when investigative moves are practiced with support before becoming independent.

Examples¶

  • Source-based investigation: Learners pursue a question using multiple texts, data, or cases and justify their conclusion.
  • Guided science or social inquiry: Learners investigate a phenomenon or issue using structured prompts and staged evidence gathering.
  • Problem-framing workshops: Learners refine a broad topic into investigable questions before researching.
  • Digital inquiry tasks: Learners navigate simulations, databases, or online sources to test and revise explanations.
  • Interdisciplinary Societal Dilemma Units — small-group deliberative inquiry into an authentic, multi-solution societal dilemma
  • Secuencia de cinco actividades que va del problema cotidiano a la construcción de un instrumento de medición
  • Learners jointly investigating a community issue and synthesizing evidence into a shared explanation.
  • Science groups developing, testing, and revising explanations around a common phenomenon.
  • Professional teams analyzing trends, gathering evidence, and updating a shared recommendation.
  • Three-part instructional strategy: build community, scaffold inquiry-based instruction while reducing cognitive load, and explicitly develop skills, habits, and mindsets
  • Students research a local issue and build a recommendation supported by sources.
  • Make Learning Relevant
  • Design Challenges
  • Use a budget-constrained image encoding challenge to force explicit tradeoffs between spatial and color resolution
  • Use jigsaw-style sharing so every student is responsible for all the material
  • Twig Science: phenomena-based K–8 science curriculum aligned to NGSS
  • Design professional learning for leaders using a 5E learning cycle
  • Science learners inferring relationships from data patterns.
  • Math learners identifying a rule or structure by comparing worked instances.
  • Professional learners examining examples of practice and deriving principles from guided prompts.

  • An after-school "Sustainability Squad" (ages 12-14) food-decay and packaging investigation in which some students' data collection prompted them to revise their hypotheses multiple times over several weeks before settling on a testable question.
