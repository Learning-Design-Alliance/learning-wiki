    Reflection¶

    Principle · All principles Evidence · 13 claims (7 for, 6 mixed) · 18 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), q2–q4 · 5 of 18 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

    Conditional relationship¶

    For a learner who has just completed a performance, an artifact or a decision (a solved problem set, a lesson taught, a clinical encounter, a draft), prompting them to examine it against a stated criterion, explain what happened and name a change for the next attempt is expected to improve later performance on comparable tasks more than unguided reflection ("write about what you learned") or no reflection. The expected change is in subsequent task performance and in the analytic quality of the reflection itself; these are different outcomes, and gains in the second do not establish the first. The relationship is conditional on four things: the prompt is specific to the task and the criterion rather than generic; the learner knows enough about the task to judge their own work (or is given an exemplar, a criterion or feedback to judge against); there is a next attempt in which the planned change can be made; and the reflection does not displace the practice or feedback it is meant to inform. The evidence linked here is mostly from adult learners in higher and professional education and from school-based writing-to-learn studies, in classroom settings, with outcomes that are pooled general-achievement or course exams at the end of a course; no claim here tests delayed-retention or far transfer.

    This page owns reflection as the examination of a past performance or experience to change the next one, including in practice settings (teaching, clinical work, studio) where no single scored criterion exists. The converted Self-Regulated Learning principle models planning, monitoring and acting within a task; its self-evaluation phase is the part of reflection that has a stated criterion, and its caveats on monitoring accuracy apply here unchanged. Where the brief is about a learner regulating an ongoing task, use that page; where it is about learning from a completed performance or from practice experience, use this one and read that page's monitoring rows alongside.

    Default design, while the relationship is untested¶

    The page's earlier guidance, kept as a concrete default a designer can act on and revise. Each step is a design proposal; the evidence for it is stated beside it. No claim here tests the whole sequence.

    1. Choose a concrete object to reflect on, soon after it happened: one performance, artifact, decision sequence or recording, not "the week" or "the course". Untested; from the earlier page.
    2. Make the criterion visible before the reflection: a rubric row, an exemplar, a scored result or an expert's version to compare against. Novices' unaided judgments of their own work are unreliable (self-assessment accuracy [~M], tested with secondary-school and university students).
    3. Use specific prompts, not an open invitation. A short default is three prompts: one thing that worked and why, one thing that did not and why, one change for next time (the earlier page's "one strong move, one weak move, one next adjustment"). Specific prompts beat generic ones on course exams in one quasi-experiment, with the amount of reflection held equal (structured reflection [+M]).
    4. Ask for the reasoning, not only the account. "Why did that step fail?" rather than "What did you do?". Untested as part of reflection here; self-explanation evidence is listed under Further evidence and has not been read against this model.
    5. For novices, focus the reflection on the process, not only the result (which step, which strategy), and move to outcome-focused reflection once the skill is secure. Untested here; the process-goal claim under Further evidence is the basis and has not been read against this model.
    6. Close the loop: the named change is written where the next attempt starts, and the next reflection opens by asking whether it was tried and what happened. Untested; from the earlier page. If no next attempt exists in the design, shorten or drop the reflection rather than keep it as a ritual.
    7. Time it twice where the design allows: a short reflection immediately after the performance, and a later comparison across two or more attempts. Untested; from the earlier page.
    8. Add an outside view: feedback, an expert comment or a peer's reading before or alongside the self-reflection. Reflection embedded with formative assessment outperformed reflection alone in one quasi-experiment with prospective teachers (formative assessment with reflection [~W]); peer interaction is a reported moderator of reflection effects in higher education, direction not stated on the claim page.

    When a condition is not met: if learners cannot yet judge their own work, supply the criterion and a worked comparison (step 2) before asking for evaluation; if no feedback is available, ask for a prediction before the result and a comparison after it; if no next attempt follows, replace reflection with a short check of the criterion.

    Fitting the design to a situation¶

    The three facts that most change the decision:

    • Can the learner judge this kind of work? A learner who cannot tell good work from weak work will reflect fluently and wrongly; the design must then supply a criterion, an exemplar or feedback first. If unstated, ask: have they seen a strong and a weak example of this task and said which is which?
    • Is there a next attempt? Reflection is expected to pay off through a changed next move; with no comparable task to follow, the expected benefit is unsupported. If unstated, ask: when will they next do something like this, and will anyone see whether they changed it?
    • What is the outcome that counts? Better reflections, better course exams and better practice are separate outcomes, and the evidence here is strongest for the first two. If unstated, ask: what would the learner do differently, observably, if the reflection worked?

    Observation, state and explanation¶

    What can be observed is a reflection text or talk under stated conditions (prompted or free, private or shared, graded or not, with or without a criterion), a self-rating set against a scored result, and what the learner does in the next attempt. "Reflective", "insight" and "metacognitive awareness" are inferred states. A fluent analytic reflection is a writing performance; it does not show that the learner will act differently, and a brief or descriptive one does not show that they did not think. Record the prompt, the audience and the stakes with every reflection collected.

    Response Plausible explanations Observation that could change the interpretation
    Recounts what happened, no analysis Prompt too open; no criterion to analyse against; too little task knowledge to see what mattered; writing burden in the language of instruction Replace the open prompt with a criterion-specific one and compare; ask the same question orally; give an exemplar pair and ask which is stronger and why.
    Analyses accurately, next attempt unchanged No comparable next attempt soon enough; no strategy available to carry out the change; the plan was not visible when the next task began Show the planned change at the start of the next task and record whether it is tried; ask what they would need to do it.
    Rates own performance highly, scored result is weak Miscalibration from too little task knowledge; criterion unclear; audience or grading rewards a positive account Ask for a rating before the result and a comparison after; score a worked exemplar against the same criterion; collect one private, ungraded reflection and compare.
    Entries become generic or identical across weeks Compliance without reflection; prompt fatigue; nothing new happened in the period; the learner does not value the activity Vary the prompt to cite a specific artefact; check whether the period contained a new performance; ask what the learner hopes to get from it.
    Declines to reflect, or writes very little Low value placed on the task; reluctance to expose failure publicly or to a grader; writing as the barrier Offer a private or spoken option and see whether the content changes; ask what the learner wants to improve.

    These rows are proposals and have not been tested with learners. A single contrast shifts confidence among explanations; it does not identify a cause. A reflection prompt is itself a learning event, so record every prompt and feedback the learner received.

    Evidence and qualifications¶

    • Reflection interventions in higher education [+M]: a random-effects meta-analysis of 23 controlled studies (n = 2,010) of reflective interventions in higher education against controls reported a positive medium pooled effect on learning outcomes (g = 0.56), varying with intervention duration, whether peer interaction was included and the reflective activity used; the claim page does not report the direction of those moderators, the outcome types pooled or the horizon. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). The claim's title says the evidence is "mixed", and its own Discussion says this synthesis does not show that; read the title as unconfirmed. It does not predict the effect of any one format, or effects outside higher education.
    • Structured reflection [+M]: a meta-analysis of 48 school-based writing-to-learn interventions found a small overall effect on academic achievement (d = 0.22) and that assignments with metacognitive-reflection prompts were associated with larger effects (b = 0.48), the only content-type moderator to reach significance; a quasi-experiment with 208 first-year engineering students found specific prompts outperformed generic ones on exams, project scores and two problem sets, with the amount of reflection held equal and no effect size reported. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). The meta-analytic result is a moderator association, not a randomized contrast of prompts, and neither study reports a delayed horizon.
    • Guided vs free reflection [~W]: in four sections of one graduate international service-learning course, coded journal and blog paragraphs were more often analytic or integrative under guided prompts, and more than half of free reflections were descriptive only. The same study (public vs private reflection [~W]) found private journal paragraphs more often analytic or integrative (64%) than public blog paragraphs (41%). Not yet checked against its sources. The outcome is the quality of the reflection text, not learning or later performance; it limits what the other claims can be read as showing.
    • Formative assessment with reflection [~W]: in a pretest–posttest nonequivalent control group design with 33 prospective pre-school teachers per group, explicit-reflective instruction with embedded formative assessment produced higher post-test scores on views of the nature of science and of scientific knowledge than explicit-reflective instruction alone; no effect size is printed. Checked by the judge: all 2 entries pass (full text). It qualifies the model: reflection alone was the weaker configuration, and the outcome is a view, not a performance.
    • Self-assessment accuracy [~M]: in a randomized 2×2 experiment with 80 Dutch pre-university students solving genetics problems, studying modelling examples of self-assessment made self-ratings more accurate (ηp² = .10); a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies found self–teacher agreement inconsistent and closer in advanced courses. Not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract). It limits the model: a reflection's evaluation of one's own work cannot be assumed accurate, especially for novices. It does not show that more accurate self-assessment improved later learning.

    Retain learners, setting, prompt type, whether feedback accompanied reflection, outcome type and horizon when transporting these findings. A pooled average over unlike reflective activities is not an estimate for any one of them; a moderator association is not a tested contrast; and a more analytic reflection is not a better next performance.

    Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

    Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model. They are evidence about the relationship, but none has yet been re-read against the model above; each says how far its own sources have been checked.

    • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions. [+M] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [~M] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Elaborated discussion of narrated emotions supports teacher professional learning [+W] — for teacher professional learning specifically, reflection that surfaces and probes the emotional dimension of a teaching episode (see Narrated Emotional Storytelling), not just the behavioral or cognitive one, was associated with groups revising their understanding
    • Course-based emphasis on reframing and stakeholder inquiry shifts pre-service teachers toward informed-designer stances [+M] — reflective practice that explicitly targets how a problem of practice is framed (see Designerly Stances), not just what happened, moved pre-service teachers toward more systemic and stakeholder-centered responses
    • Guided Reflection Produces Longer Reflections [+M]
    • Teacher educators can coach student teachers in reflective practice using personal histories, dialogue journals, and group discussions [+W] — attached 2026-10-07 from Ferraro (2000), which proposed "Teacher-coaches should create an environment of trust and build a reflection context unique to each learning situation".

    Objective and learner-valued goal¶

    Ask what the learner wants from reflecting, separately from the designer's objective. A designer may want evidence-based analysis that changes practice; a learner may want to pass the portfolio requirement, get reassurance, or fix one thing that went badly. Agreement, divergence and uncertainty are observations. A reflection written for a grader or a public audience may be shaped by that audience, so do not read a positive, generic entry as an absence of thought before asking.

    For example, a student teacher on placement may value getting a noisy class to settle, while the course's objective is reflection on what pupils learned from the lesson. A reflection log scored for length or vocabulary establishes neither. Agree which counts (the settling routine tried next week, the analysis of pupils' work, both), record each separately, and let the student's own question be one of the prompts.

    What would revise this model?¶

    The expectation should weaken if comparable learners given specific, criterion-referenced reflection prompts, and a next attempt in which to act, show no gain over a defensible comparison (generic prompts, or the same time spent on practice with feedback) on an aligned performance outcome. It should be revised if structured prompts improve the analytic quality of reflections but never the next performance; then the model describes better writing about practice, not better practice. If reflection helps only when feedback is also present, the active ingredient is the feedback and reflection is its delivery, and the page should say so. If time spent reflecting reduces practice time and outcomes fall, the opportunity cost belongs in the default design.

    Better reflections, higher course exams, changed practice, and delayed or transferred performance are separate claims. The present evidence does not establish an optimal prompt set, frequency, timing or audience, or benefit for children, for factual goals, or outside higher and professional education.
