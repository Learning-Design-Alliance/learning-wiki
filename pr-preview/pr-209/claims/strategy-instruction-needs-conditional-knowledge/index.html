Strategy Instruction Needs Conditional Knowledge¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 causal r?, 1 theoretical r? · q1–q2

Teaching learners when and why to use a strategy — not just how — is required for strategy use to transfer beyond the training context. Conditional knowledge is what allows learners to select and adapt strategies independently rather than executing a memorized procedure on near tasks only.

Evidence¶

Duffy et al. 1987¶

Duffy, G. G., Roehler, L. R., Sivan, E., Rackliffe, G., Book, C., Meloth, M. S., Vavrus, L. G., Wesselman, R., Putnam, J., & Bassiri, D. (1987). Effects of explaining the reasoning associated with using reading strategies. Reading Research Quarterly, 22(3), 347. doi:10.2307/747973

q2 · quasi-experiment (teachers assigned to trained vs. control condition; abstract does not specify randomization) · i? · no effect size reported · n=unreported (abstract does not give sample size) · causal · r?

Elementary reading teachers were trained to make explicit decisions about when and how to explain the mental reasoning behind using reading skills as strategies, and their students were compared with students of untrained control teachers. Low-reading-ability students of the trained teachers became more aware of lesson content and of the need to be strategic while reading, and scored better on several measures of reading achievement than students of control teachers. Only the publisher's/ERIC abstract was available (see provenance); the finding is reported as a comparison of outcomes, with no effect size or sample size given in that text.

Paris et al. 1983¶

Paris, S. G., Lipson, M. Y., & Wixson, K. K. (1983). Becoming a strategic reader. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 8(3), 293-316. doi:10.1016/0361-476X(83)90018-8

q1 · theoretical argument (classified by ERIC as an Opinion Paper, not an empirical study) · i? · not applicable — conceptual paper, no effect size · n=N/A · theoretical · r?

This is the source paper that introduces conditional knowledge as one of three components of strategic reading, alongside declarative knowledge (what a strategy is) and procedural knowledge (how to execute it), arguing that knowing when and why to apply a strategy is what lets a reader use it flexibly and adapt it across tasks rather than only on trained materials. It is a theoretical/synthesis paper — no full text or abstract text beyond ERIC's own descriptive summary could be retrieved (see provenance) — so it is cited here as the conceptual source of the conditional-knowledge construct rather than as an empirical test of it.

Discussion¶

Declarative vs. conditional knowledge. Strategy instruction that stops at procedural steps ("first do X, then do Y") produces execution of the strategy on near tasks but poor maintenance and transfer. Conditional knowledge — knowing under which task conditions, for which goals, and with what expected payoff a strategy applies — is what allows learners to select and adapt strategies independently. This is a core component of self-regulated learning, where strategy use is coupled to monitoring and conditional decision-making.

Design implication. Effective strategy programs pair modeling of the strategy with explicit discussion of its conditions of applicability, and prompt learners to evaluate strategy–task fit during practice. Without this pairing, learners may acquire the procedure yet fail to deploy it when the task changes — a pattern consistent with the broader finding that activation of relevant knowledge and explicit advance organizers support appropriate strategy selection [+M]. Programs that embed conditional knowledge in modeling — for example, reciprocal teaching's explicit linkage of summarizing, questioning, clarifying, and predicting to the demands of the text segment at hand — illustrate the pairing of procedure with conditions of use [+M].

Constraints on the claim. Explicit conditional instruction is not uniformly beneficial: for novices, extended discussion of when-and-why can add extraneous load before the procedure itself is fluent, diluting limited working-memory resources in line with cognitive load theory [-M]. Conversely, for advanced learners, conditional knowledge they have already induced from experience can become redundant, and re-teaching it yields little gain [~M] — an instance of the expertise reversal effect. The claim therefore applies most cleanly to learners who have at least procedural fluency in the strategy but have not yet induced its conditions of applicability.

Open questions. The evidence recorded above is one quasi-experiment and the theoretical paper that introduced the distinction, both read as abstracts. Open issues include how much conditional knowledge can be inferred from practice versus needing explicit instruction, whether conditional knowledge benefits are moderated by learner expertise, and how durable conditional knowledge is relative to procedural knowledge of the strategy itself.
