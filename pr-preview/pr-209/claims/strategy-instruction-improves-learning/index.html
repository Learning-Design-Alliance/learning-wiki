Strategy Instruction Improves Learning¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis r? · q3–q4 · i1–i2

Explicitly teaching learners cognitive and metacognitive strategies — how to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own thinking — improves learning outcomes across domains. The claim covers both domain strategies (e.g., reading comprehension strategies, problem-solving heuristics) and self-regulation strategies (goal-setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation).

Evidence¶

Dignath & Büttner 2008¶

Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. A meta-analysis on intervention studies at primary and secondary school level. Metacognition and Learning, 3(3), 231–264. doi:10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x

q4 · meta-analysis of intervention studies · i2 · medium-to-large effect, average ES = 0.69 · n=84 studies (357 effect sizes) · quant-synthesis · r?

Two separate meta-analyses pooled self-regulated learning training studies: 49 with primary school students and 35 with secondary school students, 357 effect sizes in all. Outcomes were academic performance, strategy use and motivation, and the 0.69 average covers all three together. The abstract does not give a separate figure for achievement. Meta-regression found larger effects when researchers, not regular teachers, delivered the training, and in mathematics compared with reading/writing or other subjects. The theory behind the programme and the type of strategy taught had different effects at the two school levels. Read as abstract only.

Dent & Koenka 2016¶

Dent, A. L., & Koenka, A. C. (2016). The relation between self-regulated learning and academic achievement across childhood and adolescence: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 28(3), 425–474. doi:10.1007/s10648-015-9320-8

q3 · meta-analysis of correlational studies · i1 · small correlation, r = 0.20 (metacognitive processes), r = 0.11 (cognitive strategies) · n=? · quant-synthesis · r?

Two meta-analyses of elementary and secondary school students related achievement to (1) metacognitive processes of self-regulated learning and (2) use of cognitive strategies. Both overall correlations were small: r = 0.20 for metacognitive processes and r = 0.11 for cognitive strategies. The correlations differed significantly by the specific process or strategy, academic subject, grade level, type of SRL measure and type of achievement measure. This is the correlational arm of the claim. It supports a real but modest association, not a causal effect. The number of studies was not in the abstract. Read as abstract only.

Discussion¶

Scope. "Strategy instruction" is a family of practices, not a single technique. It includes domain-specific strategies (e.g., summarization, self-questioning, graphic organizers for reading comprehension; worked-example study and heuristic prompts for problem solving) and general self-regulation strategies (goal-setting, planning, self-monitoring, self-evaluation) — see Self-regulated learning improves academic achievement.. Effects are strongest when strategies are taught explicitly, modeled, and practiced with feedback rather than merely described [~M].

Moderators and boundary conditions. Benefits depend on sustained, embedded practice: one-off strategy talks produce little transfer, while extended programs with modeling, guided practice, and fading show durable gains [~M]. Strategy instruction also interacts with learner characteristics — novices benefit most from highly structured, step-by-step strategy training, while more advanced learners may find heavy scaffolding redundant (an expertise-reversal pattern consistent with cognitive load theory) [~M]. Transfer is stronger when learners are prompted to apply strategies in varied tasks and when instruction includes conditional knowledge (when and why to use a strategy), not just procedural steps [~M].

Constraints. Strategy instruction fails or backfires under identifiable conditions. When strategies are taught as procedures without conditional knowledge, learners apply them indiscriminately or abandon them once support is withdrawn [~M]. For learners who already use effective strategies spontaneously, additional strategy training adds load without benefit and can depress performance — the same expertise-reversal pattern seen with worked examples [~M]. Short, decontextualized interventions (a single lesson on "study skills") show negligible transfer to real coursework [-M]. And when strategy training consumes time that would go to domain content without being integrated into that content, gains on strategy measures do not translate into domain achievement [-W].

Open questions. The evidence recorded above concerns self-regulated learning strategy training in primary and secondary school; other domains and learner ages are not covered here.
