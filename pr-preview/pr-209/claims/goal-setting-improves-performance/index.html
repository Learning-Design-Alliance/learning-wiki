Goal setting improves performance¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis r? · q3–q4 · i1–i2

Setting a goal raises performance or behavior change on average, by a medium effect for specific, difficult goals on work and laboratory tasks (smaller on complex tasks) and a small one across randomized behavior-change trials, mostly in health. Neither recorded meta-analysis studies academic learning outcomes, so applying the claim to learning is an inference.

Evidence¶

Wood et al. 1987¶

Wood, R. E., Mento, A. J., & Locke, E. A. (1987). Task complexity as a moderator of goal effects: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 72(3), 416–425. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.72.3.416

q3 · meta-analysis (1987, not pre-registered) · i2 · medium effect, d=.42 (complex tasks) to d=.76 (simple tasks) · n=125 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of goal-setting studies published from 1966 to 1985 on how task complexity changes the effect of goals. The authors ran three sets of analyses: hard versus easy goals, specific difficult goals versus "do your best" or no goal, and all studies combined. The authors report that goal effects were generally strongest on easy tasks such as reaction time and brainstorming (d = .76) and weakest on more complex tasks such as business game simulations, scientific and engineering work, and faculty research productivity (d = .42); the abstract does not give results for each set separately. Read as abstract only. It supports the claim for specific, challenging goals, and shows the effect is weaker on more complex tasks. The tasks studied are work and laboratory tasks, not learning tasks, so how far this applies to complex learning is an inference.

Epton et al. 2017¶

Epton, T., Currie, S., & Armitage, C. J. (2017). Unique effects of setting goals on behavior change: Systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 85(12), 1182–1198. doi:10.1037/ccp0000260

q4 · systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials · i1 · small effect, d=.34 (95% CI .28–.41) · n=141 papers, 384 effect sizes, 16,523 participants · quant-synthesis · r?

A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials that isolated goal setting from other parts of a behavior change intervention. Goal setting had a small positive effect across behaviors (d = .34). It worked best when the goal was difficult, set publicly, or a group goal. There was weaker evidence that it worked better with external monitoring by others and with face-to-face delivery. Read as abstract only. The outcomes are behaviors, mainly in health settings, rather than learning or academic performance, so they bear on the claim only by analogy.

Discussion¶

The claim is underspecified as stated. "Goal setting" and "performance" each hide the moderators that determine whether the effect holds. The goal-setting literature conventionally distinguishes specific, challenging goals from vague intentions ("do your best"), and distinguishes learning goals (focused on strategy acquisition) from performance goals (focused on outcome attainment) — with the latter sometimes counterproductive on complex or novel tasks where early errors are informative. The two meta-analyses recorded here, both read as abstracts, support the specific-and-difficult version on work and laboratory tasks (Wood et al. 1987, weaker on complex tasks) and a small general effect on health and everyday behaviors (Epton et al. 2017); neither tests learning versus performance goals, or academic learners.

Boundary conditions. The recorded entries show task complexity (Wood et al. 1987) and goal difficulty, public and group goals (Epton et al. 2017) as moderators. Other plausible moderators, not tested by them, include: goal specificity and difficulty; whether goals are assigned or self-set (see Autonomy Supports Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination Theory); whether learners receive feedback on progress toward the goal (see Assessment for Learning Improves Achievement); task complexity, which interacts with goal type in ways consistent with Cognitive Load Theory; and goal proximity, where proximal subgoals may sustain effort better than distant targets (see Achievable Micro-Goals). Goal setting is also a core phase of Self-Regulated Learning, so effects may be mediated by learners' monitoring and strategy adjustment rather than by the goal itself.

Open questions. Does the effect hold for novices as well as experienced learners? Does it transfer beyond the specific task the goal targets? Are effects on motivation durable or limited to short, monitored tasks? The two recorded meta-analyses do not answer them: neither studies academic learners or learning outcomes.
