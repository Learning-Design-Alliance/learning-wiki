Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 2 quant-synthesis r? · q4 · i2–i3

Feedback directed at the task (how well the work meets the goal) and at the process (the strategies used to do the work) supports learning more than feedback directed at the person (praise or criticism of the self).

Evidence¶

Wisniewski et al. 2020¶

Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 3087. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087

q4 · meta-analysis (random effects, not pre-registered) · i3 · very large effect for high-information feedback, d=0.99 · n=435 studies · quant-synthesis · r?

A random-effects meta-analysis of 435 studies (994 effect sizes, more than 61,000 learners) of feedback on student learning, set up to test the Hattie and Timperley (2007) model. The overall effect was medium (d = 0.48 after removing outliers) with very high heterogeneity (I² about 83%). Studies were coded by the type of information feedback carried: reinforcement or punishment (d = 0.24), corrective feedback (d = 0.46), and high-information feedback that addresses the task, the process and sometimes self-regulation (d = 0.99). Motivational outcomes showed smaller effects (d = 0.33) than cognitive ones (d = 0.51). The authors did not code self-level praise as its own category, so the self-level contrast here rests on the reinforcement/punishment group and on their summary of Hattie and Timperley.

Kluger & DeNisi 1996¶

Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254–284. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254

q4 · meta-analysis with moderator analyses · i2 · medium average effect, d=.41 · n=607 effect sizes · quant-synthesis · r?

A meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations) of feedback interventions on performance across settings, not limited to classrooms. Feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), but more than a third of interventions made performance worse, which sampling error and feedback sign could not explain. The authors' feedback intervention theory holds that feedback shifts attention among task-learning, task-motivation and self-related (meta-task) processes, and their moderator analyses found that effectiveness declined as feedback moved attention toward the self. Their levels are not the same as Hattie and Timperley's four, and the abstract reports no effect size for the level moderator itself.

Discussion¶

The task/process distinction implies a hierarchy of feedback value: process-level feedback is often the most powerful because it gives learners transferable strategy information, while task-level feedback tells them whether the current work meets the criteria and where it falls short. Feedback about the person — praise such as "good job" or trait-level judgments — carries little instructional information and can even undermine performance by directing attention to the self rather than the work.

Boundary conditions follow from this structure. Task-level feedback is most useful early, when learners need to know whether their product is correct; process-level feedback becomes more valuable as learners refine how they work. Praise may function as a motivator for reluctant learners, but it does not improve the quality of subsequent work on its own. Feedback is also only effective when learners act on it — feedback that arrives after revision is no longer possible, or that the learner cannot interpret, produces little learning regardless of level. This is why feedback must be designed as actionable guidance, not evaluation — see Action-oriented feedback.

The self level interacts with motivation as well as cognition. Person-directed praise can reinforce entity beliefs about ability, making learners more fragile in the face of subsequent failure, whereas process-directed feedback keeps attention on controllable strategy and effort. This connects to the Expertise reversal effect: for highly skilled learners, even process-level feedback can become redundant or interfering, and the optimal level shifts toward self-regulation (helping learners monitor their own work) rather than direct strategy instruction.

Feedback at the task and process levels is also a core mechanism of Assessment for learning: formative assessment only improves achievement when the information it generates is fed back in a form learners can use to close the gap between current and desired performance. And because learners must interpret feedback within working-memory limits, overly dense multi-level feedback can overload novices — see Cognitive load theory.

Open questions: how the four levels interact in real classrooms (e.g., whether process feedback presupposes accurate task feedback), and how the effectiveness of each level varies by learner expertise and subject domain. Two meta-analyses are recorded (Wisniewski et al. 2020 in education; Kluger & DeNisi 1996 across settings), and both support the claim only indirectly: one coded the information content of feedback rather than the four levels, and the other used its own attention levels, with no effect size for the level moderator in the abstract. No recorded study compares task-level with process-level feedback directly, so the ordering between those two in the paragraphs above is untested here.
