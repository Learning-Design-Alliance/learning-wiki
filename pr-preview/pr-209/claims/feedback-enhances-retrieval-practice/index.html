Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 2 studies · 1 causal r?, 1 quant-synthesis r2 · q3–q4 · i2 medium

Retrieval practice strengthens long-term retention, and providing feedback after retrieval attempts further improves learning by correcting errors and confirming correct responses. This page treats the interaction — whether feedback adds incremental value beyond retrieval alone — as a distinct claim requiring its own evidence.

Evidence¶

Yang et al. 2021¶

Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021). Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review. Psychological Bulletin, 147(4), 399–435. doi:10.1037/bul0000309

q4 · systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis · i2 · medium effect, g=0.537 with corrective feedback vs g=0.374 without · n=222 studies · quant-synthesis · r2

This review pooled classroom studies that compared quizzing with restudying and other control activities. The published abstract gives 222 independent studies and 48,478 students; the accepted manuscript gives 223 studies, 574 effects and 48,582 students. The overall testing effect was g = 0.499. Whether corrective feedback was given significantly moderated that effect (Q(2) = 11.197, p = .004). Quizzes followed by feedback (335 effects) produced g = 0.537 [0.469, 0.604], and quizzes without feedback (142 effects) produced g = 0.374 [0.278, 0.471]. Two cautions: the comparison is made across studies rather than within them, so feedback may be confounded with other study features, and the authors note that Adesope et al. (2017) found no moderation (g = 0.63 with feedback, g = 0.60 without), whereas Rowland (2014) reported g = 0.73 with feedback and g = 0.39 without.

Pashler et al. 2005¶

Pashler, H., Cepeda, N. J., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2005). When does feedback facilitate learning of words? Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 31(1), 3–8. doi:10.1037/0278-7393.31.1.3

q3 · peer-reviewed experiment, between-subjects · i? · no standardized effect size in the abstract; reported as a 494% increase in final retention · n=258 · causal · r?

258 participants studied Luganda–English word pairs twice. They were then tested on each item several times, and the presence and type of feedback varied between participants. A final test followed one week later. Supplying the correct answer after an incorrect response improved performance during learning and raised final retention by 494%. Feedback after correct responses made little difference, either immediately or after a week, even when the learner was not confident in the response. This supports the page's boundary condition that feedback matters most after failed retrievals. (Coded from the abstract only; the full text could not be retrieved.)

Discussion¶

Why feedback should help. Retrieval attempts can produce errors or partial answers; without corrective information, learners may re-encode mistaken responses. Feedback after a retrieval attempt allows learners to confirm correct retrievals and repair incorrect ones, and the act of first attempting retrieval is thought to make subsequent feedback more deeply processed than feedback delivered without an attempt — the same "generation then correction" logic that underlies Pretesting enhances learning from subsequent instruction.

Boundary conditions to investigate. The benefit of feedback likely depends on retrieval success: for items learners retrieve correctly and confidently, feedback may add little, while for failed or low-confidence retrievals it should matter most. Timing (immediate vs. delayed), format (correct-answer-only vs. elaborated explanation), and learner expertise are plausible moderators that the evidence base must establish before this claim can be rated. The general feedback literature suggests effects are heterogeneous across these conditions, so this claim should not be treated as uniformly positive until moderators are pinned down.

Relation to the base effect. This claim augments, and should be evaluated against, Retrieval practice improves long-term retention. — the question is whether feedback adds incremental value beyond retrieval alone, not whether retrieval works. It also sits within the broader claim that Feedback improves learning outcomes., which this page specializes to retrieval contexts.

Design implications while evidence is pending. Practitioners combining retrieval with feedback should prioritize feedback on failed or low-confidence retrievals, where the corrective value is greatest, and avoid interrupting successful retrievals with redundant confirmation that adds time without benefit. Feedback format should follow the same principles as elsewhere: answer feedback is efficient for factual items, while elaborated explanation is more appropriate where learners must understand why an answer is correct — consistent with the guidance in Feedback improves learning outcomes.. Because retrieval practice itself is well supported, adding feedback should not displace retrieval opportunities; the combination is best treated as retrieval practice with a corrective layer, not as a replacement for it.

Open questions. Whether feedback benefits persist on delayed tests, whether elaborate feedback outperforms simple answer feedback after retrieval, and whether feedback changes the metacognitive monitoring that retrieval practice supports are all unresolved pending addition of primary studies. Until then, designers combining retrieval with feedback should treat the combination as promising but unverified as an interaction.
