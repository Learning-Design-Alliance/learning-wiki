Rereading is less effective than retrieval practice¶

Claim · All claims Evidence · 3 studies · q3 experiment and systematic review · i0–i2 · rereading near zero

Actively recalling material from memory produces stronger, more durable learning than restudying the same material by rereading it. This is the core of the testing-effect literature: the act of retrieval itself, not the re-exposure to content, drives durable retention.

Evidence¶

Callender & McDaniel 2009¶

Callender, A. A., & McDaniel, M. A. (2009). The limited benefits of rereading educational texts. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 34(1), 30–41. doi:10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.001

q3 · i0 · causal · r?

A series of experiments with authentic educational texts rather than word lists, testing rereading against a single reading. Benefits were absent or negligible on the measures that matter for instruction. The authors' framing is the useful one for design: rereading is not harmful, it is close to inert for the time it costs.

Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (review)¶

Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). The power of testing memory: Basic research and implications for educational practice. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 1(3), 181–210. doi:10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00012.x

A narrative review of the testing-effect literature and its educational implications. Relevant here for the mechanism rather than an effect size: rereading increases perceptual fluency and immediate accessibility, both of which learners read as evidence of learning.

Dunlosky et al. 2013¶

Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58. doi:10.1177/1529100612453266

A systematic review evaluating ten learning techniques against generalisability, learner characteristics, materials and criterion tasks. Rereading and highlighting received the lowest utility ratings; practice testing and distributed practice the highest. The review is a synthesis rather than a single experiment, so its weight here is breadth of coverage.

Discussion¶

Why rereading underperforms. Rereading creates a sense of fluency and familiarity that learners misinterpret as mastery, while retrieval practice forces effortful reconstruction of memory traces that strengthens them. The contrast is a central finding of the testing-effect literature, though the specific studies supporting this page still need to be added (see Evidence).

Boundary conditions. The advantage of retrieval over rereading depends on conditions: retrieval works best when feedback follows the attempt, when retrieval is spaced rather than massed, and when learners actually succeed at recalling rather than giving up. Repeated failed retrieval without feedback can entrench errors, and very difficult retrieval can impose extraneous demands for novices — see Cognitive Load Theory. Rereading is not useless — it can be appropriate for building initial familiarity with complex or unfamiliar material before retrieval is attempted — but as a standalone study strategy it is consistently outperformed.

Fluency as a misleading cue. Learners overwhelmingly prefer rereading and highlighting because they feel easier, yet this fluency is a poor guide to actual retention. The subjective ease of restudy and the objective durability of retrieval point in opposite directions, which is why strategy training matters: learners will not spontaneously switch to retrieval on their own, and judgments of learning based on fluency systematically miscalibrate study choices.

Design implications. Designers should replace restudy opportunities with low-stakes quizzing, free recall prompts, or flashcard-style practice — see Cognitive load reduction improves learning for the related load considerations. Retrieval demands should be calibrated so learners can succeed with effort, feedback should follow each attempt, and retrieval events should be distributed across time rather than massed into a single session. Where rereading is retained — for example, as a first pass over dense or unfamiliar text — it should be positioned as preparation for retrieval, not a substitute for it.
