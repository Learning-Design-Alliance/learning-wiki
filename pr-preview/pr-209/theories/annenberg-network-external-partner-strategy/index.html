The Chicago Annenberg Challenge's network-plus-external-partner strategy for school improvement¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 6 claims (1 for, 2 mixed, 3 against) · 2 studies (1 associational, 1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The Chicago Annenberg Challenge organized school improvement around networks of schools, each required to have an external partner to help promote local improvement. The premise was that "schools that worked together and with an external partner would find greater support for local improvement and be more successful than if they worked alone." Partners served as fiduciary agents, brought resources, catalyzed improvement, and developed local leadership. The Challenge deliberately embraced pluralism rather than privileging one reform strategy.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Networks were required to have an external partner to help promote local school improvement.

Constraints¶

  • The Challenge did not articulate specific goals for individual school improvement, giving partners broad latitude but little direction.
  • The overall strategy was, per the Research Project's other reports, not particularly successful across the large number of schools.

Target Learners¶

  • Students in Chicago public elementary and high schools

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • Improved classroom practice
  • Strengthened community relationships
  • School improvement and local leadership development

Claims¶

  • Annenberg Partners Experienced But Not Particularly Successful [+M]
  • Annenberg external partners were organizationally diverse, and a majority focused their work on curriculum and instruction [~W]
  • Most Annenberg partners found the Challenge's twice-yearly written reporting requirements burdensome and detrimental to their work with schools [-W]
  • Many Annenberg schools had weak human and social resources, including low trust among teachers, limiting their capacity for school development [~W]
  • High-stakes testing pushed some Chicago schools toward test preparation and away from partners' long-term instructional improvement efforts [-W]
  • Initial improvements in Annenberg schools' organizational capacity by 1999 largely disappeared by 2001, leaving little net change [-W]

Examples¶

  • Successful external partners act as staff advocates and buffers who reduce teacher vulnerability
