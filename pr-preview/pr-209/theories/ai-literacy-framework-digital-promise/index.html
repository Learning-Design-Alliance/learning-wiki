AI literacy as critical understanding, use, and evaluation of AI systems¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Digital Promise's AI Literacy Framework defines a learner and educator capability: AI literacy includes the knowledge and skills that enable humans to critically understand, use, and evaluate AI systems and tools to safely and ethically participate in an increasingly digital world. The presentation presents this framework as the basis for rethinking teaching and learning for the AI age.

Claims¶

  • Generative AI can help people finish tasks more quickly but does not accelerate learning itself [~W]
  • Human guidance remains essential for ethical grounding when integrating AI with situated learning, including resisting complacency toward generative AI outputs [+W]
  • Generative AI systems carry strong potential for introduced and inherited bias [+W]
  • People may absorb bias from AI and carry it beyond their interactions with the algorithm [+W]
  • Six named risks from AI systems span overestimation, data collection, synthetic outputs, invisibility, bias replication, and human and environmental costs [+W]

Examples¶

  • Six AI Literacy Practices for understanding and evaluating AI
  • Ask ethics, bias, and equity questions before adopting an AI system
  • Non-linear AI literacy learning journey cycling through Understand, Evaluate, and Use
  • Adopt a systems approach to AI integration rather than spotlights or gaps
  • Design AI literacy tools with transparency in development and establish district policies for vetting AI tools
  • Ground AI integration in higher education on core principles of human agency, academic freedom, transparency, ethics, inclusivity, and critical thinking
