Five cornerstones of an effective classroom technology coaching program¶

Theory · All theories Evidence · 10 claims (10 for) · 3 studies (2 associational, 1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The report proposes that five aspects of the DLP account for its success: partnership, active learning, personalization, sustained support, and content flexibility. Partnership means a voluntary, non-evaluative, school-based collaboration among administrators, coaches, and teachers in which "teachers and coaches co-constructed lessons." Active learning structures coaching around learning by doing on each teacher's own classroom challenge; personalization fits support to teacher, school, and district context; sustained support spans weeks and months; and content flexibility lets coaches support subjects outside their own expertise by treating the teacher as the content expert.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Voluntary, non-evaluative coaching with confidentiality so teachers can experiment without fear of failure
  • Sustained support over weeks and months rather than one-off professional development events

Constraints¶

  • The report states coaching is most effective when aligned with district and school goals organically and without superseding teachers' ability to select their own challenges

Target Learners¶

  • K-12 teachers in participating schools
  • technology coaches
  • school principals

Target Learning Objectives¶

  • impactful use of technology to support teaching and student 21st-century skills

Claims¶

  • Dlp Coaching Improves Teaching Challenges [+M]
  • Dlp Versus Other Coaching Programs Comparison [+M]
  • Coaches and principals in the DLP grew in confidence and skills over the year [+W]
  • DLP-coached teachers reported greater increases in frequency of technology use than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • More DLP teachers reported strong ability to select and use technology for pedagogy and for their content area than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • DLP teachers reported higher rates of impactful student technology use and greater perceived positive impact on student engagement and learning than non-DLP teachers [+W]
  • DLP teachers reported more confidence than non-DLP teachers in using technology to develop students' six 21st-century skill categories [+W]
  • Effective coaches require content expertise, classroom experience, technology proficiency, flexible scheduling, partnership orientation, and interpersonal skills [+W]
  • Teachers coached for more than one cycle reported more powerful technology use and greater confidence, though a breadth-versus-depth tradeoff remains open [+W]
  • Four relational components—positive feedback, rapport, mutual respect, and reciprocal trust—form the relational framework of coaching [+W]
  • Teachers and coaches in the qualitative study perceived that coaching increased teacher knowledge and practice, with sustainable practices and positive student learning outcomes [+W]
