Professional Development¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 25 claims (12 for, 9 mixed, 4 against) · 23 studies (5 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 4 qualitative, 2 design, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 6 of 23 report an effect size · 20 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for changing what practising educators do in their own classrooms: choose one observable classroom practice → find out how each teacher currently enacts it and what they know of its content → show the practice and the reasoning behind it → rehearse it with colleagues → teachers enact it with their own students while a coach or peer observes (live or on video) → feedback on that enactment, tied to the practice and to what students did → repeat the enact-and-feedback cycle with support withdrawn as the practice holds → check later that it is still in use. The learners are teachers (adult, adult-workplace): the target is a complex-skill enacted in a classroom, with the knowledge and beliefs that go with it. The intended change is in observed teaching practice (skill-performance), and through it in students' behavior or achievement, at a horizon of a term or more (term-plus). Teacher knowledge, satisfaction ratings and stated intentions are not the target, though they are worth recording.

It applies the cognitive apprenticeship pattern to adult learners whose task is teaching, and it sits inside the scaffolding and fading principle, which owns the general relationship between contingent support, its withdrawal and independent performance; this page does not restate either model. Where the PD is carried mainly by a standing group of colleagues (a professional learning community, lesson study), the Communities of Practice principle holds the model for sustained participation in a shared practice, and the Fostering Communities of Learning pattern is the nearest community pattern. This page owns the narrower configuration: a cycle of modeling, enactment with one's own students, and feedback on that enactment, run for teachers over a term or more.

No claim in this wiki tests this pattern's distinguishing relationship: that sustained, practice-embedded PD with coached enactment changes practice or student outcomes more than a one-off workshop. The two syntheses the earlier page leaned on, Darling-Hammond, Hyler & Gardner (2017) and Kraft, Blazar & Hogan (2018), have no claim page here, so their findings are not used as evidence below (they stay in Key Sources). What the wiki holds is: two reviews reported second-hand in one 1994 monograph (staff-development experiments mostly changed instruction and raised achievement against control teachers; the techniques that trained instructional skills); one review of 16 supervisory-coaching studies; one small quasi-experiment in which coaching changed four mathematics teachers' small-group moves; case studies of a three-year science program and of two teachers adapting a practice; and learner-level claims on feedback, contingent support and fading, carried to teachers by extrapolation. The sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, with each step's evidence status stated; the doses are labelled proposals, and the response-dependent policy is an untested design proposal.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Before any session, observe or video one lesson per teacher (or collect a lesson plan and a sample of student work if observation is impossible) and record how the target practice is currently enacted, using the same look-for list that will be used later. Record content familiarity in the subject the practice serves, role (new teacher, experienced teacher, faculty member, teaching assistant, volunteer), and whether participation is voluntary. Do not infer readiness from years of experience; keep unknowns as unknowns.
The practice Name one practice specific enough to observe in a lesson (for example "pose a question, give think time, take three responses before evaluating", or "run the curriculum's launch–explore–discuss lesson structure"), not a broad aim ("student-centered teaching"). Whether it is a teacher behavior (praise, questioning) or the faithful running of a specific academic task or program. Whether it needs content knowledge teachers may lack.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, mixed in-room and remote), span (single-session, days-weeks, term-plus), number of teachers, coach or facilitator hours available per teacher per month, whether release time or cover exists, whether lessons can be filmed, whether teams from one school or department can take part together, and whether the PD is linked to performance evaluation.
Learner-valued goal Ask each teacher what they want from it (a calmer class, a curriculum that works, students who talk more, getting through an inspection, career progression). Record where it differs from the designer's objective.
Designer objective Which change is the target: enactment of the named practice, faithful running of a specific program, teachers' own design of lessons using a framework, a team's shared routine, or a student outcome. Name the one the evaluation will weigh.
Outcome An observation instrument for the practice (a look-for checklist or coded video), used at baseline, during, at the end, and again at least six weeks after support ends; a student measure aligned with the practice, if student change is claimed; and teacher reports kept apart from observed practice.

A request to "design PD on formative assessment" or "run a coaching program" does not specify these. Ask what practice, observed how, over what span and with how much coach time, before choosing a format.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default sequence is the earlier page's seven steps, with doses added as labelled proposals. Steps 1, 3 and 7 are untested here; steps 2, 4, 5 and 6 have neighbouring claims, each carried by extrapolation.

  1. Diagnose starting points (weeks 0–2). Observe or film one lesson per teacher with the look-for list; look at a sample of student work; ask each teacher what they want from the PD. Use the result to set each teacher's first target and the coaching intensity. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. Content familiarity, not teaching experience, predicted reform-based practice [~M] (334 science teachers, one district partnership, an association) is a reason to diagnose content familiarity rather than sort teachers by years of service.
  2. Model the practice and its reasoning (launch: proposed one or two days, about 6–12 hours, before or at the start of the term). Show the practice live, on video or by think-aloud, with the reasoning for each decision, and present the rationale for it. Evidence status: the techniques that trained teachers' instructional skills [+M] (a review by Showers, Joyce and Bennett as a 1994 monograph reports it, no effect sizes): presentation of the rationale, demonstration and modeling, practice with feedback, and coaching for transfer to the classroom.
  3. Analyze together (within the launch). Teachers pick apart the modeled practice against student work, agreeing on what the look-for list will count. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
  4. Rehearse with colleagues (within the launch, and 15–20 minutes at the start of each later team meeting). Teachers try the practice with peers acting as students and get feedback before using it with their classes. Evidence status: part of the same reported technique list [+M]; no claim tests rehearsal separately.
  5. Enact with support (proposed coaching cycles of two to three weeks each, four to six cycles across a term). Each cycle: a short planning conversation (20–30 minutes); the teacher teaches with the practice while the coach observes live or the teacher films the lesson; a debrief within two school days (30–45 minutes) that starts from the teacher's own viewing of the video, names one thing to keep and one change, and states what the students did. Feedback is about the enacted practice and students' responses, not about the teacher as a person. Evidence status: coaching aimed at the fidelity of a specific academic task [+M] and coaching to increase teacher praise [+M] (one review of 16 K–12 studies): coached behaviors rose, and studies of task fidelity met more quality indicators and showed more sustained outcomes. Group-level scaffolding training with on-the-job coaching [+M] changed two mathematics teachers' small-group moves against two matched teachers (five lessons each; more students took part in each interaction) but did not improve how well their content support was calibrated. Feedback at the task and process levels [+S] is from two meta-analyzes of learners and of performance generally, not of teachers. Video self-observation [+W] is teachers' report in one focus-group study.
  6. Reflect, iterate and fade (later cycles). Early cycles: the coach models in the teacher's class or co-teaches, and chooses the focus. Middle cycles: the teacher teaches, the coach observes and gives one focus. Late cycles: the teacher chooses the video segment and the focus, a colleague observes instead of the coach, and the coach meets monthly. Move a teacher on to the next practice, or to peer-only support, when the practice is seen at the agreed level in two consecutive observed lessons and the teacher can say why they made the choices they did (a proposal). Evidence status: contingent support [+M] and fading support [+M], both from studies of students in tutoring and example-based learning, carried to coaching by analogy; guidance that helps novices can become redundant as expertise grows [~M], from studies of trainees and students, is the reason to fade rather than hold support constant.
  7. Sustain and check maintenance (after support ends). Fold the practice into a standing team routine: lesson study, peer observation or a professional learning community meeting every two to four weeks for about an hour. Observe again at least six weeks after coaching stops, with the same look-for list. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. Most coaching studies did not measure maintenance, and where it was measured it was not always kept [~M], which is why the later observation is in the design.

Total contact under this default is about 20–30 hours over a term, close to the earlier page's "20+ hours spread across a semester or year"; neither figure rests on a claim page here. The one program with a recorded dose, three years of PD for Leader Teachers [~M], gave nearly 500 hours and reports dramatic change by the third year, in one case study.

The teacher describes the practice well in sessions but it does not appear in the observed lesson Ask the teacher to plan the next lesson's use of it aloud, and ask what got in the way last time (time, class behavior, materials, a scheme of work). If they cannot plan it, the gap is in how to do it: model it in their class or co-teach. If they can plan it but something blocks it, change the constraint (materials, pacing, a smaller first version) rather than repeat the input. Neither branch is tested.
The practice appears, but in a changed form Ask what they changed and why; check whether the change keeps the practice's purpose (for example, students still do the thinking). If the purpose holds, accept the adaptation and record it. If time pressure has turned it into something else (faster, more procedural), negotiate a smaller version that keeps the purpose. Teachers synthesize PD practices with their context [~M] (two case teachers) is the reason not to score adaptation as failure.
The practice appears, but students' responses do not change Look at what students did in the filmed segment: who spoke, what they produced. If few students take part, change who the practice reaches (pair talk before whole-class talk, cold calling). If they take part but the work is weak, the gap may be the teacher's content knowledge or the task, not the routine: work on the task. Untested.
Low uptake: missed meetings, no filming, polite agreement and no change Ask privately what the teacher wants from this and what it costs them; check whether it is tied to their evaluation. If the PD is seen as evaluation, separate coaching from appraisal. If the cost is time, shrink the practice. Buy-in is seen as mediating coaching benefit [~W] (teachers' and coaches' views in one study).
The practice was in place at the end of coaching and has gone six weeks later Ask what replaced it and when it stopped. Restart a short coaching cycle, or put the practice into a team routine with a peer observer. Untested.
A teacher can run the modeled lessons but not design new ones with the framework Ask them to plan a lesson from scratch and compare it with the framework. Add co-planning and lesson-design feedback as a target of its own. Teachers had minimal success designing Constructive and Interactive activities after ICAP PD [-M].

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How specific and observable the target practice is. Coaching studies that targeted fidelity of a specific academic task met more quality indicators and showed more sustained outcomes than those targeting general teacher behaviors, and knowing a framework did not make teachers able to design lessons with it. If unstated, ask: what will an observer see in a lesson when the practice is in place?
  • How much coach or facilitator time each teacher gets, and over what span. Short, fragmented training is reported as hindering change, and the one recorded long program gave hundreds of hours; most real PD is a few hours. If unstated, ask: how many hours of a coach's or facilitator's time per teacher per month, for how many months, and is there release time?
  • Each teacher's content familiarity and current enactment of the practice, not years of experience. If unstated, ask: what does one of their lessons look like now, and how confident are they in the subject the practice serves?

Only a single session is available (an inset day, a one-off workshop) Use it to model and rehearse one narrow practice and to set up a follow-up the school can run without the presenter: one peer observation with a look-for list within two weeks and one team meeting to review the clips. Do not promise changed practice from the session alone. Half of inservice activities lasted four hours or less, mostly talks or brief workshops [~W] (prevalence, not effect); short, fragmented training is reported to hinder a holistic view [-W]; the follow-up is untested

Coaching is run by supervisors or is tied to performance evaluation Separate the coaching record from appraisal, or name plainly what the supervisor will see; expect mixed results and measure both teacher and student change. Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • PD against no PD. Experimental staff-development programs mostly raised student achievement against control teachers [+M]: a review attributed to Gage and Needels, as a 1994 monograph reports it, found that in all but one of nine experiments with reading and mathematics achievement outcomes, staff development changed teachers' instruction and their students achieved more than control teachers' students. No effect sizes are printed, and the programs are not described on the claim page, so it says that PD aimed at student achievement can work, not which configuration did. It is the nearest this wiki has to a test of the pattern's outcome.
  • What the training contains. Effective skill training combines theory presentation, demonstration, practice with feedback and coaching for transfer [+M]: second-hand again, from a review attributed to Showers, Joyce and Bennett (1987). This is the source of steps 2, 4 and 5; it is a list of effective techniques, not a comparison of them.
  • Coaching. One review of 16 supervisory-coaching studies in K–12 classrooms, coded by quality indicators, is the base for three claims: coaching that targeted praise raised it in every such study [+M]; studies coaching the fidelity of a specific academic task met more quality indicators and showed more immediate and sustained outcomes than those coaching teacher behaviors [+M]; and the limiting claim, effects were mixed, with 6 of 16 reporting improved teacher results and 4 improved student behaviors, and only 6 meeting all quality indicators [~M]. In a quasi-experiment with four seventh-grade mathematics teachers, training and on-the-job coaching on a small-group scaffolding tool [+M] changed which scaffolding moves teachers used and roughly doubled the students taking part in each interaction (3.48 against 1.96 on average), but did not change how well-calibrated their content support was (61% against 49%, not significant); no student learning was measured. Read together: coaching reliably moves the specific behavior it targets; whether that reaches student outcomes is mixed and seldom measured.
  • Feedback, contingency and fading, from learners rather than teachers. Feedback at the task and process levels [+S] (two meta-analyzes; one coded information content, not levels; more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance); contingent scaffolding [+M] (mostly one-to-one tutoring of children); fading support [+M] (worked-example studies, near transfer); expertise reversal [~M] (trainees reading circuit diagrams, and a narrative review). Carry them to coaching as reasons for feedback on the enacted practice, support matched to the teacher's current enactment, and its withdrawal, no further.
  • Limits. Most coaching studies did not measure maintenance [~M]; teachers struggled to design Constructive and Interactive activities after ICAP PD [-M], though their students still learned more in Constructive than Active activities; Hunter's ITIP, widely implemented, lacked evidence of improved achievement [-M]; peer diffusion from lead teachers was uneven [~M].

Do not rank coaching against workshops, or one PD model against another, from these claims: none compares them, and the meta-analysis that did (Kraft et al. 2018) is not recorded here as a claim. Do not read teachers' satisfaction or stated intentions as changed practice. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (coached, practice-embedded PD against the same content as a workshop, on observed practice and a student measure at a stated delay) and the local observation that would stand in for it: the same look-for list at baseline, at the end, and six weeks after support ends.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims the earlier page cited, or that bear on teacher PD, which are not core evidence above.

  • Active learning improves exam performance. [+S] — cited by the earlier page "by analogy": two meta-analyzes of undergraduate STEM courses (0.47 SD on exams and concept inventories; only high-intensity active learning narrowed gaps). It concerns students in lectures, not teachers in PD, and is not evidence that active PD formats beat passive ones.
  • After one year of TEFA PD, videotaped lessons showed more discussion and student–student interaction [+W] — one project's observational report of practice change, with no comparison group.
  • Teachers synthesize PD practices with their own context [~M] — two ethnographic cases; used in the observation table above as a reason to judge adaptations by their purpose.
  • Non-participation in intensive PD reflected enjoyment, preparation and conceptions of PD [~W] — one program's recruitment change.
  • The expert-presenter model is the most prevalent [~W] — prevalence data from 1982, reported second-hand; not an effect.
  • Short, fragmented in-service training is reported to hinder a holistic view [-W] — a narrative review's report of preliminary results.
  • Literacy coaching responsibilities fall into two major areas: teacher mentoring and literacy program advocacy [+W]
  • The complexity of coaching responsibilities may fragment the coaching process and reduce its impact [-W]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested program. A district adopts a new middle-school mathematics curriculum. One coach serves 18 teachers across three schools for a year, with about three hours a month per teacher; four teachers at one school join meetings remotely. The table changed the default: the target is one observable practice from the curriculum (running the lesson's closing discussion so that students compare two solutions), not "the new curriculum"; the launch is one day in which teachers do two lessons as students, watch a filmed closing discussion and rehearse it in trios; coaching runs in three-week cycles, with teachers filming a 10-minute closing discussion and the coach debriefing for 20 minutes online within two days; the coach spends extra cycles on the five teachers whose baseline lessons had no closing discussion; remote teachers watch the same clips and rehearse with an in-room partner. A teacher says she wants her quieter students to speak; the designer's objective is faithful use of the closing discussion. They agree to record both how many students speak and whether two solutions are compared, and to observe again in the eighth week after coaching ends. The coaching claims come from other practices, settings and populations; these are local choices to observe, not settled ones.

Record: target practice and its look-for list → teacher's content familiarity, role and stated aim → baseline enactment → modeling and rehearsal given → each cycle's observed enactment, the feedback focus and who chose it → students' observed responses → support withdrawn and when → candidate explanations for any gap (does not know how, is blocked by a constraint, has adapted it, does not value it) and their basis → enactment at the end and at the later check. Preserve the teacher's stated aim and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing observations or assign numerical probabilities of practice change without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Coaching, modeling, demonstration, think-aloud, observation, video-based reflection, feedback, fading, reflection, class discussion, check-in and assessment. Strategies that carry parts of the cycle: instructional coaching, peer coaching, lesson study and professional learning communities. Principles it draws on: Scaffolding and Fading, Communities of Practice, Active Learning and Assessment for Learning.

This pattern is scoped to changing one observable teaching practice through enactment and feedback over a term or more. Building a standing professional community, changing a school's organization or policy, and initial teacher education as a whole need other configurations. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; whether this configuration changes practice and student outcomes more than a workshop of the same content, for which teachers, at what cost in coach time and for how long after support ends, remains to be tested here.

Related Patterns¶

  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the core learning model PD applies to adult learners: modeling → coaching → fading of expert practice
  • Collaborative Inquiry — a common PD structure in which teacher teams investigate questions from their own classrooms using student data
  • Flipped Classroom — a frequent target of PD and itself a model for PD design: content study outside meetings frees session time for active practice
  • Level 3 sustaining efforts: extend coach learning and foster independent teacher learning communities
  • Fostering Communities of Learning — the nearest community pattern, where the PD is carried by a standing group of colleagues
  • Level 1 initial steps: address teacher beliefs through individual conferencing and establish teacher learning communities

Examples¶

Reading Recovery (https://www.readingrecovery.org): Teachers receive a year of training behind a one-way mirror, observing expert lessons, teaching with live coaching, and discussing decisions afterward — a full apprenticeship cycle with fading support.

MyTeachingPartner (https://curry.virginia.edu/myteachingpartner): Web-mediated coaching in which teachers upload classroom video and receive consultant feedback on teacher–student interactions; randomized trials show improved instructional quality and student outcomes.

Japan's lesson study (jugyō kenkyū): Teacher teams collaboratively plan, observe, and refine a single research lesson over weeks — collective participation, practice focus, and sustained duration in one structure.

NCSM / Illustrative Mathematics professional learning (https://illustrativemathematics.org): Curriculum-embedded PD in which teachers experience the lessons as learners, then analyze and rehearse facilitation — coherence with enacted materials. - Use the coaching framework as a structured discussion guide with a note-taking grid and study group questions - Sustain tutor quality through layered professional development: live and asynchronous learning, observations, coaching, and weekly office hours - Instructional Commons: record and disseminate top teachers' full-year instruction for peer-to-peer professional use - Sustained classroom-embedded professional development through demonstration teachers
