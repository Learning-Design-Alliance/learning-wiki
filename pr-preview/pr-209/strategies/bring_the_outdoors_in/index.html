Bring the Outdoors In¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Bring the Outdoors In is an environmental design strategy that treats indoor and outdoor space as a continuous learning environment rather than separating "classroom" from "outside." It is carried out by physically opening the classroom to nature (roll-back doors, windows with green views, outdoor work zones), scheduling instructional time outdoors, and maximizing natural light indoors. The strategy draws on attention restoration theory and biophilic design: natural settings replenish depleted directed-attention resources and reduce physiological stress.

Design Implications¶

Natural views and daylight are associated with better attention, lower stress, and stronger engagement, particularly for learners whose attentional resources are most taxed [~M]. The effect is not automatic — the outdoor space must be designed as an instructional setting with clear task structures, or it functions as recess rather than learning. Because attention is a limited resource that nature helps restore, this strategy pairs naturally with Cognitive Load Management: a restorative environment lowers the baseline attentional cost of the lesson itself.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Physical access to an outdoor space or a view of vegetation; where neither exists, indoor plants, nature imagery, and maximized daylight are partial substitutes
  • Designated outdoor zones for group work and discussion, with seating and surfaces that support the target activity
  • Task structures that give outdoor time an instructional purpose (Activation activities, observation tasks, discussion protocols)
  • A welcoming, psychologically safe environment so the novel setting supports rather than disrupts engagement

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured outdoor time increases off-task behavior; novelty and stimulation compete with lesson goals for attention [-M]
  • Learners with sensory sensitivities or attention regulation difficulties may be more distracted outdoors, not less [~M]
  • Weather, safety, and equity of access limit reliability; learners without outdoor access at home benefit most, but schools serving them often have the least green space
  • Effects on achievement are indirect (via attention, stress, and engagement) and are weaker than effects on well-being [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full immersion: lessons held entirely outdoors (forest schools, outdoor classrooms)
  • Threshold design: roll-back doors, covered patios, and verandas that let learners move fluidly between settings within one lesson
  • Indoor substitution: daylight optimization, window views of greenery, plants, and nature murals where outdoor access is impossible
  • Curricular integration: outdoor spaces as sites for science observation, Case Studies of local ecosystems, or Active Learning tasks

Target Learners¶

  • Learners in high-density urban schools with limited green exposure — restoration effects are largest when baseline nature contact is low [~M]
  • Learners experiencing attentional fatigue or chronic stress, for whom restorative environments replenish concentration [~M]
  • Young learners in early childhood and primary settings, where outdoor pedagogy is easiest to structure
  • Less suitable without adaptation for learners with sensory-processing sensitivities or for tasks requiring sustained individual focus on screens or fine-motor work

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Engagement and motivation: comfortable, restorative settings increase willingness to participate [~W]
  • Attention-dependent learning: restored attention supports tasks requiring concentration
  • Observational and inquiry skills: outdoor spaces provide authentic phenomena for science and environmental learning
  • Social-emotional goals: reduced stress and improved classroom climate

Instructions¶

  1. Audit the learning environment: map daylight, views, and outdoor access; identify the highest-leverage change (often a window view or a single outdoor work zone).
  2. Designate and equip outdoor zones for specific activity types — discussion circles, observation stations, independent reading — so outdoor time has a defined instructional purpose.
  3. Open lessons with an Activation task that uses the outdoor setting itself (observation, sensory inventory, phenomenon notice-wonder) to bind attention to the learning goal before the novelty of the setting dissipates.
  4. Provide Guidance and clear task structures during outdoor work; circulate and use Check-ins to keep groups on task.
  5. Establish transition routines that bring learners back indoors and consolidate outdoor observations through Annotating or discussion.
  6. Where outdoor access is impossible, substitute daylight maximization, plants, and nature imagery, and schedule attention-restoration breaks.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — outdoor phenomena make effective anchors for activating what learners already know
  • Check-ins — brief affective check-ins help monitor whether the outdoor setting is restoring or distracting individual learners

Examples¶

  • Forest schools (Scandinavia and UK adaptations) — regular curriculum delivered in woodland settings; studies report improved concentration and social play, with academic gains depending on how deliberately classroom content is carried outdoors.
  • Green School Bali — a K–12 campus built around open-air, bamboo classrooms with no walls, structuring the entire curriculum around the indoor/outdoor continuum.
  • Natural Pod and biophilic classroom retrofits — daylight-first classroom design with natural materials, used where outdoor access is limited.
  • Urban schoolyard greening programs (e.g., Toronto's Evergreen Brick Works school ground greening) — transforming paved schoolyards into teaching gardens used for structured lessons.
