Debate for the Opposite Team¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners are assigned to argue a position that is opposite to their own, requiring them to see things from a different perspective. Rather than choosing a side, learners must construct the strongest possible case for the view they reject — researching its best evidence, anticipating objections, and defending it against rebuttal. The strategy converts disagreement from a threat into a structured learning task.

Design Implications¶

Arguing a position one disagrees with forces deeper processing of the opposing view than simply hearing it, countering the biased assimilation that occurs when learners evaluate counterarguments from their own stance [~M]. Structured argumentation also strengthens critical-thinking and communication skills, though effects depend on the quality of preparation and facilitation rather than the debate format alone [~M]. Perspective-taking induced by role assignment can improve intergroup attitudes, but only when learners genuinely engage with the assigned perspective rather than performing it superficially Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuinely contestable topic with defensible positions on both sides — not a settled fact
  • Research resources and adequate preparation time before the debate
  • Explicit ground rules and a skilled facilitator to keep the exchange respectful and substantive
  • Structured roles and turn-taking (Assigned Positions, Argumentation)

Constraints¶

  • Learners may resist arguing a position they find objectionable, or may treat the exercise as a game and disengage from its substance [-W]
  • If learners already hold strong attitudes, arguing the opposite side can produce counterattitudinal advocacy that backfires: they may rehearse and strengthen their own position while strawmanning the assigned one [~M]
  • Debate formats reward rhetorical fluency, which can privilege confident speakers over accurate reasoning and mask shallow understanding [-M]
  • Without a debrief, learners may remember the performance but not the perspective-taking insight; the learning is fragile unless made explicit afterward [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Two-round debate: teams argue one side, then swap and argue the other (Role Reversal), ensuring every learner engages both positions
  • Written version: learners draft the strongest opposing argument in an essay or Annotating exercise, useful when class time or emotional intensity makes live debate impractical
  • Structured academic controversy: small groups divide into pairs on each side, argue, reverse, then drop roles and seek consensus — the Johnsons' cooperative-learning variant
  • Low-stakes warm-up: use low-emotion topics (e.g., "homework should be banned") to build the skill before tackling identity-laden issues

Target Learners¶

  • Learners who tend toward rigid or one-sided thinking and resist alternative viewpoints [~M]
  • Adolescents and adults; younger learners often lack the perspective-taking capacity and argumentation skills to argue a counterattitudinal position productively [-W]
  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge of the topic — complete novices cannot construct strong arguments, and experts gain little [~M]
