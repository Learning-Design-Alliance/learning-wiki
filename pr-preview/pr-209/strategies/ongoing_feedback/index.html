Ongoing Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Ongoing feedback delivers feedback at multiple points during the writing process — planning, drafting, and revising — rather than only on the final product. Teachers may focus feedback on specific sections or stages, students may proactively request comments using digital tools such as Google Docs or Kaizena, and self-guiding stations with prompting questions let students progress independently while the teacher confers with small groups. The defining feature is that feedback arrives while revision is still possible, so it functions as instruction rather than evaluation.

Design Implications¶

Ongoing feedback operationalizes Assessment for Learning: feedback is most effective when it tells learners where they are relative to a goal and how to close the gap, and when it arrives early enough to act on Feedback improves learning when it answers where-am-I-going, how-am-I-doing, and where-to-next questions. [+S]. Feedback on drafts produces larger writing gains than feedback on final products because students can immediately apply it through revision Writing instruction improves achievement when it includes structured process support. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Multiple checkpoints built into the assignment timeline (e.g., proposal → outline → draft → revision), each with a clear feedback focus
  • A manageable feedback load: comment on one or two dimensions per stage rather than everything at once
  • Explicit time and expectation for revision — feedback without a required revision cycle rarely changes the final product
  • A rubric or success criteria shared up front so feedback references a known target

Constraints¶

  • Feedback delivered too late (at final submission) has near-zero effect on learning [-S] — students read the grade and move on
  • Overloaded or vague comments ("awkward," "expand") exceed working memory capacity and are ignored Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-M]
  • Whole-class written feedback on every draft is unsustainable; teachers who attempt it reduce frequency or quality, and effect sizes drop accordingly [~M]
  • Feedback perceived as judgment of the person rather than the work can depress motivation and subsequent performance [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Staged teacher feedback: comments targeted to one dimension per checkpoint (e.g., argument structure on the outline, evidence use on the draft, style on the revision)
  • Peer feedback: structured peer review with protocols shifts some feedback load to students and builds evaluative judgment
  • Self-guiding stations: students rotate through stations with self-checking questions while the teacher runs small-group conferences
  • Digital inline feedback: tools like Google Docs comments, Kaizena audio comments, or Turnitin Feedback Studio allow just-in-time comments during drafting
  • Student-initiated feedback: learners flag specific questions ("Does my thesis match my conclusion?") to make feedback targeted and actionable

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers in K-12 and higher education, especially those who do not yet self-monitor their drafts Feedback improves learning when it answers where-am-I-going, how-am-I-doing, and where-to-next questions. [+S]
  • Struggling writers benefit most from early, specific feedback that prevents error rehearsal across a long draft
  • Advanced writers may need less frequent feedback; excessive guidance can reduce ownership and autonomy [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Process skills: planning, drafting, revising, and self-evaluation habits
  • Quality improvement of written products through iterative refinement
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing the criteria so students can assess their own drafts

Instructions¶

  1. Share success criteria and a rubric before drafting begins, so every feedback checkpoint references a visible target (Assessment).
  2. Break the assignment into staged checkpoints (proposal, outline, draft, revision) and assign each stage one feedback focus.
  3. At each checkpoint, deliver specific, actionable comments — ideally on the section or dimension due that stage — and require a revision response (Provide Feedback if available; otherwise use inline comments or conferencing).
  4. Build in Practice through revision: students apply the feedback to their own text, not just read it.
  5. Use self-guiding stations or peer-review protocols so students who are waiting for teacher feedback are still productively evaluating drafts.
  6. Assess Performance on the revised product, making visible how feedback changed the work.

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — revision is the practice that converts feedback into learning
  • Assess Performance — each checkpoint is a low-stakes assessment event
  • Coaching — conferencing during drafting is coaching applied to writing
  • Check-In — lightweight progress monitoring between major feedback points

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Formative Assessment — ongoing feedback is the feedback loop of the formative cycle
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the coaching phase, where the expert observes work in progress and intervenes

Examples¶

  • Google Docs + Kaizena — Students share drafts with comment access; the teacher leaves inline or audio comments on the working draft, and students respond to each comment with a revision before resubmitting. (https://kaizena.com)
  • Writing workshop with stations — Students rotate through stations (thesis check, evidence check, conventions check) with self-guiding questions while the teacher holds 5-minute conferences with small groups.
  • Staged submission in a college composition course — Proposal, annotated outline, and full draft each receive targeted feedback with a required revision memo explaining what changed and why.
