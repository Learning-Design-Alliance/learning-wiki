Multimodal Assignments¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 13 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 5 of 13 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Multimodal assignments involve a variety of assessment methods that challenge learners to create video or audio projects, write personal essays, produce visualizations, or complete quizzes. The pedagogical rationale is twofold: learners encode content through multiple representational channels, and they must actively transform material into a new form — a generative process that supports retention and transfer.

Design Implications¶

Multimodal output works because combining verbal and visual representations engages both processing channels Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M], and because producing an artifact is a form of Active Learning rather than passive reception Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. However, the benefit comes from the generative transformation of content, not from the medium itself — a poorly designed video assignment is no better than a poorly designed essay.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to tools for creating video and audio projects; an LMS or platform for uploading and sharing assignments
  • Clear instructions and assessment criteria, ideally a rubric that evaluates understanding rather than production polish
  • Supportive Information describing the concepts to be represented and Procedural Information for the tools and formats

Constraints¶

  • Production demands can impose extraneous Cognitive Load: when tool manipulation is unfamiliar, working memory is consumed by the medium rather than the content [~M] — teach the tool separately or allow low-fidelity formats
  • Media richness does not guarantee learning; redundant or decorative media can depress performance Media combinations affect recall and retention. [~M]
  • Potential for inequitable access to devices, bandwidth, and quiet recording spaces
  • Grading multimodal work is more time-consuming and less standardized than grading essays or quizzes

Implementation Variability¶

  • Learner choice of format (video, podcast, essay, infographic) against a common rubric — supports autonomy and Task Value [+M]
  • Fixed format for all learners when the medium itself is a learning goal (e.g., scientific communication via video)
  • Low-stakes multimodal micro-tasks (annotated screenshots, 2-minute audio reflections) as alternatives to full projects

Target Learners¶

  • Diverse learners with varied expressive strengths; offering format choice lets learners play to strengths, which can build confidence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Learners for whom a single writing-heavy assessment underrepresents understanding
  • Less suitable when learners lack the technical fluency to operate the medium — the tool becomes the task

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: explaining ideas in one's own words and representations Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Communication and disciplinary representation skills
  • Reflection and metacognition through Individual Reflection and Peer Discussion

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning goal and the criteria for understanding, independent of format (Assessment)
  2. Provide Supportive Information on the concepts and exemplars of strong multimodal work
  3. Provide Procedural Information and a low-stakes trial run with the chosen tool
  4. Have learners produce the artifact with structured checkpoints (Practice)
  5. Deliver Feedback focused on the quality of understanding, not production polish Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  6. Assess Performance with a format-neutral rubric, then close the loop with Individual Reflection, Peer Discussion, or Whole-Class Sharing

Related Strategies¶

  • Authentic Audiences, Purposes — multimodal artifacts gain power when addressed to a real audience beyond the grader
  • Assessment for Learning — format choice and rubrics make the assignment a learning event, not just a measurement
  • Give learners control over how learning is measured via assessment choices and science-fair-type projects

Examples¶

  • A biology course where students produce a 3-minute explainer video of a cellular process instead of a lab report, uploaded to the LMS and peer-reviewed
  • VoiceThread (https://voicethread.com) — learners post audio/video commentary on shared media and respond to peers asynchronously
  • Padlet (https://padlet.com) — mixed-media boards combining text, images, and audio for collaborative multimodal submissions
  • A writing course offering essay, podcast, or photo-essay options against a single argumentation rubric
