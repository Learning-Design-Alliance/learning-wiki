Using Empathy Interviews for Family Engagement¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 0 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Empathy interviews are targeted, one-on-one conversations with a small, purposive sample of family members to learn from their lived experiences of schooling. Borrowed from human-centered design practice (notably the Stanford d.school Bootcamp Bootleg), the method prioritizes open-ended "tell me about a time…" questions over surveys or checklists, letting interviewees define what matters. In school settings, the collected stories drive district and school decisions, build trust, and strengthen family–school relationships — the listening itself is an engagement act, not just a data-collection step.

Design Implications¶

Empathy interviews work because they position families as experts on their own experience rather than as passive recipients of school decisions; this stance shift is what distinguishes them from conventional parent surveys or town halls. The quality of what is learned depends almost entirely on interviewer discipline: withholding judgment, following up on stories rather than steering them, and probing for specific episodes instead of opinions Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]. Because the sample is small and purposive, findings should be treated as formative insight for design decisions, not as generalizable needs assessments.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear purpose: which decision or design problem will these stories inform?
  • Interviewers trained in open-ended questioning and non-judgmental listening (Active Listening)
  • Purposive sampling that reaches families beyond the usual engaged voices — multilingual families, families of students who are struggling, families who have disengaged
  • A translation and interpretation plan so language is not a barrier to participation
  • A closed loop: participants and the wider community must see how their input shaped decisions, or trust erodes

Constraints¶

  • Small, self-selected samples overrepresent accessible voices; findings from ten interviews cannot stand in for a representative needs assessment [-W]
  • Interviews conducted by staff with prior adversarial contact with a family (e.g., truancy calls) can suppress candor [~M]
  • Extractive use — collecting stories without visible follow-through — damages trust more than never asking [-M]
  • Poorly framed questions ("Don't you agree that…?") elicit compliance rather than experience [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Design-cycle mode: interviews feed a redesign team (e.g., a Collaborative Inquiry cycle) that prototypes responses
  • Relationship-first mode: interviews are conducted purely to build relationships, with no decision attached — common at the start of a school year
  • Student-inclusive mode: parallel interviews with students triangulate family accounts
  • Peer-interviewer mode: trained family members interview their peers, increasing candor and reach

Target Learners¶

  • School and district leaders making decisions that affect family experience (enrollment, communication, discipline, family engagement programming)
  • Teachers seeking to understand individual families at the start of a year or intervention
  • Family and community liaisons building outreach capacity
  • The families themselves benefit relationally: being heard by school staff supports a sense of belonging that predicts engagement Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Holistic understanding of community needs, values, and barriers from families' own frames of reference
  • Empathic perspective-taking by educators Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
  • Trust and relational capital between families and schools
  • Evidence for equity-centered design decisions

Instructions¶

  1. Define the design question. Name the decision the interviews will inform (e.g., "Why do families of our multilingual learners rarely attend conferences?").
  2. Recruit purposively. Identify 5–15 families across relevant segments; use trusted intermediaries (community organizations, liaisons) for introductions.
  3. Prepare open-ended questions. Draft 5–8 story-eliciting prompts ("Tell me about a time school communication worked well for you") using careful Question Formulation; pilot them with a colleague.
  4. Interview with discipline. Meet in a comfortable setting (home, community space, or phone); open with a warm Check-In; ask "why" and "tell me more" follow-ups; avoid defending the school or problem-solving mid-interview.
  5. Capture stories, not verdicts. Record (with consent) or take verbatim notes; preserve quotes and specific episodes.
  6. Synthesize collectively. Debrief as a team, cluster themes, and identify surprises and tensions rather than averaging responses.
  7. Close the loop. Report back to participants and the community on what was heard and what will change; repeat interviews to check whether changes landed.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Listening — the core interviewer skill; without it, interviews degrade into scripted surveys
  • Home Visits — a longer-form relational engagement practice that often incorporates empathy interviewing
  • Community-Based Learning — shares the stance that learning happens in and with the community, not about it
  • Conduct empathy interviews as story-based conversations in comfortable, student-chosen settings
  • Have students interview study-abroad returnees to learn about living abroad first hand
  • Conduct a needs assessment combining baseline district data and projective opinion data before designing a middle school
  • Use paired interviews early in a group's life to build connections and surface participant information
  • Use sentence completion interviews to elicit learner concerns and generate group themes
  • Combine traditional and nontraditional approaches to family engagement for ELL families
  • Form design teams by building trust and relationships and prioritizing meaningful representation before project kick-off.
  • Use small shared-meal conversations among teachers, students, and a facilitator to surface student perspectives on assessment and strengthen relationships
  • Conduct empathy interviews to understand user experiences with technology

Related Elements¶

  • Check-In — the opening move that establishes psychological safety
  • Question Formulation — crafting the open-ended prompts that elicit stories
  • Class Discussion — a related but group-based elicitation format; interviews trade breadth for depth and candor

Examples¶

  • The Learning Accelerator documents districts using empathy interviews to redesign family communication, interviewing 8–12 families per design cycle and reporting themes back through community meetings (learningaccelerator.org).
  • Stanford d.school's K12 Lab trains educators in interview techniques as part of design challenges, with school teams interviewing students and families before prototyping solutions (dschool.stanford.edu).
  • Home-visit programs such as the Parent Teacher Home Visits model (pthvp.org) use relational, story-first conversation structures closely aligned with empathy interviewing.
