Pictionary (Pre-Reading)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Key words from an upcoming text are selected, and the class is divided into two or three teams. A learner from each team comes to the whiteboard and draws a word without using letters or numbers while teammates race to guess it. The activity primes vocabulary, activates prior knowledge, and builds visual-verbal associations before learners encounter the words in context.

Design Implications¶

Pre-reading vocabulary games work because they combine retrieval of word meanings with dual coding — pairing verbal labels with nonverbal imagery strengthens memory for word–concept links Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. The competitive, game-like format also raises engagement and participation compared with passive vocabulary presentation Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. Crucially, the guessing process forces learners to articulate what they already know about a concept, which serves as Activation of prior knowledge before reading.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A whiteboard or shared drawing surface, markers, and a timer
  • Pre-selected key words drawn from the target text — concrete, drawable concepts work best
  • Team structures that give every learner a turn drawing and guessing (Collaboration)
  • A brief debrief connecting each guessed word to the upcoming reading (Class Discussion)

Constraints¶

  • Abstract or low-frequency words (e.g., "symbiosis," "inference") are hard to draw and can produce wrong guesses that entrench misconceptions [~M] — the debrief must correct these before reading
  • Learners anxious about drawing in public may disengage; rotating roles and accepting stick figures lowers the stakes
  • The game itself teaches word–image association, not deep word meaning; without follow-up reading and use, gains are shallow [~M]
  • Time spent on the game competes with reading time; overlong rounds reduce instructional efficiency

Implementation Variability¶

  • Charades variant: learners act out words instead of drawing, useful for verbs and processes (Act It Out)
  • Learner-selected words: teams choose which words to draw, adding a selection/reasoning step
  • Digital versions: tools like Kahoot! or a shared Jamboard whiteboard support remote classes
  • Post-reading reversal: the same game after reading checks retention rather than activating knowledge

Target Learners¶

  • Language learners and younger readers who benefit from visual scaffolds for new vocabulary Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]
  • Mixed-proficiency groups: guessing is lower-stakes than drawing, so reluctant learners can participate
  • Less suitable when the text's key vocabulary is overwhelmingly abstract or technical

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Vocabulary pre-teaching and word recognition before reading
  • Prior knowledge activation: surfacing what learners already know about text concepts
  • Conceptual association: linking new terms to familiar images and experiences

Instructions¶

  1. Select 6–10 key words from the text, favoring concrete, drawable concepts; keep the list short to avoid overload Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  2. Divide the class into two or three teams and explain the rules (no letters, numbers, or gestures)
  3. A drawer from each team sketches a word while teammates guess; first correct guess scores a point
  4. After each round, briefly connect the word to the reading: "Where do you think we'll see habitat in this text?" — this step enacts Activation and is where most of the learning value lies
  5. Close with a quick review of all words before transitioning to the text (Practice follows during and after reading)

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the broader family this game belongs to; Pictionary is a game-based instance
  • Vocabulary Pre-Teaching — the direct-instruction alternative; combining explicit definitions with the game covers both form and meaning
  • Structured discussions that anchor new vocabulary in students' personal experience

Related Elements¶

  • Activation — the guessing and debrief steps surface prior knowledge before reading
  • Collaboration — team structure creates peer support and shared accountability
  • Class Discussion — the debrief that links words to the text
  • Practice — the reading itself is where pre-taught words are applied
  • Analogies — drawing an unfamiliar word requires mapping it to a familiar image, the same move as analogy-building

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Direct Instruction — the pre-teaching phase can open a direct-instruction sequence
  • Anchored Instruction — pre-activating vocabulary anchors learners to the text's context before reading

Examples¶

  • Before reading about ecosystems, words like habitat, species, and biodiversity are drawn and guessed; the debrief asks teams to predict how each word will appear in the text.
  • In an ESL class before a short story, teams draw emotion words (anxious, relieved) and then sort them by which characters might feel them.
