Explicitly Discuss Different Perspectives¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners engage in structured discussions that make diverse viewpoints explicit — examining how people with different backgrounds, disciplinary commitments, or stakeholder positions interpret the same phenomenon. The strategy goes beyond exposure: learners must articulate a perspective, compare it against alternatives, and reason about why the differences exist. It is typically carried out through facilitated whole-class discussion, structured controversy, or analysis of contrasting cases and primary sources.

Design Implications¶

Structured perspective-taking discussion supports conceptual growth because learners must reconcile conflicting interpretations, which prompts elaboration and schema revision Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying principles. [+S]. Diversity-related discussion is associated with gains in cognitive and civic outcomes when it is structured rather than merely co-occurring with a diverse group [+M]. The facilitator's role is critical: unstructured discussion tends to reproduce existing opinions, while assigned positions and evidence requirements force genuine engagement with unfamiliar views.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A psychologically safe environment with established norms for respectful disagreement
  • A facilitator skilled in guiding discussion and ensuring all voices are heard (Class Discussion)
  • Authentic source material that genuinely embodies multiple perspectives (primary sources, Case Studies, stakeholder testimony)
  • A structure that assigns positions or requires evidence, so learners argue views they may not hold

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured or poorly facilitated discussion can entrench prior beliefs and produce polarization rather than perspective-taking [-M]
  • Learners with low confidence or minority status may not voice their perspectives; cold-calling and small-group formats mitigate but do not eliminate this [-W]
  • Perspective discussion consumes substantial instructional time and is inefficient for procedural or factual objectives where a single correct answer exists [-M]
  • Superficial "tourist" treatment of perspectives can reinforce stereotypes rather than reduce them [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured controversy: learners are assigned positions, argue them with evidence, then switch sides and synthesize
  • Contrasting-case analysis: small groups compare how different sources or stakeholders frame the same event
  • Role-play and simulation: learners adopt stakeholder positions before whole-class Class Discussion
  • Written perspective-taking: learners draft an argument from an assigned viewpoint, then peer-review against an opposing draft

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who hold initial schemas firm enough to be productively challenged — complete novices lack the knowledge to evaluate competing views [~M]
  • Learners in heterogeneous groups, where exposure to genuinely different experiences drives the effect [+M]
  • Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who cannot distinguish substantive from superficial disagreement

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: recognizing how framing and assumptions shape interpretation Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying principles. [+S]
  • Argumentation and critical thinking: constructing, critiquing, and revising claims Case-based learning improves exam performance when discussion requires reasoning through cases. [+M]
  • Empathy and civic dispositions: understanding the experiences and reasoning of others [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Select a topic with genuine, well-documented disagreement and gather materials representing at least three perspectives.
  2. Establish discussion norms and activate relevant background knowledge (Activation).
  3. Assign learners to positions — ideally not their own — and have them prepare evidence-based arguments (Argumentation); assigning positions prevents learners from defaulting to familiar views.
  4. Facilitate structured exchange (Class Discussion), requiring learners to restate opposing views before critiquing them.
  5. Introduce Cognitive Conflict deliberately by highlighting genuine contradictions between perspectives.
  6. Close with synthesis: learners articulate what the disagreement reveals about underlying assumptions and where they now stand (Articulation).

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-based discussion — cases are a natural vehicle for embedding multiple stakeholder perspectives in one artifact
  • Debate — a formalized version of perspective discussion with assigned positions and evidence rules
  • Discussion-based learning — the broader pattern this strategy instantiates with a multiperspectival focus
  • Facilitate cognitive disequilibrium about ethnocentric views

Examples¶

  • Facing History and Ourselves (https://www.facinghistory.org) — published curriculum in which students analyze primary sources from multiple stakeholder positions on historical events, followed by structured discussion and reflection.
  • Structured academic controversy in a psychology course — students assigned to argue for and against a theory (e.g., nature vs. nurture in language acquisition), then switch sides before writing a synthesis paper.
  • Business school case discussion — Harvard-style case teaching requires learners to analyze a decision from the perspectives of different executives, customers, and regulators before defending a recommendation.
