Reinforce Positive Behaviors¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Reinforcing positive behaviors means deliberately noticing and responding to desired learner actions — effort, cooperation, self-regulation, correct responses — with contingent praise, acknowledgment, or rewards, rather than attending only to misbehavior. Grounded in operant conditioning Behaviorism, the strategy increases the future frequency of reinforced behaviors when the reinforcer reliably and immediately follows the behavior and is explicitly tied to it.

Design Implications¶

Contingent, specific praise for appropriate behavior is among the most consistently supported classroom management practices, increasing on-task behavior and reducing disruption when applied systematically Simonsen et al.'s review identifies group contingency and praise practices as evidence-based. [+S]. Effectiveness depends on what is praised: feedback that names the behavior or process ("you checked your work before handing it in") outperforms generic approval ("good job") and person-directed praise Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+S]. Rewards can backfire when they are tangible, expected, and unrelated to competence, undermining intrinsic motivation for inherently interesting tasks Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation under specific conditions. [~S] — though the undermining effect is smaller or absent when rewards convey competence or are delivered as informational praise Meta-analytic evidence suggests rewards do not uniformly damage intrinsic motivation. [~M]. Design for autonomy support: frame reinforcement as information about competence rather than control Autonomy-supportive environments sustain intrinsic motivation. [+M], consistent with Self-Determination Theory.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clearly defined, observable target behaviors that learners understand
  • Immediate, contingent delivery — the reinforcer must follow the behavior, not precede or accompany it
  • Specificity: praise that identifies the behavior and why it matters, not blanket approval
  • A high ratio of positive to corrective interactions (commonly cited as at least 4:1 in PBIS implementations)

Constraints¶

  • Tangible, expected rewards for tasks learners already find interesting can reduce intrinsic motivation Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation under specific conditions. [-M]
  • Praise directed at the person ("you're so smart") promotes fixed-ability beliefs and avoidance of challenge relative to process praise [-M]
  • Reinforcement that stops abruptly produces a rebound of the behavior; intermittent schedules and gradual fading are needed for maintenance [~S]
  • Public praise can embarrass older learners or those with social anxiety, suppressing rather than strengthening the behavior [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Behavior-specific praise: verbal acknowledgment naming the behavior ("You waited your turn to speak")
  • Group contingencies: the whole class earns a shared reinforcer when collective criteria are met (e.g., Classroom Dojo-style point systems)
  • Token economies: points or tokens exchanged later for privileges; useful for establishing new behaviors, then faded
  • Private or nonverbal reinforcement: nods, proximity, written comments — for learners for whom public attention is aversive
  • Self-reinforcement: learners monitor and record their own behavior, shifting locus of control toward self-regulation

Target Learners¶

  • Young learners and novices still establishing basic classroom routines and work habits [+S]
  • Learners with behavioral or attentional difficulties, for whom structured positive reinforcement is a core component of interventions like PBIS [+S]
  • Adolescents and adults, for whom private, competence-focused acknowledgment works better than public token systems [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural and behavioral routines: establishing classroom norms, participation habits, self-regulation
  • Motivational dispositions: persistence, help-seeking, effort attribution
  • Social-emotional behaviors: cooperation, turn-taking, respectful discourse

Instructions¶

  1. Define 2–3 observable target behaviors and tell learners explicitly what they are and why they matter.
  2. Actively scan for occurrences of the target behavior; reinforce within seconds, naming the behavior specifically (process-level feedback Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+S]).
  3. Pair reinforcement with a brief rationale connecting the behavior to learning ("Rereading your draft caught two errors — that's what good writers do").
  4. Prefer informational praise over tangible rewards for tasks with inherent interest Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation under specific conditions. [~S]; if using tokens or points, tie them to competence and progress.
  5. Fade continuous reinforcement to an intermittent schedule, then shift learners toward self-monitoring and self-reinforcement to build durable, intrinsically motivated behavior Autonomy-supportive environments sustain intrinsic motivation. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-In — regular one-to-one contact creates the relationship context that makes individualized reinforcement credible
  • Coaching — coaches model and prompt the specific praise behaviors teachers then apply independently

Examples¶

  • PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions & Supports) (https://www.pbis.org) — school-wide systems in which staff deliver behavior-specific praise and token reinforcement at high ratios, with data-based fading as behavior stabilizes.
  • Classroom Dojo — digital point system for immediate, visible reinforcement of named behaviors; works best when points are tied to specific feedback rather than used as a standing leaderboard.
  • Good Behavior Game — a group contingency in which teams earn privileges by collectively meeting behavioral criteria; strong experimental support for reducing disruption [+S].
