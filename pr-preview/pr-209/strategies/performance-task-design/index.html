Performance Task Design¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 11 studies (5 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 3 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Performance task design is the deliberate construction of tasks that require learners to do something with their knowledge — produce an artifact, solve an ill-structured problem, conduct an investigation, or execute a procedure — under conditions that resemble how the knowledge is used outside school. Assessment is built into the task rather than appended to it: the criteria for quality are typically made explicit in a rubric, and the task itself yields evidence of both learning and transfer.

Design Implications¶

Well-designed performance tasks sit at the intersection of Authentic Assessment and Assessment for Learning: they elicit the target performance directly rather than a proxy for it, and they generate evidence learners can act on Assessment designed to inform instruction improves achievement. [+S]. Authenticity matters for motivation and quality of work — tasks with real audiences and purposes elicit more effortful, higher-quality products Authentic audiences and purposes improve student work. [+M]. Because performance tasks impose high intrinsic and extraneous demands, they must be designed against working-memory limits; overloaded tasks produce measurement noise rather than learning evidence Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear specification of the target performance: what competent work looks like, observable and assessable
  • A rubric or scoring guide shared with learners before they begin, so criteria function as advance organizers for self-monitoring
  • Sufficient scaffolding at entry — models, worked examples, or partial tasks — that fades as competence grows
  • A meaningful product or performance, ideally with a real or simulated audience (Authentic Audiences & Purposes)

Constraints¶

  • Scoring reliability drops sharply when rubrics are vague or when raters lack training; holistic judgments of complex work are notoriously inconsistent [-M]
  • Tasks that are authentic but poorly aligned to learning goals measure general ability or effort rather than the intended constructs [~M]
  • High cognitive load from unfamiliar formats, open-endedness, or extraneous demands can depress performance for novices, making the task a poor assessment Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]
  • Time-intensive to design, administer, and score; scaling beyond small classes requires trade-offs (e.g., checklists, peer scoring) that can undermine validity Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online settings. [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured → open continuum: highly scaffolded tasks (scripted labs, guided case analyses) for novices; open-ended design briefs or capstone projects for advanced learners
  • Individual vs. collaborative: group performance tasks add coordination demands; individual accountability mechanisms (role assignments, individual write-ups) are needed
  • Embedded vs. summative: tasks can function as ongoing practice with feedback, or as summative demonstrations in Competency-Based Assessment systems

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have foundational knowledge and are ready to integrate it; pure novices often lack the schema to navigate open-ended demands [~M]
  • Learners preparing for transfer to professional or civic contexts where knowledge is applied under realistic conditions
  • Less suitable as a first exposure for learners with minimal prior knowledge, who benefit from worked examples and demonstration before independent performance

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Application and transfer: using knowledge in novel, realistic situations
  • Integration: coordinating concepts, procedures, and judgment within one task
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising extended work against explicit criteria
  • Disciplinary practices: argumentation, investigation, design, critique

Instructions¶

  1. Define the target performance. Specify what learners should be able to do, not just know; align the task so the performance is directly observable (constructive alignment).
  2. Choose an authentic scenario. Anchor the task in a realistic context with a genuine purpose and audience; consider case-based learning or anchored instruction formats.
  3. Build the rubric first. Draft scoring criteria before finalizing the task; if a criterion cannot be assessed, the task does not elicit it.
  4. Design entry scaffolds. Provide models, exemplars of strong and weak work (non-examples), and partial task structures that will be faded.
  5. Plan feedback cycles. Build in at least one draft-and-revise cycle with feedback against the rubric, so the task functions as assessment for learning, not just measurement.
  6. Pilot and calibrate scoring. Test the task, have multiple raters score sample work, and revise the rubric where judgments diverge.

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-Based Learning — a common performance-task format built around realistic cases
  • Project-Based Learning — extended performance tasks organized around a driving question and product
  • Portfolio Assessment — aggregates performance evidence across tasks over time

Examples¶

  • 4C/ID — whole learning tasks are the organizing unit; performance task design governs task-class sequencing from simple to complex with fading support.
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the articulation and exploration phases rely on performance tasks that push learners to produce and defend work.
  • PBLWorks (Buck Institute) — publishes gold-standard project designs with authentic products, public audiences, and rubrics.
  • AP Performance Tasks (College Board) — e.g., the AP Computer Science Principles Create task, where students design and program an artifact scored against a published rubric.
