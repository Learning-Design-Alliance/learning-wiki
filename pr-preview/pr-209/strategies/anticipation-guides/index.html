Anticipation Guides¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

An anticipation guide is a series of 3–8 declarative statements — some true, some plausible-but-false — that learners respond to (typically agree/disagree) before reading or instruction. After engaging with the content, learners revisit their initial responses, discuss discrepancies, and revise their answers with justification. The strategy was developed by Readence and colleagues for content-area literacy and is widely used in science, social studies, and language arts.

Design Implications¶

Anticipation guides work by activating relevant prior knowledge before new material is encountered, which improves comprehension and integration Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+S]. They also deliberately surface misconceptions: when learners commit to an answer and then encounter contradictory evidence, the resulting cognitive conflict motivates conceptual change Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]. The post-reading revisit-and-revise step is essential — guides used only as a pre-activity lose most of their benefit.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Statements that target the big ideas of the text or lesson, not trivia
  • At least some statements that learners will answer incorrectly before instruction — the guide must be diagnostic, not just a preview
  • A structured post-activity discussion or revision step where initial answers are compared against the text's evidence
  • Sufficient prior knowledge to make initial predictions meaningful (Activation)
  • Statements that target the big ideas of the text, not surface details, and that are answerable from the text itself
  • At least some statements learners will initially get wrong — a guide everyone answers correctly before reading does no conceptual work
  • A structured revisit after reading in which learners cite text evidence for confirmations and revisions
  • Brief peer discussion of initial positions to commit learners to answers they must then defend or abandon

Constraints¶

  • If all statements are easily answered correctly beforehand, the guide adds little and may function as mere busywork [-M]
  • Learners with very low prior knowledge may guess randomly, producing no productive activation or disequilibrium [~M]
  • Poorly worded or ambiguous statements can entrench misconceptions if the follow-up discussion never explicitly resolves them [-M]
  • Overly long guides (10+ statements) shift attention from ideas to compliance [~W]
  • Statements that can be answered from surface features or prior familiarity let learners respond correctly without engaging the text [-M]
  • If the post-reading revisit is omitted or treated as answer-checking, gains shrink substantially; the strategy depends on the revision-and-justify cycle [~M]
  • Poorly worded statements that are ambiguous or arguable from the text itself generate confusion rather than productive conflict [-M]
  • For narrative texts with low conceptual content, the guide format adds little beyond what prediction questions already do [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Agree/disagree with probability scale — learners rate confidence (e.g., 1–5), which sharpens the surprise when answers are contradicted
  • Think-pair-share format — individual responses followed by partner or whole-class discussion before reading (Class Discussion)
  • Digital polling — tools like Mentimeter or Google Forms collect anonymous class-wide response distributions, making misconceptions visible to the whole group
  • Extended guides — a third column asks learners to cite textual evidence for their revised answer, adding argumentation practice
  • Numeric rating (agree–disagree scale) instead of binary choice, allowing degrees of commitment
  • Anticipation/reaction guide with a second column completed after reading, making revision visible side-by-side
  • Extended discussion format where small groups must reach consensus before reading, raising the stakes of the text encounter
  • Digital versions with live polling (e.g., Mentimeter, Google Forms) that display the class's split positions before reading

Target Learners¶

  • Novices in a content domain who hold common misconceptions (e.g., naive physics or history myths) Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]
  • Reluctant readers, who gain a purpose and personal stake in reading the text Activation improves learning. [+M]
  • Less useful for learners with strong, accurate prior knowledge, for whom the guide is largely redundant [~W]
  • Struggling readers and English learners, who benefit from the explicit schema activation and purpose-setting the guide provides before tackling dense text [+M]
  • Learners holding common misconceptions in science and social studies, where committed-but-wrong predictions create the conflict needed for conceptual change [~M]
  • Whole-class use is typical; for learners with strong prior knowledge of the topic, the guide functions mainly as an advance organizer rather than a misconception intervention [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect ideas with disciplinary ones
  • Reading comprehension: setting a purpose and monitoring for confirmation or disconfirmation
  • Metacognition: committing to predictions and evaluating them against evidence
  • Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect beliefs with text-supported understanding
  • Comprehension monitoring: reading with specific claims in mind to confirm or refute
  • Argumentation from evidence: justifying revisions with citations from the text

Instructions¶

  1. Identify 3–8 key ideas from the text, including common misconceptions, and write them as declarative statements.
  2. Present the guide and have learners respond individually before any instruction (Advance Organizers can frame the activity).
  3. Briefly discuss initial responses so learners commit publicly to positions.
  4. Learners read or engage with the content (Assigned Readings, lecture, or video).
  5. Revisit the guide: learners revise answers and cite evidence for changes; resolve each statement explicitly in discussion.
  6. Close by naming the misconceptions that were overturned, connecting them to the target concepts.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the core mechanism the guide operationalizes
  • KWL Charts — a related pre/during/post framework with open-ended rather than statement-based prompts
  • Erroneous Examples — similarly exploits errors for conceptual gain, but during practice rather than before instruction
  • K-W-L charts — a sibling pre/during/post strategy that collects questions rather than committed positions

Examples¶

  • Science misconceptions — Before a unit on seasons, a guide includes "Summer is warmer because Earth is closer to the Sun" (false); post-reading revision forces confrontation with the tilt explanation.
  • Reading Rockets — Provides templates and content-area examples of anticipation guides for K–12 literacy instruction.
  • History class — A guide for a unit on the Great Depression includes "The stock market crash caused widespread bank failures immediately," prompting learners to read for causal sequence and revise.
  • Science misconceptions: Before a text on photosynthesis, students respond to "Plants get most of their mass from soil" — a widely held misconception the text directly refutes, making the revision step a genuine conceptual change event.
  • History: Before reading about the causes of the Great Depression, students judge statements like "The stock market crash caused the Depression" and revisit the more complex causal account afterward.
  • Reading Rockets publishes ready-made anticipation guide templates and guidance for expository text: readingrockets.org
