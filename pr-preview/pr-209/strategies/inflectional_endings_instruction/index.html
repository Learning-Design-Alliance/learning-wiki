Inflectional Endings Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 unmarked) · 14 studies (6 causal, 6 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 5 of 14 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Inflectional endings instruction explicitly teaches the rules for adding suffixes such as -ed, -s, -es, -ing, -er, and -est to base words. These suffixes change number, person, or tense without changing a word's part of speech. Instruction typically includes making learners aware of the sounds suffixes make (e.g., the three pronunciations of past-tense -ed), sorting words by ending sound, and explicitly teaching orthographic rules — the doubling rule (commit → committed), the drop-e rule (hope → hoping), and the change-y-to-i rule (carry → carried). Meta-analytic evidence shows morphological instruction, of which inflectional endings are the entry point, improves literacy outcomes including word reading, spelling, and comprehension Goodwin & Ahn's meta-analysis of morphological interventions. [+S].

Design Implications¶

Explicit, systematic teaching of ending rules outperforms incidental exposure because the rules are arbitrary from the learner's perspective and must be automatized before they can be applied during fluent writing Systematic review of morphological instruction effects. [+M]. Instruction should connect sound (how the ending is pronounced), meaning (what it signals grammatically), and spelling (how it is written) rather than treating it as visual rote memorization.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A sequenced word list progressing from regular patterns to rule-governed exceptions
  • Direct, teacher-led explanation of each rule with worked examples (Direct Instruction)
  • Consistent practice with word decomposition and combination, distributed over time Spaced practice improves retention of spelling patterns. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Less effective when taught as isolated rote spelling lists disconnected from reading and writing use [~M] — transfer to authentic writing drops when rules are practiced only on worksheets
  • Learners with significant phonological or language processing deficits may need smaller steps and more repetitions before rule generalization occurs [~W]
  • Teaching rules as absolute ("drop the e") without contrast cases produces overgeneralization errors (hoping spelled hopping); contrasting correct and incorrect forms reduces this Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying rule. [+M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Sound-first approach: sort words by how -ed sounds (/t/, /d/, /əd/) before teaching spelling
  • Rule-first approach: state the rule, model with worked examples, then fade to independent application
  • Embedded approach: teach endings within authentic writing conferences and shared reading rather than as a separate spelling block

Target Learners¶

  • Students in grades 2–4, when inflectional spelling patterns are typically consolidated [+M]
  • Older struggling spellers and writers who missed systematic phonics-morphology instruction Morphological interventions benefit struggling readers disproportionately. [+M]
  • English language learners, for whom explicit grammatical signaling of tense and number is often not transparent from first-language patterns [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Spelling accuracy of inflected forms in independent writing
  • Grammatical awareness: recognizing tense, number, and comparison markers
  • Word analysis: decomposing and combining words, a foundation for later derivational morphology Morphological awareness supports later vocabulary and comprehension growth. [+W]

Instructions¶

  1. Activate awareness of sounds. Have students sort words by the sound of the ending (e.g., /t/, /d/, /əd/ for -ed) before introducing spelling.
  2. Model with direct instruction. Explicitly state and demonstrate each orthographic rule (doubling, drop-e, y-to-i) using worked examples, following the modeling phase of Cognitive Apprenticeship.
  3. Guide contrastive practice. Use word pairs and triads that isolate one rule at a time (hope/hoping/hopped) so learners abstract the condition under which each rule applies Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying rule. [+M].
  4. Fade support into application. Move from structured worksheets to dictation to authentic writing, prompting students to self-explain which rule applies and why Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].
  5. Distribute review. Revisit previously taught endings in short spaced sessions rather than massed blocks Spaced practice improves retention of spelling patterns. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Phonics instruction — inflectional endings extend phonics into the morphological layer; both depend on systematic, explicit sequencing
  • Word sorting — the primary practice format for contrasting ending patterns
  • Use a morphograph-based rule-teaching routine: introduce the rule, apply it in whole-group instruction, then assign independent worksheet practice

Examples¶

  • A third-grade teacher decomposes words on the board (hoping = hope + ing), then guides students to combine base words and endings (commit + ed = committed) to illustrate the doubling rule, before students hunt for examples in their own reading.
  • Words Their Way — Published word-study curriculum built around developmental sorting of inflectional ending patterns.
  • REWARDS — Intervention program for intermediate and secondary students that explicitly teaches inflectional and derivational endings to support multisyllabic word reading.
