SEL Discussions through Literary Characters¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Students read and discuss social and emotional learning (SEL) topics through the lens of characters in literature. Teachers select texts with strong characters who face conflicts, make decisions, and change over the course of a narrative; discussions then analyze character relationships, motivations, and resolutions. Because emotional claims are made about fictional third parties rather than about students themselves, the strategy creates a psychologically safe distance for exploring sensitive social and emotional territory while still building transferable strategies for students' own lives.

Design Implications¶

Literature functions as a Case Study of human social behavior: characters provide concrete, narratively rich instances from which students abstract principles about emotion, perspective-taking, and conflict resolution. Fiction reading has been associated with improved empathy and social cognition Fiction exposure correlates with empathy and theory-of-mind performance. [+W], and structured SEL programs embedded in academic instruction improve both social-emotional competencies and academic achievement SEL programs improve behavior, attitudes, and academic performance. [+S]. The discussion structure matters as much as the text: prompts must push beyond plot recall toward reasoning about why characters act as they do, and toward connecting character dilemmas to students' own experience.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Texts with psychologically complex characters who confront recognizable social-emotional dilemmas (conflict, exclusion, moral choice, change)
  • Structured discussion protocols with verbal prompts, clarifying questions, and confirmations that explicitly teach talking and listening skills
  • A facilitator who manages turn-taking, ensures respectful communication, and prevents dominant voices from crowding out others
  • A follow-on activity that asks students to apply the insight to their own lives (Reflection or Individual Response)

Constraints¶

  • Poorly chosen texts can trigger or alienate students; text selection must anticipate the cultural and emotional backgrounds in the room [-M]
  • Discussion quality collapses without facilitation: unstructured conversation drifts to plot summary and dominant students monopolize airtime [-M]
  • Students with weak reading comprehension may be unable to access the character analysis at all, so the SEL goal is blocked by the literacy demand [~M]
  • Forced or formulaic personal connections ("when have you ever felt like this character?") can feel intrusive and reduce willingness to share [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class discussion, small-group Class Discussion protocols, or written Individual Response for students reluctant to speak publicly
  • Text choice ranges from picture books (e.g., The Librarian of Basra) to young adult novels (e.g., The Giver), scaled to reading level and maturity
  • Role-play and Acting/Role-Play extensions let students enact alternative resolutions to character conflicts
  • Can be a standalone SEL block or woven into existing Assigned Readings in ELA instruction

Target Learners¶

  • Students in grades 4–12; adaptable downward with picture books and upward with adult literature
  • Students who benefit from emotional distance before discussing personal experience — the fictional frame lowers social risk [~M]
  • Less effective for students whose decoding or comprehension skills cannot support character analysis without additional scaffolding [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' emotions and motivations Building Empathy
  • Social problem-solving: analyzing how characters resolve conflict and transferring strategies to real situations
  • Communication skills: structured turn-taking, active listening, clarifying questions, and confirmations
  • Literary comprehension: character analysis, plot structure, and theme identification as authentic academic byproducts

Instructions¶

  1. Select a text with a central character facing a social-emotional dilemma relevant to students' developmental stage; preview for potential triggers.
  2. Assign the reading (Assigned Readings) with an advance organizer naming the SEL focus (e.g., "watch how the character handles being excluded").
  3. Open discussion with conceptual questions about character motivation and decision points (Conceptual Questioning or Case Study framing: "What were the character's options at this moment?").
  4. Teach and enforce discussion skills explicitly — verbal prompts, clarifying questions, confirmations — so talking and listening are themselves learning targets.
  5. Bridge to personal application through optional Individual Response or Reflection, letting students choose whether and how to connect the character's experience to their own.
  6. Close with Whole-Class Sharing of strategies generated, and revisit the same characters in later discussions to track how conflict and resolution changed them.

Related Strategies¶

  • Acting/Role-Play — enacting character dilemmas deepens perspective-taking beyond discussion
  • Case-Based Learning — literature here functions as narrative case material for reasoning about human problems

Examples¶

  • Using Lois Lowry's The Giver to discuss community, conformity, and responsibility; exploring conflict resolution in From the Mixed-Up Files of Mrs. Basil E. Frankweiler.
  • The Morningside Center for Teaching Social Responsibility uses The Librarian of Basra in structured lessons connecting character choices to students' own lives and real-world issues.
  • CASEL publishes guidance on integrating SEL into academic content, including literature-based discussion approaches.
