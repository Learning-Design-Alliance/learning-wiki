Brain-Based Learning Through Movement¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Brain-based learning through movement integrates purposeful physical activity — movement breaks, kinesthetic tasks, gesture, and peer teaching that gets students out of their seats — into the flow of instruction. The rationale is that movement increases arousal and blood flow, engages multiple sensory and motor systems during encoding, and provides attentional reset between demanding cognitive segments. It is carried out through structured routines (e.g., short activity breaks between instructional segments, acting out content, walking peer-discussion pairings) rather than unstructured "free movement."

Design Implications¶

Movement supports cognition primarily through improved attention and executive function, especially in children and after acute bouts of activity Acute exercise has small positive effects on executive attention. [+M]. However, the learning benefit depends on the movement being connected to the content or carefully timed as a break; arbitrary movement layered on top of instruction can compete for working memory rather than support it [~M]. Movement works best as a complement to, not a substitute for, well-structured instruction and Practice.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A specific instructional purpose for each movement segment: content encoding (acting out, gesture), consolidation (break), or social processing (walking discussion)
  • Classroom space and routines that make transitions fast; movement breaks lose their value if setup costs more time than they return
  • Alignment with the curriculum — movement activities should rehearse or reset for target content, not merely occupy students

Constraints¶

  • Movement tasks that require learners to simultaneously hold and manipulate novel content while performing motor demands can overload working memory, particularly for novices [~M] — the same Cognitive Load Management limits that govern any activity apply
  • Unstructured movement framed as "brain-based" without instructional purpose consumes instructional time with no measurable learning gain [-W]
  • Some students find public movement activities socially uncomfortable or have motor disabilities; participation must be adaptable [-W]
  • Effects of acute exercise on subsequent academic performance are small and inconsistent across studies; movement is not a lever for large achievement gains on its own [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Content-embedded movement: acting out processes, gesturing concepts, human graphing — movement as encoding support
  • Movement breaks: 2–5 minute activity intervals between demanding segments, as attentional reset
  • Locomotor discussion: walking pair-shares or gallery walks combining movement with Class Discussion
  • Program-embedded activity: platforms such as GoNoodle provide short structured movement videos designed for classroom transitions

Target Learners¶

  • Children and adolescents, who show the most consistent attentional benefits from acute activity [~M]
  • Young or novice learners who benefit from multi-sensory, embodied encoding of concrete concepts [+W]
  • Students with attention difficulties, for whom scheduled movement can function as a self-regulation support [+W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Attention and engagement maintenance across longer instructional blocks
  • Embodied encoding of concrete, spatial, or procedural concepts
  • Consolidation and social processing of recently introduced content

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce new content through concise Direct Instruction or demonstration, keeping the segment short enough to hold attention.
  2. Design a movement task that rehearses the content — students Act out a process, gesture a concept, or physically sequence steps.
  3. Have students process the content socially while moving: walking pair-shares or small-group Collaboration in which students teach each other.
  4. Return to a brief consolidation activity — Practice items or a quick check — while arousal is still elevated.
  5. Schedule short movement breaks between demanding segments rather than during them; keep transitions under a minute.

Related Strategies¶

  • Act It Out — the content-embedded variant: dramatizing concepts as an encoding strategy
  • Active Learning — movement is one channel for the active engagement this principle requires
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — movement warm-ups can double as activation activities

Examples¶

  • GoNoodle — free classroom movement-break videos used as structured transitions between instructional segments in K–5 classrooms.
  • Walk-and-talk peer teaching — after a mini-lesson, pairs walk a loop while alternately explaining the content to each other, combining movement with retrieval practice.
  • Human graphing / kinesthetic sequencing — students physically arrange themselves to represent a timeline, food web, or ordered algorithm, making structure spatially and bodily salient.
