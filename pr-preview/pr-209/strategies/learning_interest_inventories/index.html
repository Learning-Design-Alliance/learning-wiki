Learning Interest Inventories¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 8 studies (5 review, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 8 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Learning interest inventories are structured surveys, questionnaires, or interview protocols that surface what learners care about, how they prefer to engage with content, and what conditions help them find joy in learning. Instructors use the results to select examples, design tasks, and offer choices that connect curriculum to individual interests. The inventory is a diagnostic tool, not an intervention itself — its value comes entirely from how the information is acted on in task design.

Design Implications¶

Interest inventories operationalize the link between personal relevance and motivation: connecting tasks to learners' existing interests raises task value, which in turn increases engagement and persistence Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]. Relevance also directs attention — learners attend more closely to material framed around their interests Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M]. The strongest documented effects come not from the inventory itself but from what follows it: giving learners meaningful choices and relevance-oriented tasks Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A developmentally appropriate inventory tool (survey, interview, drawing-and-label task for young learners)
  • Time to administer and time to analyze results before planning
  • Genuine willingness and curricular flexibility to incorporate findings into examples, project topics, and demonstration formats
  • A follow-up mechanism (e.g., periodic Check-Ins) to update interests, which shift over time

Constraints¶

  • An inventory that is administered but not acted on can feel performative and may reduce trust; the motivational benefit depends on learners seeing their input reflected in instruction Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [-M]
  • Superficial personalization (swapping surface examples while keeping tasks identical) produces weak and short-lived effects; deep integration into task structure is required for measurable gains [+M]
  • Interest inventories are not learning-style instruments — matching instruction to self-reported "learning styles" does not improve outcomes and should not be the purpose of the inventory [X]
  • Over-reliance can narrow exposure: always routing learners to existing interests limits development of new ones; interest is partly developed through instruction, not merely discovered [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class surveys at the start of a term to inform example and topic selection
  • Project-based menus where inventory results feed into Choice Boards or negotiated project topics
  • Reading interest inventories (e.g., the Elementary Reading Attitude Survey) to match texts to readers
  • Individual conferences replacing written surveys for younger learners or low-literacy contexts
  • Digital dashboards (e.g., adaptive platforms) that continuously capture interest signals rather than one-time snapshots

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and secondary learners, where interest–engagement links are strongest and interests are still forming [+M]
  • Disengaged or low-achieving students, for whom relevance interventions show the largest motivational gains — a written relevance exercise tied to personal interests improved course value and grades for students with low success expectancies Relevance interventions improve interest and performance for low-expectancy students. [+M]
  • Less critical for advanced learners already pursuing self-defined goals, where interest is typically well established [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Motivational goals: increasing task value, engagement, and persistence Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
  • Affective goals: building positive attitudes toward a domain and willingness to attempt challenging work Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Metacognitive goals: helping learners articulate what they enjoy and why, supporting self-knowledge and learner agency

Instructions¶

  1. Administer the inventory early in the term using a short, low-stakes survey or interview; keep questions concrete (favorite activities, topics, media, ways of showing what they know)
  2. Analyze and cluster results to identify shared interests and useful individual differences; note interests that can anchor examples versus those that can anchor project topics
  3. Redesign tasks with choice: build menus, project options, and demonstration formats that draw on inventoried interests — choice itself is the active ingredient Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  4. Connect content to interests explicitly during instruction ("we're using your survey data to pick these examples"), so learners see the connection rather than inferring it
  5. Revisit periodically through Check-Ins or brief re-surveys, since interests develop and change; treat the inventory as a living record, not a one-time snapshot
  6. Assess against interests by allowing multiple demonstration formats (writing, presentation, build, performance) so learners can show competence through preferred channels Assessment

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — interests are a form of prior knowledge; inventories surface material that later serves as an activation hook
  • Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting) — the structural mechanism through which inventoried interests become learner decisions
  • Action Planning — learners translate interests into goals and steps

Examples¶

  • CORI (Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction) — Guthrie and colleagues' long-running program uses interest surveys and self-selection of texts within thematic units; studies show increased reading engagement and comprehension growth [+M]
  • Elementary Reading Attitude Survey (ERAS) — a widely used pictorial inventory (the "Garfield survey") measuring reading attitudes in primary grades, used to match students with texts
  • Relevance writing intervention — Hulleman & Harackiewicz had high school science students write briefly about how course material related to their own lives and interests, improving interest and grades for low-expectancy students [+M]
  • Project menus in PBL classrooms — teachers use beginning-of-term interest surveys to build choice boards where every option hits the same learning targets but through different interest-linked topics
