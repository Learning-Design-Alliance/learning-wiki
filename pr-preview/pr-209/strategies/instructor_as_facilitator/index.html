Instructor as Facilitator¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The instructor shifts from being the primary source of content and authority to a role of guiding, questioning, and structuring learner activity. Concretely, this means eliciting student ideas before supplying answers, letting students select materials and topics, and handing discussion leadership to learners. The goal is not abdication — the facilitator still designs tasks, monitors understanding, and intervenes when needed — but a deliberate redistribution of control that supports autonomy and engagement.

Design Implications¶

Facilitation works because it supports learners' basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence, which in turn raises intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. It is most effective when the instructor still provides structure: unguided or minimally guided discovery consistently underperforms guided instruction, especially for novices, so facilitation must be paired with scaffolds, checks, and timely feedback Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. Facilitation is a stance enacted through specific moves — open questions, wait time, redirecting student questions to peers — rather than a passive presence.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Willingness to relinquish some control over pacing, topic selection, and airtime
  • Tasks rich enough to sustain student-led work (problems, cases, texts students bring in)
  • Mechanisms for surfacing student interests (questionnaires, interest inventories, check-ins)
  • Ongoing monitoring so facilitation does not drift into unguided discovery

Constraints¶

  • Pure facilitation with minimal guidance harms novices, who lack the schemas to direct their own inquiry [-S] — the facilitator role presupposes learners have enough prior knowledge to productively steer
  • Fails when students arrive unprepared; student-led discussion without accountability structures collapses into shallow exchange [~M]
  • Cultural and institutional expectations of instructor authority can make facilitation read as abdication, reducing perceived competence and trust [~W]
  • Large-enrollment settings constrain the individual attention facilitation depends on

Implementation Variability¶

  • Spectrum of control: from instructor-led with elicitation, to co-constructed discussion, to fully student-led seminars and presentations
  • Interest questionnaires can drive topic selection, reading choice, or project framing
  • Students can bring in and present their own culturally relevant reading material, positioning them as experts
  • Rotating facilitation roles (discussion leader, summarizer, questioner) distributes the facilitator function among students

Target Learners¶

  • Adult learners and advanced students, who have the prior knowledge and self-regulation to steer their own inquiry [~M]
  • Disengaged or low-autonomy learners, for whom choice and voice can rebuild motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Novices need facilitation with heavy structure; pure facilitation underserves them [-S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Intrinsic motivation, ownership, and persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Discussion, argumentation, and communication skills through student-led exchange
  • Culturally relevant learning: students selecting and presenting material tied to their own communities and identities
  • Metacognition and self-regulation, as learners take responsibility for directing inquiry

Instructions¶

  1. Survey student interests and backgrounds at the start of the course (Check-Ins); use results to shape topic and material choices.
  2. Design a rich task or case that students can productively wrestle with before instruction (Case-Based Learning).
  3. Elicit student ideas and questions rather than presenting answers first (Class Discussion); redirect student questions back to the group before answering.
  4. Coach rather than tell: probe reasoning, prompt self-explanation, and offer hints at the process level (Coaching).
  5. Hand over leadership — students present their own readings or solutions, with peers responding (Collaboration).
  6. Close each cycle with synthesis and corrective feedback, ensuring misconceptions surfaced during student-led work are addressed.

Related Strategies¶

  • Flipped Classroom — frees class time for the facilitation-driven activity that defines this stance
  • Discussion-Based Learning — the primary vehicle through which facilitation is enacted
  • Collaborative Learning — peer structures that let the instructor step back without leaving learners unsupported
  • Tutor–student co-enactment: distribute or co-operate the control devices, negotiate leadership silently, and progressively hand over agency until the student solo-enacts

Examples¶

  • Learner-centered teaching (Weimer, 2013) — a framework in which the instructor progressively transfers responsibility for content, assessment, and course policies to students across a semester.
  • Problem-based learning tutorials — the tutor asks probing questions and monitors group process rather than lecturing; students set the learning agenda for each case.
  • Student-selected reading seminars — students bring in texts from their own cultural communities, present them, and lead peer discussion, with the instructor facilitating connections to course concepts.
