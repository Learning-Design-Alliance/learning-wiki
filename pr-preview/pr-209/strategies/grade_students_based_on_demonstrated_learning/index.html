Grade Students Based on Demonstrated Learning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Instead of averaging scores over time, this strategy grades students on their ultimate level of demonstrated mastery at the end of a unit. Early low scores function as diagnostic information rather than permanent penalties, and students may reassess or revise work until they can demonstrate the target competency. This mitigates rewarding students who begin with higher prior mastery and encourages perseverance.

Design Implications¶

Grading on final demonstrated mastery aligns evaluation with the goal of learning rather than the ranking of learners, converting grades from summative verdicts into feedback loops Formative assessment and feedback improve achievement. [+S]. It is the grading counterpart of Mastery Learning: time varies, the standard does not [~S]. Averaging early failures with later successes systematically underestimates students who start behind and removes the incentive to keep trying after a poor start.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear, explicit learning targets so "mastery" is a defined standard, not an impression (Assessment aligned to criteria)
  • Multiple or reassessment opportunities that capture eventual mastery (Assess Performance)
  • A gradebook policy that records most-recent evidence rather than cumulative averages
  • Feedback that tells students what to fix before reassessment (Assessment for Learning)

Constraints¶

  • Averaging is sometimes appropriate: when performance is cumulative (e.g., a final performance under exam conditions) or when reliability of a single late demonstration is low, most-recent-evidence grading can overstate stable ability [~M]
  • Without reassessment deadlines or effort requirements, some students procrastinate and learning is distributed poorly [~M]
  • Requires significant restructuring of curriculum, gradebooks, and reporting; transcript conventions (single letter grades) can obscure the mastery profile
  • Can be perceived as unfair by students who succeeded on the first attempt unless reassessment is paired with additional practice requirements

Implementation Variability¶

  • Standards-based report cards: replace letter grades with per-standard proficiency levels (widely used in K-12 districts)
  • Reassessment policies: students may retake assessments after remediation; some schools cap the replacement grade or require evidence of further study first
  • Revision-based grading: writing and projects may be revised for full credit (as in portfolio assessment)
  • Hybrid models: a portion of the grade reflects growth or process, the rest final mastery

Target Learners¶

  • Students who begin a unit with lower prior knowledge, who are otherwise penalized by averaging Formative assessment and feedback improve achievement. [+M]
  • High-achieving students who coast after early success — mastery grading requires them to demonstrate the standard at the end, not merely early
  • Less suited to selective contexts where the purpose of grading is normative ranking rather than certification of competence

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Well-structured knowledge and skills with definable mastery criteria (procedures, concepts, competencies)
  • Long-term retention and perseverance: the expectation of eventual demonstration supports continued effort after initial failure [~M]
  • Less applicable to purely exploratory or creative goals where "mastery" cannot be specified in advance

Instructions¶

  1. Define the target competencies and the evidence that demonstrates each (Assessment).
  2. Assess early and formatively; treat initial scores as diagnostics, not grades (Assess Performance).
  3. Provide specific, actionable feedback and a route to close the gap (Assessment for Learning).
  4. Allow reassessment or revision after remediation, requiring evidence of further practice first.
  5. Report the most recent or highest-quality demonstration of each standard as the grade of record.

Related Strategies¶

  • Competency-Based Assessment — the broader system in which progression depends on demonstrated competency rather than seat time
  • Mastery Learning — the instructional model this grading policy makes coherent

Examples¶

  • Standards-based grading in K-12 districts (e.g., the model described in Guskey's work on mastery grading): report cards list per-standard proficiency levels, and reassessment after remediation replaces early scores.
  • Bloom's "Learning for Mastery" (University of Chicago, 1968): units with formative checks, corrective instruction, and a summative assessment that students may retake until mastery is demonstrated.
  • Competency-based higher education programs such as Western Governors University — students progress by passing competency assessments whenever they are ready, not by accumulating averaged course points.
