Reflection After Writing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Reflection After Writing is a structured pause following a writing assignment in which students examine how the work went: what blocked them, which strategies helped, and what they will change next time. It is typically prompted by specific questions (e.g., "What was hardest about drafting this?" "Which revision strategy did you use, and did it work?") and may be written, discussed, or conferenced. The strategy converts a completed task into a source of strategic self-knowledge rather than treating submission as the endpoint.

Design Implications¶

Reflection after writing is a form of self-assessment that develops the monitoring and planning phases of self-regulated learning Self-regulated learners who monitor and adjust their strategies outperform those who do not. [+M]. Its value depends on structure: open-ended "reflect on your writing" prompts produce vague responses, while prompts that require naming a specific strategy and evaluating its effect produce usable strategic insight [~M]. Reflection works best when it follows actionable feedback and feeds forward into the next writing task, rather than functioning as a post-hoc ritual.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A completed writing assignment the learner has invested effort in
  • Specific reflection prompts targeting strategies, obstacles, and next steps — not generic reactions
  • A mechanism for the reflection to influence future work (e.g., a strategy list carried into the next draft or assignment)
  • Facilitator modeling of what productive reflection sounds like (Articulation)

Constraints¶

  • Novice writers often lack the vocabulary to describe their own processes; without modeled examples, reflections stay superficial [-W]
  • Reflection without a follow-up application opportunity decays into compliance writing with no effect on subsequent drafts [-M]
  • Students with weak metacognitive skills — often the students who most need reflection — find unstructured reflection hardest and may disengage [~W]
  • If reflections are graded on apparent depth rather than honesty, students write what the instructor wants to hear, corrupting the self-monitoring data [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written memos attached to the submitted draft (most common; creates a record for comparison across assignments)
  • Brief teacher–student writing conferences using the reflection as an agenda
  • Portfolio cover letters that synthesize reflections across multiple assignments over a term
  • Oral or audio reflections for students who struggle to write about writing

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers who default to a single drafting strategy and need to build a repertoire [+M]
  • Students with learning and thinking differences, who benefit from explicit strategy awareness and externalized self-monitoring [~M]
  • Less useful for advanced writers who already self-monitor effectively, for whom mandatory reflection can feel redundant [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Strategic writing knowledge: knowing which planning, drafting, and revision strategies work for whom and when
  • Self-regulation: strengthening the self-monitoring and self-evaluation phases of self-regulated learning
  • Transfer: carrying revision strategies from one genre or assignment to the next
  • Metacognitive language: building the vocabulary to describe one's own composing process

Instructions¶

  1. Complete and submit the writing assignment (or a full draft) as usual.
  2. Distribute 2–4 targeted prompts, e.g., "Describe one point where you got stuck and what you did," "Name one revision strategy you tried — did it improve the text?" (Assess Performance)
  3. Model a brief think-aloud reflection on your own or a sample piece of writing so students see what specific, honest reflection looks like (Articulation).
  4. Have students write or discuss their reflections before receiving your evaluation, so the reflection records their own judgment rather than parroting yours.
  5. Deliver feedback and ask students to compare it with their self-assessment, noting agreements and surprises.
  6. Require each student to extract one concrete strategy commitment to apply on the next assignment, and open the next cycle by revisiting it.

Related Strategies¶

  • Revision — reflection identifies which revision strategies to try next
  • Portfolio Assessment — portfolios aggregate reflections over time, making growth visible
  • Writing Conferences — the reflection memo supplies the conference agenda
  • Use a Letter to the Reviewer reflective memo submitted with each draft

Examples¶

  • Process portfolios in secondary English — students attach a one-paragraph reflection to each draft in a working portfolio, then write a cover letter at term's end synthesizing what they learned about their own writing process.
  • First-year composition (e.g., "Writer's memos" common in FYC curricula) — a short memo accompanying each essay draft naming the writer's choices, difficulties, and requested feedback focus.
  • Special education writing instruction — structured self-questioning checklists ("What was hard? What helped? What will I try next time?") used after each writing session to build strategy awareness in students with dysgraphia or ADHD.
