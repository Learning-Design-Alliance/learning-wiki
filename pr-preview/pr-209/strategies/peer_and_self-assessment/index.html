Peer and Self-Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer and self-assessment ask students to evaluate their own or classmates' work against explicit criteria, producing feedback and — more importantly — developing the capacity to judge quality. The act of applying criteria to someone else's work builds the internal standards students then apply to their own drafts. This positions assessment as a learning activity rather than only a measurement event, enacting Assessment for Learning.

Design Implications¶

Peer and self-assessment work because evaluating work against criteria requires the same domain knowledge as producing good work; the evaluation task is itself practice in quality judgment Formative feedback and classroom assessment practices produce substantial learning gains. [+S]. Rubrics and exemplars are the critical supports: transparent criteria improve the validity of student judgments and reduce anxiety about the assessment process Rubrics support consistent judgment and reduce assessment anxiety. [+M]. Peer marks correlate reasonably well with instructor marks when the task is well-structured and criteria are explicit, though agreement weakens for complex, open-ended work Peer and teacher marks agree more closely with explicit criteria and simpler tasks. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria: a rubric or checklist students can actually apply, not just read (Assessment)
  • Exemplars of work at varying quality levels so students can calibrate their judgments before applying criteria to live work
  • Training or calibration activity — students practice judging a sample and compare their judgment with the instructor's or with peers'
  • A structure for delivering feedback that is actionable rather than merely evaluative (Coaching)

Constraints¶

  • Untrained students default to leniency, severity, or halo effects; without calibration, peer marks diverge substantially from expert marks Peer assessment reliability depends on training and judgment dimensions. [~M]
  • Students often rate their own work against effort rather than quality, inflating self-marks for weak submissions [~M]
  • When peer assessment carries high stakes, students game the system (reciprocal inflated grading) or experience anxiety that undermines the formative benefit [-M]
  • Feedback that only assigns a score produces little learning; feedback must address the task and process, not the self Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Less effective when students lack the domain knowledge to recognize quality — novices cannot judge what they cannot yet perceive [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Formative-only: peer feedback informs revision; instructor retains grading authority — lowest risk, most common entry point
  • Graded peer assessment: peer marks contribute to grades, typically moderated or averaged across multiple reviewers to dilute individual bias
  • Self-assessment with rubrics: students score their own drafts before submission, predicting instructor marks; accuracy itself becomes a graded metacognitive skill
  • Two-stage peer review: students assess anonymous drafts, revise, then resubmit — closing the feedback loop

Target Learners¶

  • Higher education and secondary students with enough domain exposure to recognize quality dimensions Peer assessment is feasible and beneficial across college and university settings. [+M]
  • Students developing self-regulation: judging their own work against criteria strengthens monitoring and revision habits Self-assessment supports self-regulated learning. [+M]
  • Students with low self-efficacy may initially underrate their work; calibration against exemplars helps correct this Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Evaluative judgment: knowing what quality looks like in a domain, not just how to produce it
  • Self-regulation: monitoring, revising, and taking responsibility for work quality
  • Domain knowledge deepening: applying criteria requires articulating why work succeeds or fails
  • Feedback literacy: giving, receiving, and acting on critique

Instructions¶

  1. Share the rubric and 2–3 anonymized exemplars at different quality levels before students begin their own work (Assessment).
  2. Run a calibration exercise: students judge an exemplar, then compare their scores and reasoning with the instructor's judgment in a whole-class debrief (Class Discussion).
  3. Have students exchange drafts anonymously and apply the rubric, writing comments that identify a specific strength and a specific improvement (Coaching).
  4. Ask students to self-assess their own draft against the same rubric before receiving peer feedback.
  5. Build in revision time so feedback is actually used — assessment without the opportunity to act on it is wasted Formative assessment requires follow-up action to affect learning. [+S].
  6. Compare peer and self-marks with instructor marks over time and discuss discrepancies to sharpen judgment.

Related Strategies¶

  • Rubric-based feedback — the criteria infrastructure that makes peer and self-judgment reliable
  • Portfolio assessment — a context where self-assessment is naturally embedded through reflection on collected work
  • Two-stage exams — a peer-assessment variant applied to testing itself

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — a tool in which students first calibrate their judgment against instructor-graded exemplars, then review peers' anonymous submissions; calibration accuracy gates whether a student's reviews count.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — algorithmically distribute submissions so each student reviews several peers, aggregating reviews to moderate individual bias.
  • Self-assessed draft submission — students attach a completed rubric with their essay predicting their grade; the instructor's mark and the student's mark are compared, and prediction accuracy is discussed in conference (Check-In).
