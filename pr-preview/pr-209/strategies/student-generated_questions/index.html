Student-Generated Questions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Student-generated questions place learners in the role of question-askers rather than question-answerers: students formulate the questions that drive a project, investigation, or discussion, then pursue answers through research, experimentation, or dialogue. The strategy converts curiosity into an explicit artifact — a question — that structures subsequent inquiry and gives students ownership over the direction of learning.

Design Implications¶

Question generation is itself a cognitive act: formulating a question requires identifying gaps in one's own knowledge, which supports comprehension monitoring and deeper processing Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. Student questioning also functions as a form of cognitive activation — prompting learners to reason rather than receive — which is associated with stronger achievement outcomes Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. However, the quality of student questions varies widely with prior knowledge and instruction; without scaffolds such as question stems or the Question Formulation Technique, questions tend to be shallow, closed, or answerable by recall [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit instruction in question formulation — question stems, taxonomy practice, or protocols like the Question Formulation Technique — before students are expected to generate productive questions
  • Access to resources (texts, data, experts, labs) that can actually support investigation of student questions
  • Instructor facilitation that helps students refine, prioritize, and connect questions rather than treating all questions as equally investigable
  • A classroom culture where student questions are treated as legitimate drivers of activity, not interruptions

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured question generation often yields low-level recall questions; without modeling and criteria for "good" questions, the strategy can consume time without deepening learning [-M]
  • Students with low prior knowledge generate fewer and less generative questions, so the strategy can widen gaps if used without scaffolding [~M]
  • Divergent student questions can fragment a course; instructors need mechanisms (voting, clustering, curriculum mapping) to converge on investigable questions without extinguishing ownership
  • Assessment systems that reward predetermined answers can undermine the strategy — students learn quickly that their questions are not what counts [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class question drives: students generate and vote on questions that anchor a unit (project-based learning, progressive inquiry)
  • Structured protocols: the Question Formulation Technique (Rothstein & Santana, 2011) — produce, refine, prioritize, plan use
  • Reciprocal peer questioning: students generate questions for each other before discussion, with question stems provided (King, 1994)
  • Knowledge-building communities: students continuously post and improve questions in a shared database (e.g., Knowledge Forum) over extended inquiry
  • Entry-point questioning: students generate questions at the start of a text or case, then revise them as understanding develops

Target Learners¶

  • Most effective for learners with moderate prior knowledge, who know enough to identify genuine gaps but still have questions worth asking [~M]
  • Students who experience low motivation in teacher-directed formats; ownership of the question increases investment and persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Novices need question scaffolds (stems, exemplars) before generating questions independently; unscaffolded generation favors students who already possess academic questioning habits [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Inquiry and research skills: framing investigable questions, planning evidence-gathering
  • Conceptual understanding: question generation forces learners to articulate what they do and do not understand Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Metacognition and self-regulated learning: monitoring one's own knowledge gaps
  • Epistemic dispositions: treating knowledge as something built and contested rather than delivered

Instructions¶

  1. Stimulate curiosity with an artifact, phenomenon, or Case Studies that creates a genuine knowledge gap (Activation)
  2. Generate questions individually or in small groups using question stems or a protocol; defer evaluation during generation
  3. Refine and prioritize — students improve questions for clarity and investigability, then select focus questions (class voting or clustering via Class Discussion)
  4. Investigate through research, experimentation, or Coaching-supported inquiry, revisiting and revising questions as evidence accumulates
  5. Share and assess findings against the original questions; evaluate both the answers and the quality of the questions themselves (Assessment for learning, not just of it)

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-based learning — cases provide the authentic context from which student questions naturally arise
  • Collaborative inquiry — student questions drive the shared investigative agenda
  • Flipped classroom — students bring questions from pre-work to class time, making them the agenda for face-to-face sessions

Examples¶

  • Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute) — a structured protocol used across K–12 and higher education in which students produce, improve, and prioritize questions about a "QFocus" prompt before a unit or text.
  • Knowledge Building / Knowledge Forum (IKIT) — Scardamalia and Bereiter's knowledge-building communities, where students' questions are recorded, improved, and built upon in a shared database over months; research in these environments shows progressive deepening of explanatory questions (Hakkarainen, 2003).
  • Reciprocal questioning in college biology — King's (1994) guided peer-questioning procedure, in which students use question stems to quiz each other before discussion, improved higher-order discussion and achievement relative to lecture.
  • Project-based science curricula such as EL Education — units are organized around student-developed "guiding questions" that culminate in authentic products for real audiences.
