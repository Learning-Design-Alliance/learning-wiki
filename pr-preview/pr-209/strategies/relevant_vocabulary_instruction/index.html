Relevant Vocabulary Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 6 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Instructors select and teach vocabulary that is directly useful or necessary for learners' goals, rather than working through arbitrary word lists. Selection can be learner-driven (polling learners on topics they want to discuss and compiling the most common suggestions) or goal-driven (targeting high-frequency or discipline-critical terms). After a new word is introduced, learners must use it productively — in conversation, role play, or original sentences — because relevance plus active use drives retention.

Design Implications¶

Vocabulary instruction works best when the words taught are ones learners will actually encounter and need: relevant, high-utility words are encountered more often after instruction, and repeated meaningful encounters are what consolidate word knowledge [~S]. Teaching words as tools for expressing ideas — with multiple contexts and opportunities for generative use — outperforms definitional instruction alone Stahl & Fairbanks meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction. [+M]. Relevance also serves motivation: learners attend more to words they perceive as immediately useful for their communicative goals.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Pre-planning to identify the most useful vocabulary for the group's goals (frequency lists, learner surveys, or analysis of upcoming texts/tasks)
  • Rich initial explanation: definition, example contexts, and related words (Advance Organizers can frame the semantic field)
  • Multiple follow-up encounters and productive use opportunities (Practice, Act It Out, conversation tasks)
  • Access to resources for learners whose needs diverge from the group (e.g., individualized assignments, curated online tools)

Constraints¶

  • Tailoring to diverse needs within one class is time-consuming and can dilute whole-class instruction [-W]
  • Relevance alone is insufficient: words taught once and never revisited show poor retention; spaced re-encounters are required Distributed practice improves retention. [+S]
  • Learner-selected topics may omit words that are actually critical for upcoming texts or assessments [~W]
  • Overloading a lesson with too many new words exceeds working memory capacity and degrades learning of all of them Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class relevance: compile common learner suggestions and teach the consensus set
  • Individualized relevance: assign personalized word sets with homework and online resources (e.g., Quizlet sets, graded readers)
  • Discipline-specific relevance: pre-teach the academic vocabulary of an upcoming text or unit, connecting to Disciplinary Literacy goals
  • Incidental-plus-supported: embed target words in communicative tasks (Act It Out, role play) rather than isolated drill

Target Learners¶

  • Adult English language learners, for whom immediately usable vocabulary has direct life and work payoff
  • Beginning/intermediate language learners who need high-frequency words before low-frequency ones
  • Students entering a new discipline who lack its technical lexicon Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Vocabulary breadth and depth in a second or academic language
  • Reading comprehension of texts containing the target words
  • Productive language use: speaking and writing with newly acquired terms

Instructions¶

  1. Survey learners or analyze upcoming materials to identify the highest-utility words for the group's goals.
  2. Select a small set per lesson (research suggests roughly 5–10 new items for deep treatment) and present each with definition, example contexts, and connections to known words.
  3. Have learners use each word productively — original sentences, conversation, or Act It Out role play.
  4. Schedule spaced re-encounters through review activities and deliberate reuse in later lessons Distributed practice improves retention. [+S].
  5. For learners with needs outside the group consensus, assign individualized word sets with tailored resources and check progress at Check-Ins.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — linking new words to known concepts strengthens encoding
  • Acting-Role-Play — a productive-use format that makes new vocabulary communicatively necessary

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — productive use is the consolidation step; presentation alone rarely sticks
  • Act It Out — dramatized use of target words in context
  • Advance Organizers — frames the semantic field before new words are introduced
  • Application — transferring new vocabulary to authentic tasks

Examples¶

  • Adult ESL community classes — instructor polls learners on life topics (jobs, healthcare, housing), teaches the consensus vocabulary, then has learners role-play a clinic visit using the new terms.
  • Quizlet (https://quizlet.com) — instructors create class word sets for spaced review; individual learners can maintain personalized sets for idiosyncratic needs.
  • Academic pre-teaching — before a science reading, the teacher pre-teaches the discipline-critical terms (mixture, solution, compound) as tools for expressing the chapter's ideas, then requires their use in the lab report.
