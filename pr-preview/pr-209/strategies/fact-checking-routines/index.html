Fact Checking Routines¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (5 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Fact checking routines are explicit, repeatable procedures learners follow to evaluate the accuracy of a claim, source, or piece of media before using or sharing it. Rather than treating evaluation as an occasional lesson, the routine is practiced until it becomes a habitual response to new information. The most researched variant is lateral reading — leaving a source to investigate what independent sources say about it — which professional fact checkers use and novices typically do not.

Design Implications¶

Explicit instruction in evaluation routines produces measurable gains in students' ability to judge online sources Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources. [+S]. The critical design insight is that professional fact checkers behave differently from both novices and even trained historians: they read laterally (off the page, checking the source itself) rather than vertically (scanning the page's own design cues), because surface features like polished design and .org domains are unreliable signals [~S]. Routines must therefore be short enough to execute in real time and practiced across many varied cases, since evaluation skill does not transfer from a single demonstration Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A small set of named, memorable steps (e.g., "stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, trace claims to their origin")
  • Access to open web search during the routine — lateral reading is impossible if learners must stay on the page
  • Repeated practice with authentic, current examples, including sources that look credible but are not
  • A norm that checking is expected before sharing or citing, not an admission of distrust

Constraints¶

  • Teaching learners to judge a source by its own on-page features (About pages, design, domain suffix) does not improve evaluation and can worsen it, because manipulative sites exploit exactly those cues [-S]
  • Routines taught as abstract heuristics without practice on real web content show little transfer [~M]
  • Overhead is real: full verification of every claim is impractical, so routines must include triage — deciding when checking is worth the effort [~M]
  • Checking one's own political beliefs triggers motivated reasoning; accuracy prompts help less for identity-charged content [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • SIFT (Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace to the original) — a four-move minimal routine designed for speed
  • Lateral reading drills — timed exercises where students open new tabs to research the source before reading its content
  • Accuracy nudges — prompts asking learners to rate the accuracy of a headline before judging whether they would share it
  • Full verification projects — extended case studies where students trace a viral claim to its origin

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and postsecondary students, who encounter most information online but rarely receive evaluation instruction Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources. [+S]
  • Novices, who default to vertical reading and design-based judgments; routines replace these unreliable intuitions
  • Adults sharing content on social media — simple accuracy prompts reduce intention to share false headlines [+S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Information literacy: evaluating source credibility and evidence quality
  • Civic reasoning: making informed judgments about public claims before acting on them
  • Metacognitive habits: pausing before accepting or amplifying information

Instructions¶

  1. Model the routine on a live example, narrating each move aloud — open a suspicious-looking source and demonstrate leaving the page to investigate who is behind it (Think-Aloud)
  2. Contrast expert and novice behavior by comparing lateral reading with vertical reading on the same source Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]
  3. Practice with faded support — provide the routine as a checklist first, then remove it and time students as they evaluate new sources (Fading)
  4. Embed in authentic tasks — require the routine before citing any source in research or discussion work (Annotating sources with verification notes)
  5. Debrief failures — examine cases where the routine caught a false claim and cases where it was skipped, connecting misses to the specific move that was omitted (Argumentation about why the evidence did or did not hold up)

Related Strategies¶

  • A Finder's Guide to Facts — a published framework for evaluating sources that pairs naturally with a checking routine
  • Case-Based Learning — real viral-claim cases provide the varied practice the routine requires

Examples¶

  • Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning curriculum (https://cor.stanford.edu) — free lessons and assessments built around lateral reading; classroom field experiments showed large gains in students' source evaluation after roughly 3–6 hours of instruction
  • SIFT / Check, Please! (Mike Caulfield) — a four-move routine taught as a short open course; students practice each move on live examples before combining them
  • NewsGuard browser extension — embeds a routine-like judgment (nutrition labels for news sites) at the point of encounter, scaffolding the "investigate the source" move
