Competency Based Progression¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Competency based progression (CBP) restructures pacing so that advancement depends on demonstrated mastery of explicitly defined competencies — measurable statements of what a learner can do — rather than time in seat, age, or unit completion. Learners move on when they can reliably perform the competency, and receive additional time, support, or alternative pathways when they cannot.

Design Implications¶

CBP operationalizes Mastery Learning: Bloom's argument that most learners can master most objectives given the time and instruction they individually need [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows mastery learning formats produce positive achievement effects, largest when mastery thresholds are high and corrective feedback is built in Mastery learning improves achievement, especially with high mastery standards and corrective feedback. [+M]. Because advancement is criterion-referenced, assessment must be frequent, transparent, and aligned to the competency definitions — CBP collapses the distinction between learning and Assessment into continuous Assessment for Learning Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit, observable competency definitions with performance criteria (rubrics or proficiency scales)
  • Multiple, varied assessment opportunities per competency — a single high-stakes test contradicts the model
  • Systems for tracking individual progress and routing learners to Practice or reteaching when mastery is not yet demonstrated
  • Flexible time structures: schedules, grading, and credit rules that permit variable pacing

Constraints¶

  • Group-based instruction and fixed schedules undermine CBP; without genuinely flexible pacing, "competency-based" becomes relabeling of traditional grading [-M]
  • Low or loosely enforced mastery thresholds produce shallow advancement — learners pass with fragile knowledge and accumulate gaps Mastery learning effects shrink when mastery standards are weak. [~M]
  • Advanced learners can stall in structured environments if "mastery" is defined as completing the same work rather than deeper performance; CBP requires extension pathways, not just remediation [~M]
  • Assessment burden is high: designing, scoring, and tracking competency evidence at scale strains teachers and platforms [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Fully personalized platforms (e.g., Khan Academy mastery tracks, Summit Public Schools' Basecamp/PLP) automate pacing and item routing
  • Hybrid classroom models retain whole-class anchor activities while individualizing advancement on core competencies
  • Higher education programs (e.g., Western Governors University, Purdue's degree-competency pilots) replace credit hours with direct competency assessment
  • Standards-based grading is a partial implementation: reporting by competency without changing pacing

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling learners who need more time than uniform pacing allows — the group for whom mastery learning shows the largest gains Mastery learning improves achievement, especially with high mastery standards and corrective feedback. [+M]
  • Adult and returning learners with variable prior knowledge, who can accelerate through familiar material
  • Learners motivated by visible, attainable progress markers; autonomy over pacing supports intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • Less suited, without strong self-regulation support, to learners who disengage when given pacing control Self-regulated learning must be explicitly taught [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural and foundational knowledge where later learning depends on prior mastery (mathematics, language, technical skills)
  • Cumulative skill sequences with clear prerequisite structure
  • Less appropriate for goals that are inherently developmental or discursive (e.g., aesthetic judgment, discussion-based reasoning), where "mastery" criteria are hard to specify [~W]

Instructions¶

  1. Define competencies — decompose the domain into observable, assessable competencies with proficiency criteria; sequence them by prerequisite structure (Advance Organizers can make the map visible to learners)
  2. Set a mastery threshold — decide what counts as "demonstrated" (e.g., consistent performance across two tasks, not one)
  3. Diagnose on entry — place learners by current competency, not by grade or cohort (Activation of prior knowledge doubles as placement)
  4. Provide instruction and practice — pair initial instruction with Practice and immediate, corrective Feedback
  5. Assess on demand — let learners attempt competency demonstrations when ready, with retakes after further Fading of support
  6. Advance or remediate deliberately — route non-mastery to targeted reteaching, not repetition of the same instruction; use Adaptive Difficulty where platform support exists

Related Strategies¶

  • Standards-Based Grading — the reporting layer that often accompanies CBP
  • Self-Paced Learning — the pacing mechanism CBP depends on
  • Frequent Low-Stakes Testing — supplies the ongoing mastery evidence CBP requires
  • Use external consulting and technical assistance to help colleges adapt programs to time-variant CBE models

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy — course mastery tracks gate progression on demonstrated proficiency per skill, with spaced retakes
  • Western Governors University — fully competency-based degrees; students progress by passing objective and performance assessments, not credit hours
  • Summit Public Schools — K-12 network using competency-aligned projects and a personalized learning platform for pacing and progress tracking
  • Teach to One 360 — middle-school math model that assembles daily personalized learning sequences from a competency map
