Word Processing and Technology Tools¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), q4 · 2 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy uses word processors and related digital tools (text editors, collaborative documents, speech-to-text, grammar checkers) as the medium for writing instruction and production. Because digital text is easy to add, delete, reorder, and share, the tool shifts writing from a linear transcription task toward an iterative compose–review–revise cycle, and reduces the motor and formatting burden that can consume working memory during composition.

Design Implications¶

Word processing supports writing quality primarily by lowering the cost of revision and freeing cognitive resources from transcription, which is especially valuable for weaker writers [~M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows positive but moderate effects of word processing on writing quality, with larger effects when it is embedded in a structured process-writing approach rather than used as a bare substitute for pen and paper [~M]. The tool alone does not teach writing; its benefits depend on pairing with explicit strategy instruction, feedback, and purposeful revision routines Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Reliable access to devices and files; a plan for saving, versioning, and submission
  • Instruction in the tool itself kept brief and separate from writing instruction, so tool mechanics do not compete with composing for attention
  • A revision routine that exploits the medium (e.g., tracked changes, comment-based peer review) rather than treating the document as a final transcript
  • Teacher feedback or structured peer feedback channels — digital commenting works best when feedback targets task and process levels Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Word processing without instruction in revision produces little improvement; students tend to make only surface-level edits (spelling, punctuation) unless taught to revise for meaning [-M]
  • For novice typists, transcription demands can increase cognitive load and depress writing quality relative to handwriting [~M] — keyboarding fluency is a prerequisite, not a given
  • Grammar and spell checkers catch only a subset of errors and can induce overreliance; students may accept incorrect suggestions [-W]
  • Distinct formatting and notification features of collaborative tools can distract; off-task behavior rises when tools are used without clear task structure [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Collaborative documents (e.g., Google Docs) enable real-time peer and teacher commenting during drafting, supporting Collaborative Learning
  • Speech-to-text / dictation removes transcription demands entirely for students with dysgraphia or motor difficulties
  • Planning and outlining tools (graphic organizer software, mind maps) front-load structure before drafting
  • Scaffolded templates and sentence starters embedded in the document act as Scaffolding that can be faded across assignments

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling and low-achieving writers benefit most; meta-analytic results show larger word-processing effects for weaker writers than for proficient ones [~M]
  • Students with dysgraphia, dyslexia, or fine-motor difficulties, for whom dictation and auto-formatting remove a major bottleneck
  • Students who already keyboard fluently; for hunt-and-peck typists the tool can add load rather than remove it [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: organization, coherence, and elaboration in extended compositions
  • Revision skill: learning to treat text as malleable and to revise for meaning, not just mechanics
  • Writing fluency and volume: producing more text per session, which increases opportunities for practice and feedback
  • Collaborative authorship: co-constructing and negotiating text with peers

Instructions¶

  1. Teach the tool's core operations (typing, saving, inserting, commenting) in a short separate session before academic use.
  2. Model composing and revising in the tool on a projected document, thinking aloud about why a sentence is moved or rewritten — a Demonstration of expert revision.
  3. Have students draft in the tool, then apply a taught revision routine (e.g., read-aloud check, checklist for organization) rather than free-form editing.
  4. Route the draft through structured peer or teacher commenting, with feedback focused on ideas and organization before mechanics Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  5. Require a documented revision pass (tracked changes or a revision memo) so revision is visible and assessable.
  6. Fade templates and checklists across the term as students internalize the process.

Related Strategies¶

  • Process writing approach — word processing is most effective when embedded in structured cycles of planning, drafting, and revising
  • Peer feedback and conferencing — digital commenting makes response faster and more targeted
  • Sentence combining and explicit writing strategies — the instruction layer that word processing alone cannot supply

Examples¶

  • Google Docs with version history and comment threads used for teacher and peer feedback during drafting (https://docs.google.com)
  • Write to Learn / process-writing classrooms in the Graham & Perin (2007) meta-analysis, where word processing supported structured revising across middle and high school
  • Speech-to-text tools (e.g., built-in dictation in Microsoft Word or ChromeOS) provided as accommodations for students with dysgraphia
  • Grammarly and similar checkers used as a final-edit aid after meaning-level revision, not as a substitute for it
