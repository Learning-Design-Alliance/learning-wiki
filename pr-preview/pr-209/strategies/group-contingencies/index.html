Group Contingencies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

A group contingency is a classroom management and motivational arrangement in which a consequence — typically a reward, privilege, or earned activity — is delivered to an entire group contingent on the behavior of a single member (dependent), every member meeting a criterion (interdependent), or the group as a whole (independent). The teacher announces the criterion in advance, tracks progress publicly, and delivers the consequence when the group meets it. The approach derives from operant conditioning but redistributes the reinforcement schedule across peers, harnessing social influence as an instructional lever.

Design Implications¶

Group contingencies are among the most consistently supported classroom behavior interventions, with meta-analytic evidence of moderate-to-large effects on disruptive behavior and on-task time Classroom group contingencies reduce disruptive behavior and increase academic engagement. [+S]. Their power comes from converting peers into sources of encouragement and accountability rather than leaving the teacher as the sole enforcer. The design decision that matters most is which contingency type to use: interdependent contingencies (the whole group must meet the criterion) generate the strongest peer effects but also the greatest risk of scapegoating or coercion of struggling members [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clearly defined, observable target behavior with a measurable criterion
  • Criteria attainable by all group members, with individual supports available so no learner is structurally unable to contribute
  • A progress-tracking display visible to the group (marble jar, point chart, digital counter)
  • A consequence the group actually values, identified in advance
  • Consistent, immediate delivery when the criterion is met

Constraints¶

  • Dependent contingencies (reward hinges on one member) can produce peer pressure, scapegoating, or resentment toward a struggling student [-M] — use with care or pair with individual supports
  • If criteria are set too high, groups that repeatedly fail disengage and the contingency loses all motivational force [-M]
  • Rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation when applied to tasks learners already find interesting [~M] — prefer contingencies for behaviors (participation, on-task conduct) over performance on inherently engaging tasks
  • Public tracking can shame students whose teams fail; keep displays framed around collective progress rather than individual deficits
  • Effects fade when the contingency is withdrawn abruptly; plan a fading schedule toward natural or intermittent reinforcement

Implementation Variability¶

  • Good Behavior Game — interdependent team contingency where teams earn penalties for disruptive behavior; one of the best-revalidated versions
  • Mystery Motivator — the reward is unknown until the criterion is met, adding an intermittent-reinforcement element
  • Level system — the group earns escalating privileges across a unit rather than a single session
  • Individual-within-group — independent contingency where each member's criterion is personalized but the reward is shared, protecting struggling learners

Target Learners¶

  • Whole-class settings, especially elementary and middle grades, where group identity is salient [+S]
  • Students with attention or self-regulation difficulties, who benefit from peer reminders and frequent feedback [+M]
  • Learners vulnerable to public failure need the individual-within-group variant to avoid harm [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Classroom conduct and engagement: on-task behavior, transitions, participation [+S]
  • Academic fluency practice: sustained effort during Practice or drill activities [+M]
  • Social skills: cooperation and mutual monitoring, when paired with Cooperative Learning structures [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Define one or two observable target behaviors and state the criterion concretely ("everyone finishes the exit ticket," "no more than two call-outs per round").
  2. Choose the contingency type — prefer interdependent for cohesion, independent-within-group when ability ranges are wide.
  3. Select a group reward with student input, and set criteria the group can meet within the first few trials to establish success.
  4. Track progress visibly during the activity and prompt peer encouragement rather than peer criticism.
  5. Deliver the reward immediately and predictably at first; then fade toward intermittent delivery and self-monitoring.

Related Strategies¶

  • Token Economies — the individual-reinforcement counterpart; group contingencies can layer on top of an existing token system
  • Positive Behavior Support — group contingencies are a Tier 1 tool within schoolwide PBIS frameworks

Examples¶

  • The Good Behavior Game (American Institutes for Research) — teams of students earn points for following classroom rules during a set period; teams exceeding a threshold lose a privilege. Validated across dozens of randomized trials.
  • ClassDojo (https://www.classdojo.com) — supports whole-class point goals and shared class rewards, though teachers should avoid using it for public individual shaming.
  • Reading fluency drills — a small group earns game time when every member completes a timed passage at or above their personal criterion, combining the contingency with repeated Practice.
