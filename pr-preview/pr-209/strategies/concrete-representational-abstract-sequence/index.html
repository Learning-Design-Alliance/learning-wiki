Concrete Representational Abstract Sequence¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

The Concrete Representational Abstract (CRA) sequence teaches a concept first with physical manipulatives (concrete), then with pictures or diagrams (representational), and finally with abstract symbols such as numerals and operators (abstract). Each phase is explicitly linked to the previous one so that symbolic notation inherits meaning from the earlier embodiments. The sequence descends from Bruner's enactive–iconic–symbolic stages and is a staple of mathematics special education and elementary instruction.

Design Implications¶

CRA manages the transition from embodied understanding to symbolic fluency, reducing the risk that symbols are manipulated meaninglessly Cognitive overload degrades learning when abstract notation is introduced without supporting representations. [+M]. The critical design feature is fading — the gradual handoff from concrete to abstract — rather than the concrete materials themselves; prolonged or unsupervised concrete play can distract from the target structure Concrete manipulatives show moderate but variable benefits that depend heavily on instructional design. [~M]. Explicit connections between phases ("this rod is the ten") matter more than the number of phases.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Manipulatives or concrete materials that map transparently onto the target structure (not richly decorative objects)
  • Deliberate bridging: each representational step must be explicitly linked to the one before it
  • A plan for fading to abstract symbols within a defined timeframe, not treating concrete work as an end state
  • Concrete materials that genuinely model the target mathematical structure (place value, proportionality), not merely decorate it
  • A parallel representational form (drawings, diagrams, number lines) that maps one-to-one onto the concrete materials
  • Explicit connection language and side-by-side presentation so learners see the same problem in multiple forms
  • A planned transition point to abstract notation, with continued access to representations as a fallback (Fading)

Constraints¶

  • Highly realistic or feature-rich concrete materials can draw attention to irrelevant surface features and depress transfer [-M] — learners encode the object, not the structure
  • Keeping learners at the concrete phase too long can impede development of abstract reasoning; the sequence is a bridge, not a destination [~S]
  • Poorly designed manipulatives that do not map one-to-one onto the symbolic system (e.g., non-proportional materials for place value) produce confusion rather than insight [-M]
  • For learners with strong prior knowledge of the symbols, starting concrete is redundant and slows progress [~M]
  • Concrete materials that are perceptually rich but structurally misleading (e.g., brightly colored, distracting manipulatives) can divert attention from the target concept Decorative illustrations do not improve learning and can reduce it. [-M]
  • Prolonged reliance on concrete objects without fading can bind understanding to specific materials and block transfer to symbols [~M]
  • Learners with strong prior knowledge of the abstract form may find the concrete stage redundant and demotivating Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • Poorly chosen manipulatives (e.g., using the same color for ones and tens) can actively model the wrong structure [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Concreteness fading: start concrete and fade to abstract within a single lesson sequence (Fyfe et al., 2014)
  • Simultaneous linking: show concrete, pictorial, and symbolic forms side by side rather than strictly sequentially
  • Virtual manipulatives: digital versions (e.g., Brainingcamp, Didax virtual manipulatives) trade tactile experience for scalability and clean fading
  • CRA with explicit instruction: common in special education, pairing the sequence with scripted teacher demonstration and student verbalization
  • Gradual vs. simultaneous: fade stage by stage, or present all three representations side by side from the start for learners who can handle the load
  • Virtual manipulatives: digital tools (e.g., Brainingcamp, Didax virtual manipulatives) can substitute for physical objects, with similar effects in meta-analytic comparisons [~M]
  • Domain extension: CRA originated in mathematics but the logic applies to science (physical models → diagrams → formal notation) and programming (physical artifacts → flowcharts → code)

Target Learners¶

  • Novices encountering a new symbolic system for the first time, especially in elementary mathematics [+M]
  • Learners with learning disabilities or weak working memory, for whom the sequence reduces representational load Chunking content across representations reduces working-memory load. [+M]
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners who already operate symbolically; starting concrete wastes time and can reduce engagement [~M]
  • Elementary and middle-grades students encountering a mathematical concept for the first time [+M]
  • Learners with learning disabilities or working memory constraints, for whom CRA is one of the better-evidenced mathematics interventions [+S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual foundations for symbolic procedures (place value, fractions, algebraic expressions)
  • Transfer from manipulable situations to abstract notation
  • Flexible representation use — the ability to move among concrete, pictorial, and symbolic forms
  • Conceptual understanding: grasping why an algorithm or symbolic procedure works
  • Procedural fluency grounded in meaning rather than rote steps
  • Representational flexibility: moving fluently among concrete, pictorial, and symbolic forms

Instructions¶

  1. Concrete phase: model the concept with physical materials while narrating the mapping to the target structure; students manipulate the materials themselves
  2. Representational phase: replace objects with drawings, diagrams, or number lines that preserve the same structure, explicitly connecting each picture back to the objects it replaced
  3. Abstract phase: introduce symbols, again explicitly linked to the representation ("the 4 in 34 means four of these rods")
  4. Fade and practice: withdraw the scaffolding representations as fluency grows, consistent with Scaffolding principles and Cognitive Load Management
  5. Check for representational flexibility: ask students to explain a symbolic step using a drawing or material to verify the concept, not just the procedure, transferred

Related Strategies¶

  • Concreteness fading — the general principle CRA instantiates; research supports fading across mathematics and science domains
  • Multiple representations — CRA is a specific, ordered case of teaching with linked representations
  • Use CRA-based small-group math tutoring with scripts, manipulatives, and computer fact practice as secondary prevention
  • Worked Examples — CRA stages can be delivered as worked examples with representations fading to abstract
  • Manipulatives — the concrete stage's core materials and their design constraints
  • Multiple Representations — the broader principle of linking forms; CRA is a temporal sequencing of it

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) for Math — builds from children's informal, concrete problem-solving strategies toward symbolic procedures
  • Direct Instruction — scripted CRA sequences are common in DI mathematics programs
  • Develop Understanding — the concrete and representational phases enact the concept-development component

Examples¶

Number Worlds — A prevention/intervention mathematics curriculum built explicitly on CRA progressions for number sense.

Montessori mathematics materials — Golden beads and stamp games embody place value concretely before symbolic work, an early precursor of the sequence.

Special education mathematics interventions — The National Center on Intensive Intervention's mathematics lesson plans specify CRA sequences for fractions and computation (https://intensiveintervention.org). - Cognitively Guided Instruction — builds arithmetic understanding from children's informal, concrete modeling strategies toward abstract number sentences - Concrete-Representational-Abstract instruction for fractions — Butler, Miller, Crehan, Babbitt & Pierce (2003) compared CRA and representational-only treatments for teaching fraction concepts to secondary students with learning disabilities; both improved, CRA strongest for subtraction - National Library of Virtual Manipulatives — free virtual manipulatives (base-ten blocks, algebra tiles) supporting the concrete-to-representational transition in digital contexts - Singapore Math / Math in Focus — published curriculum built on the concrete–pictorial–abstract sequence, using bar models as the representational bridge to algebraic reasoning
