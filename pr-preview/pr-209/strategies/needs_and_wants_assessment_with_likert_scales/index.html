Needs and Wants Assessment with Likert Scales¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

A needs and wants assessment uses structured instruments — typically Likert-scale items (e.g., "How confident do you feel about X?" rated 1–5) supplemented by open-ended fill-in questions — to gather learner data on interests, prior knowledge, confidence, and perceived needs before or during instruction. The educator analyzes responses to adapt planning, while learners experience that their input shapes what happens in class.

Design Implications¶

Pre-assessment of learner needs functions as a form of Activation for the educator: it surfaces prior knowledge and interest that can be built into task design rather than guessed at. When learners see their responses acted upon, it supports perceived autonomy and relevance, which sustains intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. The instrument itself is only half the strategy; the visible follow-through — adjusting content, examples, or pacing in response to data — is what produces the motivational and instructional benefit Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Carefully designed items: each Likert item should target a single, specific construct (one topic, one skill, one feeling) rather than compound statements
  • A fill-in or open-response channel alongside the scales, since closed items cannot capture unexpected interests or misconceptions
  • A documented plan for how responses will change instruction — learners disengage quickly when surveys are collected but ignored
  • Low-stakes administration (anonymous or clearly non-graded) to elicit honest responses

Constraints¶

  • Likert responses are coarse: they indicate where to probe, not why learners struggle, and cannot substitute for diagnostic conversation or error analysis [-M]
  • Acquiescence and social-desirability bias inflate agreement ratings, especially with younger learners or when responses are attributable [-M]
  • Results are only as useful as the analysis; raw scale averages hide subgroups with opposite needs [-W]
  • If instruction visibly ignores the data, repeated surveys erode trust and future responses become less honest [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pre-unit interest inventory — administered before planning a unit to select examples, contexts, and optional topics
  • Confidence self-rating — 1–5 confidence items per learning target, used to form flexible groups or set Adaptive Difficulty
  • Mid-unit pulse check — short 3–5 item check used as a Check-In to adjust pacing
  • Choice architecture — responses feed directly into Choice Boards, letting learners route themselves toward topics they rated as interesting or needed

Target Learners¶

  • Learners whose interests diverge from default curriculum examples; tailoring raises relevance and engagement Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • Reluctant or quiet students who will not volunteer needs verbally but will respond to a written scale
  • Less reliable with very young learners, who struggle with the abstract rating format; simplified emoji or 3-point scales are needed [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Instructional planning: identifying gaps in prior knowledge to target Activation activities
  • Motivational goals: increasing engagement and ownership by making learner voice consequential
  • Metacognitive goals: prompting learners to reflect on their own confidence and needs, a light form of self-assessment Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Define the decisions the data will inform (topic selection, grouping, pacing, examples) before writing any items.
  2. Draft 5–10 single-construct Likert items covering interest, confidence, and perceived relevance, plus 2–3 open fill-in questions ("What is one thing you want to get better at in this unit?").
  3. Administer anonymously or low-stakes at the start of a unit or term.
  4. Analyze for patterns and outliers; identify subgroups whose needs conflict.
  5. Make at least one visible instructional change based on the data, and tell learners what changed and why — the feedback loop is where the motivational benefit occurs.
  6. Re-administer a short version mid-unit as a Check-In to verify the adjustment worked.

Examples¶

  • A middle-school science teacher administers a 10-item Likert inventory ("I enjoy hands-on experiments" / "I feel confident reading graphs") before an ecology unit, then builds two lesson pathways — data-heavy and narrative-heavy — matched to response clusters.
  • A university instructor uses a first-week survey with confidence ratings per course topic to decide which lectures get full treatment and which get brief review, then reports the resulting syllabus adjustments to the class.
