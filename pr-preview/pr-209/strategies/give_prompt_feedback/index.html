Give Prompt Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Knowing what you know and don't know focuses learning. Feedback is an integral part of learning: learners need appropriate feedback on performance to benefit from instruction. In getting started, they need help assessing existing knowledge and competence; during practice, they need frequent opportunities to perform and receive suggestions for improvement; at intervals, they need chances to reflect on what they have learned and what they still need to know. Prompt means the feedback arrives while the learner can still act on it — not after the learning episode has closed.

Design Implications¶

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on achievement, but its effects are highly variable: it improves learning when it tells learners where they are going, how they are doing, and where to next Feedback improves learning when it addresses task-level and process-level questions. [+S]. Timing matters — immediate feedback during initial acquisition reduces the encoding of errors, while slightly delayed feedback can benefit retention [~M]. Feedback that is specific, actionable, and focused on the task (not the person) outperforms praise or grades alone [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Regular opportunities for learners to perform and be observed (Practice, Assess Performance)
  • Tutor or instructor expertise in diagnosing errors and phrasing constructive, task-focused comments
  • Low-stakes conditions so feedback informs rather than evaluates

Constraints¶

  • Feedback can depress performance when it directs attention to the self (praise, criticism, or comparison with others) rather than the task Feedback focused on the self rather than the task can reduce performance. [-S]
  • Requires significant time investment; generic or delayed feedback ("good job," comments returned weeks later) has near-zero effect
  • Overly detailed feedback can overload novices working at capacity Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M] — limit to one or two high-leverage points

Implementation Variability¶

  • Immediate automated feedback (e.g., answer-checking systems) vs. delayed human feedback on complex work
  • Erroneous Examples — presenting a flawed worked solution for learners to diagnose converts feedback into an active task [+M]
  • Peer feedback and self-assessment, which build the learner's own evaluative judgment when supported with criteria and exemplars [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Novices, who cannot yet detect their own errors and depend on external diagnosis Contingent, responsive support improves learning outcomes. [+M]
  • Struggling learners in tutoring or supplemental instruction settings, where frequent low-stakes feedback is the core mechanism
  • Less dependent: advanced learners who can self-monitor, for whom constant external feedback can become redundant or intrusive [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Skill correction: closing the gap between current and target performance
  • Metacognitive accuracy: calibrating self-assessment against actual performance
  • Self-regulated learning: using feedback to plan and monitor one's own progress

Instructions¶

  1. Establish a baseline — diagnose existing knowledge and competence before instruction begins (Check-In, Assess Performance)
  2. Create frequent, low-stakes performance opportunities so there is something concrete to respond to (Practice)
  3. Deliver feedback promptly, focused on the task and the next actionable step — not the person
  4. Limit each feedback episode to one or two high-leverage points to avoid overload
  5. Follow up with an assignment or task that requires the learner to apply the feedback and monitor their own progress

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the specific framing of feedback as a next step rather than a verdict
  • Formative Assessment — the system-level pattern this strategy enacts within tutoring and instruction

Examples¶

  • A tutor acknowledges a tutee's successes, names the single most important error, and assigns follow-up problems that give immediate feedback on whether the correction stuck.
  • Khan Academy — practice exercises deliver instant correctness feedback and hint scaffolds on every item, so learners adjust within seconds of an error.
  • Writing centers using a "higher-order concerns first" protocol give prompt, prioritized feedback on drafts before the final version is due, when revision is still possible.
