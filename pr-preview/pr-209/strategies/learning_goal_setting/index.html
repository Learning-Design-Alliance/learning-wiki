Learning Goal Setting¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 unmarked) · 9 studies (6 review, 2 theoretical, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 9 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Learning goal setting is the practice of defining clear, specific objectives before engaging in a learning experience — such as shadowing, observation, a project, or a unit of study. Learners articulate what they want to find out, write down their current assumptions, and identify what evidence would challenge or confirm those assumptions. This converts a diffuse experience into a directed inquiry: attention is pre-committed to targets, and post-experience reflection has explicit criteria to evaluate against.

Design Implications¶

Goal setting works because specific, difficult goals regulate effort, persistence, and attention more effectively than vague intentions to "do your best" Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory [+S]. For novices, goals framed around learning and process (what to understand, what strategy to try) outperform goals framed around outcomes or performance (looking competent, hitting a score) Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]. Goals also serve a self-regulatory function: they provide the reference standard against which learners monitor progress, and self-monitoring against explicit goals improves self-regulation Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dedicated time before the experience to articulate and document goals in writing
  • Goals that are specific and challenging but attainable Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S]
  • A structure for recording prior assumptions so they can be tested against observation rather than silently filtering it
  • A follow-up reflection or check-in in which progress against the stated goals is reviewed

Constraints¶

  • Vague or overly broad goals ("learn about teaching") provide no regulatory benefit; specificity is the active ingredient Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance. [-S] when violated
  • Outcome-oriented goals can harm novice performance and persistence after failure; learning-oriented goals are required for inexperienced learners Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [-M]
  • Pre-set goals can bias observation — learners may notice only what confirms their goals; explicitly documenting assumptions and counter-evidence questions mitigates this
  • Requires honest self-assessment; learners with low self-efficacy may set defensively easy goals that produce little growth Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual written goals — learners draft personal questions and assumptions before shadowing or field observation
  • Contract-style goals — goals negotiated with a mentor or instructor, then revisited at checkpoints
  • Micro-goals — breaking a large learning goal into achievable sub-goals, which raises self-efficacy through proximal success experiences Achievable micro-goals; proximal sub-goals increase self-efficacy and interest (Bandura & Schunk, 1981) [+S]
  • Assumption-auditing variant — goals paired with explicit statements of what the learner expects to see, so disconfirming evidence becomes salient

Target Learners¶

  • Novices entering an unfamiliar environment (e.g., pre-service teachers observing classrooms), where unguided observation yields little Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]
  • Learners with low task engagement, for whom goal setting increases task value and commitment Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
  • Advanced learners benefit less from externally set goals but can use self-set goals as part of self-regulated learning [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Attitudinal and motivational: building commitment, task value, and self-efficacy
  • Observational/inquiry skills: learning to gather evidence systematically rather than impressionistically

Instructions¶

  1. Elicit prior assumptions. Learners write what they currently believe and expect to observe, activating prior knowledge Activation.
  2. Set specific learning goals. Each learner drafts 1–3 specific, challenging goals framed as learning/process goals ("I want to identify three ways the teacher checks for understanding"), not outcome goals State objectives.
  3. Define evidence criteria. For each goal, specify what observations would count as evidence, including evidence that would contradict current assumptions.
  4. Engage in the experience with goals visible — e.g., carrying an observation protocol or focus question into a shadowing session.
  5. Reflect against goals. Debrief by reviewing each goal, the evidence gathered, and assumption changes; connect to next-cycle goals Check-ins.

Related Strategies¶

  • Achievable micro-goals — decomposes learning goals into proximal sub-goals that build self-efficacy
  • Activating prior knowledge — the assumption-auditing step is a form of activation that surfaces what observation should test
  • Check-ins — provides the monitoring checkpoints that make goals regulatory rather than decorative

Examples¶

  • Pre-service teacher shadowing — Before observing a classroom, a candidate writes: "My assumption is that quiet students are disengaged. Goal: document three behaviors of quiet students that indicate engagement or disengagement." The post-observation debrief compares evidence to the assumption.
  • Mastery learning programs — Learners set target competencies before each cycle and self-assess against explicit criteria.
  • Medical clerkships — Students on rotation set case-specific learning objectives (e.g., "present one patient history and receive feedback on differential diagnosis") rather than passively following the team.
