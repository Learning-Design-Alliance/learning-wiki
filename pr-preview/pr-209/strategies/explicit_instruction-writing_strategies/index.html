Explicit Instruction: Writing Strategies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 10 studies (4 review, 2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Explicit instruction in writing strategies involves directly teaching students specific, named methods for planning, drafting, revising, and editing their work. The instructor breaks the writing process into manageable steps, models each step (often via think-aloud), guides practice, and fades support as students internalize the strategy. The most researched instantiation is Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD), which adds goal setting, self-monitoring, and self-instruction so students eventually manage the strategy on their own.

Design Implications¶

Explicit strategy instruction works because it converts the tacit processes expert writers use into learnable procedures, reducing the unguided search that overwhelms novice writers' working memory Cognitive Load Management. Meta-analytic evidence shows strategy instruction is among the most effective writing interventions for adolescents, with SRSD producing some of the largest gains in writing quality [+S]. Effects are strongest when modeling makes reasoning visible and when instruction includes self-regulation components, not just the strategy steps themselves Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clearly specified strategy with memorable steps (e.g., POW+TREE for opinion essays)
  • Think-aloud modeling of the strategy by the instructor, including decisions and revisions, not just the finished product
  • Guided practice with collaborative writing before independent application, followed by performance assessment
  • Sufficient instructional time — SRSD typically spans multiple sessions per genre

Constraints¶

  • Overly rigid implementation can produce formulaic writing; students may apply strategies mechanically without adapting them to purpose or audience [~M]
  • Effects diminish if support is never faded — students remain dependent on external prompts unless responsibility is gradually released Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]
  • Requires sustained teacher implementation fidelity; one-off workshops rarely change writing outcomes [~M]
  • Less effective for students who already use comparable strategies spontaneously; redundant instruction can reduce engagement [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • SRSD — the full model: develop background knowledge, discuss the strategy, model it, memorize steps, support practice, promote independent use
  • Strategy-specific instruction — teaching a single strategy (e.g., planning via outlining, peer revision protocols) without the full self-regulation scaffold
  • Genre-based variants — the same instructional sequence applied to narrative, persuasive, or informational writing, with genre-specific prompts and criteria
  • Embedded vs. standalone — strategies taught within content-area writing tasks versus dedicated writing lessons

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling writers in grades 3–12, including students with learning disabilities, show the largest gains [+S]
  • Middle and high school students who lack planning and revision routines benefit from structured process support Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]
  • Strategy instruction that includes goal setting and self-monitoring raises writing self-efficacy, supporting persistence on extended tasks Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency in the writing process: planning, drafting, revising, editing
  • Genre knowledge: understanding the structural demands of persuasive, narrative, and expository forms
  • Self-regulated writing: goal setting, self-monitoring, and independent strategy adaptation

Instructions¶

  1. Develop background knowledge — activate what students know about the genre and topic (Activating Prior Knowledge)
  2. Discuss the strategy — introduce the named steps and their purpose; connect each step to how it improves the text
  3. Model — instructor think-alouds while composing, making planning and revision decisions visible
  4. Memorize the steps — students internalize the strategy sequence (charts, mnemonics) so working memory is freed for content
  5. Guided practice — collaborative and supported writing with instructor coaching and feedback at the process level Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  6. Independent practice — students apply the strategy alone; support is faded as proficiency grows

Related Strategies¶

  • Process Writing — strategy instruction is often embedded within a process-writing classroom
  • Peer Revision — a structured revision strategy that can be explicitly taught
  • Goal Setting — the self-regulation component that makes SRSD more than procedure training
  • Break the writing process into explicit components students move among flexibly

Examples¶

  • SRSD for persuasive writing (POW+TREE) — Graham & Harris's widely replicated approach: Pick my idea, Organize notes, Write and say more; Topic sentence, Reasons, Explanations, Ending — taught over multiple sessions with modeling and fading
  • The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler) — explicit sentence-level and composition strategies taught through structured sequences (https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)
  • Graham & Perin's Writing Next recommendations — strategy instruction ranked among the top-effect practices for adolescent writers in the meta-analysis summarized in this report
