Goal Setting In Writing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 mixed, 1 against) · 6 studies (3 review, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Goal setting in writing asks learners to establish a specific, attainable target before and during composing — for example, "include three counterarguments," "write a clear topic sentence for each paragraph," or "cut my draft by 20%." Goals can target the product (features of the final text), the process (planning, revising behaviors), or self-regulation (sustained effort, monitoring). The strategy is typically paired with Assessment against the goal and teacher or peer feedback.

Design Implications¶

Goal setting works because it directs attention and effort toward concrete features of text rather than the diffuse aim of "write well," and it converts writing from a knowledge-telling task into a self-regulated one Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory: specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals. [+S]. In writing specifically, goal setting is one of the most consistently effective elements of adolescent writing instruction, with effect sizes comparable to strategy instruction Graham & Perin meta-analysis of writing instruction. [+S]. Goals are most powerful when combined with Self-Regulated Learning instruction, where learners set goals, monitor progress, and revise strategies.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Goals that are specific and observable ("include two pieces of evidence per paragraph") rather than vague ("write a good essay") Specific goals outperform "do your best" goals. [+S]
  • Goals matched to current skill level — moderately challenging but attainable
  • A way to check progress against the goal: rubrics, checklists, or Check-Ins during composing
  • Feedback or self-evaluation that closes the loop between goal and revision
  • Clear understanding of the writing purpose, audience, and genre so goals map onto quality criteria
  • Self-assessment tools (rubrics, checklists) learners can apply to their own drafts
  • A feedback mechanism — teacher, peer, or structured conference — tied to the stated goals
  • Consistent monitoring: goals revisited at planning, drafting, and revision points

Constraints¶

  • Overly difficult goals depress performance and motivation when learners lack the skills to reach them Goal-setting theory: assigned goals must be attainable to sustain commitment. [-S]
  • Too many simultaneous goals overload novice writers, who have limited working memory for planning while composing Chunking reduces working memory load. [-M] — limit to one or two goals per draft
  • Product goals alone can encourage surface compliance (hitting counts, inserting required phrases) without improving reasoning; pairing with Authentic Audiences & Purposes [~M] or process goals mitigates this
  • Goals imposed without learner input yield weaker effects than collaboratively or self-set goals Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [~M]
  • Vague or purely quantitative goals ("write 200 words") do not improve quality and can encourage padding [-M]
  • Overly difficult goals depress motivation and self-efficacy for struggling writers; goals must be attainable at current skill levels Specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals. [~M]
  • Goals set for learners without their involvement produce weaker ownership and less goal-directed revision [~W]
  • Without monitoring or feedback, stated goals are quickly abandoned during drafting [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Product goals target text features (structure, evidence, audience awareness) — most common in genre instruction
  • Process goals target behaviors ("spend 10 minutes planning before drafting") — central to Self-Regulated Strategy Development approaches
  • Self-set vs. assigned goals — self-set goals build ownership; assigned goals are faster to deploy and safer for novices who cannot yet judge what to work on
  • Goal + rubric — pairing goals with rubric criteria makes evaluation criteria visible and supports Assessment for Learning
  • Teacher-set goals for novices, shifting to learner-set goals as expertise grows
  • Product goals (genre features) vs. process goals (planning, revising behaviors) Process-focused goals outperform outcome-focused goals for learners without developed skills. [+M]
  • Individual goals vs. shared class goals with peer support for planning and revision
  • Short-cycle goals per draft vs. longer-term goals across a writing unit

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling writers and students with learning disabilities, who benefit most from explicit, concrete targets Goal setting combined with strategy instruction improved essay elements for adolescents with disabilities. [+S]
  • Novice writers who otherwise default to knowledge-telling without a revision agenda
  • More experienced writers benefit less from assigned goals and more from self-set goals tied to their own revision priorities [~W]
  • Developing writers building self-regulation, since goal-directed self-monitoring is a core self-regulation skill Self-monitoring improves self-regulated performance. [+M]
  • Attaining goals builds writing self-efficacy, which predicts persistence on difficult writing tasks Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Text quality: improving specific features such as organization, elaboration, or argumentation
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising as habitual composing behaviors
  • Revision: giving learners a concrete agenda for evaluating and improving drafts
  • Writing quality: genre-appropriate content, organization, and revision
  • Self-regulated writing: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own composing process
  • Motivation and self-efficacy as a writer

Instructions¶

  1. Diagnose the learner's current draft or writing profile to identify one or two high-leverage focus areas.
  2. Co-construct a specific, challenging-but-attainable goal with the learner, phrased in terms of observable text or process features.
  3. Provide a mini-lesson or model showing what the goal looks like when met (Demonstration if available).
  4. During composing, prompt learners to check progress against the goal (Check-Ins).
  5. After drafting, have learners self-evaluate against the goal and revise, supported by teacher or peer feedback.
  6. Fade teacher-set goals toward learner-set goals as self-regulation develops.

Related Strategies¶

  • Achievable Micro-Goals — decomposing large writing tasks into small, immediately attainable goals
  • Action Planning — converting goals into concrete next steps
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — a natural setting for setting and checking individual writing goals
  • Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting) — structures learner ownership of goals and plans

Examples¶

  • Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD) — Graham and Harris's framework has students set goals for including genre elements (e.g., "state my opinion, give three reasons with facts") before writing persuasive essays; extensively validated across dozens of studies (SRSD Online).
  • Page-Voth & Graham (1999) — middle school students given specific goals for essay content (number of supporting arguments and counterarguments) wrote essays with more and higher-quality elements than students given "do your best" goals.
  • The Writing Revolution — Hochman and Wexler's method sequences sentence-level goals ("because/but/so" sentences) so each activity has a single explicit target before paragraph- and essay-level goals are layered on (The Writing Revolution).
  • Graham & Perin's meta-analysis (2007) — identified goal setting for writing, alongside summarization and collaborative writing, as one of the most effective instructional practices for adolescent writers, based on effect sizes from experimental and quasi-experimental studies.
  • Zimmerman & Kitsantas' revision studies — students given process goals (apply one revision rule at a time) learned revision skills better than those given outcome goals ("make it better"), and self-recorded progress added further gains.
  • The Write Tools / SRSD-based curricula — Self-Regulated Strategy Development lessons open each genre unit by having students set goals for what their text must accomplish, then graph progress across drafts.
  • Google Docs + teacher comment threads — teachers post the student's stated goal at the top of the draft and anchor feedback comments to it, making progress toward the goal visible across revisions.
