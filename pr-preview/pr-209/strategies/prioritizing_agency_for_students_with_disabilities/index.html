Prioritizing Agency for Students With Disabilities¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 7 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Prioritizing agency for students with disabilities means deliberately building students' capacity to understand their own rights and needs, communicate them, set goals, solve problems, and make consequential decisions about their learning. It is carried out through explicit instruction in self-determination skills, authentic decision-making opportunities (such as leading their own IEP meetings), and hands-on learning contexts where choices have real consequences. The approach treats students as primary agents in their education rather than recipients of services designed for them.

Design Implications¶

Self-determination research links student-directed learning skills to stronger post-school outcomes in employment, independent living, and community participation Self-determination predicts postschool employment and education outcomes for students with disabilities. [+M]. Agency is not developed by exhortation alone: students need explicit instruction in component skills (goal setting, self-monitoring, self-advocacy), supported practice with real decisions, and gradual transfer of control from adults to the student Self-determination interventions improve academic and transition outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit, direct instruction in self-determination component skills: goal setting, self-monitoring, problem solving, decision making, and self-advocacy (Application, Articulation)
  • Authentic opportunities to exercise agency — leading IEP meetings, choosing accommodations, selecting learning activities (Choice Boards)
  • Educators and families willing to transfer decision-making control incrementally, with Coaching rather than direction
  • Knowledge of rights, accommodations, and personal learning needs, taught explicitly (Accommodations)

Constraints¶

  • Requires a significant shift in adult mindset and investment of time, training, and resources; schools that treat IEPs as compliance documents rarely create authentic agency opportunities
  • Overly open choice without skill instruction can overwhelm students with executive function or working memory challenges Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-M] — agency must be scaffolded, not simply granted
  • Token participation (attending a meeting without speaking) produces no measurable benefit and can mask continued adult control [~M]
  • Effectiveness drops when self-determination instruction is isolated from academic content rather than embedded in real tasks [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Student-led IEPs range from attending and answering questions to fully facilitating the meeting; the self-advocacy benefit scales with the student's actual role
  • Embedded vs. standalone instruction: self-determination curricula (e.g., the Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction) can be taught as discrete lessons or integrated into subject-area tasks
  • Age of transfer: agency-building can begin in elementary grades with simple choices and progress toward full self-advocacy in transition planning

Target Learners¶

  • Students with disabilities at secondary level, especially those in transition planning Self-determination predicts postschool employment and education outcomes for students with disabilities. [+M]
  • Students with high-incidence disabilities (learning disabilities, ADHD) benefit from self-advocacy instruction for accommodation use; students with intellectual disabilities benefit from supported decision-making structures [~M]
  • Students who have experienced learned helplessness or passivity in service-oriented special education placements

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-advocacy: articulating needs, rights, and effective accommodation use
  • Self-regulation: goal setting, self-monitoring, and strategy adjustment (Self-Regulated Learning)
  • Transition readiness: employment, postsecondary education, and independent living skills

Affordances¶

  • Autonomy — agency-building directly satisfies the autonomy need that drives intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Belonging — exercising voice in one's education strengthens students' sense of membership and ownership Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — modeling and coaching of self-advocacy moves makes invisible metacognitive and communicative skills learnable

Personalization¶

Agency work is inherently individualized: each student's goals, communication mode, and level of support differ. Supports should be matched to the student — AAC systems for non-speaking students, visual decision frameworks for students with intellectual disabilities, accommodation self-selection for students with learning disabilities.

Instructions¶

  1. Assess current self-determination skills using a validated measure (e.g., the AIR Self-Determination Scale) to identify instructional targets (Assessment)
  2. Explicitly teach component skills — goal setting, self-monitoring, problem solving — through modeling and Articulation of expert reasoning
  3. Provide structured choice in academic tasks before high-stakes decisions (Choice Boards, Application)
  4. Coach authentic self-advocacy — prepare students to describe their disability, needs, and effective supports in real meetings (Coaching)
  5. Transfer meeting leadership to the student, fading adult prompting as competence grows
  6. Embed in hands-on contexts — service learning, work-based learning — where decisions have real consequences and feedback is authentic

Assessment Evidence¶

Track student participation and leadership in decision-making meetings, students' ability to articulate their needs and accommodation preferences, goal-attainment data from student-set goals, and postschool outcomes. Meta-analytic evidence associates self-determination interventions with improved transition and goal-attainment outcomes [+M].

Impact¶

Students who receive self-determination instruction show greater goal attainment and stronger postschool employment and education outcomes than comparable peers who do not [+M]. Effects are strongest when instruction is sustained across years rather than delivered as a single unit [~M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting) — a structured choice routine that builds the decision-making muscles agency requires
  • Self-Regulated Learning — the goal-setting and self-monitoring cycle underlying student-directed learning

Related Elements¶

  • Accommodations — students who understand and select their own accommodations use them more consistently
  • Choice Boards — a low-barrier structure for early agency practice
  • Coaching — the adult stance that supports rather than directs student decisions
  • Articulation — making self-advocacy reasoning explicit and teachable

Tools¶

  • Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction (SDLIM) — a teacher-implemented goal-setting and problem-solving model with strong evidence support
  • I'm Determined (Virginia Department of Education) — free tools for student-led IEPs and self-advocacy instruction (https://www.imdetermined.org)
  • AIR Self-Determination Scale — student, teacher, and parent forms for assessing self-determination capacity

Examples¶

  • Student-led IEPs: students prepare and present their strengths, needs, and goals at their own IEP meeting, using slide templates and rehearsal sessions; widely implemented through programs like I'm Determined.
  • SDLIM in secondary content classes: teachers at high schools implementing the Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction have students set and monitor academic goals within algebra or English coursework.
  • Work-based learning with self-advocacy coaching: transition programs pair community job placements with explicit instruction in disclosing needs and requesting workplace accommodations.
