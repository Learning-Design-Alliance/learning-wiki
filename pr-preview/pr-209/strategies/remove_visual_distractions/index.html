Remove Visual Distractions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

This strategy reduces the visual clutter of the learning environment — wall displays, desk materials, on-screen widgets, decorative graphics — so that learners' limited attention and working-memory resources are directed at the task rather than at incidental stimuli. It is carried out by auditing the physical or digital workspace, removing or hiding anything not serving the current learning goal, and restoring relevant materials only when they become relevant.

Design Implications¶

Attention is selective and capacity-limited; salient but irrelevant visual stimuli are involuntarily attended, especially by young children, consuming working-memory resources that would otherwise support learning Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves recall of emphasized content. [+M]. This is a direct application of Cognitive Load Management: every off-task visual element is extraneous load that competes with germane processing Chunking and reducing displayed information reduces working-memory load. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An audit of the learning space (classroom walls, desk surface, screen layout) identifying which stimuli serve the current task and which do not
  • A way to stage materials — e.g., storage bins, progressive disclosure of wall content, screen regions revealed only when needed
  • A task-appropriate baseline: the goal is relevant visual support, not an empty room

Constraints¶

  • Overly barren environments can feel institutional and demotivating, and may remove advance organizers or reference materials learners genuinely need [~M]
  • Learners with strong attentional control may gain little, while some learners with under-stimulation needs may seek out alternative distractions [~W]
  • In shared classrooms and open-plan spaces, individual learners cannot always control their visual field; personal workspaces or screen-based solutions are partial workarounds
  • Removing all decoration can eliminate the contextual cues and Advance Organizers that support orientation, so curation must be goal-driven rather than absolute [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Physical: clear desk surfaces, cover or rotate wall displays to match the current unit, seat learners away from high-traffic or window-facing sightlines
  • Digital: close unused tabs, hide toolbars and notifications, use reader modes, present one problem or step at a time (Chunking)
  • Instructional materials: strip decorative-but-irrelevant graphics from slides and worksheets; keep graphics only when they explain the content (the coherence principle) Clark & Mayer, 2016 [+S]
  • Individualized: a low-distraction corner or carrel for learners with sensory sensitivities or attention difficulties

Target Learners¶

  • Young children, whose attention is captured involuntarily by salient visual stimuli; classroom displays positioned in the line of sight to the teacher measurably reduce attention and learning in kindergarten Fisher, Godwin, & Seltman, 2014 [+S]
  • Learners with attention difficulties or sensory sensitivities, for whom extraneous stimuli impose disproportionate costs
  • Novices on high-load tasks, who have the least spare working-memory capacity to absorb distraction Chunking and reducing displayed information reduces working-memory load. [+M]
  • Less critical for experts, who can filter irrelevant stimuli efficiently [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Sustained attention and on-task behavior during demanding tasks
  • Retention of instructed content (reducing encoding of competing stimuli)
  • Efficient processing of high-element-interactivity material where working memory is the bottleneck

Instructions¶

  1. Audit the visual field from the learner's actual position — what is in the line of sight during instruction?
  2. Remove or stage away materials unrelated to the current task; store wall displays and rotate them in as units change
  3. Apply the same audit to digital environments: one task per screen, notifications off, unused tabs closed
  4. Curate rather than empty — retain only visuals that serve the current goal (reference charts, Advance Organizers, worked models)
  5. Observe on-task behavior and adjust; some learners need more stimulation than others, so treat the level of reduction as tunable per learner

Related Strategies¶

  • Chunking — the information-side counterpart: reduce load by structuring content, not just the environment
  • Clear Structure Presentation — visual simplicity works together with clear sequencing of what is shown
  • Acoustics and Noise Management — the auditory parallel of the same extraneous-load argument

Examples¶

  • Fisher et al. (2014) classroom experiment — kindergarten classrooms with a heavily decorated wall display in the teaching zone; children in the decorated classroom spent more time off-task and scored lower on the instructional unit than children in a sparse classroom (Psychological Science)
  • Coherence principle in multimedia design — stripping decorative illustrations from science slides improves transfer; e-learning courses designed per Clark & Mayer (2016) remove gratuitous graphics and background imagery
  • Test-environment design — covering classroom word walls and anchor charts during assessments so students rely on retrieval rather than environmental cues
