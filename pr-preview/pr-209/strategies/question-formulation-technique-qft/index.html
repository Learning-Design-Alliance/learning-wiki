Question Formulation Technique (QFT)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

The Question Formulation Technique (QFT), developed by Dan Rothstein and Luz Santana of the Right Question Institute, is a structured procedure in which learners generate, improve, and prioritize their own questions. Learners are presented with a Question Focus (QFocus) — a deliberately neutral stimulus such as a statement, image, or video that is answered by questions, not by discussion — and produce questions under four rules: ask as many questions as possible; do not stop to discuss, judge, or answer; write every question down exactly as stated; and change any statement into a question. The sequence moves from divergent production to convergent refinement (categorizing questions as open- or closed-ended, converting between types, prioritizing against a stated purpose) and closes with metacognitive reflection on what the questioning process taught them. The facilitator enforces the process without evaluating or improving the content of questions, preserving learner ownership.

Design Implications¶

QFT treats question-asking as a teachable skill rather than a fixed disposition, inverting the usual pattern in which instructors ask and students answer. The no-judgment rule during generation is the load-bearing design decision: it separates divergent production from evaluation, protecting fluency from premature criticism [~W]. The later convergent phase — categorizing, converting, and prioritizing — is where quality emerges, and it doubles as metacognitive practice Self-questioning and self-explanation during learning improve comprehension. [+M]. Because learners choose which questions to pursue, QFT supports autonomy and ownership of inquiry Autonomy-supportive learning environments strengthen intrinsic motivation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-designed QFocus: a stimulus that provokes multiple questions but is not itself a question, and does not reveal a preferred answer
  • Time for the full sequence (typically 30–60 minutes); compressing it to generation alone forfeits the convergent and reflective phases
  • A facilitator who enforces the four rules while remaining neutral about question content — offering examples or "better" questions undermines the technique's purpose
  • A concrete next step (research task, discussion, experiment) that uses the prioritized questions, so questioning connects to Application of Knowledge

Constraints¶

  • Learners with little topical background knowledge may generate shallow or off-target questions; some prior Activation of relevant knowledge improves question quality [~M]
  • The no-judgment rule can feel artificial to learners accustomed to immediate evaluation, and facilitators frequently violate it by praising or editing questions
  • Without a genuine follow-on task, prioritized questions become a dead-end exercise; questioning for its own sake shows weak transfer to later inquiry performance [-W]
  • Groups with strong status hierarchies may converge on a dominant member's questions unless generation is individual before sharing

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual generation followed by small-group pooling, versus whole-group generation from the start
  • QFocus formats: text statements, images, data displays, primary sources, or scenarios
  • Shortened "QFT-lite" (generate → prioritize → next steps) for single sessions; full protocol including open/closed conversion for extended inquiry units
  • Learner-authored QFocus for advanced groups, shifting ownership further toward self-directed inquiry

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 through adult learners; the protocol scales with the sophistication of the QFocus rather than the learners' age
  • Students who rarely volunteer questions — the written, non-judged format lowers the social risk of asking [~W]
  • Professionals (educators, health workers, community organizers) using questions to clarify goals and advocate for themselves — the population for which the technique was originally developed
  • Less effective for novices with no relevant prior knowledge, who lack the raw material to formulate substantive questions [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Question-asking fluency and question quality as explicit learning outcomes
  • Divergent thinking (generation), convergent thinking (prioritization), and metacognitive reflection in a single sequence
  • Preparation for inquiry, research, and discussion — supplying learner-generated questions that drive subsequent Case-Based Learning or investigation
  • Civic self-advocacy: the original purpose — formulating questions to navigate institutions and decisions

Instructions¶

  1. Design a QFocus tied to the unit's central objective; test it by brainstorming the questions it should provoke.
  2. Present the QFocus and the four rules; give groups 5–10 minutes to generate questions without discussion or judgment.
  3. Have groups categorize questions as open- or closed-ended, discuss the value of each type, and practice converting between them.
  4. Ask groups to prioritize 3 questions against a specific criterion (most important for the research task, most debatable, etc.).
  5. Move prioritized questions into the next activity — Class Discussion, research, or Case Studies — so the questions do real work.
  6. Close with reflection: what did learners learn from producing and choosing questions, and how might they use the process again?

Related Strategies¶

  • Socratic Questioning — instructor-led questioning; QFT reverses the direction of questioning to learners
  • KWL — a lighter-weight question-generation routine; QFT adds systematic refinement and prioritization
  • See-Think-Wonder — an observation-first routine whose "Wonder" step is an informal precursor to structured question generation

Examples¶

  • Right Question Institute — maintains the official QFT protocol, free classroom resources, and a large educator community; source of the original formulation in Rothstein & Santana (2011).
  • NHPRC/Smithsonian primary-source lessons — students generate questions from historical documents as the entry point to document analysis, using the QFocus-as-image variant.
  • Health-care advocacy training — the original adult-education application: patients formulate prioritized questions before medical appointments to improve information-gathering and self-advocacy.
