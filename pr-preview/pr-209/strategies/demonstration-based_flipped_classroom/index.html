Demonstration-Based Flipped Classroom¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 10 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 review), q1–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

In a demonstration-based flipped classroom, the instructor records narrated demonstrations — worked problems, software walkthroughs, lab techniques, geometric constructions — and assigns them as pre-class viewing. Learners can pause, rewind, and rewatch at their own pace, then attend class for practice, feedback, and resolution of difficulties. The approach suits procedural, precision-dependent subjects (mathematics, chemistry, physics, engineering, software) where observing exact expert steps matters.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines two evidence-backed mechanisms: observing a modeled performance before attempting it, and relocating first-pass instruction outside class so contact time is spent applying rather than receiving. Pre-class demonstrations function as worked examples [+M], reducing unguided search for novices, while in-class practice converts observation into transferable skill Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S]. Video's pause-and-rewind affordance lets learners self-pace through high-precision steps that a live demonstration would expose only once.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Screen-recording and narration tools (e.g., OBS, Camtasia, Loom) and skill in producing clear, concise demonstrations
  • Narration that makes reasoning explicit, not just the actions (Think-Aloud style commentary)
  • An accountability mechanism — embedded questions or notes — so viewing is active rather than passive
  • In-class activities that require applying what the demonstration showed, with instructor circulation for feedback

Constraints¶

  • Watching a demonstration without an application task creates illusions of understanding; learners overestimate what they learned from viewing alone Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [-S]
  • Producing high-quality demonstrations is time-intensive; poorly paced or overloaded videos degrade learning Cognitive overload impairs learning outcomes. [-M]
  • Requires reliable access to devices and connectivity outside class; inequitable access undermines the model
  • Less effective for open-ended, discussion-based, or ill-structured goals where there is no single correct procedure to model

Implementation Variability¶

  • Partial flip: only the most precision-critical topics are demonstrated on video; the rest stays live
  • Interactive video (e.g., Edpuzzle, H5P) embeds questions to enforce active viewing
  • Demonstration–problem pairs: each video is followed immediately by an isomorphic problem, mirroring example–problem sequencing [+M]
  • Student-generated demonstrations as an assessment form, deepening processing through teaching

Target Learners¶

  • Novices in procedural domains who benefit from a complete expert model before attempting tasks Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Learners who need to revisit steps at their own pace (second-language learners, students with processing-speed differences)
  • Less beneficial for learners with strong prior knowledge, for whom explicit modeling becomes redundant Guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency: executing multi-step methods accurately (algebraic manipulation, titration, CAD operations)
  • Schema formation: building a mental model of the task's structure before independent problem solving
  • Self-paced review and remediation of precise techniques

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the procedures or problem types where observing exact expert steps adds the most value; script the reasoning, not just the clicks.
  2. Record short (under ~10 minute) narrated demonstrations, applying cognitive load management: segment steps, highlight relevant screen regions, and remove extraneous content.
  3. Assign the video with an embedded question or brief written summary so viewing is active.
  4. Open class with a quick check-in on what was confusing, then run in-class practice on isomorphic problems.
  5. Circulate and provide feedback targeted at the task and process level Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [+S].
  6. Fade support across the unit: move from full demonstrations to partial examples to unscaffolded problems as competence grows.

Related Strategies¶

  • Direct Instruction — shares the model-then-practice sequence; the flip relocates the modeling phase outside class
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the pre-class video serves as the modeling phase before in-class coaching
  • Four-Component Instructional Design — demonstrations supply the supportive/procedural information for learning tasks

Examples¶

  • Engineering courses using screencasts to demonstrate CAD software techniques, then dedicating lab sessions to instructor-supervised modeling tasks.
  • Chemistry lab safety and technique videos (e.g., pre-lab demonstrations of titration) viewed before practical sessions, freeing lab time for coaching.
  • Khan Academy — narrated step-by-step problem demonstrations paired with practice exercises, widely used in flipped math classrooms.
