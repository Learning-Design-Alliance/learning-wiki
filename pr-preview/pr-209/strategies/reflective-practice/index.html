Reflective Practice¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 2 mixed) · 8 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 6 of 8 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Reflective practice is the deliberate, structured examination of one's own experience — what happened, why it happened, what one thought and felt, and what to do differently — carried out through writing, dialogue, or guided questioning. It converts raw experience into transferable insight by forcing articulation of tacit reasoning rather than letting experience pass unexamined.

Design Implications¶

Reflection works when it is structured and prompted, not when it is left to chance; unguided "think about your learning" prompts produce little change Reflective practice improves professional capability only when structured with prompts or frameworks. [+M]. Effective designs give learners a framework (e.g., Gibbs' cycle, "What? So what? Now what?"), time to write or discuss, and a follow-through step that connects insight to future action. Reflection is most powerful when it occurs close to the experience and when the learner articulates reasoning aloud or in writing (Articulation).

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A concrete, recent experience to reflect on (a task, case, performance, or Practice episode)
  • A guiding framework or prompt sequence, not an open invitation to "reflect"
  • A mechanism for capturing reflection — journal, discussion, portfolio entry (Annotating or structured writing)
  • A loop back into action: revised goals, plans, or next attempts

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured reflection can devolve into venting or vague narration with no learning gain [-M]
  • Reflection without feedback or external perspective risks entrenching misconceptions — learners may confidently draw wrong lessons from experience Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, where to next?" improves learning. [~M]
  • Overly long or frequent reflective writing tasks produce compliance writing and fatigue [-W]
  • Novices may lack the knowledge to diagnose what went wrong; reflection on unfamiliar domains can mislead [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written journals or learning logs — individual, asynchronous, deep but low-feedback
  • Structured dialogue — Check-In rounds, Class Discussion, or peer debriefs that add external perspective
  • Post-performance debriefs — short, immediate, focused on specific decisions (standard in simulation and case-based teaching)
  • Portfolio reflection — cumulative, connecting multiple experiences over time

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with enough experience to have something substantive to analyze — intermediate performers benefit most [~M]
  • Professionals and pre-service practitioners in clinical, teaching, and other practice-based fields Reflective practice is widely used and valued in professional education, though evidence for isolated reflection activities is mixed. [~W]
  • Novices need more scaffolding: prompts, exemplars of good reflection, and instructor feedback on the reflection itself

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognition: recognizing one's own reasoning patterns, assumptions, and errors
  • Transfer: extracting generalizable principles from specific experiences
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and adjusting one's own learning Self-regulated learning strategies improve academic performance. [+S]
  • Professional identity and judgment formation

Instructions¶

  1. Anchor the reflection in a specific recent experience — a completed task, Case Studies session, or performance.
  2. Provide a framework with sequenced prompts: describe what happened → analyze why → plan what to change (Gibbs' cycle or "What? So what? Now what?").
  3. Have learners articulate their reasoning in writing or speech (Articulation), not just rate their feelings.
  4. Add an external perspective — peer dialogue, instructor response, or comparison against expert reasoning — so reflection is checked, not just emitted.
  5. Close the loop: require one concrete commitment for the next attempt, then revisit it at the next Check-In.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action Research — reflective practice formalized as systematic inquiry into one's own practice
  • Learning Journals — the most common written vehicle for reflection

Examples¶

  • Simulation debriefing in health education — after a mannequin scenario, facilitators run a structured debrief (advocacy-inquiry style) in which learners analyze their own decisions; debriefing quality is a stronger predictor of learning than scenario fidelity.
  • Gibbs' Reflective Cycle in teacher education — student teachers write cycle-structured analyses of lessons they taught, then revise their lesson plans accordingly.
  • Design studio crits — architecture and design programs institutionalize reflection through public critique of work in progress, combining self-explanation with expert and peer feedback.

Related Patterns¶

  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — reflection is the "articulation and reflection" phase in which learners compare their reasoning to expert reasoning
  • Case-Based Learning — post-case debriefs are structured reflection on diagnostic and decision reasoning
  • Experiential Learning Cycle — reflection is the processing stage that converts concrete experience into abstract principles
