Evaluating Sources¶

Principle · All principles Evidence · 11 claims (5 for, 6 mixed) · 23 studies (11 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 qualitative, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 design), q1–q4 · 2 of 23 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Conditional relationship¶

This page owns one judgment: whether to trust a source, and on what grounds. For an adolescent or adult learner who is a novice at judging unfamiliar sources, and whose starting response is to judge a web page by its own features (an official-looking logo, a .org or .edu domain, professional design, an "About" page), teaching a move that takes the reader off the page (search what independent sources say about who is behind it, then decide) and practising that move on live sites across several lessons is expected to produce better credibility judgments of new, unfamiliar sites (near-transfer, a procedure applied with judgment) than the learner's ordinary instruction. The gain has been measured at an immediate posttest and, in one small randomised pilot, five weeks later; nothing here measures it later than that, or in what learners do when they browse, share or cite outside class.

A claim tests part of this. Three studies of teaching professional fact-checkers' moves (lateral reading) compare taught classes with untaught ones: a district-wide high-school field study with matched controls, a randomised pilot in four university course sections, and a single-group study of eleventh graders (Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M]). None reports an effect size. What the evidence does not show: that the off-page move beats a checklist when each is taught in a single session (in one library session with nursing students the two did not differ, and both groups improved), that the gains last beyond five weeks, or that the same instruction helps judge research articles, policy documents or a primary source with no outside coverage. For those kinds of source no claim here tests any design.

The relationship is conditional on the kind of source (an organisation's website or a news story can be checked against what others say about it; a single historical document or a dataset often cannot); whether the learner has open web access and knows which outside sources are themselves reliable; the number of sessions; and whether the judgment is scored on reasons or only on the verdict. Learners who already check sources off the page have less to gain (an expertise-reversal pattern carried from cognitive-load research, not tested here).

How this sits beside the converted siblings. Epistemic Cognition holds the general relationship (teach the standard for one kind of evidence explicitly, have learners apply it to real claims and justify each judgment; gains stay with the standard taught) and its evidence across source evaluation, critique of studies and argumentation. This page is that model applied to source credibility, and adds what a designer of source-evaluation teaching needs that the general page does not: which move to teach, the dose and progression across sessions, what to do without open web access, and how to read a learner's trust judgment. Inquiry-based Learning uses source evaluation inside an investigation; Scaffolding and Fading holds how coaching is reduced, which the default design below uses and does not repeat.

Default design¶

Each step is a design proposal. A claim tests the whole package of teaching fact-checkers' moves over several lessons against no such teaching; it does not test any step on its own, so each step's evidence status is stated beside it. The earlier page's guidance (real source comparisons, explicit criteria, justification, contingent coaching) is kept and made concrete.

  1. Start with a judgment, not a lesson. Before any teaching, give learners two or three unfamiliar sources on one topic (one polished but unreliable, one plain but reliable) and ask which to trust and why, in writing, with a confidence rating. Five to ten minutes. This records the starting response and makes the problem visible. Untested proposal; the earlier page suggested "comparing a polished but unreliable site against corroborated sources"; the pretest-then-teach form follows the studies' designs.
  2. Teach one move first: leave the page. Model, thinking aloud, opening a new tab and searching the source's name (with words such as "funding" or "who runs") before reading the page closely, then deciding. Ten to fifteen minutes of demonstration on two sites. Evidence: the taught package in Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M]. Domain names and design are taught as weak signals, never as the test: in both one-session comparisons learners kept leaning on domain and design after training (One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction did no better than the alternatives it was compared with [~M]).
  3. Practise on live sources where the verdict matters. Pairs or individuals judge new sites tied to a question the class is working on (a claim for an essay, a decision, a debate motion), so a wrong verdict would change the conclusion. Three or four sources per lesson, 20–30 minutes. Untested as a separate step; all the studies used live or realistic sites.
  4. Require a reason with every verdict. The learner writes what they found off the page, who is behind the source, and why that warrants trust or not; a verdict without a reason does not count. Carried from a different task: Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S] concerns explaining worked examples and texts in mathematics and science, not source judgments.
  5. Coach the weakness the attempt shows. Read the reasons, not only the verdicts: a learner who stayed on the page needs the move modelled again; one who searched but trusted an unreliable outside site needs a list of reference sources for that topic; one who reached the right verdict for a wrong reason needs a case where that reason misleads. Give the least help that gets the next attempt right. Carried from one-to-one tutoring of mathematics and science: Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]; untested for source evaluation or at class scale.
  6. Spread it over several lessons, and fade the teacher's part. A default arc, based on the doses in the studies (two 75-minute lessons at university, six 50-minute lessons in high school, eight lessons in eleventh grade): early lessons, teacher models each move on a new kind of source and learners practise with a prompt card (who is behind this? what do others say? what is the evidence?); middle lessons, learners work in pairs without the card and compare verdicts and reasons with a model answer shown afterwards; late lessons, individual judgments on unseen sources of mixed kinds, including some that look poor but are reliable, with help only on request. Plan at least four lessons of 45–75 minutes, two or more weeks apart in total span, embedded in a subject course rather than as a stand-alone unit. The lesson counts come from the claim; the three-stage fading, the four-lesson floor and the spacing are untested proposals (Scaffolding and Fading holds the fading model).
  7. Move a learner on when the reasons, not only the verdicts, are right. A learner is ready for unsupported work after correct verdicts with an off-page reason on three consecutive unfamiliar sources; a learner who already does this on the first-lesson judgment skips to step 8. Untested proposal; skipping guidance for those who already succeed follows Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M], carried from cognitive-load studies.
  8. Check later, on new sources, scoring the reason. Several weeks after the last lesson, give unseen sources and score both the verdict and whether the reason rests on outside evidence. Untested as a design step; the randomised pilot tested five weeks after its lessons, and the eleventh-grade study used constructed-response tasks.

When a condition is not met: with one session only (a library visit), teach step 2 and one practice round, expect modest gains, and say so; without open web access, supply a packet of outside documents about each source and teach learners to look for them; for research articles, teach the standards for judging a study separately (see the Epistemic Cognition page), since gains in one did not carry to the other.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • What kind of source, and what follows from the judgment. An organisation's website or a news story can be checked laterally; a research article is judged by its methods; a primary document with no outside coverage is read closely. If unstated, ask: what will the learner have in front of them when they decide what to trust, and what will they do with it (cite it, share it, act on it)?
  • How many sessions there are. The tested doses are two to eight lessons; one-session teaching of either method produced modest gains that did not differ by method. If unstated, ask: how many lessons, over what span, and is there a later task where the learner must judge new sources?
  • What the learner already does and knows. Learners who already leave the page have less to gain; learners who leave the page but consult unreliable sites need to know which reference sources are trustworthy in the topic. If unstated, ask: given an unfamiliar site, what does the learner do first, and can they name a reliable reference source for this topic?

Observation, state and explanation¶

What can be observed is a verdict on a named source, the reason given, the confidence stated, and what the learner did before deciding (stayed on the page, searched, which outside sources they opened, how long it took). "Is a critical consumer of information" or "has digital literacy" are inferred states. A correct verdict does not show a sound method: it can come from agreeing with the source's conclusion, or from a domain-name rule that happens to fit.

Evidence and qualifications¶

  • Civic Online Reasoning Instruction Improves Evaluation [+M]: three studies of teaching fact-checkers' strategies. Teachers given professional development taught six 50-minute lessons on lateral reading in high-school government classes; treatment students (n = 271) grew significantly more than matched peers (n = 228) in judging the credibility of digital content (coded q2; the abstract calls it matched-control, the authors' project page cluster-randomised). In a randomised pilot in four sections of a university critical-thinking-and-writing course (n = 67), two 75-minute lessons made treatment students significantly more likely to gain from a pretest six weeks before to a posttest five weeks after (q3). Sixty-eight eleventh graders given eight lessons improved on three of four constructed-response tasks, with no control group (q2). No effect sizes. The claim (merged with the former "Lateral reading improves source evaluation", which is now its alias) predicts better judgment of unfamiliar web content after several lessons; it does not predict durability beyond five weeks, a change in behaviour outside class, or benefit for a primary source with no outside coverage. Its rigour codes are r2 for the pilot and r? for the other two. Not yet checked against its sources.
  • One-session checklist (CRAAP) instruction did no better than the alternatives it was compared with [~M]: limits the relationship. A think-aloud study of ten historians, ten fact checkers and 25 undergraduates found those who stayed on a site were misled by logos and domain names and the fact checkers, who left it, judged better and faster (descriptive; it taught nothing). In one library session with first-year business students, CRAAP and six question words did not differ on quizzes; papers from the question-word sections scored higher on a rubric (2.81 against 2.28 of 4), possibly because of prior library instruction. In one 110-minute session with first-term nursing students, accuracy in the CRAAP group rose from 41% before to 59% five weeks later and never differed from a networked, lateral-reading-style framework; there was no untrained control. So one session of either method gives modest gains, and on this evidence the off-page move has not been shown to beat a checklist in a single session. The claim's title was narrowed on 2026-10-05 from "checklist-based evaluation instruction does not reliably improve online source evaluation"; its Discussion still carries strength tags from before, which it says overstate the evidence.
  • Collaborative critique-and-redesign instruction shifts students from a value-free view of objectivity toward a procedural one [~M]: 120 first-year psychology students, a 12-week course with three small-group critique-and-redesign tasks; appeal to valid methods rose from 2% to 38% of written evaluations and to replication from 24% to 55%, while judgments about source expertise and outlet credibility did not change significantly. Single group, no control. It limits this page from the other side: teaching how to judge a study did not improve judging a source, so the two are separate targets.
  • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [+S]: a meta-analysis of 64 reports (g = .55) and studies in mathematics and science find that prompting learners to explain while studying improves learning. It is the basis, carried, for requiring a reason with each verdict; no study here tests reason-giving in source evaluation. Not settled: most entries are read from abstracts.
  • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [+M]: fully contingent one-to-one tutoring of fourth and fifth graders in long division beat fixed, partly contingent or no support at immediate and one-month tests (8 per condition); a review notes most evidence is one-to-one. It is the basis, carried, for coaching the weakness a learner's reasons show; untested in source evaluation and at class scale. The earlier page cited it for this use.
  • Instructional guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows. [~M]: a review of cognitive-load studies. It is the reason to let learners who already judge sources off the page skip the modelling; it says nothing about source evaluation or when an individual crosses over.

Populations differ (US high-school and university classes on the open web; first-year business and nursing students in one library session; one psychology course), so do not rank these by effect label. None reports an effect size for source-evaluation teaching, none follows learners beyond five weeks, and two of the core studies have no control group.

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims the earlier page cited that are not used in the model above, and neighbouring claims found while rewriting it. None has been re-read against the model.

  • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] — high-school writing revision and a theoretical chapter; the earlier page cited it for giving novices explicit evaluation routines rather than "use credible sources". A neighbouring finding about goal setting, carried to this skill; verification status not recorded here.
  • Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [+M] — one narrative review of goal-setting research, mostly in work settings; the earlier page cited it for making credibility criteria explicit. Its marker was [+S], above the claim's cap (one study, q2), and is lowered here to [+M]. Bears on source evaluation only by analogy.
  • The problem-based group achieved significantly larger content-knowledge gains, while the simulation group made no knowledge gains [~M], The participatory simulation group achieved significantly larger application gains than the problem-based group [~M] and The participatory simulation group showed significantly larger overall (combined knowledge and application) gains than the problem-based group after four weeks [~M] — one randomised trial with 54 seventh and eighth graders comparing two four-week methods of teaching information problem-solving; knowledge and application gains went in opposite directions. It concerns information literacy broadly, not source credibility, and suggests scoring knowledge of criteria and their application separately.

Objective and learner-valued goal¶

The designer's objective is usually that the learner, given an unfamiliar source, can say whether to trust it and why, on outside evidence, weeks after teaching. Elicit separately what the learner wants: to settle a question that matters to them (is this treatment safe, is this job offer real, who is right about a local issue), to finish an assignment with acceptable references, or not to be fooled in front of others. Agreement and divergence are observations. A learner who distrusts a source the evidence supports is not thereby a poor evaluator; ask for the reason.

For example, an adult returning to college may want to know whether a website offering student loans is legitimate, while the course objective is evaluating sources for a research essay. Using the loan site as the first practice case serves the learner's goal and teaches the move; the course objective is then checked separately on unfamiliar academic and news sources several weeks later. Record both, so that a correct verdict on the loan site is not read as the capability the essay needs.

What would revise this model?¶

The expectation should weaken if controlled comparisons with effect sizes find that several lessons of off-page evaluation do not improve judgments of new sources against ordinary teaching, or that gains fade within a few months. If multi-session checklist teaching matches multi-session lateral-reading teaching, the choice of move matters less than the dose and practice, and step 2 should say so. If learners who were taught evaluate sources no differently in their own assignments, the model should be limited to classroom tasks. If the doses in the studies prove larger than needed, the four-lesson floor should go. Do not protect the model by calling every failure too few lessons or too little background knowledge after the fact.

A better verdict on new sites, a better reason, durability over months, and changed browsing, sharing or citing outside class are separate claims. The evidence here reaches the first two, for web sources, at horizons up to five weeks. It establishes no optimal dose, no effect size, and nothing for research articles, primary documents or curated databases.

Related Principles¶

  • Inquiry-based Learning — inquiry quality depends on source quality
  • Annotating — annotation can focus attention on credibility signals, claims, and evidence
  • Clear Structure & Presentation — learners need explicit frameworks for evaluation, not just warnings about misinformation
  • Debate — debates become more rigorous when learners are accountable for source quality, not just rhetorical performance
  • Epistemic Cognition — the general model (teach the standard for one kind of evidence; gains stay with the standard taught) that this page applies to source credibility

Examples¶
