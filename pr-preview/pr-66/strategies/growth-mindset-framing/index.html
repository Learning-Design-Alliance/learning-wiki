    Growth Mindset Framing¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Growth mindset framing presents ability as malleable — built through effort, effective strategies, and help-seeking — rather than fixed. It is carried out through how instructors talk about difficulty ("this task is designed to stretch you"), how they deliver feedback (praising process and strategy rather than labeling ability), and how they normalize error and revision as part of learning. The framing targets learners' implicit theories of intelligence, which shape how they interpret setbacks.

    Design Implications¶

    Mindset framing works as a small but real motivational lever: brief interventions that teach a malleable view of ability improve achievement for some learners, with effects concentrated among lower-achieving and at-risk students [~S]. Framing alone is insufficient — it must be paired with instructional conditions that make improvement actually achievable, or the message rings hollow [~M]. Because mindset beliefs interact with belonging and autonomy, framing is most effective inside a broader motivational design that also supports Self-Determination Theory needs Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Authentic opportunities to improve: revision, retakes, or progressive tasks where effort visibly pays off (Assessment structures that allow growth)
    • Feedback language that attributes performance to strategy and effort, not fixed traits
    • Instructor modeling of fallibility — normalizing mistakes and demonstrating recovery
    • Consistency across the course: a single "you can grow" speech contradicted by fixed grading practices backfires

    Constraints¶

    • Effects are small on average and largely absent for already high-achieving students [~S] — a national study found meaningful gains only for lower-achieving and low-SES students
    • Framing without structural support (rigid grading, no revision, no help channels) is perceived as empty rhetoric and can reduce trust [-M]
    • Effort praise alone can backfire if it implies the learner lacks ability ("at least you tried") or rewards ineffective strategies [-M] — praise must pair effort with strategy use
    • Learners in performance-evaluated, competitive climates often cannot sustain growth beliefs regardless of framing [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Embedded framing: woven into task instructions, feedback comments, and error responses (the format used in the National Study of Learning Mindsets)
    • Standalone modules: short reading-and-reflection activities on brain plasticity and how ability develops
    • Feedback protocols: structured comment templates that name the strategy, its effect, and the next step
    • Error-normalizing routines: "my favorite mistake" analysis, revision-before-grading policies

    Target Learners¶

    • Lower-achieving students and students at risk of disengagement, who show the largest and most reliable gains [~S]
    • Students transitioning into challenging new environments (first-year college, advanced coursework) where early setbacks can trigger fixed-ability attributions
    • Students from groups subject to stereotype threat, for whom attributing difficulty to strategy rather than ability buffers disengagement [+W]
    • Least effective for high achievers, who typically already hold growth beliefs [~S]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Academic persistence and resilience in the face of difficulty
    • Adaptive help-seeking and strategy revision after failure
    • Long-term achievement trajectories in gateway courses, particularly mathematics

    Instructions¶

    1. Audit the course's implicit messages: grading policy, revision options, and how errors are treated. Remove contradictions before adding framing.
    2. Frame difficulty as design: introduce challenging tasks with language signaling that struggle is expected and productive ("this problem is meant to make you think hard — that's where learning happens").
    3. Deliver process-focused feedback: name the strategy used, its effect, and a next step; avoid trait labels ("smart," "gifted," "not a math person") in both praise and correction.
    4. Normalize error publicly: analyze common mistakes as a class and model your own recovery from errors.
    5. Pair framing with actionable support: teach specific strategies and make help-seeking a normal, expected behavior (Coaching, office hours, peer support).
    6. Reinforce through assessment structures: allow revision or resubmission so improvement is demonstrable, consistent with Assessment for Learning Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

    Related Strategies¶

    • Process-focused feedback — the primary delivery vehicle; mindset framing lives or dies in feedback language
    • Belonging interventions — companion social-psychological interventions targeting the same at-risk populations
    • Error analysis routines — operationalize the "mistakes are information" message

    Examples¶

    • National Study of Learning Mindsets (Yeager et al., 2019) — A preregistered, nationally representative experiment in 65 US high schools; a 25-minute online module framing ability as malleable raised GPA among lower-achieving students (https://mindsetscholarsnetwork.org).
    • Brainology — Dweck and colleagues' online curriculum teaching students how the brain grows with effortful learning, used in middle-school settings.
    • "Not Yet" grading — Carol Dweck's widely adopted reframing of failing grades, signaling that learning is in progress rather than concluded.
    • Jo Boaler's youcubed (https://www.youcubed.org) — Mathematical mindset lessons and teacher materials that combine mindset framing with open, low-floor/high-ceiling tasks so the framing is backed by genuine opportunity to improve.
