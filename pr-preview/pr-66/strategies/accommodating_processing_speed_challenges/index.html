    Accommodating Processing Speed Challenges¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Accommodating processing speed challenges means adjusting pacing, task volume, and response formats so that learners who process information slowly can demonstrate knowledge without speed acting as a confound. Processing speed — the rate at which simple cognitive operations are executed — varies widely across learners, develops through childhood and adolescence, and declines with age Salthouse, T. A. (1996). The processing-speed theory of adult age differences in cognition. Psychological Review, 103(3), 403–428 [+S]. The strategy is carried out through extended time, reduced item volume, chunked tasks, extended wait time in discussion, and scaffolds that reduce the amount of novel information a learner must hold and manipulate at once.

    Design Implications¶

    Processing speed is a bottleneck resource: slow execution of basic operations consumes working memory that would otherwise go to reasoning and comprehension Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. (1998). Cognitive architecture and instructional design. Educational Psychology Review, 10(3), 251–296 [+M]. Effective accommodations therefore do two things — give more time and reduce unnecessary load — rather than lowering the intellectual demand of the task. The goal is to separate speed from competence: assess what learners know, then build fluency deliberately so the accommodation becomes less necessary over time.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A clear picture of where the bottleneck occurs (reading rate, note-taking, oral processing, written output), gathered through observation and assessment
    • Materials structured for low-load delivery: chunking of content, clear structure, and advance organizers so learners can orient before high-speed input begins
    • Explicit norms that time pressure is not part of the learning goal, so extended time is actually used rather than abandoned under peer pressure

    Constraints¶

    • Extended time alone produces modest and inconsistent gains; it helps most when paired with strategy instruction, and it can inflate scores for students who do not need it, threatening validity Lovett, B. J. (2010). Extended time testing accommodations for students with disabilities: Answers to five fundamental questions. Review of Educational Research, 80(4), 611–638 [~M]
    • Reducing pace and volume for learners who have already automatized the component skills is redundant and can depress engagement Expertise reversal: guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows. [~M]
    • Removing all time pressure can eliminate retrieval effort that supports retention Spaced repetition improves retention. [~W] — some timed practice remains valuable once accuracy is established
    • Over-accommodation without fading can undermine self-efficacy and self-advocacy; supports should be contingent and progressively withdrawn Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [~M]
    • Accommodations applied uniformly without diagnosis can mask the actual bottleneck — a slow reader given extra time on a listening task gains nothing

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Assessment: extended time (typically 1.5×), reduced item sets, or separate quiet settings
    • Instruction: increased wait time after questions — extending teacher wait time from ~1 second to 3+ seconds lengthens and improves student responses Rowe, M. B. (1986). Wait time: Slowing down may be a way of speeding up! Journal of Teacher Education, 37(1), 43–50 [+S]
    • Materials: guided or partially completed notes, audio versions of text (audiobooks), text-to-speech tools
    • Output: speech-to-text, scribing, or oral responses in place of timed written output

    Target Learners¶

    • Students with identified disabilities involving processing speed (ADHD, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum conditions) — chronic time pressure erodes the confidence these learners need to persist Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+S]
    • Second-language learners processing in a non-dominant language, for whom slower lexical access mimics low processing speed
    • Older adult learners, given the robust general slowing of processing with age Salthouse, T. A. (1996). The processing-speed theory of adult age differences in cognition. Psychological Review, 103(3), 403–428 [+S]
    • Less beneficial for learners with high automaticity in the relevant skills, for whom extra time adds little Expertise reversal: guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows. [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Knowledge and comprehension goals where speed is incidental to the objective — the default case for most content learning
    • Strategy acquisition, supported by coaching and explicit strategy instruction that makes slow processing more efficient over time
    • Fluency goals (reading rate, math fact retrieval) — accommodated during acquisition, then addressed directly through practice rather than permanently bypassed

    Instructions¶

    1. Diagnose the bottleneck. Use observation and assessment data to determine whether the difficulty lies in input (reading, listening), processing (reasoning under load), or output (writing, producing).
    2. Reduce input load. Pre-teach structure with advance organizers, supply guided notes for annotating, and segment content into manageable units Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S].
    3. Adjust pacing in interaction. Extend wait time after questions, slow delivery of novel information, and repeat key points across modalities Media combinations affect recall and retention. [~M].
    4. Accommodate output. Allow extended time, speech-to-text, or oral responses on tasks where the goal is reasoning rather than writing fluency.
    5. Build fluency deliberately. Schedule short, frequent, timed practice on component skills so automaticity develops and the accommodation becomes less necessary Part-task practice reduces load for novices. [+M].
    6. Fade and monitor. Use check-ins and assessment for learning to review which accommodations remain necessary, withdrawing supports as automaticity grows Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M].

    Related Strategies¶

    • Achievable micro-goals — breaking tasks into small steps reduces the per-step processing burden and makes progress visible
    • Acoustics and noise management — reducing auditory distraction preserves processing resources for the task itself
    • Accessible vocabulary and syntax — lowering linguistic processing demands complements pacing adjustments at the input stage

    Examples¶

    • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) — pauseable, self-paced video with transcript controls lets learners set their own processing rate rather than the instructor's.
    • Microsoft Immersive Reader (https://www.microsoft.com/immersive-reader) — text-to-speech, syllabification, and line-focus tools that slow and structure text input for readers with slow decoding.
    • CAST UDL Guidelines (https://udlguidelines.cast.org) — the "timing" and "executive function" guidelines operationalize this strategy as universal design rather than individual exception.
    • College Board accommodations (https://accommodations.collegeboard.org) — documented extended-time (50% or 100%) accommodations on SAT/AP testing, the most widely researched formal implementation of this strategy Lovett, B. J. (2010). Extended time testing accommodations for students with disabilities: Answers to five fundamental questions. Review of Educational Research, 80(4), 611–638.
