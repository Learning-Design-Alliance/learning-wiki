    Precision Requests¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Precision Requests are a low-intensity classroom management strategy in which the teacher makes a clear, specific, positively stated request of a student ("Please begin the worksheet now"), pauses to give the student compliance time, and follows a pre-planned hierarchy of consequences — typically a soft reprimand, then a choice, then a more formal consequence — if compliance does not occur. The technique, developed by Kame'enui and Darch, replaces vague, repeated, or escalating verbal directives with a single precise request and a predictable follow-through sequence.

    Design Implications¶

    Precision Requests work because they reduce the ambiguity and social friction of teacher directives: a single, specific, positively framed request paired with consistent follow-through functions as a clear discriminative stimulus for behavior Behaviorism [+M]. The built-in wait time and planned consequence hierarchy prevent the reinforcement cycle in which repeated nagging or emotional escalation inadvertently maintains off-task behavior [-M]. Consistency across requests is essential — the strategy's effectiveness depends on the student's ability to predict what follows compliance and non-compliance [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Requests that are brief, specific, and stated positively (what to do, not what to stop doing)
    • A deliberate pause (roughly 5–10 seconds) after the request, giving the student time to comply
    • A pre-planned, low-key consequence hierarchy the teacher can apply calmly and consistently
    • Praise or acknowledgment when the student complies, so compliance is reinforced

    Constraints¶

    • Inconsistent follow-through undermines the strategy rapidly — if consequences are applied unpredictably, requests lose their signaling value [-S]
    • Repeating or rephrasing a request ("I asked you to start… how many times…") converts it into nagging, which trains students to wait for escalation [-M]
    • Less effective when the underlying behavior is maintained by factors the request cannot address (anxiety, skill deficits, escape-motivated behavior tied to task difficulty) [~M] — pair with academic supports such as Scaffolding in those cases
    • Requires teacher composure; delivered with anger or sarcasm, the request becomes a reprimand and loses its precision [-W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Whole-class framing: group precision requests ("Everyone, pencils down and eyes on me") for transitions
    • Choice-embedded requests: offering two compliant options ("You may start with the even or the odd problems") to preserve student Autonomy [+W]
    • Tiered use: as a Tier 1 universal practice for all students, or intensified within individual behavior support plans

    Target Learners¶

    • K–12 students, particularly in elementary settings where teacher-managed transitions dominate [+M]
    • Students with attention or behavioral difficulties, for whom vague or repeated directives provide poor behavioral cues [+M]
    • Less appropriate as a standalone response for students whose off-task behavior reflects unmet academic needs rather than unclear expectations [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Classroom conduct and engagement: increasing time on task and compliance with instructional routines
    • Self-regulation: predictable request–consequence contingencies help students internalize behavioral expectations
    • Instructional efficiency: protecting instructional time by reducing teacher–student conflict cycles

    Instructions¶

    1. State the request precisely — brief, specific, positively framed, one behavior at a time ("Please open your book to page 42").
    2. Pause and wait — allow 5–10 seconds of compliance time without repeating or rephrasing.
    3. Acknowledge compliance — deliver brief, specific praise so the behavior is reinforced.
    4. If no compliance, deliver a soft reprimand — a quiet, private restatement of the request, avoiding public confrontation.
    5. If non-compliance continues, present a choice — two acceptable options paired with the stated consequence.
    6. Follow through calmly — apply the pre-planned consequence without further negotiation or emotional escalation.
    7. Reset the relationship — re-engage the student positively once the sequence concludes, so the strategy does not erode rapport.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Praise-and-ignore strategies — the reinforcement complement: compliance is strengthened when acknowledged
    • Proximity control — a lower-intensity precursor that often prevents the need for a formal request
    • Behavior-specific praise — the follow-through that makes compliance more likely next time

    Examples¶

    • Elementary transition routine: A teacher says "In one minute, everyone will line up at the door," waits, then delivers the precision request "Please line up now," and names students who comply — reducing transition time and off-task talk.
    • Individual behavior plan: A student who refuses to begin independent work receives a single precision request with wait time, then a choice ("Start the first problem now, or work with me at the side table"), followed by a planned consequence — replacing the previous cycle of repeated verbal escalation.
    • Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS): Precision Requests are commonly included in Tier 1 and Tier 2 teacher training within schoolwide PBIS frameworks (https://www.pbis.org).
