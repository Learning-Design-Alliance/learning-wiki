    Fidget Utilization Protocol¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Fidgets are tools to aid focus and self-regulation, not toys. This strategy establishes explicit rules for using fidgets at home or in the classroom: being mindful of when a fidget is needed, using it only to focus or calm down, not distracting others, and returning it to a designated place after use. Learners experiment with different fidget types to find what works, and the rules are often formalized in a signed fidget contract.

    Design Implications¶

    The protocol treats fidget use as a trained behavior rather than an open accommodation. Evidence for fidget tools is genuinely mixed: task-relevant movement in children with ADHD is associated with better working memory performance, suggesting movement can serve a regulatory function for this population Sarver et al. found hyperactive movement predicted working memory performance in children with ADHD. [~M]. But unstructured fidget use — especially with visually interesting objects — can reduce attention for typically developing learners and for children without ADHD Fidget spinners impaired attention in children without ADHD. [-S]. The protocol's rules exist precisely to capture the potential benefit while limiting the distraction cost: quiet, non-novel, tactile-only objects, used at the learner's discretion, with a clear return-to-storage routine.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A small set of quiet, visually simple fidgets (putty, textured strips, foot bands) — novelty and visual appeal undermine the purpose
    • Explicit, co-created rules covering when, how, and where use is permitted
    • A designated storage place and a return routine
    • A written contract signed by learner and instructor, reviewed periodically
    • Open communication so the learner can report whether a fidget helps or distracts

    Constraints¶

    • Fidgets used as toys — spun, shown to peers, dropped loudly — degrade on-task behavior for the user and nearby peers Fidget spinners impaired attention in children without ADHD. [-S]
    • No reliable benefit for typically developing learners without attention difficulties; universal fidget access tends to add distraction rather than focus [-M]
    • Requires sustained commitment from learner and instructor; abandoned contracts leave the fidget functioning as a toy
    • Visually engaging fidgets compete for the same attentional resources as the lesson itself Decorative or attention-grabbing visuals do not improve learning. [-M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Individual contract (one learner, negotiated rules) vs. whole-class protocol with shared norms
    • Choice boards offering several fidget types during a trial week, then narrowing to what works
    • Alternative movement channels for learners who need more: Act It Out tasks, standing desks, or scheduled movement breaks aligned with evidence that classroom physical activity can improve attention Classroom physical activity improves attention. [+M]

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners with ADHD, for whom mild task-relevant movement may support working memory during demanding tasks [~M]
    • Learners with sensory processing or self-regulation difficulties, as one component of broader Accommodations
    • Not recommended as a default for whole-class use or for learners without attention difficulties [-S]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Self-regulation: recognizing when attention is drifting and selecting a tool rather than disrupting others
    • Executive function: building the metacognitive habit of matching a strategy to a need
    • Sustained attention during seatwork, reading, and independent practice

    Instructions¶

    1. Introduce the purpose. Use Direct Instruction to explain that fidgets are focus tools, not toys, and model correct vs. incorrect use.
    2. Co-create rules. Negotiate with the learner: when use is permitted (e.g., during independent work, not during discussion), what "quiet hands" means, and the return-to-storage routine.
    3. Trial and select. Let the learner experiment with 3–4 fidget types for a week; keep the ones that go unnoticed by others and unremembered by the learner's eyes.
    4. Formalize. Write and sign a fidget contract; post rules visibly at home or in the classroom.
    5. Practice the routine. Rehearse taking out, using, and returning the fidget via Practice until the routine is automatic.
    6. Review. Use brief Check-Ins weekly: does the fidget help, distract, or no longer matter? Adjust or fade accordingly.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Movement Breaks — a higher-intensity alternative channel for the same regulatory need
    • Check-In — the monitoring routine that keeps fidget use honest and adjustable

    Related Elements¶

    • Accommodations — the fidget protocol is one individualized accommodation within a broader support plan
    • Practice — the take-out/use/return routine must be rehearsed like any other procedure

    Examples¶

    • A family sets up a fidget basket at the homework table with a laminated rules card; the child signs a contract and returns fidgets to the basket each evening.
    • A second-grade teacher implements class-wide fidget contracts: each student who opts in keeps one putty egg in their desk, used only during independent reading, with a weekly check-in on whether it helps.
