# Question Formulation Technique (QFT)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
The Question Formulation Technique (QFT), developed by Dan Rothstein and Luz Santana of the Right Question Institute, is a structured procedure in which learners generate, improve, and prioritize their own questions. Learners are presented with a Question Focus (QFocus) — a deliberately neutral stimulus such as a statement, image, or video that is answered by questions, not by discussion — and produce questions under four rules: ask as many questions as possible; do not stop to discuss, judge, or answer; write every question down exactly as stated; and change any statement into a question. The sequence moves from divergent production to convergent refinement (categorizing questions as open- or closed-ended, converting between types, prioritizing against a stated purpose) and closes with metacognitive reflection on what the questioning process taught them. The facilitator enforces the process without evaluating or improving the content of questions, preserving learner ownership.

## Design Implications

QFT treats question-asking as a teachable skill rather than a fixed disposition, inverting the usual pattern in which instructors ask and students answer. The no-judgment rule during generation is the load-bearing design decision: it separates divergent production from evaluation, protecting fluency from premature criticism [~W]. The later convergent phase — categorizing, converting, and prioritizing — is where quality emerges, and it doubles as metacognitive practice [Self-questioning and self-explanation during learning improve comprehension.](../claims/self-questioning-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Because learners choose which questions to pursue, QFT supports autonomy and ownership of inquiry [Autonomy-supportive learning environments strengthen intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-designed QFocus: a stimulus that provokes multiple questions but is not itself a question, and does not reveal a preferred answer
- Time for the full sequence (typically 30–60 minutes); compressing it to generation alone forfeits the convergent and reflective phases
- A facilitator who enforces the four rules while remaining neutral about question content — offering examples or "better" questions undermines the technique's purpose
- A concrete next step (research task, discussion, experiment) that uses the prioritized questions, so questioning connects to [Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)
- A carefully designed **QFocus** — a prompt, image, data set, or provocation that stimulates questions but is *not itself a question*
- Clear ground rules for the generation phase: write as many questions as possible, do not stop to discuss or answer, record every question verbatim
- Time and structure for the full cycle: generate → categorize (open vs. closed) → improve → prioritize → plan use → reflect
- A genuine downstream purpose for the questions (inquiry project, discussion, research, interview) so prioritized questions are actually pursued

#### Constraints
- Learners with little topical background knowledge may generate shallow or off-target questions; some prior [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of relevant knowledge improves question quality [~M]
- The no-judgment rule can feel artificial to learners accustomed to immediate evaluation, and facilitators frequently violate it by praising or editing questions
- Without a genuine follow-on task, prioritized questions become a dead-end exercise; questioning for its own sake shows weak transfer to later inquiry performance [-W]
- Groups with strong status hierarchies may converge on a dominant member's questions unless generation is individual before sharing
- A weak or leading QFocus produces shallow or off-target questions; the prompt carries most of the design burden [-W]
- If prioritized questions are never used, learners learn that question-asking is performative rather than consequential [-W]
- Groups with low trust may self-censor "risky" questions; anonymity or written-first protocols mitigate this [~W]
- The generation phase consumes substantial class time; for tightly content-constrained objectives, instructor-framed questions may be more efficient [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual generation followed by small-group pooling, versus whole-group generation from the start
- QFocus formats: text statements, images, data displays, primary sources, or scenarios
- Shortened "QFT-lite" (generate → prioritize → next steps) for single sessions; full protocol including open/closed conversion for extended inquiry units
- Learner-authored QFocus for advanced groups, shifting ownership further toward self-directed inquiry
- **Full protocol** (60+ min): complete cycle with reflection, used to launch projects or units
- **Mini-QFT** (10–15 min): generate and prioritize only, used to open a lesson or reading
- **Individual vs. group**: solo generation avoids anchoring on peers' questions; group generation increases the diversity of question types
- **Domain adaptations**: used for Socratic seminars in ELA, hypothesis generation in science labs, patient-interview preparation in medical education, and primary-source interrogation in history

### Target Learners
- K–12 through adult learners; the protocol scales with the sophistication of the QFocus rather than the learners' age
- Students who rarely volunteer questions — the written, non-judged format lowers the social risk of asking [~W]
- Professionals (educators, health workers, community organizers) using questions to clarify goals and advocate for themselves — the population for which the technique was originally developed
- Less effective for novices with no relevant prior knowledge, who lack the raw material to formulate substantive questions [~M]
- Adolescents through adults; the protocol scales across levels with prompt difficulty
- English learners and quieter students, when questions are written before being spoken, since the protocol legitimizes partial or imperfect phrasing [+W]
- Less suitable as a first exposure for complete novices in a technical domain, who lack the schema to generate productive questions [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Question-asking fluency and question quality as explicit learning outcomes
- Divergent thinking (generation), convergent thinking (prioritization), and metacognitive reflection in a single sequence
- Preparation for inquiry, research, and discussion — supplying learner-generated questions that drive subsequent [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) or investigation
- Civic self-advocacy: the original purpose — formulating questions to navigate institutions and decisions
- Inquiry and research skills: formulating answerable, investigable questions
- Metacognition: monitoring one's own understanding ("what don't I know yet?")
- Epistemic agency: taking ownership of the direction of learning
- Discussion and civic participation: preparing questions for interviews, seminars, and public engagement

### Instructions
1. Design a QFocus tied to the unit's central objective; test it by brainstorming the questions it should provoke.
2. Present the QFocus and the four rules; give groups 5–10 minutes to generate questions without discussion or judgment.
3. Have groups categorize questions as open- or closed-ended, discuss the value of each type, and practice converting between them.
4. Ask groups to prioritize 3 questions against a specific criterion (most important for the research task, most debatable, etc.).
5. Move prioritized questions into the next activity — [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), research, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) — so the questions do real work.
6. Close with reflection: what did learners learn from producing and choosing questions, and how might they use the process again?

## Examples
- **[Right Question Institute](https://rightquestion.org)** — maintains the official QFT protocol, free classroom resources, and a large educator community; source of the original formulation in Rothstein & Santana (2011).
- **NHPRC/Smithsonian primary-source lessons** — students generate questions from historical documents as the entry point to document analysis, using the QFocus-as-image variant.
- **Health-care advocacy training** — the original adult-education application: patients formulate prioritized questions before medical appointments to improve information-gathering and self-advocacy.
- **Right Question Institute** — publishes the full QFT protocol, free classroom resources, and recorded exemplars across grade levels and disciplines: [rightquestion.org](https://rightquestion.org)
- **Science lab launch** — students view an anomalous demonstration (the QFocus) and generate questions; the class's prioritized questions become the hypotheses tested in the investigation.
- **Medical education** — before standardized-patient interviews, students generate and prioritize questions to ask, then debrief which questions yielded diagnostic information.
- **Primary-source analysis** — students examine an uncontextualized historical photograph and generate questions; the prioritized questions structure the subsequent document set.

5. **Prioritize.** Groups select 3 top questions against a criterion the instructor sets (most important, most investigable, most relevant to the task).
6. **Plan use.** Groups identify how the prioritized questions will drive the next activity — lab investigation, [case-based learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) discussion, research, or interview.
7. **Reflect.** Learners note what they learned about question-asking itself, closing the metacognitive loop.
