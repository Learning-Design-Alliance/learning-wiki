# Verbal Feedback on Writing

## Description
Verbal feedback uses voice recording (audio files, embedded audio in documents, or screen-capture narration) to deliver feedback on student writing in place of, or alongside, written comments. The instructor records spoken commentary — often keyed to symbols or numbers on the draft — typically opening with high expectations and a growth-oriented framing, and students listen to the recording before revising. Because speech conveys tone, nuance, and far more content per minute than typing, recorded commentary tends to be longer, more detailed, and more personal than written margin notes.

## Design Implications

Verbal feedback works because it increases feedback *uptake*: students report audio comments feel more personal and less judgmental, and they engage more often with the revision task [~M]. Its learning benefit depends on the content of the message, not the medium — feedback that addresses the task and the writing process outperforms feedback about the self [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. A growth-mindset opening only helps if the rest of the comment gives concrete, actionable next steps; praise alone does not improve writing.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A recording workflow the instructor can sustain (audio-in-document tools, LMS audio comments, or screen recording)
- Student access to devices for listening, and a routine that requires listening *before* revision
- A rubric or focus list (typically 2–3 priority areas per draft) so commentary targets what matters most
- A revision requirement — feedback listened to but not acted on produces little learning [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Requires recording technology and device access for all students; inequitable access undermines the approach
- Time-consuming for instructors on large-enrollment courses unless recordings are short and focused
- Audio is transient and hard to skim or search — students cannot easily re-locate a specific point the way they can with written comments; providing a short written summary of action items mitigates this [~M]
- Benefits shrink when feedback is generic praise or when students listen passively without a revision task [-M]
- Speech carries emotional tone that can be misread; overly critical delivery can feel more personal — and more wounding — than written notes

#### Implementation Variability
- **Audio-only comments** replacing written marks entirely
- **Hybrid**: brief written anchor comments plus a recorded narrative explaining priorities
- **Screen-capture feedback**: instructor talks while scrolling or annotating the draft on screen, linking commentary to specific passages
- **Peer audio feedback**: students record comments for one another using the same protocol
- **Conferencing alternative**: recorded feedback substitutes for in-person writing conferences when schedules don't allow them

### Target Learners
- Upper secondary through postsecondary students producing multi-draft writing [~M]
- Students who disengage from written margin comments; audio's conversational tone increases perceived instructor presence, especially in online and distance courses [+M]
- Less suitable for very young learners without independent device access, or for students with auditory processing or hearing accommodations unless transcripts are provided

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision and self-editing skills: turning feedback into concrete revisions [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Growth mindset and writing self-efficacy: framing ability as improvable through revision [+W]
- Instructor presence and relationship-building in online writing instruction [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Set the focus.** Identify 2–3 priority areas per draft from the rubric rather than commenting on everything.
2. **Record.** Open with a high-expectation, growth-oriented statement, then give specific task- and process-level commentary tied to marked passages; keep recordings to 3–5 minutes.
3. **Deliver.** Embed audio in the returned document or LMS; include a one-line written list of required actions so students can re-locate key points.
4. **Require revision.** Students listen, then revise; a brief revision memo naming what they changed closes the loop.
5. **Follow up selectively.** Use short [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) or 5-minute writing conferences for students whose revisions stall.

## Examples
- **Online composition courses**: Instructors return drafts with embedded audio comments (via Word/LMS audio annotation or tools such as [Kaizena](https://kaizena.com)), and students submit a revision memo listing changes made in response to each comment.
- **Screen-cast feedback**: An instructor records a 4-minute screencast scrolling through a research paper, narrating strengths and the two highest-priority revisions; the student revises before the next draft.
- **Hybrid marking protocol**: The instructor writes only symbols (①②③) on the draft and records spoken explanations for each symbol, cutting marking time while increasing comment length.
