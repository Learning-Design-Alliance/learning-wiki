# Peer Mediation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Peer mediation trains selected students in a structured conflict-resolution protocol — typically opening, storytelling, issue identification, solution generation, and agreement — so they can facilitate disputes between classmates as neutral third parties. The mediator does not judge or impose solutions; the disputants generate and own the agreement. Programs are usually whole-school: a cadre of trained mediators, a referral system, adult supervision, and classroom instruction in conflict-resolution skills for all students.

## Design Implications

Peer mediation operationalizes [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md): mediators model constructive conflict behavior for the wider school community, and disputants rehearse perspective-taking and negotiation in a low-stakes, peer-run setting. Reviews find that programs can reduce referrals and suspensions and improve students' conflict-resolution knowledge, but effects on actual conflict behavior are modest and depend heavily on implementation quality [Johnson & Johnson, 1996](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066004459) [~M]. Because mediators must listen, reframe, and remain neutral under emotional pressure, training should include [Role-Play](role-play.md) with coached practice, not just instruction in the protocol steps.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Systematic mediator training (typically 10–20 hours) including the mediation script, active listening, neutrality, and confidentiality
- [Role-Play](role-play.md) rehearsals with feedback from adults before mediators take live cases
- A visible referral process and a private space for mediations
- Adult coordinator who supervises, screens cases (excluding bullying, violence, or serious offenses), and provides ongoing [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)
- Whole-school buy-in so staff refer conflicts rather than only adjudicating them

#### Constraints
- Inappropriate for conflicts involving power imbalance — bullying, harassment, or abuse — where neutrality is impossible and mediation can re-victimize the target [Smith, Daunic, & Miller, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.10035) [-M]
- Effects on school-wide climate are inconsistent; programs with weak training, low referral rates, or no adult coordination show little benefit [Johnson & Johnson, 1996](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066004459) [~M]
- Agreements are often short-lived without follow-up; unresolved or recurring disputes need adult intervention
- Younger students can learn the protocol but struggle with the neutrality and reframing demands; expect more adult support in elementary settings

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mediator-selected cadre** vs. **whole-class training**: training all students in conflict-resolution skills spreads benefits but loses the formal mediation structure; cadre models preserve the protocol but concentrate skills in a few students
- **Same-age vs. cross-age mediation**: older students mediating younger disputes simplifies neutrality and builds leadership for the mediators
- **Stand-alone program vs. embedded curriculum**: programs paired with classroom instruction in negotiation and perspective-taking show broader skill transfer than referral-only models

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through secondary students, who have the perspective-taking capacity the mediator role demands
- Mediators benefit most: repeated authentic practice in listening, framing, and negotiation builds transferable communication skill [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Disputants in low-stakes peer conflicts (rumors, teasing, resource disputes) where neither party holds structural power
- Not appropriate as the primary response for students involved in bullying or harassment [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional skills: active listening, perspective-taking, emotional regulation during conflict
- Procedural knowledge: executing a multi-step facilitation protocol
- Transferable negotiation and communication skills applicable beyond school

### Instructions
1. Select and train a mediator cadre using the standard protocol, with heavy emphasis on [Role-Play](role-play.md) and adult [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)
2. Teach all students basic conflict vocabulary and the referral process so disputes reach mediation early
3. Screen referrals: route bullying, violence, and repeated conflicts to adults, not mediators
4. Conduct mediations in pairs (one mediator facilitates, one records) with a private space and a written agreement
5. Follow up on agreements within a week; provide [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and debrief mediators after each case
6. Review program data (referral rates, agreement durability, suspensions) with staff each term

## Related Strategies
- [Cooperative Learning](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md) — reduces the frequency of peer conflict by structuring positive interdependence, complementing mediation's reactive role
- [Restorative approaches](../patterns/collaborative-evaluation.md) — alternative conflict-response frame focused on repairing harm rather than negotiating interests

## Examples
- **Comprehensive school-wide programs** (e.g., the Community Boards program, San Francisco, https://communityboards.org) train student mediators and coordinate referrals school-wide; the model most heavily represented in the research base
- **Cross-age elementary mediation**: fifth- and sixth-grade mediators staff a playground mediation table during recess, handling disputes among younger students under a teacher-coordinator's supervision
