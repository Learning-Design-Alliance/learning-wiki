# Encourage Active Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Active learning positions learners as generators, not receivers, of cognitive work: they discuss, write, solve, teach, and apply material during instruction rather than only afterward. The strategy spans a continuum from simple (think-pair-share, minute papers) to complex (problem-based inquiry, peer instruction), but its defining feature is that learners must do something with the content — retrieve it, connect it to prior experience, or use it to produce an answer — while the instructor is present to guide and correct. Making the learning process itself explicit ("why we are doing this activity") is part of the strategy, not an add-on.

## Design Implications

Active learning reliably improves performance relative to lecture-only instruction, with the largest gains in conceptual understanding and failure-rate reduction [Active learning improves exam performance and reduces failure rates.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The mechanism is generative processing: activities that require learners to select, organize, and integrate information produce deeper encoding than reception alone [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Not all "activity" is equal, however — what matters is the cognitive work demanded, not physical or verbal busyness; a lively discussion that never moves beyond opinion produces less learning than a quiet written reconstruction of an argument [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tasks that require a generative act: answering, predicting, explaining, applying, or critiquing — not merely attending
- Instructor modeling of the target behaviors (asking questions, listening, giving feedback) so learners know what active engagement looks like
- Time and structure: activities must be bounded, with a clear product, and followed by instructor synthesis or feedback
- Psychological safety for discussion and error-making, since active tasks surface misconceptions publicly

#### Constraints
- Increases preparation load and demands skilled facilitation; poorly run activities consume time without producing learning [~M]
- Novices can be overloaded by open-ended inquiry with minimal guidance; unguided discovery underperforms guided forms of active learning [-S]
- Students accustomed to passive roles may resist and rate active formats lower even while learning more — perceived effort and felt learning can diverge from actual learning gains [-M]
- Large classes and fixed lecture halls constrain some formats, though peer-discussion variants scale well

#### Implementation Variability
- **Individual** — minute papers, predict-then-observe, self-explanation prompts, muddiest-point reflections
- **Pair/small group** — think-pair-share, peer instruction with clicker questions, jigsaw reading
- **Whole class** — structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), debate, case analysis
- **Course-level** — flipped designs move exposition outside class and reserve contact time for active work ([Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md))

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and adult learners, where the evidence base is strongest [Active learning improves exam performance and reduces failure rates.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Learners with some prior knowledge, who have enough schema to generate and test ideas; complete novices need more structure first [~M]
- Struggling students benefit disproportionately — active formats narrow achievement gaps relative to lecture [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and misconception repair — active tasks expose and confront faulty models
- Application and transfer — using knowledge in novel or authentic situations ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Metacognition and self-regulation — reflection and self-monitoring built into activities [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Open with a task, not a transmission: pose a question, prediction, or short problem before explaining ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
2. Give learners an individual generative step — write, solve, or commit to an answer ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
3. Structure peer interaction: compare answers, defend reasoning, reach consensus ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
4. Have groups or individuals report out; make thinking visible and respond to surfaced misconceptions ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
5. Close with instructor synthesis that connects activity outcomes to the target concepts — the debrief, not the activity itself, consolidates learning
6. Name the process: tell learners why the activity is structured this way so the strategy itself becomes learnable

## Examples
- **Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — students answer a conceptual question individually, discuss with neighbors, then re-answer; widely adopted in physics and beyond ([Peer Instruction](https://www.peerinstruction.net))
- **Flipped STEM courses** — pre-class videos replace lecture; class time runs problem-solving workshops ([Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md))
- **Case-based teaching** — learners analyze real scenarios and defend decisions, as in business and medical education ([Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md))
- **Tutoring context** — a tutor gives tutees real-life situations to analyze and models active listening and questioning behaviors, making the learning process explicit
