# Electricity and Circuit Tasks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Electricity and circuit tasks are hands-on physical science activities in which learners investigate how electricity flows by building, testing, and troubleshooting simple circuits. Learners use tools such as electric testers, batteries, bulbs, wires, and puzzle boards to identify complete circuits, predict where current will flow, and test their predictions against observable outcomes. The strategy pairs physical manipulation with prediction-and-explanation cycles, so that manipulation of materials is deliberately connected to conceptual reasoning about current, closed loops, and conductivity.

## Design Implications

Hands-on circuit tasks support conceptual change because learners' intuitive models of electricity (e.g., current being "used up" by a bulb) are directly confronted by observable outcomes [~M]. The tasks work best when prediction precedes testing: asking learners to commit to a prediction before closing the circuit creates the cognitive conflict that drives revision of naive models. Unguided exploration alone rarely produces correct scientific models; tasks should be structured with explicit prompts to explain *why* a circuit works or fails [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Physical materials: batteries, bulbs, wires, electric testers, circuit or puzzle boards
- Structured prediction sheets or prompts that require learners to commit to an answer before testing
- A discussion phase in which results are explained, not just recorded ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Safety rules appropriate to low-voltage materials (battery-only circuits for elementary learners)

#### Constraints
- Access to specialized materials limits scalability; shared kits reduce individual prediction-and-test cycles [-W]
- Without structured prompts, learners manipulate materials without connecting actions to concepts, and naive models persist [-M]
- Learners often interpret a lit bulb as evidence of their prior model rather than revising it; teacher facilitation is needed to force model comparison [-M]
- Misconceptions about electricity are remarkably resistant to brief hands-on exposure; single sessions are insufficient for durable conceptual change [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mystery Card format (NYSED):** learners use an electric tester to determine where electricity flows between points on a card, turning circuit logic into a deduction puzzle
- **Pathfinder puzzle boards (KDE):** learners identify complete circuits on a board, supporting self-paced [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with immediate feedback
- **Predict–Observe–Explain cycles:** each task begins with a written prediction, followed by testing and explanation
- **Complexity scaling:** add series/parallel combinations, switches, or conductivity testing of unknown materials for advanced learners

### Target Learners
- K–4 learners studying physical science, for whom concrete manipulation is developmentally appropriate [~M]
- Learners holding common naive models of electricity (current consumption, unipolar flow), which the tasks are designed to surface and challenge [~M]
- Older learners benefit when tasks move beyond identification to designing circuits that meet constraints

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of closed circuits, current flow, and conductivity
- Prediction and hypothesis-testing skills: stating, testing, and revising claims against evidence
- Problem-solving and troubleshooting: diagnosing why a circuit fails
- Science process skills: systematic testing and evidence-based reasoning

### Instructions
1. Present the materials and pose a concrete challenge (e.g., "Make the bulb light" or "Find all the paths where electricity flows") ([Application](../elements/application.md))
2. Require each learner or pair to record a prediction before touching the materials ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or prediction sheets)
3. Have learners build and test the circuit, observing the outcome ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
4. Prompt learners to explain discrepancies between prediction and outcome, connecting observations to a model of current flow ([Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S])
5. Discuss results as a group, contrasting naive and scientific models ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
6. Increase complexity — series vs. parallel, switches, unknown conductors — as models stabilize ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))

## Examples
- **Mystery Card (NYSED elementary science assessment tasks):** learners use an electric tester to determine where electricity flows between circles on a mystery card, combining circuit logic with deductive reasoning
- **Pathfinder (KDE):** learners identify complete circuits on puzzle boards, receiving immediate feedback from whether the bulb lights
- **FOSS Magnetism and Electricity module (Lawrence Hall of Science):** structured circuit-building investigations with prediction sheets for grades 3–5 ([https://www.fossweb.com](https://www.fossweb.com))
