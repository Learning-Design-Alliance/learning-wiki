# KWL Chart

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A KWL chart is a three-column graphic organizer completed across a lesson: before instruction, students list what they already **Know** about a topic and what they **Want** to know; after instruction, they record what they **Learned**. Developed by Donna Ogle for expository reading, it functions as a pre-reading activation routine, a goal-setting device, and a post-hoc self-assessment in a single low-cost artifact.

## Design Implications

KWL works because it forces retrieval of prior knowledge before new material arrives, making existing schemas available for assimilation and surfacing misconceptions the instructor can address [Activation of prior knowledge improves comprehension of new material.](../principles/activation.md) [+M]. The W column converts passive reception into goal-directed reading — students attend selectively to information that answers their own questions. The L column prompts learners to compare outcomes against their initial questions, a form of self-monitoring that supports comprehension [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible chart (paper, whiteboard, or digital) with K, W, and L columns
- Time before instruction for genuine K and W generation — rushing this step reduces the routine to fill-in-the-blank compliance
- Instructional content that plausibly answers the questions students raise; the instructor should be prepared to address misconceptions surfaced in the K column
- A structured debrief when completing the L column, ideally asking students to justify each entry ("How do you know?")

#### Constraints
- The K column can *reinforce* misconceptions if students list inaccurate "facts" that are never explicitly corrected during instruction [-M]
- The L column invites copying from the text rather than generative summarization; without prompts to paraphrase or connect, entries become shallow extraction [-W]
- Effectiveness drops when the W column is ignored — if instruction does not visibly answer students' questions, the goal-setting benefit is lost and engagement declines [~M]
- Less effective for topics where students have genuinely no prior knowledge to activate; the K column then produces guessing that may be encoded as fact [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **KWL+ / KWHL**: add a fourth column — *How* will I find out (KWHL) or *How* does this connect to what I know (KWL+) — to make the inquiry process explicit
- **KWLQ**: a final *Question* column for new questions raised, sustaining inquiry beyond the lesson
- **Digital variants**: shared documents or tools like Padlet allow collaborative K/W brainstorming and revisiting the chart asynchronously
- **Individual vs. collective**: whole-class charts surface diverse prior knowledge and misconceptions for discussion; individual charts elicit more honest self-assessment

### Target Learners
- Elementary and secondary students reading expository text, where the routine was developed and most validated [~M]
- Multilingual learners, when the K column lets them contribute knowledge in either language and lowers the entry barrier to a new topic
- Less valuable for advanced learners with well-organized domain knowledge, for whom the K column adds little and may feel redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Prior knowledge activation and misconception diagnosis before instruction
- Purpose-setting and question formulation for reading or inquiry
- Metacognitive monitoring: comparing what was learned against what was wanted
- Summarization and consolidation of expository content

### Instructions
1. Distribute or display the chart and introduce the topic. Have students brainstorm and record what they **Know** — treat this as [Activation](../elements/activation.md), and flag any misconceptions to revisit later.
2. Students generate questions for the **Want** column; cluster and prioritize them as a class so instruction can visibly address them.
3. Deliver the learning experience (reading, lecture, video, investigation), referring back to the W questions to maintain purpose.
4. Students complete the **Learned** column in their own words, matching each entry to a W question where possible — a form of [Application](../elements/application.md) of new knowledge.
5. Debrief: compare K and L columns, explicitly correct misconceptions from the K column, and record remaining questions.

## Examples
- **Expository reading (Ogle's original use)**: before a unit on rainforests, students list known facts and questions in K and W; after reading, they complete L and verify which questions were answered. See [Reading Rockets' KWL overview](https://www.readingrockets.org/classroom/classroom-strategies/k-w-l).
- **Understood.org** — [KWL charts as an accommodation](https://www.understood.org/en/classroom-resources/classroom-strategies/kwl-charts) for students with learning and attention differences, providing structure for organizing new information.
- **Science inquiry (KWHL)**: before a unit on electricity, students add a "How will we find out?" column, turning the chart into a plan for hands-on investigation.
