# Structured Discussion Techniques

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structured discussion techniques are routines and supports — talk protocols, discussion roles, sentence starters, and norms — that organize peer conversation around academic content. Rather than relying on learners to spontaneously produce productive dialogue, the instructor makes the *moves* of good discussion explicit and routinized, so that [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) can run productively even without direct instructor supervision.

## Design Implications

Structured discussion converts discussion from an unguided activity into a scaffolded one: norms, roles, and language frames reduce the social and cognitive demands of participation, freeing attention for the content itself [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The structure is most powerful when it is modeled and then faded — instructors demonstrate discussion moves (rephrasing, challenging respectfully, citing evidence) before expecting learners to use them independently. Sentence starters are particularly valuable for English language learners and reluctant participants, lowering the linguistic barrier to entry without lowering the intellectual demand [Scaffolding supports learning by reducing unproductive cognitive load.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit norms and expectations for talk, established and revisited over time
- Modeling of discussion moves by the instructor ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of reasoning and respectful disagreement)
- Language supports: sentence frames, word banks, or discussion prompts matched to learners' proficiency
- A meaningful task or text worth discussing — structure cannot compensate for low-quality content
- Repeatable routines so learners invest attention in ideas, not in decoding a new protocol each time

#### Constraints
- Over-structuring can suppress authentic dialogue; learners may follow the protocol's form without genuine engagement with ideas [-M]
- Rigid protocols disadvantage learners whose conversational styles differ from the norm; norms must be explicitly inclusive, not just enforced [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized learners.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Discussions dominated by high-status voices persist unless roles and turn structures actively redistribute participation [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when the discussion task has no genuine problem or ambiguity to resolve

#### Implementation Variability
- **Protocol-based**: fixed routines such as think-pair-share, Socratic seminar, or gallery walk, with defined turns and timing
- **Role-based**: assigned roles (facilitator, recorder, skeptic, summarizer) that rotate across sessions ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md))
- **Language-scaffolded**: sentence starters and frames ("I agree with ___ because…", "What evidence supports…") scaled to learner proficiency
- **Written-first hybrids**: brief individual writing before discussion raises the quality of oral contributions and broadens participation

### Target Learners
- English language learners, who benefit most from sentence frames and modeled academic language [+M]
- Novices who lack the discussion norms of academic discourse and need them made explicit
- Reluctant or low-status participants, when structures guarantee turns and roles
- Less necessary for learners already fluent in academic discussion, for whom heavy scaffolds can feel constraining [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through verbal elaboration and peer explanation
- Reasoning and argumentation skills [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Academic language development and discourse competence
- Collaborative and social norms of intellectual exchange

### Instructions
1. Establish and post discussion norms; co-construct them with learners where possible.
2. Model the target discussion moves explicitly, narrating your reasoning as you demonstrate ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Prepare a genuine task — a problem, text, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) with real ambiguity.
4. Provide language supports: sentence starters, word banks, or prompt cards matched to learner needs.
5. Run the discussion using a routine (think-pair-share, role-based [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or [Debate](../patterns/debate.md)), with [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) moments to monitor understanding.
6. Debrief both content and process: what claims were made, and which discussion moves were effective?
7. Fade supports over time — remove sentence frames and assigned roles as learners internalize the discourse.

## Examples
- **Socratic seminar** — a text-based discussion protocol in which learners question each other using evidence from the text, with an inner/outer circle structure that makes participation observable and equitable.
- **Think-Pair-Share** — individual thinking, rehearsal with a partner, then whole-class sharing; the staged structure lowers the risk of public contribution.
- **[Facing History and Ourselves](https://www.facinghistory.org)** — publishes classroom discussion protocols (barometer, big paper, identity charts) with sentence starters designed for discussing difficult social topics.
- **[EL Education](https://eleducation.org)** curriculum — embeds structured academic talk routines ("Think-Write-Pair-Share," discussion sentence frames) throughout its K–12 language arts modules, with explicit scaffolds for English language learners.
