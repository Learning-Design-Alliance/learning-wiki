# Connect Writing to Real-World Applications

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Instructors make explicit how writing functions in learners' lives, communities, and future careers — through authentic tasks, professional examples, and guest speakers who describe the role writing plays in their work. The strategy draws on utility-value research: learners persist and perform better when they see course content as useful to their goals [Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009] [+S].

## Design Implications

Relevance connections work best when they are made by learners themselves or tied to their specific interests, not merely asserted by the instructor [Hulleman & Harackiewicz, 2009] [+S]. Generic "you'll need this someday" messaging shows weak effects; concrete, discipline-specific examples and authentic audiences produce stronger gains in motivation and writing quality [authentic audiences and purposes increase engagement and effort.](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Real examples of professional writing in the target genre (reports, proposals, publications, workplace correspondence)
- Guest speakers or practitioner artifacts that make the connection concrete, not abstract
- Writing tasks that simulate real communicative situations with a genuine purpose and audience
- Knowledge of learners' career interests so connections can be personalized

#### Constraints
- Instructor-asserted relevance without learner buy-in can backfire, especially for learners with low initial interest or negative self-perceptions of writing ability [~M] — forced relevance messaging reads as inauthentic
- Guest speakers and professional examples are hard to source for every career interest in a diverse class; over-reliance on a few professions alienates other learners
- Connections that emphasize external utility alone can crowd out intrinsic interest in writing itself [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Utility-value writing prompts**: learners briefly explain in their own words how a writing skill applies to their life or intended career — a low-cost adaptation with meta-analytic support [+S]
- **Practitioner modeling**: professionals share and annotate real workplace documents, functioning as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of expert writing in context
- **Authentic assignments**: tasks addressed to real audiences (letters to officials, proposals to community organizations, lab reports in publication format) rather than simulated ones
- **Career-interview assignments**: learners interview a professional in their field of interest about the writing the role requires

### Target Learners
- Older secondary and postsecondary learners who are forming career identities and can articulate future goals [+S]
- Learners with low perceived value of writing tasks, for whom utility-value interventions show the largest motivational gains [+S]
- Less effective for young learners whose career concepts are still abstract; concrete near-term purposes (audience, publication) work better [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: increasing value appraisals of writing tasks
- Transfer: applying writing skills to authentic communicative situations [Application of knowledge in authentic contexts supports transfer.](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Genre knowledge: understanding how purpose, audience, and conventions vary across professional contexts

### Instructions
1. Identify the real-world genres and audiences native to the discipline and to learners' likely career paths.
2. Present a practitioner example or invite a professional to discuss and annotate real workplace writing, making expert decisions visible ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
3. Design a writing task with an authentic purpose and audience ([Application](../elements/application.md)), ideally one learners select or shape themselves to support autonomy [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].
4. Have learners articulate the connection — e.g., a short utility-value reflection on how the task relates to their goals ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. Provide feedback focused on how effectively the writing accomplishes its real-world purpose, not just its formal correctness [feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

## Examples
- A science instructor invites a working researcher to walk through a published paper and a rejected draft, showing how revision and clarity affect publication outcomes.
- An engineering program has students write incident reports addressed to an actual client or safety board, with feedback from a practicing engineer.
- A first-year composition course replaces a generic essay with a grant proposal or op-ed submitted to a real outlet, following the [National Writing Project](https://www.nwp.org) emphasis on authentic audiences.
- Hulleman & Harackiewicz's (2009) science intervention had students write brief connections between course material and their own lives — directly transferable to writing courses as utility-value reflections.
