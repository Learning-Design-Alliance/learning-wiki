    Teacher Action Research¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    A good teacher evaluates their own practice and reflects on how to improve — analyzing data, assessing student work, talking to students about what they found difficult, or asking a colleague to observe a lesson. This kind of evaluation shapes future planning, but it doesn't automatically count as research. Research is "systematic investigation towards increasing the sum of knowledge," and a piece of inquiry or evaluation work becomes research specifically when: the work involves capturing data (recording conversations or lessons, collecting student work as evidence) so it can be used outside the moment it happened; participants are asked to do something out of their ordinary routine (a focus group, a disruptive interview); and the output is public, which means the knowledge behind it needs to be defensible — based on reliable evidence, valid conclusions, and properly conducted work — before it can contribute anything to the wider field.

    The advantage of treating an inquiry as research rather than only as reflection is that its knowledge is evidence-based, can be defended and explained, and is more likely to be taken seriously than an account of personal experience alone; conducted over a meaningful stretch of time, it can reveal not just what works but why. This has further implications: the work should take account of other studies in the field (which also gives ideas for how to tackle the issue), and it should be systematic and purposeful, underpinned by a clear set of beliefs, specific research questions, and an ethical design likely to produce results others will consider valid.

    For a teacher investigating their own classroom, this is most often small-scale and short-term, and action research is the strategy best suited to it — best understood as a strategy rather than a single specific method (McNiff & Whitehead, 2011; Wilson, 2013), involving practitioners systematically investigating their own practice with a view to improving it. Action research is inherently cyclical, moving through repeated intervention and analysis until the practitioner genuinely understands the issue and has done something about it.

    Design Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A specific problem the teacher wants to solve in their own classroom — something as narrow as why certain students avoid answering questions, or as broad as how to organize group work effectively
    • Willingness to consult existing literature on the issue before designing an intervention, rather than intervening on intuition alone

    Constraints¶

    • Reflection and good practice alone (without captured data, an out-of-the-ordinary element, and a public, defensible output) don't rise to the level of research, even when genuinely valuable
    • A small-scale, single-classroom study cannot claim the same generalizability as larger, publicly-scrutinized educational research

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Case studies are the other methodology well-suited to small-scale, single-classroom teacher research, alongside action research

    Target Learners¶

    • Practicing teachers investigating and improving their own classroom practice, distinct from student-led inquiry (see Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR))

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • A specific, evidence-grounded understanding of one classroom problem, and a defensible account of what did or didn't help

    Instructions¶

    1. Identify a specific problem to solve in your own classroom.
    2. Define the purpose and clarify what the intervention might look like, consulting the existing literature on the issue.
    3. Plan an intervention designed to tackle the issue.
    4. Collect empirical data and analyze it.
    5. Plan another intervention based on what you found, to further understand the issue.
    6. Repeat the cycle — action research is cyclical, not a single pass.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) — a related but distinct methodology: student-led inquiry into their own communities, rather than teacher-led inquiry into their own classroom practice
    • Peer Observation — one data-collection method commonly used within an action research cycle
    • Build Time for Self-Reflection — the reflective habit action research systematizes into a research cycle

    Examples¶

    • A teacher noticing that certain students rarely volunteer answers, reviewing the literature on classroom participation, trying a cold-call-with-think-time intervention, collecting participation data, and refining the approach for the next cycle
