# Build Community Through Peer Feedback

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students regularly exchange structured feedback on one another's work and use it to revise. The strategy serves two goals simultaneously: improving work quality through iterative critique, and building a classroom community in which students see peers as legitimate sources of expertise and support rather than competitors.

## Design Implications

Peer feedback works when it is treated as formative [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) rather than evaluation: feedback is most effective when directed at the task and the process, not the person [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Structured peer feedback also benefits the giver — critiquing a peer's work requires evaluating it against criteria, which deepens the giver's own understanding of quality [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The community-building effect depends on psychological safety and repeated, reciprocal exchange; one-off peer review rarely changes classroom culture.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit criteria or rubrics so feedback targets the work, not the writer ([Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) or annotated exemplars)
- Training in giving constructive feedback — sentence stems, critique protocols (e.g., "warm/cool feedback"), and norms of respect
- Time and structure for students to act on feedback through [Revision](../elements/revision.md); feedback that is never used teaches students it was pointless
- Instructor monitoring of feedback quality, especially early on

#### Constraints
- Untrained students default to vague praise ("good job") or personal comments, which neither improves work nor builds trust [-M]
- Power and status dynamics can distort exchange: low-status students' feedback is discounted regardless of quality [~M]
- If feedback is graded or tied to evaluation, students hedge and withhold honest critique [-M]
- Feedback quality suffers when givers lack familiarity with the criteria or the content domain [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Gallery walks and [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) critiques for whole-group norm-building
- Written asynchronous exchange (discussion boards, document comments) for depth and reflection
- Reciprocal pairs or rotating small groups for relationship-building
- Author-recipient dialogue ("stars and wishes," critical friends protocols) to close the feedback loop

### Target Learners
- K–12 and higher-education learners; particularly valuable for students who experience the instructor as the sole authority [~M]
- Students with fragile academic confidence benefit from seeing peers struggle and improve [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less effective for novices in a domain who cannot yet judge quality — they need [worked examples](../principles/worked-examples.md) or annotated exemplars of "good feedback" first [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Formative improvement of drafts, projects, and presentations
- Development of evaluative judgment — internalizing criteria for quality work
- Collaborative and communication skills; classroom community and belonging
- Self-regulation: using external feedback to plan revisions [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish norms and train students in constructive critique using exemplars of strong and weak feedback.
2. Provide clear criteria or a rubric so feedback is anchored to the work, not the person.
3. Structure the exchange — pairs, rotating groups, or gallery walk — with a protocol and time limit.
4. Require recipients to respond: identify one change they will make and why.
5. Build in time for [Revision](../elements/revision.md) based on the feedback received.
6. Debrief the process itself ([class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)) to reinforce norms and improve feedback quality over time.

## Related Strategies
- Peer critique and revision cycles pair naturally with portfolio and draft-based writing approaches; see [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) for the underlying formative-assessment principle.

## Examples
- **El Education (Expeditionary Learning) "Critique and Revision" protocol** — students use kind/specific/helpful norms to critique drafts of expedition products before public exhibition ([https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)).
- **Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms** — algorithmically distribute submissions so each student gives and receives calibrated feedback on written work.
- **Writing workshop models (e.g., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project)** — regular peer conferring and author's chair sessions embed feedback in the writing cycle.
