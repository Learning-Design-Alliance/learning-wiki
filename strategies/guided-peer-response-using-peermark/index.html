Guided Peer Response using PeerMark™¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q3 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Guided peer response using Turnitin PeerMark™ involves instructors pre-setting specific questions — scaled items, open prompts, and criteria-linked prompts — that learners answer when providing feedback on a peer's draft. The prompts act as a review rubric embedded in the workflow, directing attention to the qualities that matter for the assignment and producing feedback that is collatable and supervisable at scale.

Design Implications¶

Structured prompts raise the specificity and actionability of peer feedback; unguided peer review tends to produce vague praise ("good essay") that does not support revision Peer assessment improves learning when feedback is specific and criteria-referenced. [+M]. Prompt design is the active ingredient: questions that ask reviewers to locate evidence in the text ("point to the sentence where the claim is weakest") produce more usable feedback than yes/no checklists. The reviewing act itself benefits the reviewer — evaluating a peer's work against criteria deepens the reviewer's own understanding of quality Peer assessment benefits reviewers as much as recipients. [+M], and in writing contexts structured peer response contributes to improved text quality on revision Collaborative writing and structured peer feedback improve text quality. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A Turnitin PeerMark™ (or equivalent) instance with the assignment draft submitted in advance
  • Review questions aligned to the assignment rubric, mixing scaled and open-response items
  • Time allocated in class or online for the review window, plus a revision task that requires learners to act on the feedback received
  • Brief training on giving constructive, criterion-referenced comments (Peer Interaction)

Constraints¶

  • Overly rigid prompts suppress the spontaneous, holistic reactions that sometimes catch problems a rubric misses Structured prompts narrow the range of issues reviewers notice. [~M]
  • Novice reviewers may answer prompts superficially to complete the task, generating low-quality feedback unless reviews are graded or sampled by the instructor
  • Prompt-writing is a significant instructor time investment; poorly worded questions ("Is this good?") reproduce the vagueness the structure was meant to fix
  • Anonymity settings matter: identifiable reviews can suppress candor, but fully anonymous review removes accountability for feedback quality

Implementation Variability¶

  • Scale-only, open-ended-only, or mixed question sets; mixed sets balance efficiency with depth
  • Calibrated review: show learners a sample draft with expert comments before their first review
  • Two-stage review — a quick scaled pass, then targeted open comments — reduces reviewer load
  • Instructor moderation: spot-check reviews and redistribute egregious or empty ones

Target Learners¶

  • Higher education and adult learners with drafts substantial enough to warrant criterion-based review
  • Novice writers, who benefit most from prompts that make implicit quality criteria explicit Structured criteria-referenced feedback improves achievement. [+M]
  • Less suitable for very short or low-stakes work, where review overhead exceeds value

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Formative feedback for writing quality: argument structure, evidence use, clarity (Formative Assessment)
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing criteria by applying them to others' work
  • Revision behavior: converting feedback into concrete improvements to a draft

Instructions¶

  1. Align review questions to the assignment rubric, ordering them from global (thesis, structure) to local (sentence-level issues) — global-first sequencing prevents reviewers from fixating on surface errors.
  2. Publish the draft assignment in Turnitin and configure the PeerMark™ review window with 2–4 reviewers per paper.
  3. Train reviewers briefly on constructive, evidence-pointing comments (Peer Interaction).
  4. Run the review; monitor early submissions and intervene on low-quality reviews (Provide Feedback).
  5. Return reviews and require a revision memo stating which comments the writer acted on and why — closing the loop makes feedback consequential (Formative Assessment).

Tools¶

  • Turnitin PeerMark™ — pre-set review questions, anonymous or attributed allocation, and instructor oversight of reviews

Examples¶

  • A first-year composition course configures PeerMark™ with four questions (claim clarity, evidence, organization, one-sentence revision suggestion) for a persuasive essay draft; writers then submit a revised version with a revision memo.
  • An online graduate course uses anonymous PeerMark™ review of literature-review drafts, with the instructor sampling 20% of reviews for quality and awarding participation credit for criterion-referenced comments.
