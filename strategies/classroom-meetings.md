# Classroom Meetings

## Description
A classroom meeting is a recurring, structured gathering — often daily or weekly — where the whole class discusses community issues, plans activities, solves interpersonal problems, and makes shared decisions. The teacher facilitates rather than directs, using protocols (greeting, sharing, problem-solving agenda items) that give every student voice. The format originates in Dewey's democratic education and Glasser's class meetings, and is institutionalized in programs such as [Responsive Classroom](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org) and the Tribes Learning Community.

## Design Implications

Classroom meetings operationalize the classroom as a community of practice, building the relational trust and shared norms on which collaborative learning depends. Regular meetings strengthen students' sense of belonging, which predicts engagement and persistence [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M], and shared decision-making supports autonomy, a core driver of intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Meetings also give teachers formative insight into classroom climate that individual check-ins miss.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A predictable structure and schedule (e.g., Responsive Classroom's greeting–sharing–activity–news sequence)
- Agreed norms for speaking and listening, taught explicitly ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
- A visible agenda or problem-solving protocol so discussion stays purposeful
- Teacher facilitation skill: open-ended questioning, neutral mediation, and willingness to share real decision-making power

#### Constraints
- Meetings without genuine decision authority become performative and erode trust [~M] — students detect token consultation quickly
- Poorly facilitated meetings can be dominated by a few voices, marginalizing quieter students [~M]; structured turn-taking or talking-piece protocols mitigate this
- Time cost is real: meetings displace instructional minutes, and benefits depend on the meeting actually improving climate and self-regulation rather than consuming time [~W]
- In classrooms with weak norms or unresolved conflict, open discussion can escalate rather than resolve problems [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Morning meeting** (Responsive Classroom): daily 15–25 minute opening ritual for K–8
- **Problem-solving class meeting** (Glasser): convened ad hoc around a specific behavioral or academic issue
- **Democratic meeting** (Sudbury/Summerhill tradition): full voting authority over class rules and budgets
- **Circle formats** (restorative practices): talking-piece circles for conflict repair and community building

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-school students, for whom community rituals strongly shape engagement [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students with weak self-regulation or social skills, who benefit from modeled and rehearsed prosocial routines
- Adolescents, when meetings carry authentic decision-making weight; token versions backfire with this age group [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional competencies: empathy, perspective-taking, conflict resolution
- Self-regulation and shared responsibility for classroom norms
- Civic and discussion skills: turn-taking, argument, collective decision-making
- Academic uses: goal-setting, reflection on [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) results, planning [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) work

### Instructions
1. Establish norms first: co-create speaking and listening rules and rehearse them ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Open with connection: a greeting round and brief sharing so every student speaks early ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
3. Work the agenda: address one or two student- or teacher-submitted items using a problem-solving protocol (identify the problem, brainstorm, agree on an action).
4. Close with commitment: restate decisions, assign follow-ups, and revisit them at the next meeting.
5. Fade facilitation: shift agenda-setting and chairing roles to students as they gain competence ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **[Responsive Classroom](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)** Morning Meeting — daily greeting, sharing, group activity, and news-and-announcements; supported by a three-year longitudinal study linking implementation fidelity to reading and math gains (Rimm-Kaufman et al., 2007).
- **Glasser-style problem-solving meetings** — a class convenes to address recurring disruption, brainstorming solutions and voting on a class agreement.
- **Restorative practice circles** in secondary schools — weekly talking-piece circles used to build community and repair harm, as implemented in Denver Public Schools' restorative justice program.
