# Restorative Practices

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (4 causal), `q3`–`q4` · 0 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Restorative Practices (RP) is a whole-school strategy that replaces punitive discipline with practices that build community and repair harm. It operates on two tiers: proactive community-building (circles, check-ins, affective language) that strengthens relationships before conflict occurs, and responsive repair (restorative conferences, mediation) that brings together those harmed and those responsible after harm occurs. The underlying assumption is that misbehavior is a violation of relationships, not rules, and that learning conditions improve when students feel connected and fairly treated.

## Design Implications

RP improves climate and can reduce exclusionary discipline, but effects on academic outcomes are mixed and depend on faithful, sustained implementation [Whole-school restorative interventions show inconsistent effects on academic outcomes.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~M]. Its learning-design value comes indirectly: students who feel a sense of belonging and psychological safety participate more, persist longer, and engage more deeply with instruction [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Teachers' relational tone matters at the micro-level — brief empathic communication cuts subsequent discipline incidents in experimental work [A brief empathic-discipline intervention reduces suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Training and ongoing coaching for all staff, not just discipline personnel — RP implemented by a few trained specialists fails
- Administrative commitment to reduce exclusionary consequences; RP layered on top of unchanged zero-tolerance policies produces cynicism
- Structured protocols (talking circles, restorative questions: *What happened? Who was affected? What is needed to repair harm?*)
- Protected time for community-building routines, not just reactive conferences
- Facilitator training in circle protocols, affective questioning ("What happened? Who was affected? What's needed to make things right?"), and conference facilitation
- Protected time for community-building routines, not only for responding to incidents
- School or program leadership willing to reduce reliance on exclusionary discipline, since classroom practice collapses when punitive channels remain the default
- Voluntary participation from the harmed party; restorative processes cannot be imposed as a lesser punishment

#### Constraints
- Effects on suspensions are inconsistent; some large evaluations (e.g., the RAND Pittsburgh RCT) found no district-wide reduction in suspensions and some increases in certain subgroups [~M]
- Poorly facilitated conferences can re-traumatize victims or pressure harmed parties into premature forgiveness [-W]
- Implementation without adult behavior change — teachers continuing punitive interactions while circles are added — produces no measurable benefit [-M]
- Time-intensive; circles and conferences consume instructional minutes, a real cost in high-stakes schedules [~W]
- Implementation without sustained training and leadership support produces superficial "circles" that do not change discipline outcomes [~M] — RAND's evaluation of the Pittsburgh initiative found improved school climate but no reduction in arrests or days lost to suspension in the first two years
- Can re-traumate victims if facilitators pressure participation or prioritize quick resolution over the harmed party's needs
- Inadequate facilitation can let dominant voices control circles, reproducing the inequities the approach aims to fix
- Poorly run restorative conferences can function as a lighter punishment students game, undermining accountability [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Proactive tier:** daily [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md), community circles, affective statements ("I noticed… I feel…") woven into instruction
- **Responsive tier:** restorative conferences, victim-offender mediation, harm-repair agreements
- **Whole-school vs. classroom-level:** classroom-only adoption limits effects to that teacher's relationships; whole-school adoption shifts norms but requires multi-year investment
- **Hybrid models:** RP combined with clear behavioral expectations outperforms RP as a replacement for all structure
- **Responsive tier:** restorative chats (teacher–student), mediation, formal conferences with families
- **Whole-school models:** International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP) SaferSanerSchools; discipline-code reform paired with practice training
- **Classroom-only adoption:** circles and restorative language without formal conference infrastructure — lower cost, weaker effect on school-level discipline disparities

### Target Learners
- Students at risk of exclusionary discipline; relational approaches keep them in class where instruction can reach them [A brief empathic-discipline intervention reduces suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]
- Students from marginalized groups disproportionately affected by suspensions; RP is frequently adopted as an equity strategy [~M]
- Learners whose engagement depends on feeling known — belonging is a precondition for risk-taking in discussion and collaborative work [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- K–12 students, especially in schools with high suspension rates or discipline disparities affecting students of color [~M]
- Adolescents developing perspective-taking and conflict-resolution skills [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Students whose sense of belonging has been damaged by exclusionary discipline; re-entry conferences after suspension support reintegration [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: perspective-taking, accountability, conflict resolution
- Climate conditions for academic goals: participation, attendance, persistence — RP serves academic objectives indirectly by stabilizing the learning environment
- Community norms: shared ownership of classroom rules and repair
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, accountability, conflict resolution
- Classroom climate: discourse norms, psychological safety, [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md)
- Civic and ethical reasoning: understanding harm, responsibility, and repair

### Instructions
1. **Train all adults** in affective language and restorative questions before introducing student-facing structures.
2. **Establish proactive routines** — open lessons with a brief [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) circle; use affective statements during instruction to build relational capital.
3. **Teach listening and dialogue skills** explicitly, using [active listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) as a taught skill rather than an assumed one.
4. **Respond to harm restoratively** — convene affected parties with a trained facilitator using the restorative questions; agree on repair actions with follow-up.
5. **Build empathy deliberately** across the curriculum, not only in discipline moments — see [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md).
6. **Monitor data** on referrals, suspensions, attendance, and climate surveys; adjust tier intensity based on evidence rather than ideology.

## Examples
- **Oakland Unified School District (CA)** — whole-school RP adoption since 2010 with a dedicated Office of Restorative Justice; associated with reduced suspensions for African American students in district reports ([ousd.org/restorative-justice](https://www.ousd.org/restorative-justice)).
- **Pittsburgh Public Schools / RAND study** — a 22-school randomized evaluation of the SaferSanerSchools Whole-School Change program; the most rigorous test to date, showing improved teacher–student relationships but mixed discipline effects ([rand.org](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2840.html)).
- **Classroom circles in EL instruction** — EL Education schools use crew circles as a daily proactive structure, integrating RP with academic discussion norms ([eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)).
- **[International Institute for Restorative Practices](https://www.iirp.edu)** — graduate programs and the SaferSanerSchools whole-school implementation program used in hundreds of schools.
- **Pittsburgh Public Schools (RAND study)** — a district-wide restorative practices initiative evaluated by Augustine et al. (2018), which found improved teacher–student relationships and school climate, with mixed effects on discipline outcomes.
- **Oakland Unified School District** — adopted restorative justice district-wide after a 2010s discipline reform; associated with reduced suspensions and narrowed racial discipline gaps in district reports.

## Description
Restorative practices build community and resolve conflict through structured dialogue in which all affected parties participate in understanding what happened, who was harmed, and how to repair the harm. The approach operates on two tiers: proactive community-building (circles, check-ins, affective language) that prevents conflict, and responsive facilitation (restorative conferences, mediation) that addresses harm when it occurs. It replaces exclusionary discipline — suspension and expulsion — with processes that keep students in community while holding them accountable.

## Design Implications

Restorative practices function as a learning design strategy because they create the relational safety and belonging that cognitive engagement depends on [Students who feel they belong persist and engage more.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Proactive circles and check-ins also serve instructional functions: they surface prior knowledge, build discourse norms, and give every student structured speaking turns that discussion alone often fails to distribute. ### Instructions
1. Establish proactive routines: run regular community circles and daily [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) so dialogue norms exist before conflict does.
2. Use affective language in everyday interactions — express feelings and ask about impact rather than issuing blame.
3. When harm occurs, hold a restorative chat or mediation with trained facilitation; identify harms, needs, and obligations rather than assigning punishment.
4. Convene a restorative conference for serious incidents, including affected parties and families, and agree on a written repair plan with follow-up.
5. Support re-entry after any exclusion with a restorative re-entry meeting so the student rejoins the [community of inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) rather than returning as an outsider.
6. Sustain the work with ongoing facilitator coaching and data review of discipline referrals and disparities.
