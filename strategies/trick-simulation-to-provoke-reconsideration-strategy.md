# Use a trick simulation showing an incorrect phenomenon to prompt unsure learners to reconsider their shadow reasoning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
The authors used a deliberately incorrect simulation display — selecting the "what's wrong" section makes the intersection of two penumbras turn illuminated — as an instructional probe. The strategy targets learners who chose the correct alternative (umbra) but expressed uncertainty: "This trick simulation used to change the pre -service teachers' view that had chosen umbra and explained that s/he is not sure." The incorrect display confronts learners with a contradiction to their prediction, prompting them to articulate and examine their reasoning about penumbra structure.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A simulation that can display an incorrect representation of the phenomenon (the 'what's wrong' mode), used after learners have made and justified a prediction.
#### Constraints
- The article warns that if the simulation reflects the wrong phenomenon, subjects may use the same (particle-of-light) reasoning to justify it rather than correcting their view.

### Target Learners
- Pre-service physics teachers

### Target Learning Goals
- Reasoning about light and shadow phenomena
- Confronting uncertainty in physics predictions
