# Relate Math to Student Strengths

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (4 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q3` · 1 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
This strategy anchors new mathematical content in domains where a student already demonstrates competence — sports statistics, music, gaming, crafts, commerce, or community practices — and frames the math as an extension of that competence rather than a replacement for it. It is carried out through strength-based task design: teachers inventory student interests and funds of knowledge, then select or adapt problems, contexts, and representations that recruit those assets. The goal is both motivational (positioning students as capable) and cognitive (giving new concepts a familiar structure to attach to).

## Design Implications

Connecting new content to well-developed prior knowledge reduces the working-memory burden of learning and improves comprehension, because familiar contexts supply ready-made schemas for the new material [Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension of new material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Strength-based framing also supports self-efficacy, which predicts persistence on challenging tasks [~M]. However, the connection must be structurally meaningful: surface-level "wrapper" contexts (e.g., basketball-themed worksheets with the same abstract procedures) do not produce transfer and can add extraneous load if the context must itself be learned [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine inventory of student interests and out-of-school competencies (surveys, family conversations, community [funds of knowledge](../principles/activation.md) approaches)
- Mathematical analysis of the chosen context: the strength domain must actually embody the target structure (ratio, function, proof), not merely decorate it
- Willingness to let students' own contexts generate problems, not just receive them ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md))
- Systematic knowledge of learners' interests and strengths (interest surveys, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), family and community input) — not assumptions
- Genuine structural alignment between the interest domain and the math concept (percentages ↔ shooting statistics; ratios ↔ mixing paint; geometry ↔ design), not surface-level theming
- Willingness to revise examples as interests shift; a bank of contexts across several domains so no single interest is overused

#### Constraints
- Contexts unfamiliar to some students invert the intended benefit: a "relatable" example drawn from one culture's practices can disadvantage others and add extraneous cognitive load [~M]
- Over-familiar contexts can encourage students to answer from everyday intuition and skip the mathematical modeling step, producing "real-world" answers that are mathematically wrong [~W]
- If the connection is cosmetic, students perceive it as inauthentic and it yields no motivational gain [~W]
- Strength-based entry points must not become permanent tracks; capping students at "their" contexts limits exposure to transferable abstraction [-W]
- Forced or inauthentic connections reduce credibility and can depress engagement [-M] — learners detect token relevance quickly
- Learners with narrow or low-confidence self-identities ("I'm not a math person") may resist even well-matched contexts [~M]
- Over-reliance on a single interest domain can narrow learners' sense of where math applies, undermining transfer to unfamiliar contexts [-W]
- Interest-based contexts add working-memory load if the context itself is unfamiliar or requires its own explanation, particularly for learners who don't share the interest [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Context-first design**: begin a unit with a shared strength-based scenario (e.g., designing a skate ramp) and derive the mathematics from it ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md))
- **Student-generated problems**: students write problems from their own expertise, which the class solves — strongest when paired with [Peer Instruction](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- **Representation bridging**: use a strength domain (music rhythm, beadwork patterns) as one of several representations, then explicitly connect it to formal notation via [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)
- **Asset-based framing of errors**: treat student strategies from their strength domain as legitimate starting points to refine, not obstacles
- **Individual personalization**: adaptive platforms or teacher-written problem sets tailored per student (e.g., Walkington's personalized algebra work)
- **Class-level theming**: one shared context (a fantasy football league, a school garden) that the whole class analyzes mathematically
- **Strength-based roles**: learners contribute their domain expertise to group problem-solving, positioning them as authorities on the context while peers supply other strengths
- **Project-based**: extended [Application](../elements/application.md) tasks where learners choose their own context and apply target concepts to it

### Target Learners
- Students with negative math self-concept or math anxiety, who benefit from entering through a domain of demonstrated competence [~M]
- Learners from communities whose everyday practices embody mathematical structure (e.g., measurement in trades, pattern in textile arts) [~M]
- Students with strong interest-domain knowledge but weak formal math vocabulary — the bridge supports vocabulary mapping [Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension of new material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Less effective when applied uniformly: students with high prior math achievement often need less contextual scaffolding, and expertise can reverse the benefit of extra contextual support [Guidance that helps novices can hinder learners with higher expertise.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Students with negative math attitudes or math anxiety, for whom a familiar context lowers the affective barrier to engagement [~M]
- Struggling learners, who show the largest performance gains from personalized problems [~M]
- Learners with strong non-mathematical identities (athletes, artists, gamers) whose expertise can be recruited as an asset
- Less effective as a stand-alone fix for learners with significant gaps in prerequisite skills — relevance motivates but does not substitute for instruction [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: mapping formal structures onto familiar ones (ratios via cooking, functions via game mechanics)
- Attitudinal goals: math self-efficacy, identity, and belonging in mathematics classrooms
- Transfer: recognizing mathematics in out-of-school situations — though transfer requires explicit abstraction, not just contextual familiarity [~M]
- Attitudinal goals: confidence, self-efficacy, and identity as a capable math learner
- Engagement and persistence on challenging tasks
- Conceptual understanding of specific content (percentages, ratios, geometry) when the context maps structurally onto the concept
- Transfer is a *secondary* goal and must be deliberately supported by contrasting multiple contexts [~W]

### Instructions
1. **Inventory strengths**: collect student interest and expertise data through surveys, interviews, or family [funds-of-knowledge](../principles/activation.md) conversations before planning the unit.
2. **Analyze for structure**: identify which target concepts genuinely live inside the chosen strength domains (e.g., proportional reasoning in scaling recipes, not merely counting basketball points).
3. **Design the anchor task**: open with a problem in the strength domain that cannot be solved without the target mathematics ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md)).
4. **Bridge representations**: explicitly connect the informal solution path to formal notation using side-by-side [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and worked examples ([Worked Examples](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)).
5. **Fade the context**: progressively move toward abstract representations so learning does not stay bound to the original domain [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
6. **Invite student expertise**: have students generate and pose problems from their own domains, positioning them as mathematical authorities ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).

## Examples
- **[Rethinking Schools — "Rethinking Mathematics"](https://rethinkingschools.org/books/rethinking-mathematics/)** — Published curriculum using social-justice and community contexts (sweatshop economics, gentrification data) where students' lived knowledge is the entry point for algebra and statistics.
- **[YouCubed](https://www.youcubed.org)** (Stanford, Jo Boaler) — Low-floor, high-ceiling tasks and mindset materials designed so students enter through visual and pattern strengths before formal notation.
- **[Funds of Knowledge for Teaching project](https://www.sonoma.edu/users/f/fonseca/funds_of_knowledge.html)** (Moll, Amanti, & González, Tucson) — Teacher ethnography of household competencies used to design math and literacy instruction from student assets.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)** — Teachers build instruction from students' informal problem-solving strategies, treating those strategies as mathematical strengths to be extended.
- **Personalized algebra at scale**: Walkington's research with adaptive algebra platforms rewrote word problems to match individual students' reported interests (e.g., basketball, fashion, gaming) and found faster solving and higher accuracy, especially among previously low-performing students.
- **Basketball percentages**: a class that follows the NBA computes field-goal percentages, plus/minus, and shooting splits — the statistics students already read become the dataset for percentage and ratio instruction.
- **Geometry through art and design**: learners apply similarity, symmetry, and area to logo design or mural planning, with the artistic product as an [authentic audience](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) for the math.
- **Money and music**: learners who care about streaming analyze royalty splits (fractions, percentages, linear functions) for their own hypothetical releases.

# Relating Math to Student Strengths

## Description
This strategy connects mathematical content to learners' existing interests, hobbies, and areas of competence — sports statistics, art, music, gaming, finance — so that new math concepts land on familiar ground. The instructor gathers information about what learners care about and are good at, then designs examples, problems, and projects that route the math through those domains. The goal is twofold: raise motivation by making content personally meaningful, and raise confidence by positioning learners as experts who bring valued knowledge to the math classroom.

## Design Implications

Personal relevance increases situational interest, and interest in turn supports attention, persistence, and deeper processing [~M]. The strongest evidence comes from personalized word problems: when algebra problems are adapted to individual students' interests (favorite sports, hobbies, media), students solve them faster and perform better, with the largest gains for struggling learners [~M]. However, relevance works through *meaningful* connection — superficial dressing-up of problems ("Sally likes skateboards, so…") does not produce the same benefit and can feel patronizing [-M]. The strategy is best understood as an entry point: interest-based contexts recruit engagement and [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge, but the math itself must still be taught explicitly.

### Instructions
1. **Survey strengths and interests** — use an interest inventory, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), or portfolio review to map each learner's passions and competencies.
2. **Map concepts to contexts** — for each upcoming math concept, identify interest domains where the concept does real work (not decoration); use [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to make the structural mapping explicit.
3. **Activate prior knowledge in the interest domain** — begin with what learners already know about the context before introducing the math ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
4. **Design problems and tasks through the context** — write or select problems where the interest domain is integral to the reasoning, not just the cover story.
5. **Apply and extend** — move from teacher-chosen contexts to learner-chosen ones ([Application](../elements/application.md)), then explicitly contrast multiple contexts to support abstraction and transfer.
6. **Assess both math and attitude** — monitor engagement, confidence, and participation alongside mathematical accuracy.
