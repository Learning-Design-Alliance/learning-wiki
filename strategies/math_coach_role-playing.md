# Math Coach Role-Playing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Organize students into small groups where they take turns acting as "math coaches." One student reads a word problem aloud, and the other students (the math coaches) advise the reader on which mathematical operation to use in solving the problem. Math coaches listen closely, underline key words in the problem, and use flash cards to "flash" the mathematical operation they decide to use. The role-play structure converts problem-solving from a solitary act into a social one: the reader must articulate the problem situation, and coaches must justify their operational choice before the group commits to it.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it forces verbalization of the problem schema — selecting an operation requires mapping the problem's semantic structure (joining, separating, comparing) onto arithmetic, and explaining that mapping aloud is a form of self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Peer dialogue of this kind also produces feedback at the process level ("why did you choose subtraction?") rather than the outcome level, which is where feedback has its strongest effects [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of word problems at an appropriate difficulty range, ideally spanning multiple problem types (change, compare, combine)
- Operation flash cards (+, −, ×, ÷) or equivalent signaling devices
- Small groups of 3–4 students with rotating roles
- Norms for justification: coaches must state *why* an operation fits, not just which one

#### Constraints
- Without structured turn-taking, confident students dominate and hesitant students disengage; the coach role can become a spectator role [-M]
- Keyword-matching ("altogether means add") can reinforce a shallow strategy that fails on problems designed to defeat keyword cues [~M] — facilitators should include problems where keywords mislead
- Groups without a shared understanding of the underlying problem schemas produce inaccurate peer advice, which can consolidate errors when unchecked [-M]
- Less effective if problems are all of one type; contrasting problem types is what drives schema discrimination [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Silent flash variant:** all coaches flash their chosen operation simultaneously, then defend; disagreement triggers discussion
- **Written variant:** coaches write the operation and one sentence of justification before revealing, adding a brief individual-accountability step
- **Error-problem variant:** groups receive a worked solution with a wrong operation and coach each other to diagnose the error [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **Reciprocal variant:** the reader, after solving, explains why the coaches' advice was or was not taken, reversing the explanation burden

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle school learners (Grades 3–8) still consolidating the mapping between problem situations and operations
- Struggling readers and multilingual learners benefit from hearing the problem read aloud and from the physical act of underlining key information [~M]
- Less beneficial for students who already reliably select operations; the coach role becomes redundant for them [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Problem schema formation: recognizing which semantic situation calls for which operation
- Mathematical communication: justifying a decision with reference to the problem text
- Metacognitive monitoring: checking an interpretation against the problem before computing

### Instructions
1. Form groups of 3–4 and assign the first reader; distribute operation flash cards to the coaches ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md)).
2. The reader reads the word problem aloud while coaches underline key quantities and relationships ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. Each coach selects and flashes the operation they believe fits, then states a justification tied to the problem's meaning ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. The group resolves disagreements through discussion before the reader solves ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. The reader applies the agreed operation and reports the result ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
6. Rotate roles and move to a new problem, deliberately varying problem types across rounds ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).

## Examples
- **Grade 4 multiplication/division unit:** groups cycle through equal-groups, comparison, and missing-factor problems; the flash-card disagreement round surfaces the classic "more means multiply" misconception when a comparison problem requires division.
- **Grade 6 ratio problems:** coaches flash × or ÷ and must point to the specific phrase in the problem ("3 times as many" vs. "one-third of") that justifies the choice.
- **Intervention settings:** the error-problem variant is used in small-group Tier 2 support, where diagnosing a wrong operation in a peer's solution is less threatening than having one's own work corrected.
