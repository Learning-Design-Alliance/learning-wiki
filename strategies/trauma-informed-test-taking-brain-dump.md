# Use trauma-informed educational practices and have students write memorized information on blank paper before reading the first question to buffer stress responses

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 3 studies (2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), `q2` · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
Because perceived threat can trigger an amygdala hijack that interrupts critical reasoning, working memory, and executive function, the article recommends trauma-informed educational practices and a concrete countermeasure: students should always use the blank sheet of paper at the testing site and write down memorized information before reading the first question, helping overcome an amygdala hijack. Faculty are also encouraged to examine their own implicit biases and check examination items for bias.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Students write memorized information on the testing site's blank sheet before reading the first question
- Educators trained in or committed to trauma-informed practices and bias examination
#### Constraints
- The neurocognitive account (amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex effects of trauma) is cited literature, not data from this article

### Target Learners
- midwifery students with histories of anxiety, depression, or trauma; students from historically marginalized or underrepresented groups

### Target Learning Goals
- maintaining executive function and critical reasoning during high-stakes multiple-choice testing
