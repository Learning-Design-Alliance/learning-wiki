Identifying Feelings¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal), q3 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners identify, label, or match facial expressions, body cues, and situational contexts to feelings — their own and others'. Activities range from categorizing feeling words by intensity, to analyzing characters' emotions in literature, to "affect labeling" of one's own internal states. The strategy treats emotion vocabulary as a learnable skill rather than assumed prior knowledge.

Design Implications¶

Putting feelings into words — affect labeling — reliably reduces amygdala reactivity and improves emotional regulation Lieberman et al. (2007) found affect labeling dampened amygdala response. [+M]. Precise emotion vocabulary also supports empathy and perspective-taking, since recognizing others' states is a prerequisite for responding to them Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]. Instruction should move beyond the basic six expressions toward granularity — distinguishing frustrated from disappointed, nervous from excited — because finer differentiation predicts better regulation outcomes Emotional granularity is associated with improved self-regulation. [+W].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Rich feeling-word resources: emotion wheels, facial expression cards, graded vocabulary lists (e.g., RULER Mood Meter)
  • Authentic contexts for application — literary texts, video vignettes, real classroom situations (Case Studies)
  • A safe classroom climate; disclosure of feelings requires trust (Check-In routines help establish it)

Constraints¶

  • Facial expression recognition is not universal: display rules and interpretation of expressions vary across cultures, so decontextualized face-matching can mislead Emotion perception is culturally situated rather than universal. [~M]
  • Forcing public emotional disclosure can harm learners with trauma histories or alexithymia; labeling should always be optional and private where possible [-M]
  • Isolated face-card drills produce recognition of posed expressions but transfer weakly to real social perception; context-rich practice is needed [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Literature-based: analyzing characters' feelings in fiction, which supports both comprehension and emotion vocabulary Dialogic reading improves expressive language. [+M]
  • Somatic: learners scan bodily sensations and match them to labels ("tight chest = worried?")
  • Intensity mapping: placing feelings on a pleasantness/energy grid (Mood Meter) rather than binary categories
  • Game formats: matching, charades, or Act-It-Out for younger learners

Target Learners¶

  • Early childhood and elementary learners, for whom emotion vocabulary is still developing [+M]
  • Learners with autism or alexithymia who benefit from explicit instruction in emotion recognition [-M] (they need it most, but require more scaffolding and repetition)
  • Adults in social-emotional learning, counseling training, or workplace emotional-intelligence programs

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: building differentiated vocabulary for internal states
  • Self-awareness and self-regulation: recognizing one's own feelings as they arise
  • Social perception: reading others' emotional cues in context
  • Reading comprehension: inferring character motivation and affect

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce a small set of feeling words with Clear Structure, pairing each label with facial, bodily, and situational cues (avoid single-cue teaching).
  2. Model the labeling process aloud on a vignette or text passage — "Her shoulders dropped and she looked away; I think she's disappointed, not just sad" — as in Explicit Teaching.
  3. Provide Practice with graduated contexts: matching cards → situational vignettes → ambiguous real scenarios.
  4. Use a brief Check-In routine (e.g., Mood Meter entry) so learners label their own feelings daily, not just fictional ones.
  5. Give Coaching and feedback on granularity — push learners from "mad" to "frustrated, embarrassed, resentful" as appropriate.

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a game format for the same labeling skill
  • Active Listening — depends on accurately identifying the speaker's feelings
  • Acting-Role-Play — enacting feelings deepens recognition of their cues

Examples¶

  • RULER Approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — the Mood Meter has learners locate feelings on a pleasantness–energy grid; a whole-school SEL curriculum built around emotion labeling.
  • Zones of Regulation (Kuypers) — color-coded zones (blue, green, yellow, red) that young learners use to categorize and communicate their arousal states.
  • Literature discussion — during read-alouds, pausing to ask "What is this character feeling, and how do you know?" links emotion identification to text evidence.
