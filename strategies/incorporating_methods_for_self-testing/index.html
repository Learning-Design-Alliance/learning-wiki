Incorporating Methods for Self-Testing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (3 for, 2 mixed) · 15 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 2 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 5 of 15 report an effect size

Description¶

Self-testing reframes assessment from a grading event into a learning activity: students actively retrieve information — via flashcards, practice questions, or free recall — instead of rereading notes. In culturally responsive and low-stakes classrooms, frequent non-graded checks let students monitor progress without the anxiety attached to high-stakes testing. The strategy has two components: the instructor builds frequent retrieval opportunities into instruction, and students are explicitly taught self-testing techniques they can apply independently.

Design Implications¶

Retrieval practice produces substantially more durable learning than restudying, yet students rarely choose it spontaneously because it feels harder and less productive than rereading Testing enhances long-term retention more than restudying. [+S]. Because self-testing also functions as self-assessment, it builds accurate metacognitive monitoring — students discover which topics they only recognize versus can actually reproduce Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]. Feedback on self-tests should target the task and process, not just correctness Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A bank of low-stakes questions or prompts aligned to learning goals (Practice)
  • Explicit instruction in self-testing techniques — free recall, flashcards, question generation — since students default to passive rereading
  • Rapid feedback loops so errors are corrected before they consolidate (Provide Feedback)
  • A classroom norm that errors during self-testing are diagnostic information, not failure
  • A bank of questions that targets the same knowledge later assessed summatively, at comparable difficulty
  • Immediate or rapid feedback so errors are corrected while the retrieval attempt is still active (Practice followed by Assess Performance)
  • Explicit framing that these are learning tools, not surveillance — no grade penalty for wrong answers
  • Time in class or in the platform workflow for retrieval attempts, not just content delivery

Constraints¶

  • Students frequently judge rereading to be more effective than testing, so they abandon self-testing when unsupervised Learners' judgments of learning are often inaccurate and favor passive restudy. [~M] — fluency from rereading creates an illusion of mastery
  • Self-testing of isolated facts can promote memorization without conceptual integration unless questions require inference or application Retrieval practice benefits are smaller for complex, inference-based tasks. [~M]
  • If self-tests carry grades, anxiety and surface strategies return, undermining the reframing that makes the strategy work [-M]
  • Students with very low prior knowledge may retrieve little and experience frustration; early self-tests may need open-book or cued formats [~W]
  • Testing only recognition (multiple choice) when the summative assessment demands recall produces weaker transfer; format should match the target task
  • Learners with high prior knowledge may gain little from simple factual retrieval and benefit more from generative formats such as Self-Explanation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • If stakes creep back in (leaderboards, public scores), anxiety and surface strategies return, undermining the reframing that makes the strategy work

Implementation Variability¶

  • Free recall ("brain dumps") after a lesson or reading segment
  • Flashcard systems with spaced scheduling (e.g., Anki, Quizlet)
  • Clicker or low-stakes online quizzes at the start of class (Quizzes)
  • Student-generated questions exchanged with peers, which adds generative processing
  • Cued or open-book self-testing for novices, fading supports as competence grows
  • Instructor-paced: opening or closing each class with a two-question quiz (low-tech, no grading)
  • Learner-paced: flashcard or quiz apps with spaced scheduling; learners choose topics where confidence is lowest
  • Free recall: brain dumps or concept mapping from memory after reading, requiring no question bank
  • Pretesting: asking questions before instruction; even failed retrieval attempts prime subsequent learning [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • All learners benefit from retrieval practice, but it is especially valuable for students who rely on rereading and highlighting as their default study method Testing enhances long-term retention more than restudying. [+S]
  • Students developing self-regulated learning habits, who need structured opportunities to practice monitoring their own understanding Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Anxious test-takers, for whom frequent ungraded checks reduce the stakes of assessment [~W]
  • Anxious learners, because frequent no-stakes exposure to test formats reduces test-specific arousal

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Long-term retention of factual and conceptual knowledge Testing enhances long-term retention more than restudying. [+S]
  • Metacognitive accuracy: knowing what you know and what needs restudy
  • Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and adjusting one's own study behavior
  • Metacognitive calibration: accurate judgments of what is and is not yet known

Instructions¶

  1. Teach the rationale: show students evidence that testing is a learning event, not just a measurement event, and contrast it with the illusion of mastery from rereading.
  2. Model a self-testing technique with a Think-Aloud — demonstrate generating questions from a text, attempting free recall, then checking and repairing gaps.
  3. Build frequent low-stakes retrieval into class sessions using Quizzes or Practice — short, ungraded, with immediate feedback (Provide Feedback).
  4. Schedule repeated retrieval over time rather than massing it in one session Testing enhances long-term retention more than restudying. [+S].
  5. Have students track their own results and plan restudy accordingly, connecting self-testing to Self-Regulated Learning cycles.
  6. Fade instructor-provided questions over time, shifting question generation to students (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Spaced retrieval scheduling — self-testing is most powerful when retrieval attempts are distributed over time rather than massed
  • Student question generation — converts self-testing from recognition to generative practice
  • Low-stakes formative quizzing — the instructor-side complement to independent self-testing
  • Spaced repetition — scheduling self-tests over time compounds the retrieval benefit
  • Formative feedback loops — self-testing supplies the data that feedback acts on
  • Rereading replacement — substituting retrieval for passive review in study-skills instruction
  • Incorporate GenAI-based pronunciation tools to give anxious learners additional low-stakes speaking opportunities outside the classroom

Examples¶

  • Anki — spaced-repetition flashcard software that schedules each card for review just before predicted forgetting; widely used in medical education.
  • Quizlet — self-testing modes (Learn, Test) that convert study sets into retrieval practice with feedback.
  • Retrieval practice routines (e.g., "brain dumps") — teachers end a lesson with two minutes of ungraded free recall, then students compare their recall against notes to identify gaps (RetrievalPractice.org).
  • Khan Academy — mastery-based practice questions with hints and immediate feedback, letting students self-test before unit assessments.
  • Anki — spaced-repetition flashcards that schedule each item for review just before predicted forgetting; widely used in medical education.
  • Khan Academy — mastery quizzes between video segments; wrong answers route learners to hints and prerequisite content rather than a grade.
  • Daily low-stakes quizzing in introductory psychology — Roediger and colleagues' classroom studies showed students earned roughly a grade-step more on exam questions that had appeared on prior quizzes.
  • "Brain dump" closures — students write everything they remember from a lesson on a blank sheet, then compare against notes; no technology or question bank required.
