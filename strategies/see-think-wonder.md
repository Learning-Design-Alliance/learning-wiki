# See, Think, Wonder

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
See, Think, Wonder is a thinking routine developed by Harvard Project Zero's Visible Thinking project. Learners observe a stimulating image, artifact, or primary source, then respond to three prompts in sequence: *What do you see?* (pure observation), *What do you think about it?* (interpretation), and *What does it make you wonder?* (question generation). The routine is typically carried out with a three-column organizer, individual writing time, and then [peer discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and whole-class sharing.

## Design Implications

The routine makes thinking visible and separates observation from interpretation, which trains learners to distinguish evidence from inference. Because every response is grounded in what is directly observable, every learner can succeed from the first contribution, lowering the stakes for participation and normalizing tentative or partially formed ideas [active-learning-improves-exam-performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The "wonder" step activates curiosity and generates authentic questions that can drive subsequent inquiry [activation-improves-learning](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A carefully selected, engaging image, artifact, or primary source appropriate to the learners and content
- A three-column organizer (See / Think / Wonder) or equivalent recording format
- Protected time for individual brainstorming before sharing, so responses are not anchored by peers
- A structure for sharing: individual response, [peer discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), then whole-class synthesis

#### Constraints
- Poorly chosen images produce shallow engagement; the stimulus must be rich enough to sustain multiple interpretations [-M]
- Some students stall at surface-level observation ("I see a red shape") and need prompting or sentence stems to move to interpretation and questioning [-W]
- The routine opens inquiry but does not by itself build deep knowledge; without follow-up investigation, wonderings remain unresolved [-M]
- Overuse as a generic warm-up can make the three prompts feel formulaic, reducing genuine curiosity [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Object-based:** use physical artifacts, specimens, or lab setups instead of images
- **Text-based:** apply the three prompts to a primary source document or data display
- **Digital:** use collaborative boards (e.g., Padlet, Jamboard) so learners see peers' wonderings in real time
- **Personalized:** let students choose which of their three entries to share with a peer; adjust image complexity to skill level
- **Extended:** return to the "wonder" column at the end of a unit to evaluate which questions the learning has answered

### Target Learners
- All levels from elementary through higher education; the low-floor structure makes it especially valuable for reluctant or anxious participants
- Multilingual learners and novices, because observation-based responses do not depend on prior vocabulary or background knowledge [activation-improves-learning](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Advanced learners benefit when images are complex and ambiguous enough to support competing interpretations [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking: distinguishing observation from inference
- Curiosity and question-asking: generating authentic inquiry questions
- Activation of prior knowledge before new content [activation-improves-learning](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Discussion norms and intellectual risk-taking: normalizing tentative ideas and error

### Instructions
1. **Gain attention** — display the image or artifact without explanation or context ([Gain Attention](../elements/gain-attention.md)); silence and observation time first
2. **See** — learners record only what they objectively observe, no interpretation yet ([Individual Response](../elements/individual-response.md))
3. **Think** — learners record interpretations, using the stem "I think… because…" to force evidence-based reasoning
4. **Wonder** — learners generate questions the image raises for them
5. **Share** — [peer discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of chosen entries, then whole-class sharing that surfaces [cognitive conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) between differing interpretations
6. **Bridge** — connect the class's wonderings to the upcoming content or inquiry investigation

## Examples
- **Science:** students observe a microscopic image of an unfamiliar cell, record observations, propose what it might be, and generate questions that launch a unit on cell structure.
- **History:** students observe a primary source photograph from a historical event; competing "think" interpretations expose gaps between evidence and inference, and "wonder" questions frame document analysis ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)).
- **Project Zero / Visible Thinking** ([pz.harvard.edu](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines)) — the routine is one of the core Visible Thinking routines, documented with classroom video and implementation guides.
