# Emotion Emojis

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q3` · 0 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Students express how they are feeling by drawing or selecting an emoji at the start of a lesson or check-in point. Volunteers then share their emoji and explain how it reflects their current feelings, converting a private affective state into a brief, low-stakes public signal the instructor can respond to.

## Design Implications

Emotion emojis function as a structured [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md): a brief affective self-report that supports emotional awareness and gives the instructor real-time data on learner readiness. Naming an emotion — even through a simple icon — supports affect labeling, which research in affective neuroscience associates with reduced amygdala reactivity and improved emotional regulation [~W]. The activity also builds self-monitoring, a core component of self-regulated learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M], by prompting students to attend to and represent their internal state.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of emojis (drawn, printed, or digital) representing a range of emotions and intensities
- Time for individual reflection followed by voluntary whole-class or small-group sharing
- A safe, normed environment where sharing is invited but never coerced
- Access to emojis (drawn or digital) and a shared display surface (whiteboard, slide, LMS poll)
- 2–3 minutes for individual reflection before any sharing
- A classroom climate where declining to share is explicitly acceptable

#### Constraints
- Compulsory sharing of emotional states can feel intrusive and reduce trust, particularly for adolescents; sharing must remain voluntary [-W]
- Students unfamiliar with emoji conventions or with limited emotional vocabulary may select icons that misrepresent their state; a brief emotion-vocabulary orientation improves accuracy [-W]
- As a one-off activity it produces little benefit; effects depend on being embedded in a routine of regular check-ins [~W]
- Emoji scales collapse nuanced affect into coarse categories and may miss clinically significant distress that a quick icon check cannot surface [-W]
- Coerced or graded sharing undermines the safety the activity depends on and suppresses honest responses [-M]
- A limited emoji set can flatten complex or mixed emotions; students may disengage when no option fits [~W]
- Without follow-up from the teacher (acknowledging, adjusting the lesson), the check-in becomes ritual and students stop taking it seriously [-W]
- Repeated public sharing can expose vulnerable students; anonymity options (private submission, aggregate display) mitigate this

#### Implementation Variability
- **Entry/exit tickets:** students select an emoji at the start or end of class, privately or publicly
- **Mood meters:** a 2×2 grid (e.g., the RULER approach's Mood Meter) mapping emojis by valence and energy, adding granularity
- **Drawing variant:** students draw their own emoji, adding a generation step that deepens reflection
- **Digital check-ins:** LMS polls or tools like Google Forms let students report anonymously, lowering social risk
- Anonymous digital polls (Mentimeter, Google Forms) with only aggregate results shown
- Mood meters or emoji scales (e.g., the RULER Mood Meter) replacing single emojis for finer granularity
- Private journal variant: students select an emoji in a notebook, sharing only with the teacher
- Entry/exit ticket format to track emotional trends across a unit

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, especially younger students who may lack the vocabulary to articulate emotions verbally
- Students developing self-regulation skills, for whom regular affect self-report builds the monitoring habit [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a diagnostic for older or privacy-sensitive learners without an anonymous option [~W]
- All ages; especially effective for pre-K through middle grades where emotional vocabulary is still developing [+W]
- Students with limited verbal expression skills or language barriers, for whom the visual format removes a participation obstacle
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for adolescents who may find public emoji sharing juvenile; anonymous or small-group variants work better [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional awareness and affect labeling: recognizing and naming one's own emotional state
- Self-regulation: creating a pause between feeling and response, supporting intentional choices
- Social-emotional classroom climate: normalizing talk about feelings and building empathy through peer sharing
- Emotional awareness and literacy: building vocabulary for internal states
- Self-regulation: affect labeling as an early step in managing emotion [~M]
- Classroom community and belonging: normalizing the expression of feelings [belonging interventions improve outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Formative awareness for the teacher: gauging readiness to learn before instruction

### Instructions
1. Distribute an emoji sheet or display an emoji menu; students privately select or draw the emoji that best matches their current feeling.
2. Invite volunteers to share their emoji and briefly explain their choice; model this first with your own emoji to establish norms.
3. Acknowledge the range of feelings without judgment and note any signals (e.g., widespread anxiety before an assessment) that warrant adjusting the lesson.
4. Close by connecting the check-in to regulation: students name one small action they can take to move toward a productive state.

## Examples
- A middle-school teacher opens each class with an emoji entry ticket on a Google Form; a cluster of anxious emojis before an exam prompts a brief review session instead of new content.
- An elementary classroom uses a magnetic emoji board by the door; students move their emoji on arrival, giving the teacher a quick scan of who may need a morning check-in.
- Elementary teachers use a daily emoji board (e.g., a whiteboard grid students initial) as a morning routine to gauge class readiness and surface students needing support.
- Online instructors open synchronous sessions with a one-click emoji poll in Zoom or Mentimeter, displaying aggregate results so individuals are not exposed.
- Secondary advisory programs use private emoji check-ins via Google Forms, with the advisor following up individually with students who report persistent negative states.

# Emotion Emojis Check-In

## Description
Students express their current feelings by drawing or sharing an emoji that represents their emotional state. Volunteers then share their emoji and explain how it reflects their current feelings. The activity functions as a low-stakes [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) that surfaces affect at the start of a session and builds a shared emotional vocabulary.

## Design Implications

Naming an emotion — even via a simple visual symbol — supports affect labeling, which reliably reduces the intensity of felt emotion and improves self-regulation [~M]. The emoji format lowers the barrier to entry: students who would not volunteer a verbal disclosure can still participate non-verbally, making the activity a practical entry point for social-emotional learning. Its value depends on voluntary sharing and a supportive climate; compulsory disclosure can backfire.
