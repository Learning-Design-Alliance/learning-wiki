# Maintaining Sensitivity to Language Concerns

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 review, 1 causal), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 3 report an effect size

## Description
In traditional classrooms, students who are not native speakers of the language of instruction often feel marginalized, lost, and pressured into discarding their home language. Maintaining sensitivity to language concerns means treating linguistic diversity as a resource: instructional materials are matched to learners' current proficiency, key materials and supports are provided in the primary language where possible, and students are encouraged to develop full proficiency in English *while* continuing to build their first language. The strategy draws on the distinction between conversational fluency and academic language proficiency — surface fluency can mask continuing academic needs [Cummins, 1979/2008].

## Design Implications

Language-sensitive instruction reduces the extraneous cognitive load of processing unfamiliar language, freeing working memory for content learning, and supports the well-documented cross-linguistic transfer of academic skills between a learner's two languages [Cummins, 1979/2008] [+M]. It also protects motivation: students who feel their language and identity are valued show greater engagement and persistence [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Assessment of each learner's proficiency in *both* languages, distinguishing conversational from academic language
- Access to materials in multiple languages or at controlled language levels ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Teacher training in second language acquisition and in scaffolding techniques such as [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and visual supports
- A classroom climate in which home languages are visibly welcomed, not merely tolerated
- Knowledge of learners' language profiles (home language, proficiency levels, prior schooling)
- Explicit teaching of discipline-specific academic language, not just everyday English
- Multiple representations of key concepts (visuals, manipulatives, gestures, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)) so meaning does not depend on a single linguistic channel
- Classroom norms that position home languages and dialects as resources (e.g., allowing drafting or peer discussion in the first language)

#### Constraints
- Full bilingual materials may be unavailable for lower-enrollment language groups; partial scaffolds (glossaries, translated key terms) are the realistic fallback [-W]
- Some students or families may prefer English-only immersion and resist primary-language use; forcing L1 use against student preference can backfire [~W]
- Teachers without second-language training often conflate conversational fluency with academic readiness, withdrawing supports too early [-M]
- In large classes with many language backgrounds, individualized language support strains time and resources [-W]
- Simplifying language too far removes exposure to academic syntax and can depress long-term language growth [~M] — the goal is *comprehensible input*, not simplified input
- Treating multilingual learners as a homogeneous group fails: newcomers, long-term English learners, and students with interrupted schooling need different supports
- If home-language use is permitted but never leveraged (no translanguaging tasks, no bilingual resources), the affirmational stance is cosmetic and produces little measurable benefit [~W]
- Assessment in a learner's weaker language underestimates content knowledge; scores then drive inappropriate placement [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Sheltered instruction** (e.g., the SIOP model): grade-level content delivered with deliberate language scaffolds, no translation required
- **Translanguaging**: students draft, discuss, or brainstorm in their strongest language before producing work in English
- **Dual-language programs**: sustained content instruction in both languages for all students
- **Lightweight adaptations**: bilingual glossaries, extended time, sentence frames, and pre-taught key vocabulary
- **Sheltered instruction** (e.g., the [SIOP Model](https://www.cal.org/siop/)): content and language objectives taught together with scaffolded English
- **Translanguaging approaches**: learners draft, discuss, or brainstorm in their strongest language before producing output in the target language
- **Materials adaptation**: glossaries, dual-language texts, sentence frames, and reduced linguistic density in [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)
- **Peer structures**: pairing multilingual learners for discussion so meaning-making precedes formal output

### Target Learners
- K–12 and adult English learners at intermediate proficiency, who have enough English to access scaffolded instruction but still need support for academic language
- Newly arrived students with limited English, who need heavier primary-language support
- "Long-term English learners" whose conversational fluency conceals gaps in academic language — these students are frequently under-served because their spoken English masks academic needs [~M]
- Multilingual learners and non-native speakers of the language of instruction, who otherwise spend disproportionate working memory decoding language rather than learning content [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Students with strong conversational fluency but underdeveloped academic language — a group easily misidentified as fully proficient [Cummins' distinction between BICS and CALP.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049002222) [+M]
- All learners benefit from clearer language and multiple representations, though the largest gains accrue to those with the least proficiency in the language of instruction [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic language acquisition alongside content-area learning
- Content knowledge in subjects where language of instruction differs from the learner's stronger language
- Identity and belonging: sustaining bilingualism as an asset rather than a deficit
- Content-area learning (science, mathematics, social studies) delivered in a second language
- Academic language development: discipline-specific vocabulary, registers, and discourse conventions
- Identity and belonging: learners' home languages treated as intellectual resources rather than deficits

### Instructions
1. Assess proficiency in both languages and identify each learner's academic-language gaps, not just conversational fluency.
2. Pre-teach key vocabulary and use [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) in materials; control sentence complexity without dumbing down content.
3. Provide primary-language supports where feasible — translated glossaries, bilingual texts, or permission to draft and discuss in L1 ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) can include bilingual or adapted versions).
4. Scaffold comprehension with visuals, graphic organizers, and [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of dense text.
5. Use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to monitor comprehension privately, since English learners often hide confusion to avoid standing out.
6. Normalize multilingualism publicly — display multilingual work, invite home-language use for brainstorming — so support never reads as remediation.

## Examples
- **SIOP (Sheltered Instruction Observation Protocol)** — a widely used professional development and lesson-design model in U.S. schools; teachers deliver grade-level content with explicit language objectives, visuals, and scaffolded interaction (see Echevarría, Vogt, & Short, 2017).
- **WIDA standards and CAN DO descriptors** (https://wida.wisc.edu) — provide proficiency-level descriptors teachers use to match task demands to learner language levels.
- **Dual-language programs such as Utah's state-wide model** (https://schools.utah.gov/duallanguage) — content delivered in two languages across grades, treating bilingualism as the goal for all students.
- **SIOP Model** ([Center for Applied Linguistics](https://www.cal.org/siop/)) — sheltered instruction in which every lesson carries both a content objective and a language objective, with scaffolded vocabulary, visuals, and interaction structures.
- **WIDA standards** ([wida.wisc.edu](https://wida.wisc.edu)) — U.S. framework used by schools to differentiate instruction and assessment by English language proficiency level.
- **Science instruction for English learners** — synthesis research shows inquiry-based science with explicit language support improves both science and language outcomes for multilingual learners [Lee's synthesis of research on science education with English language learners.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543075004491) [+M]

## Description
Sensitivity to language concerns is a strategy in which instructors treat learners' diverse language backgrounds as assets and deliberately adjust instruction — vocabulary load, syntax, discourse norms, and assessment language — so that non-native speakers can access content while continuing to develop academic language. It combines two moves: an affirmational stance toward home languages and dialects, and concrete material design choices such as plain syntax, glossed vocabulary, and multiple representations of key ideas.

## Design Implications

Language proficiency interacts directly with cognitive load: unfamiliar vocabulary and complex syntax consume working memory that would otherwise be spent on content learning [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Reducing unnecessary linguistic burden — without diluting content — frees capacity for disciplinary thinking, while connecting new terms to learners' prior knowledge and home language improves encoding [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Research on bilingual education shows that continued development of the first language supports, rather than competes with, second-language academic growth [Cummins' linguistic interdependence hypothesis.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049002222) [+M].

#### Requirements
- Knowledge of learners' language profiles (home language, proficiency levels, prior schooling)
- Instructional materials using [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) — short sentences, common words where possible, defined technical terms
- Explicit teaching of discipline-specific academic language, not just everyday English
- Multiple representations of key concepts (visuals, manipulatives, gestures, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)) so meaning does not depend on a single linguistic channel

### Instructions
1. Survey learners' language backgrounds and proficiency levels at the start of instruction; record home languages and prior schooling.
2. Audit upcoming materials for unnecessary linguistic load; rewrite for [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and break dense passages into [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) units.
3. Identify the academic language demands of the lesson (key terms, sentence structures) and teach them explicitly alongside content.
4. Activate learners' prior knowledge, inviting connections to home-language concepts [Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].
5. Provide multiple representations — visuals, demonstrations, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) — so comprehension does not hinge on one linguistic formulation.
6. Allow structured use of home languages for drafting and peer discussion; require target-language output at the production stage.
7. Assess content knowledge with linguistically accessible tasks, or accommodate by separating language proficiency from content mastery ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)).
