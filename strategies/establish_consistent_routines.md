# Establish Consistent Routines

## Description
Use consistent routines and methods of giving instructions to help students know what is expected of them.

A routine is a set of rules organized around a particular time (e.g., beginning of the day), context (e.g., group work), or place (e.g., library or playground) that guides students through recurring classroom demands — materials checked in and out, activities begun and ended, students needing the bathroom or forgetting supplies. The **amount of time learners spend actually thinking about, acting on, or working on a learning task** is called engaged learning time (Savage, 1991), and it is often much less than the time allocated to an activity — a 35-minute activity might produce only 15 minutes of real engagement, the rest lost to passing out materials, announcements, and handling requests. Teachers who taught routines to students during the first few weeks of school were the most successful at maximizing engaged learning time (Emmer et al., 1994), and engaged learning time is significantly related to achievement — arguably the strongest single argument for investing in well-established routines.

Effectively teaching a classroom routine has five elements (EIS, 2022): (1) define the behavioral expectations for the routine — developmentally appropriate, culturally responsive, positively stated, specific, and observable; (2) explicitly teach the routine to students and review it often, rather than assuming it will be picked up implicitly; (3) practice the routine; (4) provide positive reinforcement when students demonstrate it correctly; and (5) provide visual prompts of the routine, keeping it to as few steps as possible. Routines are especially valuable for time-consuming noninstructional activities, which can consume up to 50 percent of the time initially allocated to a topic or lesson if left unmanaged (Jones & Jones, 1990) — and should be taught with as much planning and thoroughness as the learning objectives themselves, then followed up with monitoring of how well they're actually working (Jones & Jones, 1990; Pasch, Sparks-Langer, Gardner, Starko, & Moody, 1991).
