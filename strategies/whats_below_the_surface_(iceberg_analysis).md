# What’s Below the Surface? (Iceberg Analysis)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Iceberg analysis uses the waterline metaphor to separate observable events from their underlying causes. Students select a key event in a text — typically featuring an adolescent protagonist — and record the visible "above the surface" causes (explicit scenes, actions, dialogue) on the iceberg's tip, then generate "below the surface" explanations (character traits, identity facets, social pressures, unspoken motivations) beneath the waterline. Discussion follows, connecting the hidden layers to a fuller understanding of the event. The visible/hidden structure makes inference an explicit, scaffolded act rather than an unmarked expectation.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it externalizes the inference process: separating evidence (above the line) from interpretation (below the line) mirrors the evidence–claim structure of analytical reasoning and prompts generative processing of the text [Generating explanations during learning improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The graphic organizer functions as an advance organizer, giving students a stable schema for literary analysis that can be reused across texts. Because the below-surface layer is inherently interpretive, the strategy benefits from contrasting multiple plausible readings rather than converging on one "correct" answer [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deeper principles.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text with a psychologically rich key event and an adolescent or relatable protagonist
- An iceberg graphic with a clear waterline; space for textual evidence above and inference below
- A focal question that is genuinely contestable (e.g., "Why does Johnny kill Bob?") — a question with an obvious answer collapses the above/below distinction
- Facilitated discussion where students must defend below-surface claims with above-surface evidence

#### Constraints
- If the focal question is too literal, students fill the below-surface region with plot summary, defeating the purpose [~M]
- Students with weak text evidence skills may produce speculation untethered from the text; require a citation or scene reference for every below-surface claim [-W]
- The metaphor can imply a single hidden "true" cause; complex events have multiple interacting causes, and single-cause icebergs oversimplify character analysis [~W]
- Effectiveness depends on facilitator skill in probing shallow responses; without follow-up questioning, the iceberg becomes a completion exercise [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Historical events:** above the line = documented actions; below = economic, cultural, and ideological causes (widely used in [Facing History and Ourselves](https://www.facinghistory.org) units)
- **Identity icebergs:** students analyze their own public behavior vs. private identity, supporting social-emotional learning and self-management
- **Writing analysis:** above = surface features of a mentor text; below = author's craft decisions
- **Collaborative icebergs:** groups build separate layers, then compare — surfacing interpretive differences

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students reading narrative texts, who often stop at plot-level comprehension [~M]
- Developing readers who benefit from an explicit structure distinguishing evidence from inference
- English learners: the visual metaphor reduces language load while preserving analytical demand [+W]
- Less suitable for advanced readers who already infer fluently; for them, the organizer can feel mechanical [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Inferential comprehension: moving from literal recall to causal analysis of character and motivation
- Evidence-based reasoning: grounding interpretations in textual citation
- Social-emotional learning: recognizing hidden influences on behavior supports self-management and responsible decision-making [+W]
- Perspective-taking and empathy: reconstructing a character's below-surface world [Building empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)

### Instructions
1. Select a key event and pose a contestable focal question about why it occurred.
2. Activate relevant knowledge about the characters and setting before analysis ([Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)).
3. Have students reread the event closely, marking relevant passages ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. Students complete the iceberg: above the waterline, cite explicit scenes and actions that caused the event; below, infer traits, identities, and pressures ([Case Study](../elements/case-study.md) structure applied to a textual episode).
5. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) in which students compare below-surface claims and evaluate which are best supported.
6. Close with individual reflection connecting the character's visible/hidden layers to students' own decision-making.

## Examples
- Using S.E. Hinton's *The Outsiders*: above the surface, students cite the Socs' attack and Johnny's prior beating; below, they infer class identity, family neglect, and fear as causes of Johnny killing Bob.
- [Facing History and Ourselves](https://www.facinghistory.org) uses iceberg diagrams to analyze historical events, with visible actions above the waterline and underlying beliefs, pressures, and choices below.
- Middle school SEL advisory: students iceberg their own conflict incident — visible behavior above, emotions and unmet needs below — then discuss self-management strategies.
