# Morphological Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (2 for, 2 unmarked) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
Morphological instruction makes students aware of morphemes — the smallest units of meaning in words — beginning with inflections (–ed, –s, –es, –ing, –er, –est), which change number, person, or tense without changing part of speech, and extending to prefixes and derivational suffixes. It is carried out through [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) on word structure, word sorting and analysis tasks, and structured [practice](../elements/practice.md) in spelling and decomposing words.

## Design Implications

Morphological instruction works because English orthography is morphophonemic: spelling encodes meaning as well as sound, so teaching learners to analyze word parts gives them a systematic basis for spelling and word meaning rather than rote memorization [Morphological interventions improve literacy outcomes.](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows interventions teaching morphemes improve word reading, spelling, vocabulary, and comprehension, with effects strongest when instruction is explicit and integrated with other literacy work [Goodwin & Ahn meta-analysis.](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Word lists organized by morpheme family (e.g., *act, action, active, react*) rather than by superficial letter patterns
- Morphological charts or matrices showing how bases and affixes combine
- Explicit, systematic instruction — this is not learned incidentally from wide reading alone [Morphological interventions improve literacy outcomes.](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [+S]
- Ongoing feedback on spelling attempts with inflections ([Provide Feedback](../strategies/provide_feedback.md))
- A curated set of high-frequency, productive morphemes (e.g., *re-, un-, -tion, -able, struct, spect*) rather than exhaustive lists
- Explicit explanation of how affixes change meaning *and* part of speech, connected to words learners already know ([Activation](../principles/activation.md) of prior vocabulary)
- Repeated opportunities to analyze, compare, and generate words — not just define morphemes ([Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md))
- Attention to morphophonological shifts (*sign → signature; sane → sanity*) that make the system less transparent

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops when instruction is incidental or unsystematic; scattered exposure to word parts does not produce measurable gains [Morphological interventions improve literacy outcomes.](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [-M]
- Inflectional morphology taught before learners have secure [automatic word recognition](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) can overload working memory; sequencing matters [~M]
- Overemphasis on rules with many exceptions (e.g., *y* → *i* before –ed) can produce overgeneralization errors; [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) and contrasting cases reduce this [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Transparent, consistent morphemes yield the largest gains; low-frequency or opaque morphemes produce weak or null effects [~M]
- Instruction limited to rote memorization of affix definitions without word-generation practice shows markedly smaller effects [~S]
- For very young learners still consolidating phonemic decoding, heavy morphological emphasis can compete with foundational [Phonics](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) work [~M] — sequencing matters
- Effects on reading comprehension are indirect and smaller than effects on word-level outcomes; morphology alone does not substitute for comprehension strategy instruction [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- One-on-one or small-group instruction focused on the specific morphemes a learner misspells
- Whole-class word study routines (e.g., word sorts, morpheme matrices) in structured literacy programs
- Embedded in vocabulary instruction for content areas using derivational morphology (Latin and Greek roots)
- **Embedded**: brief morphological analysis within regular vocabulary or reading lessons
- **Standalone**: dedicated word-study units (e.g., structured word inquiry) organized around morpheme families
- **Inquiry-based**: learners investigate etymology and word structure with teacher guidance, versus teacher-led presentation ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md))
- **Language-specific**: especially powerful in morphologically rich languages and in academic English, where Latinate morphology dominates content-area vocabulary

### Target Learners
- Students in grades 2–6, when inflectional spelling is consolidating and derivational morphology becomes productive [Morphological interventions improve literacy outcomes.](../claims/morphological-instruction-improves-literacy.md) [+S]
- Struggling spellers and students with dyslexia, who benefit from meaning-based spelling strategies that supplement phonological decoding [~S]
- English learners, for whom morphological awareness transfers from many first languages [~M]
- Struggling readers and students with dyslexia, who benefit from a structural, meaning-based supplement to phonics [Goodwin & Ahn, 2013] [+M]
- Upper-elementary and middle-grade learners encountering Latinate academic vocabulary, where morphological analysis has the highest payoff [Bowers, Kirby, & Deacon, 2010] [+M]
- English learners, who can leverage cognate morphology across languages [+W]
- Less effective as a stand-alone intervention for students who cannot yet decode single-syllable words [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Spelling accuracy with inflected and derived forms
- Vocabulary growth through recognizing shared morphemes across word families
- Word structure awareness as a foundation for reading comprehension
- Vocabulary growth: inferring meanings of unfamiliar polymorphemic words
- Morphological awareness as a transferable analysis skill: decomposing novel words independently
- Syntax and grammar awareness: recognizing how suffixes signal part of speech

### Instructions
1. Select a target morpheme based on learner error analysis (e.g., past-tense –ed).
2. [Direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md): explicitly name the morpheme, its meaning or grammatical function, and its spelling conventions.
3. Model word analysis aloud — decompose *jumped* into *jump* + *ed* and explain why the spelling stays stable even though the pronunciation shifts.
4. Guided [practice](../elements/practice.md): word sorts, morpheme matrices, and sentence completion requiring correct inflected forms.
5. [Provide feedback](../strategies/provide_feedback.md) on spelling attempts, contrasting correct and incorrect forms ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)).
6. Extend to derivational morphemes and word families to build vocabulary.

## Examples
- **Words Their Way** (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — developmental word study program using word sorts organized by spelling and morphological patterns; widely used in grades 2–6. [https://www.pearson.com](https://www.pearson.com)
- **The Morpheme Matrix routine in structured literacy curricula** — students build words by combining bases and affixes on a matrix, testing which combinations are legal English words.
- **REWARDS** (Archer, Gleason, & Vachon) — intervention teaching multisyllabic word reading through prefixes, suffixes, and roots for intermediate and secondary students.
- **Structured Word Inquiry** (Bowers & Kirby) — classroom approach in which students investigate word families and etymology (e.g., why *do* appears in *does* and *done*), treating spelling as evidence of meaning. See [WordWorks Literacy Centre](https://wordworkskingston.com).
- **REWARDS** (Archer, Gleason, & Vachon, Sopris Learning) — an intervention teaching adolescent struggling readers to flexibly segment multisyllabic words using prefixes and suffixes. [https://www.voyagersopris.com](https://www.voyagersopris.com)
- **Greek and Latin roots vocabulary programs** (e.g., *Vocabulary from Classical Roots*, [https://eps.schoolspecialty.com](https://eps.schoolspecialty.com)) — content-area vocabulary built around root families such as *spect, dict, port*.

# Morphological Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Morphological instruction explicitly teaches the meaningful units of words (morphemes) — base words, roots, prefixes, suffixes, and inflectional endings — and how they combine. Learners are taught to analyze, manipulate, and generate words structurally (e.g., *act → action → inactive → reaction*), rather than memorizing words as unanalyzed wholes. Instruction typically combines direct explanation of common morphemes with word-formation activities, word sorts, and structured inquiry into word origins and families.

## Design Implications

Morphological instruction works because it converts a large memory task (thousands of words) into a generative system (a few hundred productive morphemes), reducing the load of vocabulary acquisition [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows moderate positive effects on literacy outcomes, strongest for vocabulary and spelling, across elementary and secondary grades [Goodwin & Ahn, 2013] [+S]. Its benefits are strongest when instruction emphasizes *how* morphemes signal meaning and syntax (e.g., *-tion* marks a noun; *un-* reverses), not merely their definitions [Bowers, Kirby, & Deacon, 2010] [+M].

2. Model analysis of a familiar word family aloud, showing how meaning is preserved or shifted across derived forms ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) between known and new words).
3. Contrast words where the morpheme behaves predictably with words where it does not, to sharpen boundaries of the generalization [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
4. Have learners generate new words from the morpheme and justify their meanings — production, not recognition, is the goal.
5. Spiral previously taught morphemes into reading and writing tasks so analysis becomes habitual during authentic literacy work.
