Playworks TeamUp Recess Program¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q3 · 1 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

TeamUp is a variant of the Playworks program model in which school staff — rather than a full-time Playworks coach — run a structured recess. Schools receive training, a game guide, and equipment, then treat recess like an instructional period: games are planned in advance, taught explicitly, and led by rotating adults or older students. The design goal is to convert unstructured playground time into a predictable social-learning environment that builds cooperation, conflict resolution, and physical activity while reducing bullying and post-recess classroom disruption.

Design Implications¶

TeamUp applies the logic of Direct Instruction to a non-classroom setting: rather than hoping social skills emerge from free play, it models and rehearses them through repeated, structured game routines. The evidence base for recess-based structured interventions is generally positive — playground interventions reliably increase moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and can reduce antisocial behavior [~M] — and the Mathematica randomized controlled trial of Playworks found improvements in recess safety, student inclusion, and teachers' reports of students' conflict-resolution abilities, with weaker and less consistent effects on academic outcomes [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Trained adults (teachers, paraprofessionals, or parent volunteers) who are present and leading, not merely supervising
  • A small repertoire of well-taught games with explicit rules, so disputes have an agreed reference point (Clear Structure)
  • A conflict-resolution routine (e.g., rock-paper-scissors as the Playworks "junior referee" mechanism) that students learn once and apply independently

Constraints¶

  • Effects depend on implementation fidelity; schools where staff turnover is high or training is superficial see the program degrade into ordinary supervision [~M]
  • Structured games can crowd out the developmental benefits of genuinely free, self-directed play — creativity, self-organized negotiation, and autonomy — if every recess minute is scripted [~M]
  • Gains in recess climate do not reliably transfer to classroom behavior or achievement; the RCT found academic and school-climate effects were smaller and less consistent than recess-level effects [~M]
  • Requires buy-in from playground staff; where adults see recess as "their break," leadership routines collapse

Implementation Variability¶

  • TeamUp (school-led) vs. full Playworks coach model: the coach model embeds a dedicated site coordinator; TeamUp trades intensity for cost and sustainability
  • Junior coach / peer-leader variants: older students run games, adding a learning-by-teaching [+M] benefit for the leaders themselves
  • Partial adoption: schools may implement only the game library and conflict-resolution routine without full scheduling

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary students (K–6), the population in which nearly all recess-intervention evidence has been gathered
  • Students who struggle with unstructured social settings — the predictable rules and adult facilitation lower the social-executive demands of the playground [~M]
  • Older students serving as game leaders, who benefit from leadership responsibility and modeling roles [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Social-emotional skills: turn-taking, inclusion, and peer conflict resolution
  • Behavioral regulation: returning to class settled and ready to learn
  • Physical health: increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity during the school day
  • Procedural fluency in a shared game culture that reduces rule disputes

Instructions¶

  1. Train all recess staff in the game library and the conflict-resolution routine before launch (Coaching).
  2. Publish a weekly recess plan — which games, which zones, which adults — like a lesson plan (Clear Structure).
  3. Teach each new game explicitly during the first days: model it, play a demonstration round, then release students (Practice).
  4. Establish a universal dispute-resolution move (e.g., rock-paper-scissors) so students resolve conflicts without adult adjudication (Fading [+M]).
  5. Rotate student leaders into game-leadership roles to build ownership and reduce adult dependence.
  6. Check in with teachers weekly about post-recess classroom behavior and adjust the game plan (Check-In).

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Recess — the broader category of structured-activity recess interventions; TeamUp is one branded implementation
  • Cooperative Learning — TeamUp games are designed so success requires interdependence among players of mixed skill
  • Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports — TeamUp's predictable routines and explicit expectations operate on the same reinforcement logic in a non-classroom setting
  • Guide students in organized activities during recess and improve the recess play yard to raise physical activity, especially for girls

Examples¶

  • Playworks TeamUp (playworks.org) — the program itself: training, game guides, and equipment kits enabling schools to run structured recess without a full-time coach.
  • Ready for Recess (Omaha, NE) — a similar structured-recess intervention using activity zones and staff training, which increased children's physical activity at recess (Huberty et al., 2013).
  • SPARK Active Recreation (sparkpe.org) — a commercial structured-recess and physical-education program with a comparable game-library model.
