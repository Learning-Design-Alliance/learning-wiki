Interactive Modeling¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 13 studies (5 review, 4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 1 of 13 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Interactive Modeling is a structured instructional routine, developed by the Responsive Classroom approach, for teaching academic, social, and behavioral skills. The educator names the skill and its purpose, demonstrates it while students observe, asks students what they noticed, invites one or more students to model it in turn, and then has the whole group practice immediately. Unlike a one-way Demonstration, the routine alternates modeling with student observation, verbalization, and rehearsal, so learners both see expert performance and generate it themselves.

Design Implications¶

Interactive Modeling combines observational learning with immediate guided practice, which is more effective than modeling alone Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S]. The "what did you notice?" prompts function as structured Self-Explanation, directing attention to the specific behaviors that made the model successful Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. Peer modeling additionally builds self-efficacy: observing similar others succeed strengthens learners' belief that they can perform the skill Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A specific, observable skill that can be demonstrated in under a minute (e.g., entering a discussion, lining up, using a tool, checking work)
  • A rationale the educator can state in one sentence — students learn faster when they know why the skill matters
  • Time for at least one full cycle: model → notice → student model → notice → practice
  • A supportive climate where imperfect first attempts are treated as information, not failure

Constraints¶

  • Inefficient for complex, multi-session skills; better suited to discrete routines than extended procedures — break large skills into part-tasks first Part-task practice reduces load for novices. [+M]
  • Student models who perform the skill incorrectly can propagate errors; the educator must be prepared to re-model and highlight corrections
  • Overuse for skills students already know wastes time and can feel patronizing Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • Requires repetition across days for routines to become automatic; a single cycle rarely produces durable behavior

Implementation Variability¶

  • Academic skills: modeling how to annotate a text, set up a lab notebook, or check math work before Practice
  • Social-emotional skills: modeling how to join a game, disagree respectfully, or lead a class meditation (as in the second-grade example of a student leading mindfulness with educator-provided framing)
  • Teacher-only modeling: for high-stakes or safety-critical skills, the educator models all rounds before students try
  • Video-based: recorded models allow replay and slow-motion noticing, useful for motor skills or remote settings

Target Learners¶

  • Novices learning a classroom routine, tool, or procedure for the first time
  • Young learners and students who benefit from concrete, observed examples rather than verbal directions alone
  • Students with low self-efficacy for the skill, who gain confidence from watching peers succeed Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less appropriate for students already fluent in the skill, for whom the routine adds little

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency: making routines and skills automatic so working memory is freed for content Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Self-regulation: teaching self-checking and monitoring behaviors
  • Social skills: observable interpersonal behaviors that are difficult to teach through explanation alone

Instructions¶

  1. Name the skill and its purpose. State what students will learn and why it matters, in one or two sentences.
  2. Model the skill. Demonstrate it exactly as students should do it, at normal speed, keeping narration minimal so observation stays clean (see Think-Aloud when reasoning, not just behavior, is the target).
  3. Ask what students noticed. Elicit specific observations ("What did I do with my hands? What did I say first?") rather than general impressions.
  4. Invite one or more students to model. Choose volunteers; ask the group again what they noticed about the peer's model.
  5. Have all students practice immediately. Run a short, whole-group rehearsal of the skill (Practice).
  6. Provide feedback and repeat. Reinforce specific correct behaviors; re-model if the practice reveals misunderstandings.
  7. Revisit over subsequent days. Brief re-practices until the routine is automatic, then fade the modeling (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Modeling — the broader category; Interactive Modeling adds structured noticing, peer modeling, and immediate practice
  • Direct Instruction — shares the explicit, teacher-led opening but ends in student rehearsal rather than extended teacher talk
  • Role Play — students enact scenarios; Interactive Modeling supplies the observed model that makes role play productive

Related Elements¶

  • Modeling — the core demonstration step
  • Practice — the immediate rehearsal step that converts observation into performance
  • Coaching — the feedback and correction during and after practice rounds
  • Fading — withdrawing modeling support as the routine becomes automatic

Examples¶

  • Responsive Classroom — The Center for Responsive Schools publishes the Interactive Modeling routine and books (e.g., Interactive Modeling: A Powerful Technique for Teaching Children, Wilson, 2012); widely used in elementary classrooms for routines like morning meeting behaviors, transitions, and materials care.
  • Second-grade mindfulness routine — An educator models leading a brief meditation, students notice the specific moves (voice level, posture, pacing), a student then leads with the educator observing, and the class practices — building both the routine and the student leader's self-efficacy.
  • Middle school lab safety — The teacher models lighting a burner and securing goggles, students articulate what they noticed, two students re-model, and the class rehearses before independent lab work.
