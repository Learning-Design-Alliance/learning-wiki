# Community Involvement

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Community involvement brings people beyond the instructional core — family members, local experts, community organizations, and online communities — into the learning process. It is carried out through guest participation and mentoring in physical classrooms, family partnership programs, and online communities or social media that extend collaboration beyond class time.

## Design Implications

Community involvement works primarily through motivation and belonging rather than direct instruction: learners engage more persistently when work has an authentic audience and when they feel connected to a community of people who value it [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. It is most effective when community participation is structured around specific learning goals — an expert critique, a family data-collection task — rather than generic presence [~M]. Programs that combine school, family, and community spheres with clear role definitions outperform ad hoc involvement.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A defined learning purpose for each community contribution (audience, expertise, or resource), not presence for its own sake
- Coordination and scheduling structures so participation is predictable rather than episodic
- A welcoming classroom environment where additional adults and online interactions are integrated into routines ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- For online communities: moderation, norms of interaction, and privacy safeguards

#### Constraints
- Unstructured adult presence can disrupt routines or reduce learners' sense of ownership; some learners are distracted or inhibited by observers [~W]
- Online communities without moderation expose learners to off-task interaction and uneven quality of contributions [-W]
- Participation is inequitable: family involvement programs tend to over-recruit already-advantaged families unless outreach is deliberately designed [-M]
- One-off events (career days, single visits) show weak effects on achievement; sustained partnership matters [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical:** guest experts, family learning nights, community-based projects, intergenerational mentoring
- **Online:** moderated discussion communities, social media showcases of work, expert Q&A (e.g., scientist office hours)
- **Hybrid:** [Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md) projects where learners produce something for a real local audience

### Target Learners
- Learners at risk of disengagement, for whom visible community connection strengthens belonging [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Adolescents developing identity and social competence through authentic adult relationships
- Less automatically beneficial for all learners equally — benefits depend on the quality and relevance of the connection, not mere presence [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: connecting learning to real audiences and purposes
- Social and collaborative skills: working with varied partners and adults
- Citizenship and empathy: understanding community roles and perspectives ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))

### Instructions
1. Identify a learning goal that a community contribution would genuinely serve (authentic audience, missing expertise, or a resource).
2. Recruit and brief participants — family members, local experts, or online community members — on their specific role and the relevant content.
3. Prepare learners with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or [Activation](../elements/activation.md) activity so the interaction builds on prior knowledge rather than starting cold.
4. Structure the interaction as collaboration with defined roles ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), with group rewards tied to individual accountability [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S].
5. Debrief and showcase: have learners present outcomes back to the community, closing the loop between contribution and audience.

## Related Strategies
- Community-based project designs — the curricular form of this strategy, where the community is the subject and audience of the work
- Family partnership programs — the home-side complement to classroom involvement

## Examples
- **[Edmodo](https://www.edmodo.com) / Google Classroom guardian summaries** — moderated online communities connecting families to ongoing class activity rather than one-off events.
- **[Epstein's Six Types of Involvement framework](https://www.corwin.com)** — the National Network of Partnership Schools model structuring parenting, communicating, volunteering, learning at home, decision-making, and collaborating with the community.
- **Scientist-in-the-classroom programs** (e.g., [Skype a Scientist](https://www.skypeascientist.com)) — online expert Q&A sessions that give learners access to authentic disciplinary communities.
- **[Community-based learning projects](../principles/community-based-learning.md)** — learners produce reports, designs, or services for local organizations, making the community the authentic audience.
