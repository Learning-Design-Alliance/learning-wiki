# Traffic Lights Self-Assessment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students mark their own confidence in a just-completed assignment or homework using a traffic-light color: red means unsure of success, orange means partially unsure, and green means confident of success (Black & Wiliam, 2006). The colors then drive differentiated grouping rather than functioning as a mere display of feeling: students who marked orange or green work together in mixed groups evaluating each other's work through peer assessment, while the teacher works directly with the students who marked red, giving them more direct support rather than expecting them to benefit as much from peer evaluation alone.

Self-assessment more generally depends on students having a clear enough sense of a learning goal, the steps to reach it, and their own progress toward it (Sadler, 1989), and effective self-assessment research finds it can genuinely enhance learning and motivation, not just provide teachers an efficiency shortcut (Black & Wiliam, 2006). Because students often find it easier to judge quality by comparing their own work against others', self-assessment in practice tends to blend into peer assessment, and having explicit criteria — such as an analytical scoring rubric, whether teacher-provided or co-developed with students — makes both far more reliable than an unstructured judgment call.

One classroom-culture precondition matters here: self and peer assessment work as intended only when the classroom atmosphere is genuinely built around incremental views of ability and learning goals (see [Goal-Orientation Theory](../theories/goal-orientation-theory.md)). In a classroom that instead emphasizes interpersonal competition for limited rewards, students have a real incentive to inflate their own (and their friends') self-assessments, since accuracy costs them relative standing.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit, shared criteria (ideally a rubric) that students can actually judge their own work against, rather than an unstructured sense of "how well did I do"
- A classroom culture oriented toward incremental ability and learning goals rather than competitive ranking — the strategy's honesty depends on this
#### Constraints
- In a competitively-oriented classroom, self-assessment (traffic lights included) creates a direct incentive to over-report confidence, undermining the very information the strategy is meant to surface
- Requires the teacher to actually have capacity to work directly with the "red" group while the rest self-organize — the strategy assumes this differentiated capacity, not just the color-coding step itself

### Target Learners
- All students; the differentiated response (teacher attention to "red," peer evaluation for "orange"/"green") specifically targets students who are least confident with the most direct support

### Target Learning Goals
- Accurate self-monitoring of understanding, and efficient allocation of teacher attention toward students who most need it, driven by student-reported rather than teacher-guessed confidence

### Instructions
1. After an assignment or homework is complete, have students mark their own confidence in their success: red (unsure), orange (partially unsure), or green (confident).
2. Group students who marked orange or green together to evaluate each other's work using shared criteria (ideally a rubric).
3. Work directly with students who marked red, rather than routing them into peer evaluation alone.
4. Maintain a classroom culture oriented toward incremental ability and learning goals — without it, the color-coding will tend to inflate rather than inform.
