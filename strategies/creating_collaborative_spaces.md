# Creating Collaborative Spaces

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Creating collaborative spaces means structuring the classroom — physically, socially, and procedurally — so that peer interaction and joint problem-solving are the default mode of learning rather than an occasional add-on. This includes room layout, group formation routines, discussion norms, and task designs that require interdependence. The strategy treats collaboration as an environmental condition to be engineered, not a personality trait to be hoped for.

## Design Implications

Collaboration improves achievement when it is structured — with positive interdependence, individual accountability, and explicit interpersonal skills — rather than merely permitted [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Small-group learning produces reliable gains over lecture in undergraduate STEM settings [Small-group learning improves achievement and attitudes in undergraduate STEM.](../claims/small-group-learning-improves-stem-achievement.md) [+S], and active formats broadly outperform traditional lecture on exam performance and failure rates [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The environment must make collaboration the path of least resistance: seating, group size, and task design should make talking to peers easier than working alone.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tasks with genuine [positive interdependence](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — a shared goal that cannot be completed by one person alone
- Individual accountability mechanisms so free-riding is visible and costly
- Deliberate group composition and rotation routines (size 2–4 for most tasks)
- Physical or virtual layout that supports face-to-face interaction: movable seating, shared displays, or breakout structures
- Explicit norms and taught interpersonal skills, not assumed ones

#### Constraints
- Unstructured group work degrades into social loafing and status-based participation gaps; without individual accountability, low-status students contribute and learn less [-S]
- Groups formed without attention to composition can reproduce classroom hierarchies, with high-achieving students dominating explanation-giving [~M]
- Collaboration imposes extraneous load for novices on complex tasks; negotiating with peers while simultaneously learning new content can exceed working memory capacity [~M]
- Poorly specified tasks let groups converge prematurely on shallow answers instead of productive disagreement [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Think-pair-share and peer instruction** — low-structure entry points that require no room redesign
- **Jigsaw structures** — each member holds unique information, making interdependence structural rather than exhortative
- **Studio or workshop layouts** — permanent reconfiguration of space for team-based work (e.g., SCALE-UP classrooms)
- **Online collaborative spaces** — shared documents, breakout rooms, and discussion forums substituting spatial design for procedural design

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary learners across achievement levels, with the largest gains for underrepresented and previously lower-achieving students [Small-group learning improves achievement and attitudes in undergraduate STEM.](../claims/small-group-learning-improves-stem-achievement.md) [+S]
- Learners who benefit from articulating reasoning aloud, since explaining to peers drives [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Less effective when groups are heterogeneous in a way that lets stronger students simply perform the task for others without dialogue [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding that benefits from negotiation and articulation of ideas
- Communication, perspective-taking, and teamwork as explicit outcomes
- Problem-solving where multiple approaches can be compared across group members

### Instructions
1. Define a task that genuinely requires multiple contributors; if one person could finish it alone, redesign it or use [Jigsaw](../elements/jigsaw.md) to distribute essential information.
2. Form groups of 2–4 deliberately, assigning roles where accountability needs support ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)).
3. Arrange the physical or virtual space so members face each other with a shared work surface.
4. Establish and teach interaction norms — how to disagree, how to check understanding — as explicit content, not assumptions.
5. Build in individual accountability: random reporter selection, individual exit tickets, or [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) components credited individually.
6. Monitor groups, intervening on group process (not just content) when participation is unequal ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
7. Debrief both the content and the collaboration itself so group process improves over time.

## Examples
- **SCALE-UP (Student-Centered Active Learning Environment with Upside-down Pedagogies)** — round tables of nine students in purpose-built rooms; institutions adopting it report reduced failure rates in introductory physics (https://www.scaleup.ncsu.edu).
- **PBLNetwork / Buck Institute PBL works** — project-based learning classrooms where teams share a driving question over weeks, with roles and accountability structures built in (https://www.pblworks.org).
- **Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — clicker questions followed by mandatory peer discussion before revoting, converting any lecture hall into a collaborative space.
