# Turn Around Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Turn around time is the interval between a learner submitting or performing work and receiving feedback on it. The strategy treats that interval as a design variable: instructors and course systems deliberately shorten it — through rapid grading cycles, automated checks, in-class conferencing, or live commentary — so learners can act on feedback while their reasoning about the task is still retrievable.

## Design Implications

Feedback loses instructional value as the mental context of the performance decays; immediate or near-immediate feedback supports error correction and prevents errors from consolidating, particularly for procedural skills [~M]. Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and the process rather than the person, and timeliness is what makes process-level feedback actionable at all [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Fast turn around also sustains formative assessment cycles: feedback only functions formatively if learners have time and opportunity to use it before the next performance ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A feedback mechanism sized to the volume of work: rubrics, comment banks, automated checking, or peer review structures
- A scheduled opportunity for learners to *apply* the feedback — a revision, retry, or subsequent task — otherwise speed is wasted
- Clear priorities in the feedback itself; rapid feedback on everything becomes noise ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))

#### Constraints
- Immediate feedback during complex problem solving can promote guessing and trial-and-error rather than deliberation, and can increase cognitive load for novices [~M] — delayed feedback sometimes benefits retention of complex material
- Speed achieved by cutting feedback quality (bare scores, generic comments) negates the benefit; task- and process-level information matters more than raw latency [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- Automated instant feedback works well for well-defined answers but misleads on open-ended work where judgment is required
- Very fast turn around is unsustainable for instructors and can produce burnout or superficial grading late in a term

#### Implementation Variability
- **Automated**: auto-graded quizzes and coding checkers (e.g., Codecademy, WebAssign) give second-level turn around on well-structured tasks
- **In-class**: live circulation, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), and [5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md) deliver feedback during production rather than after
- **Staged**: rapid low-stakes feedback on drafts, slower holistic feedback on final work
- **Peer-mediated**: structured peer review shortens the loop while building evaluative judgment

### Target Learners
- Novices acquiring procedural skills, where early error correction prevents consolidation of wrong methods [~M]
- Learners in iterative production contexts (writing, coding, design) who can revise
- Less suitable when learners lack the background to interpret fast feedback without [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) support

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural accuracy and skill refinement
- Formative improvement of drafts and performances
- Self-regulation: learners calibrate their own judgment against rapid external feedback ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))

### Instructions
1. Identify which tasks most need speed — typically those where errors compound or where a revision will follow.
2. Build the fastest adequate mechanism: rubrics, comment banks, or automated checks ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
3. Focus rapid feedback on task and process, not scores alone [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]; see [Action-Oriented Feedback](../strategies/action-oriented-feedback.md).
4. Schedule the follow-up act — revision, retry, or next task — so feedback is used, not just received.
5. For complex or open-ended work, deliberately delay feedback until learners have attempted self-assessment.

## Examples
- **Codecademy** ([codecademy.com](https://www.codecademy.com)) — code runs and returns errors within seconds, the extreme case of automated turn around on well-structured tasks.
- **Writing process pedagogy** — instructor comments on drafts returned before the revision deadline, so feedback feeds the next version rather than terminating the assignment.
- **WebAssign / auto-graded homework systems** — immediate right/wrong feedback with worked hints, trading depth for latency.
