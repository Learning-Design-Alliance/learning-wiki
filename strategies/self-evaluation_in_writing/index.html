Self-Evaluation in Writing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 causal, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Self-evaluation involves teaching students how to review their writing to decide if changes are needed. By modeling when to stop and evaluate writing and providing specific questions to ask about each paragraph, students learn to identify areas for improvement. It is a core self-regulation behavior: writers who can judge their own drafts against criteria need less external feedback and can revise independently.

Design Implications¶

Self-evaluation is the self-assessment step within self-regulated writing cycles (planning → drafting → evaluating → revising). Its effectiveness depends on giving students explicit criteria and models of evaluation, not just an instruction to "check your work" — unguided self-assessment produces inflated judgments and little revision Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]. Evaluation prompts work best when they direct attention to the task and the writing process rather than a global grade, mirroring the conditions under which feedback itself is most effective Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A writing assignment with clear, visible evaluation criteria (rubric, checklist, or question set)
  • Instructor modeling of the evaluation process — thinking aloud while judging a sample paragraph (Think-Aloud)
  • A follow-on revision opportunity so evaluation leads to action, not just judgment
  • Consistent practice and feedback on the accuracy of students' self-judgments

Constraints¶

  • Novice writers systematically overestimate the quality of their drafts; without calibrated criteria and comparison to models, self-evaluation reinforces errors rather than correcting them [-M]
  • Students with weak metacognitive skills may complete checklists mechanically without genuine judgment, producing no revision benefit [~M]
  • Effectiveness drops when criteria are vague ("make it better") or when evaluation is decoupled from revision time — judging without the opportunity or motivation to revise yields little improvement [-M]
  • Requires sustained practice; one-off self-evaluation lessons do not establish the habit [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Checklist-based evaluation (itemized criteria per paragraph) vs. question-based evaluation (open prompts like "Does my topic sentence state the main idea?")
  • Comparison-based evaluation: students judge their draft against a model text or contrasting examples
  • Structured peer evaluation as a stepping stone: evaluating a peer's draft with the same criteria builds the judgment skills needed for self-evaluation
  • Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD) embeds self-evaluation as one of four self-regulation strategies alongside goal setting, self-instruction, and self-reinforcement

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling writers, particularly students with learning and thinking differences, who often lack spontaneous revision strategies [+M]
  • Upper elementary through secondary students developing independent writing; benefits increase as students gain basic composition skills [~M]
  • Less effective for very young or novice writers who cannot yet apply abstract criteria — these learners need teacher-led evaluation first [-W]
  • Students with low writing self-efficacy benefit from documenting their own improvements, since visible progress supports persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Revision and editing skills: identifying and fixing weaknesses in drafts
  • Metacognitive monitoring: judging one's own performance against criteria Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Transferable self-assessment: building evaluation habits that generalize to other domains
  • Explaining and justifying writing decisions Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Set the task and share explicit evaluation criteria (rubric or question set) aligned to the genre.
  2. Model evaluation: think aloud while judging a sample paragraph against the criteria, showing how to detect a problem and decide on a fix (Think-Aloud).
  3. Have students evaluate their own draft using the checklist or questions, marking strengths and weaknesses (Assess Performance).
  4. Require a revision pass based on the self-identified weaknesses (Practice).
  5. Provide feedback on the accuracy of students' self-evaluations, not only on the writing itself (Provide Feedback).
  6. Fade the supports: move from full checklists to student-generated criteria as evaluation skill grows (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Self-Monitoring — the broader self-regulation behavior of which writing self-evaluation is a specific instance
  • Revision — the action step that converts evaluation judgments into improved text
  • Peer Review — externalizes the same evaluation criteria onto a peer's draft before self-application

Related Elements¶

  • Assess Performance — the evaluation act itself, whether self- or instructor-directed
  • Provide Feedback — external feedback calibrates students' self-judgments over time
  • Rubrics — the criteria structure that makes self-evaluation concrete
  • Think-Aloud — the modeling method for teaching evaluation moves

Examples¶

SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development) — Graham and Harris's extensively tested framework has students set goals, self-instruct, and self-evaluate their persuasive and narrative writing against genre-specific charts; dozens of studies show large effects on writing quality for students with and without learning disabilities.

Read, Write, Think — NCTE/IRA lesson plans include self-evaluation checklists and peer-editing guides students apply to their own drafts before revision.

Portfolio self-assessment in Writers' Workshop — Students select pieces, evaluate them against published criteria, and write reflective cover letters justifying their judgments, combining evaluation with self-explanation.
