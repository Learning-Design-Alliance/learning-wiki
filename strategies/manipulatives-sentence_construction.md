# Manipulatives: Sentence Construction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners write or compose sentences using physical objects — color-coded blocks, magnetic word tiles, or word cards — where each object or color represents a word, phrase, or part of speech. By physically arranging and rearranging these objects, learners visualize sentence structure (subject–verb–object, clause boundaries, phrase order) rather than encountering grammar purely as abstract rules. The strategy converts syntactic relationships into a spatial, manipulable form that can be built, tested, and revised.

## Design Implications

Manipulatives work by grounding abstract grammatical categories in concrete, spatial representations, consistent with dual coding of verbal and visual/spatial information [media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on the mapping between object and concept being explicit and consistently color-coded; arbitrary or shifting mappings add extraneous load rather than reducing it [chunking-reduces-working-memory-load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Physical activity itself can support comprehension when the action maps meaningfully onto the language being learned [Glenberg et al. found physical manipulation improved children's reading comprehension.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.424) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, explicit color or shape code for grammatical categories (e.g., nouns blue, verbs red), introduced via [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) before independent use
- Sufficient tiles/blocks per learner or pair, plus a writing surface for recording composed sentences
- A follow-on step connecting the physical arrangement back to written text — learners must translate the block arrangement into an actual sentence ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Poorly designed manipulatives can increase cognitive load: learners may attend to the objects themselves rather than the syntax they represent [Concrete manipulatives can hinder learning when they demand more processing than they offload.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031084) [~S]
- Physical manipulation can become the activity itself; without prompts to explain *why* a tile was moved, learners may complete the task mechanically [self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [-M]
- Material preparation is time-consuming, and some learners find the tactile component distracting from the linguistic goal
- Benefits fade as learners internalize syntax; continued reliance on tiles can become a crutch that delays automatic written production

#### Implementation Variability
- Magnetic tiles on whiteboards (whole-class modeling), individual block sets, paper word cards, or digital drag-and-drop equivalents
- Sentence-building from fixed word banks (constrained) versus open composition with blank tiles learners label themselves (open-ended)
- Error-based variants: learners repair a deliberately malformed tile sentence, leveraging [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) to sharpen grammatical discrimination

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-school learners acquiring parts of speech and basic sentence patterns [+M]
- Learners who benefit from embodied, multi-sensory input, including many struggling readers and English language learners [Glenberg et al. found physical manipulation improved children's reading comprehension.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.424) [+M]
- Less valuable for learners with fluent syntax, for whom the concrete layer is redundant and slows production [Concrete manipulatives can hinder learning when they demand more processing than they offload.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031084) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Syntactic awareness: identifying subjects, verbs, objects, and modifiers within sentences
- Grammar generalization: applying parts-of-speech knowledge to compose original sentences
- Revision skills: diagnosing and fixing fragments, run-ons, and agreement errors by physically rearranging components

### Instructions
1. Introduce the color/shape code and model composing one sentence with tiles, narrating each decision ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
2. Learners compose a target sentence with tiles, then read it aloud and record it in writing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. Prompt learners to explain why each tile sits where it does, or to justify a rearrangement ([self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]).
4. Present a malformed tile sentence for learners to diagnose and repair ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)).
5. Fade the tiles: move from tile-first composition to writing first and checking with tiles, then to writing alone ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **Magnetic word tiles** (e.g., Magna-Tiles-style word kits or Dowling Magnets sentence-building sets) used on classroom whiteboards: the teacher builds a model sentence, then pairs rearrange tiles to vary sentence openings.
- **Montessori grammar symbols** — a long-established system in which each part of speech has a distinct shape and color (noun = black triangle, verb = red circle); children place symbols above words in written sentences, the same mapping principle applied to analysis rather than construction ([Montessori grammar materials](https://www.montessori-n-such.com)).
- **Sentence-building board games** such as Learning Resources' Sentence Building Dominoes, where learners physically sequence color-coded word tiles and read the result aloud.
