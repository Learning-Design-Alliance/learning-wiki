Consider explicit morphology instruction given the limitations of natural print exposure¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 associational), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article argues that because natural reading exposure provides detectable, repeated affix experience for only a small set of affixes, a discussion about explicit morphology instruction may be in order. It notes that "pupils in English-speaking countries typically receive little explicit morphological instruction in school", that current instruction focuses on accurate spelling with limited time, and that teacher knowledge of morphology is variable. The strategy is to revisit how morphology is taught in light of the quantified limitations of incidental print exposure.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Instruction would need to go beyond the current focus on accurate spelling, given the article's report that instructional time is limited and teacher knowledge of morphology is variable.

Constraints¶

  • The article frames this as a discussion to be had rather than a tested intervention; no instructional programme was evaluated in the study.

Target Learners¶

  • Primary and secondary school children learning to read in English

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Derivational morpheme knowledge enabling vocabulary growth and morphological analysis of unfamiliar words

Affordances¶

  • Print Exposure Account Of Affix Learning

Claims¶

  • Morphologically complex words in children's books are used rarely and are poorly distributed across individual books [+M]
  • Only a few affixes occur with many distinct stems in children's books, with prefix exposure especially impoverished [+M]
  • Orthographic detectability of derivational affixes is low and highly variable, with pseudo-affixation rare overall but concentrated in some affixes [+M]
  • Exposure to suffixed words is far greater than exposure to prefixed words in popular children's books [+M]
  • Morphologically complex words comprise about half of the distinct words in children's books, and their proportion increases with book target age [+M]
  • Children's books contain many morphologically complex words absent from children's television language [+W]
