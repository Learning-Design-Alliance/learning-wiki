# Discussion (Pre-Reading)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A pre-reading discussion asks learners to take a position on the topic of an upcoming text *before* reading it — often by physically moving to a spot representing their stance (e.g., agree/disagree corners), constructing arguments for that position, and debating peers who hold opposing views. The goal is not to reach consensus but to activate prior knowledge, generate questions, and create a felt need to read: learners arrive at the text with stakes in the outcome.

## Design Implications

Pre-reading discussion works by activating relevant prior knowledge and creating cognitive disequilibrium when learners encounter peers with opposing claims — conditions that increase engagement with and comprehension of the subsequent text [Activating prior knowledge improves comprehension of related texts.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [~M]. Committing publicly to a position before reading also sets up a verification goal: learners read to test their arguments rather than to passively absorb, which supports deeper processing [Productive failure improves conceptual learning compared with direct instruction first.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]. The strategy is a discussion-based variant of anticipation guides and [Activation](../elements/activation.md) techniques.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A provocative, genuinely contestable proposition drawn from (or adjacent to) the reading topic
- A mechanism for public commitment — physical positioning, written stance, or vote — so positions are visible and accountable
- Structured argumentation time before debate, so positions rest on reasons rather than volume ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- A debrief that connects the debate to the text: what would change your mind? What do you need the reading to settle?

#### Constraints
- If the proposition is factual rather than contestable, learners with no relevant knowledge cannot participate meaningfully and the discussion collapses into guessing [~M]
- Debate can entrench misconceptions when learners lack the background knowledge to evaluate arguments; the follow-up text or instruction must explicitly confront any errors surfaced [~S]
- Dominant voices can suppress minority positions unless structures (turns, written positions, assigned roles) equalize participation
- Learners who commit strongly to a wrong position may resist textual evidence that contradicts it; the teacher must frame the reading as adjudication, not as confirmation or humiliation

#### Implementation Variability
- **Four-corners debate**: learners move to corners representing strongly agree / agree / disagree / strongly disagree, then defend their corner
- **Anticipation guide + discussion**: learners respond in writing to statements, then discuss in pairs before whole-group debate
- **Structured academic controversy**: teams argue one side, then switch sides and argue the opposite before reading
- **Silent discussion**: written positions on the board or shared document, commented on asynchronously, for classes where verbal debate disadvantages some learners

### Target Learners
- Learners with some prior knowledge of or opinions about the topic, who can generate at least a tentative argument [~M]
- Reluctant readers, for whom a stake in the outcome supplies a purpose for reading
- Less suitable for complete novices on technical topics, where pre-reading debate risks rehearsing errors without the knowledge to correct them [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: building a schema and purpose before encountering the text
- Argumentation skills: constructing and defending claims, considering counterarguments
- Conceptual change: surfacing intuitions and misconceptions so the text can address them [Productive failure improves conceptual learning compared with direct instruction first.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Select or write 2–4 contestable propositions central to the reading; avoid statements with an obvious answer.
2. Have learners commit publicly to a position — move to a corner, sign a position sheet, or post a vote ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Give 3–5 minutes for learners to generate arguments and evidence for their position before debate begins.
4. Run the debate with equalized turns or assigned speaking roles; require each side to respond to at least one opposing argument ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md)).
5. Ask "what would change your mind?" to focus attention on the evidence the reading will supply.
6. Read the text; afterwards, return to the propositions and have learners revisit their original positions, citing textual evidence for any change ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can bridge debate to text structure).

## Examples
- **Reading Apprenticeship (WestEd)** — uses pre-reading "talk to the text" routines in which students discuss predictions and stances on a text's topic before reading, as part of its metacognitive conversation framework ([https://readingapprenticeship.org](https://readingapprenticeship.org))
- **Four-corners debate before a civics unit** — students physically position themselves on "voting should be mandatory" before reading source documents on compulsory voting, then annotate the texts for arguments supporting or challenging their corner
- **Structured Academic Controversy (Cooperative learning, Johnson & Johnson)** — students argue one side of an issue, switch sides, and then read to synthesize; the pre-reading debate phase is the commitment-and-argument component
