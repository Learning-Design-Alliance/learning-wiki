# Discussion Web

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A discussion web is a graphic organizer that enables students to examine both sides of an issue before agreeing on a conclusion, promoting deeper processing of texts through meaningful conversational discussion. Students first generate individual ideas from their reading, then pair up to record, discuss, and resolve their perspectives on the web's pro/con branches; pairs then join into foursomes to reconcile positions, and the foursome nominates a speaker to present a reasoned conclusion to the whole class. The structure guarantees that every student produces written arguments before speaking, converting an open-ended question into a visible map of evidence for and against.

## Design Implications

Structured peer discussion of this kind is a form of [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) that outperforms lecture-only formats for measurable outcomes [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The web's two-column structure creates the cognitive conflict that motivates conceptual change: students must confront evidence that contradicts their initial position [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. Because positions must be written before they are spoken, the organizer also functions as rehearsal for [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md), lowering the verbal demands of whole-class participation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text or issue genuinely open to multiple defensible positions; a closed or factual question collapses the pro/con structure
- A discussion web graphic organizer (paper or digital) with a central question, two evidence columns, and a conclusion space
- Explicit instruction and modeling of the think–pair–square–share sequence before independent use
- Facilitation that enforces turn-taking and requires conclusions to cite text evidence

#### Constraints
- Without modeling, groups default to one dominant voice; the written-first structure must be enforced or the discussion degrades [~M]
- Time-intensive: the full sequence typically consumes most of a class period, crowding out other content
- Students with limited reading fluency or difficulty expressing ideas verbally may be disadvantaged unless texts and organizers are scaffolded
- Works poorly when the question has a single correct answer — forced "both sides" framing of settled questions undermines the argumentation [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Scale the sequence down (think–pair–share with a mini-web) for younger learners or shorter texts
- Use pre-reading to build background, or post-reading to consolidate interpretation
- Extend into formal [Debate](debate.md) by assigning the web's columns as [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)
- Digital variants (shared documents, discussion boards) allow asynchronous webs in online courses

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through adult learners; the organizer adapts across grade levels and content areas (literature, social studies, science, mathematics)
- Reluctant discussants benefit most: writing before speaking gives processing time that impromptu discussion denies [~M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for students still developing basic collaboration routines or verbal expression

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking: weighing evidence for competing positions on an open-ended question
- Argumentation: constructing claims supported by textual evidence and responding to counterarguments
- Comprehension: revisiting, questioning, and clarifying ideas from a text through social negotiation
- Communication: listening, speaking, and writing in service of a shared intellectual goal

### Instructions
1. Select a text and pose a genuinely open question with two defensible sides; display the web's structure.
2. Students read and individually jot ideas for both columns of the web.
3. Pairs merge their ideas, discuss disagreements, and record a shared position on the web ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)).
4. Pairs join into foursomes, reconcile remaining differences, and draft a conclusion with supporting reasons.
5. Each foursome's nominated speaker presents the conclusion to the class; the teacher synthesizes positions during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
6. Students individually write a final response, converting discussion into durable written reasoning.

## Examples
- **Literature (secondary ELA):** After reading a short story, students web the question "Was the protagonist's final decision justified?" — pairs cite textual evidence for and against before the class synthesis.
- **Social studies:** A web on "Was the New Deal effective?" prepares students for a formal [Debate](debate.md), with each column becoming a team's brief.
- **Science (elementary):** Duthie's original classroom work used webs around questions like "Should the town build the dam?" to integrate reading, discussion, and persuasive writing.
- **Mathematics:** A web on "Is zero a number?" surfaces competing conceptions before instruction on number systems.
