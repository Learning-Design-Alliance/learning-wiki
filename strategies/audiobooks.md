# Audiobooks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Audiobooks deliver text through narrated audio, giving learners an alternative channel for accessing the same content as print reading. They can replace print for learners whose listening comprehension outstrips their decoding ability, or supplement it through combined audio-plus-print presentation. The strategy is carried out by curating recordings matched to learners' interests and reading levels, embedding them in reading time or assigned readings, and following up with comprehension activities.

## Design Implications

Audiobooks exploit the fact that listening comprehension typically develops ahead of reading comprehension in children, letting learners engage with age-appropriate content and vocabulary before they can decode it independently [~M]. Evidence on equivalence is mixed: listening to text produces comprehension roughly comparable to reading for many learners, but not reliably better, and combined audio-plus-print presentation can outperform either alone [Rogowsky et al. found no significant comprehension difference between listening and reading for adults, with combined presentation showing an advantage.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220973.2015.1065217) [~M]. Because audio is transient, pairing it with print or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) supports re-inspection of difficult passages and reduces extraneous load from holding spoken content in working memory [Media combinations affect recall and retention depending on how channels are used.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to audiobook recordings and playback devices (library services, school subscriptions, or platforms such as [Learning Ally](https://learningally.org) or [Audible](https://www.audible.com))
- Titles matched to learners' interests and appropriate difficulty — listening level, not just decoding level
- A follow-on comprehension activity ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), quizzes, or written response) so listening does not remain passive

#### Constraints
- Audio alone is transient: learners cannot easily re-read a complex sentence, which hurts comprehension of dense, technical, or unfamiliar material [-M]
- Narration pace is fixed; learners who process slowly or need to pause and rehearse may fall behind unless playback speed and pausing are learner-controlled [-M]
- For skilled readers, substituting audio for print can remove valuable practice in decoding and fluency, and may reduce retention relative to reading [~M]
- Passive listening without a purpose or follow-up task produces shallow engagement, mirroring the illusion-of-knowing problem in passive observation [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Audio-only substitution** — for learners whose decoding is far below their listening comprehension (e.g., dyslexia, emerging readers)
- **Ear-and-eye reading** — learner follows print while listening; supports fluency, word recognition, and access to harder texts
- **Preview listening** — listen before reading to build background knowledge and vocabulary, then read print independently
- **Learner-controlled playback** — adjustable speed, bookmarking, and replay to restore the re-inspection affordance of print

### Target Learners
- Learners with auditory processing strengths or decoding difficulties (e.g., dyslexia) whose listening comprehension exceeds their reading level [~M]
- English language learners, who benefit from hearing prosody and pronunciation while building vocabulary [~W]
- Emerging readers building vocabulary and background knowledge beyond their independent reading level [~W]
- Less appropriate as a print replacement for learners who need decoding practice or who comprehend better from visual text [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of narrative and informational content when decoding is the bottleneck
- Vocabulary growth and exposure to rich language models through fluent narration
- Reading motivation and stamina for learners who experience print as aversive
- Fluency support when audio is paired with print (ear-and-eye reading)

### Instructions
1. Select audiobooks matched to learners' interests and listening-comprehension level, not their decoding level ([Personalization](../principles/personalization.md))
2. Decide the mode: audio-only for access, or audio-plus-print when fluency and word recognition are also goals
3. Set a purpose for listening before playback ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
4. During listening, have learners [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or note questions if following print; allow pausing and replay
5. Follow with a comprehension activity — [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), retelling, or written response — to consolidate and assess
6. Compare comprehension on audio versus print tasks over time to decide when to fade audio support

## Examples
- **Independent reading time with audiobooks** — students listen to novels during sustained silent reading, accessing texts above their decoding level; recommended in the Friday Institute's Auditory Processing micro-credential (NC State)
- **[Learning Ally](https://learningally.org)** — human-narrated audiobooks designed for students with reading disabilities, used with print for ear-and-eye reading
- **[Storyline Online](https://storylineonline.net)** — actors reading picture books aloud, used in early-grade classrooms to model fluent reading
