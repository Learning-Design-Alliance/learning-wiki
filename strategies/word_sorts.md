# Word Sorts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A word sort presents learners with a set of words (typically on cards) to be grouped under category headers according to a shared feature — vowel pattern, initial sound, inflectional ending, or derivational morpheme. Learners compare and contrast words, articulate why each belongs where it does, and check their sorts against known words. The activity is inductive: the pattern is discovered through classification rather than stated as a rule first.

## Design Implications

Word sorts turn spelling and vocabulary instruction into guided [active learning](../principles/active-learning.md): learners generate the generalization themselves through repeated comparison, which supports deeper encoding than copying or rule recitation [~M]. The sorting structure functions like a [graphic organizer](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) for word knowledge, reducing the working-memory demand of holding multiple words in mind at once [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Reading each word aloud during sorting builds automatic word recognition, freeing attention for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A carefully chosen word set that contrasts the target feature with confusable features (e.g., *short a* vs. *long a* words)
- Category headers or key words that anchor each group
- A follow-up step where learners verbalize the rule they discovered ("sort and check," then "sort and say")
- Repeated sorts across days with speed and blind sorts to build fluency

#### Constraints
- If learners sort correctly by surface features without articulating the underlying pattern, no generalization occurs [-M] — the "sort and say" discussion is essential, not optional
- Word sets that mix too many features at once overload novices and produce guessing rather than analysis [~M]
- For irregular or exception words, sorting by pattern breaks down; these words need whole-word memorization instead [-M]
- Learners who already know the pattern gain little from re-sorting; sorts should target the learner's developmental spelling stage [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Closed sort** — teacher provides the categories (appropriate for introducing a new pattern)
- **Open sort** — learners devise their own categories (appropriate for consolidating and extending known patterns)
- **Blind/speed sort** — words are read aloud without showing the card, or sorted against a timer, shifting emphasis to phonological processing and automaticity
- **Concept sorts** — categories are semantic (e.g., animals vs. tools) rather than orthographic, useful for vocabulary development
- **Writing sorts** — learners record sorted words in a notebook, adding a production component

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers working on letter-sound correspondences and short/long vowel patterns [+M]
- Upper-elementary and adolescent learners studying derivational morphology (Latin roots, suffix changes) to support multisyllabic word reading and vocabulary [+M]
- Struggling spellers, when the sort targets their specific error patterns rather than grade-level lists [~M]
- Less useful for learners who have already consolidated the target pattern — re-sorting known material adds little [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Phonological and orthographic pattern knowledge (spelling-sound generalizations)
- Morphological awareness: recognizing roots, prefixes, and suffixes across word families
- Vocabulary depth: connecting word meanings through shared structure
- Fluency: rapid recognition of high-frequency patterns

### Instructions
1. **Model a closed sort** with a small set of words, thinking aloud about the features you are comparing ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
2. **Distribute the word set** and have learners sort individually or in pairs, reading each word aloud as they place it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. **Check and discuss** — compare sorts with a key or partner and have learners state the generalization in their own words ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. **Re-sort across days** — add speed sorts and blind sorts to build automaticity, then a writing sort to transfer the pattern to production ([Spaced Practice](../principles/spaced-practice.md)).
5. **Hunt for the pattern** in authentic texts, extending the sort beyond the card set ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)).

## Examples
- **Words Their Way** (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — the developmental spelling program built entirely around closed, open, blind, and speed sorts organized by five orthographic stages; widely used in K–8 classrooms ([pearson.com](https://www.pearson.com))
- **Reading A–Z word sort activities** — printable sort sets paired with decodable texts for each phonics pattern ([readinga-z.com](https://www.readinga-z.com))
- **Morphology sorts in adolescent intervention** — sorting derived word families (*sign, signal, signature, designate*) to teach the spelling-meaning connection in middle school vocabulary instruction
