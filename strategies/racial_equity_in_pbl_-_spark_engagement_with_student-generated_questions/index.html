Racial Equity in PBL - Spark Engagement with Student-Generated Questions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy positions student-generated questions — not teacher-assigned prompts — as the driving force of project-based learning (PBL). Instead of presenting content and tasks, the facilitator invites students to ask what they genuinely want to know about a topic, then organizes the project around those questions. The equity rationale is explicit: traditional transmission-style instruction can function as one more site of disempowerment for Black and Latinx students, whereas authoring the questions that drive inquiry positions them as intellectual agents Ladson-Billings' culturally relevant pedagogy [+M].

Design Implications¶

Student question generation increases engagement because it builds on authentic curiosity and gives learners ownership of the inquiry path; research on classroom questioning shows that student-authored questions promote deeper cognitive engagement and richer discussion than teacher-only questioning Chin & Osborne's review of student questioning [+M]. The strategy also connects to evidence that autonomy-supportive instruction raises intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S] and that belonging and agency matter for outcomes among marginalized students Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]. Engagement gains are strongest when student questions are treated as consequential — when the project visibly changes shape in response to them Engle & Conant on productive disciplinary engagement [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A facilitator genuinely open to student-driven inquiry, including questions that diverge from the planned unit
  • Classroom norms that make questioning safe and expected (see Check-In routines for building discussion culture)
  • A protocol for generating, sharing, and prioritizing questions (e.g., the Question Formulation Technique)
  • Willingness to map student questions back to curriculum standards during planning

Constraints¶

  • Student questions may not align with mandated standards or pacing guides; alignment work shifts to the planning phase and can increase teacher workload [-M]
  • Open-ended question generation can produce diffuse, unfocused inquiry if the facilitator does not help students converge on workable questions [-M]
  • Tokenizing risk: if student questions are collected but the project proceeds as planned, students perceive the invitation as inauthentic, which can depress rather than raise trust and engagement [-W]
  • Novice learners may generate questions that are too broad or factual to sustain a project without Scaffolding of question quality [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full question-driven projects: the entire PBL arc stems from a prioritized set of student questions
  • Hybrid entry points: teacher supplies the anchor topic or Case Studies; students generate the sub-questions that structure investigation
  • Question walls / parking lots: ongoing collection of questions that feed mini-inquiries within a larger unit
  • Structured protocols: the Question Formulation Technique (Rothstein & Santana) adds scaffolds — producing, improving, prioritizing, and planning use of questions

Target Learners¶

  • All grade levels; question generation scales from early elementary (oral questioning) to adult education
  • Black and Latinx students who may experience teacher-directed instruction as disempowering; authoring questions affirms intellectual agency and supports culturally responsive practice Ladson-Billings, culturally relevant pedagogy [+M]
  • Disengaged or historically marginalized learners for whom autonomy and relevance are key motivational levers Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Students new to inquiry may need explicit modeling of question quality before generation is productive [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Engagement and investment: increasing motivation to sustain a multi-week project
  • Inquiry skills: formulating, refining, and prioritizing researchable questions
  • Empowerment and agency: positioning students as co-designers of learning
  • Content mastery: standards-aligned understanding reached through student-owned investigation

Instructions¶

  1. Open with an anchor experience — a text, Case Study, community issue, or provocative artifact related to the unit theme, to activate curiosity Activation improves learning. [+M]
  2. Generate questions — students produce as many questions as possible without evaluation, using a protocol such as the Question Formulation Technique (produce → improve → prioritize)
  3. Prioritize and connect — the class sorts questions into open/closed and researchable/not, then selects the questions that will drive the project; the facilitator maps them to standards
  4. Make questions consequential — post the driving questions visibly and revisit them at each project milestone so students see their questions steering the work Engle & Conant, productive disciplinary engagement [+M]
  5. Structure inquiry — organize investigation, Class Discussion, and Choice Boards around the prioritized questions, offering Belonging-affirming opportunities for students to pursue questions connected to their own communities
  6. Close the loop — in final reflections and presentations, students answer their original questions and evaluate which remain open, treating unanswered questions as legitimate intellectual outcomes

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating prior knowledge — anchor experiences work best when they surface what students already know and wonder about
  • Community-based learning — student questions about their own communities ground projects in authentic local contexts
  • Choice boards — a structure for letting students pursue their prioritized questions through differentiated pathways

Examples¶

  • The Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute) — a published protocol used across K–12 and higher education in which students produce, refine, and prioritize questions that then drive lessons and projects; documented in Rothstein & Santana's Make Just One Change (2011).
  • A middle school science class identifies environmental problems in its own neighborhood (air quality near a highway, access to clean water) and formulates the research questions that structure a data-collection project, connecting standards-aligned content to lived experience.
  • A history class studying a standard unit on civil rights generates questions about local housing and school segregation, then investigates archival records from their own city, presenting findings to a community audience.
