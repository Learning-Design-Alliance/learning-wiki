# Focus on Enjoyable Moments

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy entails consciously choosing to fill one's mind with positive images and thoughts, making it a habit to bring the mind back every time it goes astray. It encourages gratitude for what one has presently, aligning self-talk with joy and fostering a state of gratitude that benefits the psyche. By emphasizing the good times and recognizing life's ups and downs, individuals can maintain a more positive attitude and better manage challenges. In learning contexts, it functions as an affect-regulation practice: learners deliberately attend to moments of enjoyment, competence, and gratitude to sustain the positive emotions that broaden attention and build durable resources for engagement [Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.56.3.218) [+M].

## Design Implications

Positive affect broadens attention and supports flexible, creative thinking, while sustained negative affect narrows focus and depletes the resources needed for persistence [Broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.56.3.218) [+M]. Deliberate attention to enjoyable moments is a low-cost way to raise task value and support intrinsic motivation, since learners who experience genuine enjoyment in an activity are more likely to engage with it voluntarily [Experiencing task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. The strategy works best when the enjoyable moments are *connected to the learning activity itself* — savoring a solved problem, a productive discussion — rather than used as escapism from it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Self-awareness and a willingness to shift focus from negative to positive thoughts; [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) or brief [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) routines help learners notice enjoyable moments as they occur
- A consistent practice structure — gratitude journaling, savoring exercises, or end-of-session reflection — rather than a one-off activity
- For persistent negativity, external support such as counseling or coaching; the strategy is a supplement to, not a substitute for, mental-health care

#### Constraints
- For learners with clinical depression or anxiety, forced positive thinking can backfire, producing rumination or guilt about failing to "feel better" [-M] — such learners need professional support before benefitting from gratitude or savoring practices
- Used as avoidance, focusing on pleasant distractions reduces engagement with challenging material and undermines the productive struggle that learning requires [~M]
- Effects of gratitude and positive-activity interventions are modest and decay without repetition; one-time exercises show little lasting benefit [~S]
- Instructing learners to *be* positive can feel controlling and reduce autonomy, undermining the intrinsic motivation the strategy is meant to support [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M] — offer the practice as a choice, not a mandate

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gratitude journaling**: writing three good things and why they happened, daily or weekly (weekly shows slower decay)
- **Savoring**: deliberately attending to and sharing a positive moment during or after a learning task
- **Positive reframing**: pairing reflection on difficulties with explicit recognition of what went well, useful after [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) or feedback events
- **Group variants**: [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) rounds in which learners share a recent success, building collective positive climate

### Target Learners
- Learners prone to negativity, stress, or catastrophizing about academic challenges [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M] — noticing moments of competence rebuilds efficacy beliefs
- Students in high-stakes or prolonged programs (exam preparation, dissertations) where sustained motivation is at risk
- Less appropriate, without adaptation, for learners experiencing acute distress or clinical depression [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and self-regulatory goals: managing frustration, sustaining persistence through difficulty
- Metacognitive goals: noticing and labeling emotional states during learning
- Community-building goals: normalizing the sharing of positive experiences within a cohort

### Instructions
1. **Introduce the practice with choice** — explain the rationale (positive affect supports persistence) and let learners select a format (journal, savoring pause, sharing round), preserving autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
2. **Anchor it to the learning routine** — attach a brief reflection to an existing event, e.g., the end of a [Practice](../elements/practice.md) session or a [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) opening
3. **Make it specific** — prompt learners to identify one concrete enjoyable or successful moment and *why* it happened, connecting positive affect to actual learning behavior rather than generic positivity
4. **Pair with difficulty, not instead of it** — follow challenging tasks with a "what went well / what was hard" reflection so positive focus coexists with honest appraisal
5. **Repeat and fade** — maintain the routine long enough to become habitual, then reduce frequency; sustained benefit requires repetition [Positive activity interventions show modest, decay-prone effects.](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.9.2.111) [~S]

## Examples
- **Three Good Things (Penn Resilience Program / positive psychology curricula)** — students write three things that went well each day and their causes; a validated exercise from Seligman et al.'s positive psychology interventions [https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu)
- **Greater Good Science Center gratitude practices** — classroom-tested gratitude journaling and savoring activities [https://greatergood.berkeley.edu](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu)
- **End-of-studio reflection in project-based courses** — design and coding studios close with a round in which each learner names one moment of the session they enjoyed or felt proud of, before reviewing what to improve
