# Problem Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Problem Based Learning (PBL) organizes instruction around an authentic, ill-structured problem that students encounter *before* receiving formal instruction on the underlying content. Working in small groups with a facilitator rather than a lecturer, students analyze the problem, identify what they need to know (a "learning needs" step), self-direct their inquiry, and return to apply new knowledge to a proposed solution. The problem functions as both the trigger for learning and the organizing structure for the knowledge built around it.

## Design Implications

PBL aims to build flexible, usable knowledge by anchoring it in the context of application, consistent with [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) and [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md). Evidence for its effects is conditional: meta-analyses find PBL improves application and transfer-oriented outcomes and retention of knowledge over time, though it can reduce initial knowledge acquisition compared with conventional instruction [~M]. The facilitator's role is critical — effective PBL requires structured scaffolding of the inquiry process, not unguided discovery [Kirschner, Sweller & Clark's critique applies to minimally guided variants; scaffolded PBL with expert facilitation avoids the working-memory failures of pure discovery.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Context
#### Requirements
- An authentic, ill-structured problem with no single correct answer, matched to learners' prior knowledge
- Small groups (typically 5–8) with a trained facilitator who models questioning rather than supplying answers ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- Explicit scaffolds for the inquiry cycle: problem analysis, hypothesis generation, learning-needs identification, self-study, synthesis ([Scaffolding](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md))
- Time and curricular space for self-directed study between problem sessions
- Assessment aligned with the goals of reasoning, self-direction, and collaboration — not only content coverage

#### Constraints
- Novices in highly complex domains can be overwhelmed by ill-structured problems without worked structure; unguided variants produce inferior knowledge acquisition [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction because search imposes extraneous load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [-S]
- PBL groups without trained facilitators drift toward content-free discussion; facilitator expertise in questioning and steering is a major moderator of outcomes [~M]
- Requires substantial curricular resources: problem development, facilitator time per small group, and library/information access for self-study
- Assessment misalignment (e.g., high-stakes content-coverage exams) undermines the self-directed learning the method is meant to build

#### Implementation Variability
- **Hybrid/anchored models** pair PBL with concurrent lectures or [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md), recovering content-acquisition efficiency while keeping contextual learning [~S]
- **Problem-first vs. lecture-first sequencing**: "productive failure" variants let learners generate and test their own solutions before instruction, improving conceptual understanding even when initial solutions fail [Productive failure improves conceptual learning relative to direct instruction first.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]
- **Single-problem vs. curriculum-wide**: medical schools (e.g., McMaster, Maastricht) run fully PBL curricula; most other settings use PBL for units within conventional courses
- **Individual vs. collaborative formats**: the small-group tutorial is the canonical form; individualized versions lose the peer-explanation benefits of [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who have enough schema to direct their own inquiry without being overloaded [Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant — and can even hinder — as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Professional and pre-professional students (medicine, engineering, business, law) who must learn to reason with ill-structured, real-world problems
- Less suitable as a first exposure for complete novices in knowledge-dense domains, who benefit from prior structure or concurrent explicit teaching

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied reasoning and diagnosis: using knowledge flexibly in authentic contexts
- Self-directed learning skills: identifying gaps, locating and evaluating resources
- Collaborative problem-solving and communication
- Long-term retention of knowledge, where PBL shows advantages over conventional instruction [~M]

### Instructions
1. **Present the problem** — introduce an authentic, ill-structured scenario before any instruction on the content; use [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) or a realistic artifact as the trigger.
2. **Facilitate problem analysis** — the group identifies facts, hypotheses, and learning needs; the facilitator asks questions rather than answering them ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. **Self-directed inquiry** — students research their identified learning needs individually or in subgroups, using [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) or open resources.
4. **Return and synthesize** — the group reconvenes to apply new knowledge to the problem, test hypotheses, and construct a solution ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)).
5. **Reflect** — close with structured reflection on both the solution and the group's learning process, connecting to [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md).

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — uses cases as illustrations *after* content teaching, whereas PBL puts the problem first; the two are often hybridized
- [Project Based Learning](project-based-learning.md) — similar authentic framing but culminates in a concrete artifact rather than a diagnostic solution
- [Jigsaw Method](../patterns/jigsaw-method.md) — a complementary structure for distributing the self-directed inquiry across group members

## Examples
- **McMaster University Medical School** — the original PBL curriculum (1969), in which small tutorial groups work through patient problems; adopted widely, notably at Maastricht University ([https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl))
- **Republic Polytechnic, Singapore** — runs its entire curriculum on a daily PBL cycle: problem, facilitated analysis, self-study, synthesis, reflection
- **Aalborg University, Denmark** — problem-oriented project work in engineering, where semester-long group projects address real technical problems ([https://www.en.aau.dk](https://www.en.aau.dk))
