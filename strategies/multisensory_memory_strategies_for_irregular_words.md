# Multisensory Memory Strategies for Irregular Words

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Multisensory memory strategies for irregular words involve using multiple senses (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, and tactile) to enhance memory and retention of irregularly spelled words. Typical techniques include grouping words with memorable similarities, calling attention to the "odd" parts of words, repeated practice through copying and saying letters while writing, discussing the irregularities explicitly, and using mnemonics. The approach exists because irregular words do not follow typical phoneme–grapheme correspondences and therefore cannot be fully decoded with standard phonics rules; they must be secured in memory as whole-unit orthographic representations [Ehri's orthographic mapping account.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S].

## Design Implications

Multisensory encoding supports retention by giving learners redundant retrieval routes and by directing attention to the specific letters that violate expected grapheme–phoneme mappings [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. The critical mechanism, however, is not the sensory channel itself but the formation of complete letter–sound connections through attention and repeated, spaced practice [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]; tracing letters in sand without attending to letter identities adds activity, not learning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated list of high-frequency irregular words, sequenced from most to least useful
- Multisensory materials (letter tiles, sand or textured trays, finger-painting media, colored markers for highlighting irregular parts)
- Direct, explicit instruction from a teacher or tutor who names the irregularity ("in *said*, the *ai* says /e/")
- Consistent, distributed practice with cumulative review of previously taught words

#### Constraints
- Time-intensive: each word requires individualized attention, which limits coverage when many words must be learned [-W]
- Tracing and tactile activity can become off-task motor activity that does not engage attention to letter order, producing no advantage over plain writing [~W]
- Teaching words as whole visual units without analyzing their letter–sound structure undermines the mapping process and yields weaker generalization than systematic phonics [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word methods for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [-S]
- Mnemonics add memory load of their own; if the mnemonic is more complex than the word, it can interfere rather than support [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Simultaneous oral spelling** (Spalding/Fernald-style): teacher says the word, student repeats it, names each letter while writing it from memory, then reads the word back
- **Arm-tapping and air-writing**: writing the word in large motor movements while spelling aloud, useful for learners who struggle with pencil control
- **Highlighting the irregular part**: printing the word with the unexpected grapheme in red or bold, focusing attention on only the non-decodable portion rather than the whole word
- **Word-family grouping**: clustering irregular words that share the same irregularity (*could, would, should*) to reduce memory load through [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)
- **Mnemonic embedding**: memorable sentences or images anchored to the word's spelling ("there is *a rat* in *separate*")

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers in grades 1–3 who have not yet secured high-frequency irregular words [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Older struggling readers and students with dyslexia, for whom structured, explicit, multisensory instruction is a common component of remedial programs [+M]
- Less necessary for learners who have already absorbed the word through reading volume; redundant for words already mapped [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Automatic recognition of high-frequency irregular words, freeing working memory for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Spelling accuracy for non-phonetic words
- Metalinguistic awareness: understanding that some spellings are historically irregular and must be memorized by attending to letter structure

### Instructions
1. Select the target word and explicitly identify which part is irregular and why ("we would expect *ee*, but this word uses *ea*").
2. Say the word, have the student repeat it, and pronounce it syllable by syllable as it is spelled.
3. Have the student trace or write the word while naming each letter aloud, engaging visual, auditory, and kinesthetic channels simultaneously.
4. Highlight or mark the irregular grapheme so attention lands on the non-decodable part.
5. Erase or cover the word and have the student write it from memory, then compare against the model and self-correct.
6. Distribute review of the word across days and cumulatively mix it with previously learned words [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
7. Follow word-level work with connected-text reading containing the target words ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) in context).

## Tools
- Letter tiles and magnetic letters (e.g., Wilson Reading System letter boards)
- Sand/textured trays for tracing (Fernald VAKT technique)
- [Spelling You See](https://spellingyousee.com) and [Barton Reading & Spelling System](https://bartonreading.com) — structured programs incorporating multisensory irregular-word work

## Examples
- A second-grade teacher uses letter tiles to build *friend*, highlighting the irregular *ie*, then has students write the word from memory while naming each letter.
- A fifth-grade teacher teaches the mnemonic "there is *a rat* in *separate*" after students trace the word and mark the problematic middle syllable.
- Orton–Gillingham-based tutoring sessions (e.g., [Barton System](https://bartonreading.com)) use simultaneous oral spelling with cumulative review of irregular words such as *said*, *was*, and *one*.
