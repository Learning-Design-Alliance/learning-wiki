# Incorporate Crafts to Remember Positive Self-Talk

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners create a physical artifact — an affirmation card, "self-talk flower," bookmark, poster, or decorated object — inscribed with positive self-statements they have authored themselves. The craft serves two functions: the act of making it deepens encoding of the self-talk, and the finished object acts as a portable cue that re-triggers the self-statements at moments of difficulty.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines three mechanisms: self-generated content benefits from the generation effect [Self-generated information is remembered better than passively read information.](../claims/generation-effect-improves-retention.md) [+S]; self-affirmation of personally valued statements buffers stress and supports adaptive responses to threat [Self-affirmation interventions improve outcomes by protecting a sense of integrity under threat.](../claims/self-affirmation-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]; and physical cues in the environment support retrieval and habit formation by making the desired response salient at the point of need [+W]. The craft must be paired with explicit instruction in *what* positive self-talk is and *when* to use it — an artifact alone, without modeling and practice of the self-statements, is decoration rather than intervention [-W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Learners first author their own affirmations; adult-supplied generic slogans ("You can do it!") are less effective than self-referential, credible statements [+M]
- Brief modeling of positive self-talk in use, e.g., the instructor narrating their own coping statements during a challenging task ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- Simple, low-friction craft materials and a short time box (10–20 minutes) so the activity does not displace the learning it supports
- A plan for *use*: where the artifact will live (desk, notebook, pencil case) and when the learner will consult it

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends entirely on learner engagement and the personal meaning attached to the artifact; a craft completed to satisfy the teacher has no measurable benefit [-W]
- Learners who find craft activities childish or off-putting (common with older adolescents) may experience the activity as patronizing, undermining the intervention [-M]
- Positive self-statements that conflict with a learner's self-view can backfire: individuals with low self-esteem who repeat implausible affirmations may feel worse, not better [Wood, Perunovic, & Lee, 2009] [-S]
- Time-intensive relative to the direct teaching of self-talk; the craft component adds cost without adding benefit if learners already use self-talk fluently [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Affirmation cards or flip books** — small, portable, suited to test-anxiety contexts
- **Classroom anchor charts** — collective crafts that normalize self-talk use across the group
- **Digital equivalents** — lock-screen images or desktop wallpapers for older learners who reject physical crafts
- **Integration with goal-setting** — pairing each affirmation with a specific strategy ("When I get stuck, I say… and then I…") converts the artifact into a coping-plan cue [+M]

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grade learners, who respond well to tangible, personalized objects [+W]
- Learners who benefit from visual and hands-on modalities, including many learners with attention or anxiety-related [accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)
- Learners facing a known stressor (tests, performances, transitions) where a portable coping cue is most likely to be consulted [+M]
- Less suitable for older learners without adaptation, and for learners whose low self-view makes the chosen affirmations implausible [-S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: building a repertoire of coping statements for managing frustration and anxiety
- Metacognition: recognizing internal dialogue and deliberately reshaping it
- Social-emotional learning: self-efficacy and identity ("I am someone who can persist")

### Instructions
1. **Model self-talk in use.** Narrate your own coping statements aloud while working through a genuinely challenging task, so learners see the strategy applied, not just described ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
2. **Elicit, don't assign, affirmations.** Prompt learners to recall a past success and phrase a credible, specific self-statement; check each statement is plausible to the learner ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
3. **Create the craft.** Learners produce the artifact with their statements, choosing format and decoration — personalization increases ownership and subsequent use [+M].
4. **Rehearse the cue.** Have learners practice consulting the artifact and saying the statement during a low-stakes challenging task ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
5. **Revisit.** Build brief check-ins where learners report whether and how they used the artifact; revise statements that feel stale or implausible.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Self-Talk Flower craft** — a paper flower with affirmations written on each petal ("I can try a different strategy"), small enough to keep in a pencil case and consult before independent work.
- **Test-prep affirmation cards** — before a state assessment unit, students make wallet-sized cards pairing a coping statement with a concrete test strategy, kept in the desk and used during practice tests.
- **"I can" anchor charts** — a class co-creates a large poster of self-talk phrases generated during a challenging math unit, referenced aloud by the teacher when students hit difficulty.
