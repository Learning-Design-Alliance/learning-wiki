# Collaborative Writing with Online Platforms

## Description
Collaborative writing with online platforms involves students jointly composing texts through digital tools such as Google Docs, Microsoft 365, or wikis. The platforms' affordances — simultaneous co-authoring, suggestion mode, inline commenting, and version history — make the writing process visible and revisable in ways paper cannot. Students draft segments, comment on each other's contributions, negotiate revisions, and often publish the result to an authentic audience such as a class blog.

## Design Implications

Collaborative writing works because composing together forces learners to articulate and defend text decisions, and peer feedback supplies revision information at the task and process levels where feedback is most effective [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Structured collaboration with individual accountability is what drives gains; simply placing students in a shared document without roles or interdependence produces uneven participation [Cooperative learning improves outcomes when group goals are paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A co-authoring platform with commenting, suggestion mode, and version history (e.g., Google Docs, Microsoft Word Online, Wikispaces-style wikis)
- Explicit roles or section ownership so each learner contributes and is accountable for a portion of the product
- Training in giving constructive peer feedback — sentence stems, rubrics, or modeled examples ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- A revision cycle: feedback must be followed by opportunities to act on it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Free-riding: without individual accountability, stronger writers dominate and weaker writers disengage [Cooperative learning improves outcomes when group goals are paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Untrained peer feedback tends to be vague praise ("good job") that does not support revision [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]
- Requires reliable internet access and digital fluency; tool navigation can consume working memory that should go to composing, especially for novices [Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Simultaneous editing without coordination can produce incoherent voice and structure; low-level "coordination costs" rise with group size

#### Implementation Variability
- **Parallel then merged:** students draft segments answering similar prompts, then merge and harmonize into one text
- **Reciprocal review:** pairs write separately, then exchange documents for structured peer review before revision
- **Asynchronous wiki writing:** contributions accumulate over days, suiting larger products and distributed schedules
- **Teacher-as-commenter:** instructor uses suggestion mode for formative feedback on live drafts ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))

### Target Learners
- Middle school through adult learners; particularly well studied with adolescent and second-language writers [Collaborative writing improves text quality compared with writing alone.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Developing writers who benefit from seeing peers' composing decisions modeled in the shared document
- Less beneficial for students lacking basic keyboarding and platform fluency — teach tool skills separately first

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: organization, argumentation, and revision skills [Collaborative writing improves text quality compared with writing alone.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Peer feedback and evaluation skills
- Digital literacy and online communication norms
- Negotiation and academic discourse

### Instructions
1. Form groups of 2–4 with defined roles (lead author per section, reviewer, editor) to establish accountability.
2. Activate relevant knowledge with a short planning task or exemplar text ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Students co-draft in a shared document, using suggestion mode rather than silent overwriting so changes are visible and discussable ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
4. Peers leave comments tied to a rubric, targeting organization and argument rather than surface errors ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
5. Authors revise in response to comments; the teacher reviews version history to assess individual contribution ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
6. Publish the final text to a class blog or shared space for an authentic audience ([Authentic Audiences and Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).

## Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — co-authoring enacts this principle by making peer negotiation of ideas intrinsic to the task rather than an add-on discussion
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — comments and suggestion mode turn the draft itself into a formative feedback channel; version history documents growth
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — distributing planning, drafting, and editing across group members reduces the load on any one writer
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — students produce and critique text rather than passively receiving writing instruction

## Tools
- [Google Docs](https://docs.google.com) — real-time co-authoring, suggestion mode, version history, and commenting
- [Microsoft Word Online](https://www.office.com) — similar co-authoring within the Microsoft 365 ecosystem
- [Wikis (e.g., Wikimedia, Fandom)](https://www.mediawiki.org) — asynchronous collaborative authoring with full revision tracking

## Examples
- A high school English class co-writes a research paper in Google Docs: each student owns a section, peers comment against a rubric, the teacher reviews version history for individual contribution, and the final paper is posted to a class blog.
- Second-language courses use shared documents for collaborative paragraph construction, with pairs negotiating sentence-level choices — a setup shown to produce more attention to language form than solo writing (Storch, 2005).
- University writing programs use scaffolded web-based reciprocal peer review (SWoRD, now Peerceptiv, https://peerceptiv.com) to manage large-scale peer feedback with accountability built in (Cho & Schunn, 2007).
