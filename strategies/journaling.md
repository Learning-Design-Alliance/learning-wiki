# Journaling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Journaling asks learners to write regularly — often briefly and informally — about what they are learning, how they are learning it, or how they are progressing toward goals. Entries may be reflective (what did I understand?), metacognitive (what strategies worked?), or expressive (how do I feel about this material?). The writing is typically low-stakes, personal, and iterative rather than a polished product.

## Design Implications

Journaling works by forcing generative processing: converting experience into language requires elaboration and organization that passive review does not [~M]. Meta-analytic evidence on writing-to-learn shows small but reliable positive effects on content learning, strongest when prompts require reasoning about relationships rather than summary or emotional expression alone [~M]. The prompt design is the critical variable — open "write about today" prompts produce far less learning than prompts that demand explanation, connection, or self-explanation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A focused prompt tied to learning goals (explain, connect, predict, evaluate — not merely recount)
- Regular cadence; one-off journaling has little measurable effect
- Some form of response or follow-through — instructor feedback, peer sharing, or self-review against criteria — or entries become performative busywork
- Low-stakes grading or no grading; evaluative pressure shifts writing toward what the instructor wants to hear

#### Constraints
- Unprompted or purely expressive journaling shows weak effects on content learning [~M] — emotional venting without cognitive engagement does not consolidate understanding
- Journaling is time-expensive relative to its effect size; for well-structured procedural content, [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) prompts may achieve the same elaboration more efficiently
- Without privacy assurances, learners write for the audience rather than for thinking, stripping out the honest confusion that makes reflection useful
- Weak writers may spend working memory on transcription rather than reflection; effects are smaller for learners with low writing fluency

#### Implementation Variability
- **Learning journals** — ongoing entries across a course, reviewed periodically for growth over time
- **Double-entry journals** — left column for source material or observations, right column for the learner's response; enforces the connect step
- **Error journals / exam wrappers** — post-assessment entries analyzing what went wrong; pairs well with [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)
- **Goal-setting journals** — written goals and progress monitoring; structured journaling about goal progress improved academic performance in randomized studies [+M]
- **Digital micro-journaling** — short entries in an LMS discussion or tool like Perusall, trading depth for frequency

### Target Learners
- Learners in complex, cumulative domains where integrating new material with prior knowledge matters (sciences, humanities, clinical fields)
- Learners developing self-regulation; journaling externalizes the monitoring and planning phases of self-regulated learning [+M]
- Less effective for novices who lack enough knowledge to generate meaningful connections — early entries may need sentence stems or prompt scaffolds

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition and self-regulated learning: monitoring comprehension and strategy use
- Conceptual integration: connecting new material to prior knowledge and across topics
- Attitude and identity formation: professional reflection (teaching, nursing, clinical practice)
- Retention of course content, when prompts require elaborative explanation [~M]

### Instructions
1. Define the journal's purpose (conceptual, metacognitive, or goal-oriented) and choose a matching prompt type.
2. Write a specific prompt requiring explanation or connection, not summary — e.g., "Explain today's concept to someone absent, including one thing that confused you."
3. Set a regular cadence (weekly or per-session) and a time limit (5–10 minutes) to keep entries generative rather than exhaustive.
4. Model an entry once using a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) so learners see what genuine reflection sounds like.
5. Respond periodically — brief written comments, a shared excerpt, or a mid-term self-review against earlier entries — so writing feeds back into learning.
6. Close the loop with an end-of-term synthesis entry in which learners reread and evaluate their own trajectory.

## Examples
- **Double-entry journals in introductory biology** — students quote a passage from assigned reading on the left and explain its significance or question it on the right, then bring entries to [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
- **Clinical reflection journals in nursing education** — students journal after clinical placements using structured prompts (what happened, why, what would I change), a standard practice in nursing curricula.
- **Morisano-style goal journaling** — students in a randomized study wrote structured entries about goals, obstacles, and progress over several sessions and improved GPA relative to controls.
- **Perusall / LMS reading journals** — short annotated responses to readings, visible to a small group, combining journaling with [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md).
