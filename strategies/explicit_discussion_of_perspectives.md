# Explicit Discussion of Perspectives

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
The instructor deliberately surfaces, names, and compares multiple perspectives on a contested or multi-faceted topic — including perspectives students do not hold — and structures discussion so learners must articulate, evaluate, and respond to viewpoints other than their own. The strategy goes beyond open discussion: the instructor makes the existence of alternative framings explicit and models how to characterize a position fairly before critiquing it.

## Design Implications

Explicitly comparing perspectives supports abstraction and transfer, because learners must identify what varies across viewpoints and what remains constant [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The strategy also builds argumentation skill: learners who are prompted to generate and address counterarguments produce stronger, more defensible reasoning [Strategies for teaching critical thinking, including perspective-taking and argument analysis, produce meaningful gains.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654308326084) [+M]. Structured controversy — requiring learners to argue for an assigned position and then switch — improves achievement and perspective-taking relative to debate-without-switching [Cooperative learning with structured controversy improves achievement and relationships.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X08318457) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A topic with genuine, defensible disagreement or multiple legitimate framings
- Instructor preparation: knowing the major positions well enough to represent them accurately, including the strongest version of each ([Steelmanning](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Discussion structures that require every learner to engage, not just volunteers ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Norms for respectful disagreement established before contested content is introduced
- Prepared discussion prompts, cases, or texts that genuinely contain multiple defensible viewpoints
- Ground rules and a skilled facilitator who can manage disagreement without suppressing it
- Sufficient time for learners to articulate, compare, and reflect on perspectives — not just state them
- A mechanism for every learner to contribute (turns, written pre-work, small groups) so discussion is not dominated by a few voices

#### Constraints
- If perspectives are presented as a list without comparison or evaluation, learners encode them as isolated facts rather than as positions in tension [~M]
- Learners with low prior knowledge may lack the schema to evaluate competing claims, producing confusion rather than discrimination [~M] — pairing with [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) mitigates this
- Unstructured discussion of polarized topics can entrench existing views rather than broaden them; assigned positions and structured turn-taking reduce this risk [~S]
- Overloading learners with too many perspectives at once exceeds working memory limits; two to three well-chosen viewpoints outperform an exhaustive survey [~M]
- Unstructured open discussion tends to be dominated by high-status or confident speakers, reducing participation and learning for others [-M]
- Learners often resist or dismiss perspectives that conflict with strongly held identities or beliefs; simple exposure can entrench rather than change views [-W]
- Perspective-taking exercises can backfire when learners imagine others' perspectives without accurate information, reinforcing stereotypes rather than reducing them [-W]
- Sensitive topics require skilled facilitation; poorly handled conflict can harm classroom climate and psychological safety [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-led comparison**: instructor presents two or three positions and models the comparison aloud ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- **Assigned-position controversy**: learners argue an assigned side, then switch sides and re-argue (Johnson & Johnson's constructive controversy)
- **Case-embedded perspectives**: a [Case Study](../elements/case-studies.md) presents stakeholders with conflicting interests; learners analyze each stakeholder's reasoning
- **Written perspective-taking**: learners write the strongest counterargument to their own position before responding ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) or structured [Debate](../patterns/debate.md): learners argue a perspective they did not select, forcing engagement with unfamiliar reasoning
- [Role-Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md): learners embody a perspective, which can deepen affective understanding but requires debriefing to consolidate learning
- Written variants: perspective journals or multi-voice essays for learners who do not participate comfortably in oral discussion

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to evaluate claims but have not yet encountered the field's major disagreements [~M]
- Learners prone to premature closure, who benefit from prompts to consider alternatives before committing to an answer [Strategies for teaching critical thinking, including perspective-taking and argument analysis, produce meaningful gains.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654308326084) [+M]
- Less effective for complete novices, who cannot yet distinguish strong from weak claims across perspectives [~M]
- All levels, K–12 through professional education, though the depth of perspective analysis should scale with development
- Learners in heterogeneous classrooms where peers hold genuinely different backgrounds and views — the diversity itself is the resource
- Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge of the topic, who lack the content needed to construct any perspective meaningfully [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking: evaluating evidence and reasoning across competing claims
- Conceptual understanding: understanding why a discipline frames a problem in multiple ways
- Perspective-taking and empathy: recognizing the internal logic of positions one rejects ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Argumentation: constructing and defending a position while addressing counterarguments
- Social awareness and empathy: understanding how others experience events differently
- Critical thinking and argumentation: evaluating the evidence and values underlying competing viewpoints
- Conceptual understanding: recognizing that interpretation depends on position and evidence, not just facts
- Communication and civic discourse: practicing respectful disagreement and justification

### Instructions
1. Select a topic with genuine disagreement and identify two to three defensible perspectives.
2. Activate relevant prior knowledge before introducing the tension ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Present each perspective in its strongest form, clearly labeled; use a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) to model how an expert compares them.
4. Assign learners to articulate a perspective — ideally one they do not hold — using structured [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) or a [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) format.
5. Require learners to state the strongest counterargument to their own position before responding ([Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]).
6. Debrief by having learners synthesize: what does each perspective explain well, and what does it miss?

## Examples
- **Constructive controversy in teacher education** (Johnson & Johnson): pre-service teachers are assigned positions on tracking vs. mixed-ability grouping, argue, switch sides, then synthesize a personal position — reliably improving achievement and attitude change over debate and individual study.
- **Stakeholder analysis in case teaching** ([Harvard Business School case method](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)): learners discuss a business case by articulating the distinct interests and reasoning of managers, employees, customers, and shareholders before evaluating decisions.
- **History classrooms using document-based perspective work**: learners compare primary sources from opposing sides of an event (e.g., Reading Like a Historian, Stanford History Education Group, https://sheg.stanford.edu), explicitly discussing how each author's position shaped the account.
- **Facing History and Ourselves** (https://www.facinghistory.org) — published curriculum using historical cases in which students analyze how victims, perpetrators, bystanders, and resisters experienced the same events, followed by structured reflection.
- **Structured Academic Controversy in cooperative learning** — Johnson & Johnson's format in which pairs argue one side of an issue, switch sides, and then drop roles to seek consensus, explicitly building perspective-taking into the procedure.
- **Literature discussion of a multi-voice text** — e.g., discussing a novel told from multiple narrators, with small groups each responsible for reconstructing one narrator's interpretation before comparing.

## Description
Explicit discussions of perspectives are structured conversations in which learners deliberately examine how different individuals or groups might experience, interpret, or evaluate the same event, text, or problem. The instructor makes perspective-taking an explicit goal — naming the perspectives, prompting learners to articulate them, and requiring learners to justify or critique each viewpoint rather than merely acknowledging it.

## Design Implications

Perspective-taking does not reliably emerge from exposure to diverse material alone; it improves when learners are prompted to actively construct another person's viewpoint and reasoning [Perspective-taking interventions improve interpersonal outcomes when they require active construction of another's viewpoint.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Structured discussion formats that require learners to defend positions they did not choose produce deeper engagement with alternative viewpoints than unstructured sharing [Cooperative goal structures with structured controversy improve learning and relationships compared with individualistic structures.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The strategy works best when perspectives are anchored in concrete cases rather than abstract categories, so learners reason about specific people and situations [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].

1. Select or construct a case, text, or event with multiple defensible perspectives ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)).

3. Assign or invite learners to adopt and articulate specific perspectives, in small groups or as a whole ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)).
4. Structure comparison: require learners to identify where perspectives agree, conflict, and why ([Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md)).
5. Rotate or swap perspectives so learners argue a view they did not initially hold.
6. Debrief: have learners step out of role, reflect on what was difficult or surprising, and connect to the broader concept ([Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md)).
