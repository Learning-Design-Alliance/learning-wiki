# Collect My Data

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Collect My Data is a learner-driven data-collection strategy in which students select a question or category of items to investigate (e.g., favorite fruit, types of pets, weather observations), record observations using tally charts, and then translate the tallies into graphs. The strategy moves learners through the full data cycle — posing a question, gathering data, representing it, and interpreting results — using their own choices as the content.

## Design Implications

Giving learners ownership of the data question and collection process turns an abstract representational skill into a personally meaningful investigation, consistent with evidence that [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) improves exam performance [active-learning-improves-exam-performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The tally chart serves as an intermediate representation that reduces the working-memory demands of simultaneous counting and recording [chunking-reduces-working-memory-load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]: each mark is a small, repeatable unit that can be made in the moment, deferring the summary step to graph construction.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine, answerable question the learner cares about ("How many of each color car pass the school?")
- A manageable data set — small enough to tally by hand and graph in one sitting
- A structured recording template (tally chart with pre-labeled or learner-labeled categories)
- A follow-up step requiring interpretation: comparing categories, finding the most/least, or making a claim from the graph

#### Constraints
- If the teacher supplies both the question and the categories, the activity collapses into a transcription exercise and loses its motivational and inquiry benefits [-M]
- Learners who tally without a consistent counting scheme (double marks, missed items) produce corrupted data; without a check on collection accuracy, the resulting graphs teach flawed procedures [-W]
- Graphing very large or continuous data sets by hand overloads the recording step and shifts attention from reasoning to mechanics [-M]
- If the lesson ends at graph construction with no interpretation or claim-making, learners treat graphs as pictures rather than arguments [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class shared data** — the class collects one data set together; suitable for the first iteration and for modeling the tally convention
- **Small-group investigations** — groups choose their own questions and compare graphs across groups, supporting discussion of how representation choices affect conclusions
- **Longitudinal collection** — repeated tallies over days or weeks (weather, plant growth) introduce change-over-time representations
- **Digital extension** — transferring tallies into a simple graphing tool connects hand-made and computer-generated representations

### Target Learners
- Early elementary and primary learners (roughly ages 5–9) encountering data representation for the first time; tally marks are developmentally accessible because they map one mark to one observation [+W]
- Learners who benefit from concrete, self-generated content before working with secondhand data sets [+W]
- Older learners can use the same cycle with more complex categories and sampling decisions, though tally charts give way to frequency tables [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Representational understanding: how tallies and graphs encode frequency
- Categorization: deciding what counts as "the same" — the conceptual core of classification [multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Data literacy practices: posing questions, collecting evidence, and making claims from data
- Self-regulated inquiry: managing an extended observation task [self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Pose the question.** Learners choose or refine a data question with a small set of answer categories; the teacher checks that categories are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
2. **Plan the collection.** Decide where, when, and how observations will be made; agree on the tally convention (four vertical strokes plus a diagonal for five).
3. **Collect and tally.** Record each observation as a tally mark in the chart at the moment it occurs, keeping the recording step separate from any summarizing.
4. **Graph the data.** Transfer tally counts into a bar graph or pictograph, one axis per category, discussing why the bars must be scaled consistently.
5. **Interpret and share.** Learners state one claim their graph supports ("Most people chose dogs"), compare across groups, and discuss what the data cannot tell them.

## Examples
- **First-grade classroom survey** — students tally classmates' favorite fruits on a class chart, then build a pictograph with fruit stickers and report the "winner" to the class.
- **Schoolyard ecology investigation** — third-graders tally bird species observed at a window feeder over a week, graph the counts, and compare their class results with data from a partner school.
- **[Tally Charts and Frequency Tables in Everyday Mathematics](https://www.mheonline.com/everydaymathematics)** — the published elementary curriculum sequences learner-collected tally data into bar graphs across grades 1–3.
