# Gradual Release of Responsibility in Multimodal Composition

## Description
This strategy applies the [Gradual Release of Responsibility](gradual-release-of-responsibility.md) model ("I do, we do, you do") to multimodal composition — the design of texts that combine image, sound, video, and layout (e.g., book trailers, blogs, infographics, websites). The teacher first directly models how to select and analyze multimodal resources, then facilitates paired or small-group analysis, and finally releases students to analyze exemplars and compose their own multimodal texts independently. The goal is to build both critical analysis of multimodal artifacts and recursive design skills (drafting, evaluating, revising across modes).

## Design Implications

Multimodal composition imposes high extraneous load on novices, who must simultaneously learn the content, the tools, and the design conventions of each mode [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]. Sequencing responsibility transfer manages this load: each phase narrows what learners must handle alone, and contingent support — more help when struggle appears, less when it does not — improves outcomes [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because the "I do" phase is a form of modeling, the teacher should narrate design decisions aloud rather than only showing finished artifacts.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of authentic multimodal exemplars (book trailers, infographics, blogs, websites) spanning quality levels
- Time for three distinct phases: modeled analysis, collaborative analysis, independent analysis and composition
- Teacher fluency with the design vocabulary of the modes being taught (layout, color, sound, pacing, audience)
- A composition task with a real audience and purpose so design choices have consequences

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops when the modeling phase shows only finished products without narrated reasoning — students imitate surface features rather than design decisions
- Releasing responsibility too early overloads novices juggling new tools and new conventions simultaneously [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Releasing too late produces dependence; students who never compose independently do not develop recursive design routines
- Heavy preparation burden: locating and vetting appropriate exemplars for each mode is time-intensive
- Access inequities (devices, software, bandwidth) can make the independent phase uneven across students

#### Implementation Variability
- Mode-specific sequencing: teach one mode (e.g., video) through the full release cycle before adding others, rather than cycling all modes at once
- Collaborative phase can use structured roles (director, editor, audience tester) to distribute the analysis load
- The independent phase can be individual or small-group; collaborative composing improves text quality in writing contexts [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Fading can be by task complexity (simpler → more complex artifacts) or by support type (full analysis guides → checklists → no support)

### Target Learners
- K–12 students encountering multimodal composition for the first time, who lack the design vocabulary to critique or plan multimodal texts on their own
- Adolescent learners transitioning from print-only writing instruction, who need explicit bridging from essay conventions to design conventions
- Less beneficial for students already fluent in a mode, for whom modeled analysis is redundant and slows production

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical multimodal analysis: evaluating how image, sound, and layout work together for an audience
- Recursive design skill: planning, drafting, testing, and revising across modes
- Disposition: strengthening interest and self-efficacy in multimodal design work

### Instructions
1. **Model (I do):** Select an exemplar and analyze it aloud, narrating design decisions ("I notice the trailer cuts to black right before the title — that makes the reveal land"). Use [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) moves and [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) of the artifact to make criteria visible.
2. **Guide (We do):** In pairs or small groups, students analyze a second exemplar using the same criteria, with the teacher circulating and using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) to consolidate observations.
3. **Collaborative analysis (You do together):** Groups choose or are assigned exemplars of varying quality and compare them, sharpening discrimination between strong and weak design choices.
4. **Independent application (You do alone):** Students analyze an exemplar of their choice, then plan and compose their own multimodal text, applying the criteria recursively through draft–feedback–revision cycles.
5. **Assess:** Evaluate both the analysis (can students justify design judgments?) and the composition (do students apply criteria in their own work?), using [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) rubrics co-developed during the modeling phase.

## Examples
- **Book trailer unit (upper elementary/middle school):** Teacher models analysis of a published book trailer, pairs analyze a second trailer against a criteria chart, groups compare strong and weak trailers, then students storyboard and produce trailers for self-selected books using free tools such as [Canva for Education](https://www.canva.com/education/) or [WeVideo](https://www.wevideo.com/).
- **Blog critique sequence (secondary ELA):** Modeled analysis of an exemplar student blog, guided analysis of a second, then independent analysis of a student-chosen blog before students design their own posts — the sequence described in Dalton's multimodal composition work with the [Reading Teacher](https://doi.org/10.1002/trtr.01117).
- **[Wixie](https://www.wixie.com/) / [Book Creator](https://bookcreator.com)** classroom projects where teachers scaffold from whole-class co-design of one page to independent student-authored multimedia books.
