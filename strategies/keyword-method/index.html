    Keyword Method¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    The keyword method is a mnemonic strategy for vocabulary learning, developed by Atkinson and Raugh (1975). In the first stage, learners identify a keyword — a familiar word that sounds similar to (or shares part of) the target word. In the second stage, they construct a mental image in which the keyword and the target word's meaning interact. For example, to learn Spanish pato (duck), an English speaker might use the keyword "pot" and picture a duck sitting in a pot. The strategy is typically taught explicitly, with the instructor modeling keyword selection and image construction before learners generate their own.

    Design Implications¶

    The keyword method works by converting abstract, arbitrary sound–meaning mappings into concrete, imageable associations, leveraging both verbal and imagistic memory codes Dual coding improves recall. [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the interactive image: images in which the two items physically interact produce far better recall than images of the items merely co-occurring. Because it is a generative strategy, learners benefit most when they construct their own keywords and images rather than only receiving them, though instructor-provided keywords are effective for novices who cannot yet generate good ones.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Target vocabulary with pronounceable forms that can be mapped to familiar sound-alike words
    • Explicit instruction in both stages (keyword selection, then interactive imagery), ideally with worked demonstrations
    • Learners with sufficient vocabulary in the base language to supply keywords
    • Retrieval practice opportunities that test the word–meaning link from both directions

    Constraints¶

    • Gains are strongest for recall of word meaning given the foreign form; effects on production (retrieving the foreign word from the meaning) and on long-term retention are weaker and decay without review [~M]
    • Less effective when target words have no good phonological match in the learner's language, forcing awkward or abstract keywords
    • Can produce errors when the keyword meaning bleeds into the target meaning (e.g., learners misremember pato as "pot"); images must clearly mark which item carries the meaning
    • Adds processing steps that can slow initial reading fluency; overreliance on mediation may delay direct form–meaning access [~M]
    • Less useful for abstract words, function words, or words whose meaning is hard to depict in an image

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Instructor-provided vs. learner-generated keywords: provided keywords support novices; generation adds generative processing [+M] but risks poor keyword choices without feedback
    • Spoken vs. written presentation: spoken keywords strengthen phonological links; written forms support orthographic learning
    • Extended keyword method: keywords also embedded in a sentence using the target word, adding syntactic and collocational context
    • Group variants: learners share and vote on the most memorable images, adding social elaboration

    Target Learners¶

    • Beginning language learners encountering large volumes of new vocabulary [+S]
    • School-age children and adolescents, who typically show strong gains with imagery-based mnemonics [~M]
    • Less beneficial for advanced learners, who can map form to meaning directly and may find mediation steps redundant or interfering [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Declarative vocabulary acquisition: pairing unfamiliar forms with meanings
    • Retention of arbitrary associations (terminology, symbols, names) in any domain
    • Not suited for conceptual understanding, procedural skill, or productive language use

    Instructions¶

    1. Present the target word with its meaning and pronunciation (Clear Structure).
    2. Model keyword selection: identify a familiar word that sounds like part of the target word, thinking aloud through the choice (Think-Aloud).
    3. Model interactive image construction: describe an image in which the keyword and the meaning interact vividly, with the meaning-carrying item clearly dominant (Analogies).
    4. Have learners generate their own keywords and images for new words, with feedback on keyword quality (Practice).
    5. Schedule retrieval practice testing word–meaning links from both directions, spaced over time (Distributed practice improves retention. [+S]).

    Related Strategies¶

    • Acronyms and Acrostics — verbal mnemonics serving the same arbitrary-association goal through letter-based rather than image-based encoding
    • Act It Out — enactive counterpart: dramatizing meaning instead of imaging it
    • Spaced Repetition — the scheduling mechanism that addresses the keyword method's retention decay

    Examples¶

    • Atkinson & Raugh's Russian vocabulary experiments (1975) — Stanford learners using the keyword method learned roughly 72% of 120 Russian words on the test versus 46% for control learners.
    • Duolingo-style illustrated vocabulary — pairing new words with memorable images enacts the imagery half of the method, though without the phonological keyword stage.
    • Classical language courses — teachers of Latin or Greek vocabulary commonly supply keywords and sample images for high-frequency words, then require students to generate their own for new lists.
