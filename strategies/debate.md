# Debate

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Debate assigns learners opposing positions on a genuinely contested question and requires them to construct, deliver, and defend arguments against live opposition. Structured formats (e.g., timed speeches, cross-examination, rebuttal rounds) impose turn-taking and accountability that distinguish debate from open [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md). Because positions are typically assigned rather than chosen, learners must sometimes argue views they do not hold, which forces genuine engagement with the strongest version of the opposing case.

## Design Implications

Debate is a high-intensity form of [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md): learners generate arguments, monitor an opponent's reasoning, and respond in real time, placing them in the interactive-construction mode associated with the largest learning gains in the ICAP framework [Chi & Wylie's ICAP framework predicts greater learning for interactive engagement.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Its learning value depends on preparation quality — debate amplifies whatever argument quality learners bring, so structured preparation (research time, [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md) supports, claim-evidence-reasoning frames) is essential. Assigned-position debate can improve perspective-taking and argument complexity, but only when learners are required to prepare *both* sides or rebut strong opposing arguments, not merely perform a script.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely contested question with defensible positions on both sides — not a question with a settled answer
- Sufficient preparation time with access to evidence sources, or the debate collapses into opinion exchange
- Explicit argument structure support (claim–evidence–warrant scaffolds, [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) rubrics)
- Clear rules: speaking order, time limits, and a defined rebuttal obligation
- A debrief that separates evaluation of argument quality from winning, ideally with [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the process level [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Debates on questions with a factual answer waste time and can entrench misconceptions [~M]
- Weak preparation produces fluent but evidence-free rhetoric; learners may reward confidence over accuracy, and high-confidence errors can persist [High-confidence errors improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M]
- Competitive framing can suppress participation among anxious learners and shift goals from understanding to winning [~W]
- Assigned positions without a perspective-taking debrief can leave learners anchored to their assigned side
- Large classes need modified formats (team debates, fishbowl, structured mini-debates) or most learners become spectators

#### Implementation Variability
- **Formal competitive debate** (policy/Lincoln-Douglas style): full research cycle, judged rounds
- **Structured academic controversy** (Johnson & Johnson): pairs argue one side, switch sides, then drop roles and synthesize — the synthesis step is what converts advocacy into conceptual learning
- **Fishbowl debate**: inner circle debates while outer circle observes with an analytic checklist, then swaps
- **Team debate in large courses**: groups prepare collectively with assigned roles (researcher, speaker, rebuttal lead)

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary learners who already have baseline content knowledge to draw on; novices without preparation time gain little [~M]
- Learners developing oral communication and argumentation skill; public performance builds confidence through mastery experience [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less suitable for learners with very limited language proficiency unless preparation and sentence frames are provided

### Target Learning Goals
- Argument construction: building claims supported by evidence and warrants
- Perspective-taking and multi-sided understanding of contested issues
- Oral communication and real-time rebuttal skill
- Deepening conceptual understanding through self-explanation during rebuttal [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Select a contested question and assign positions (or use structured controversy with side-switching)
2. Provide preparation time with evidence sources and an [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md) scaffold
3. Run the debate with explicit rules: timed openings, rebuttals, and cross-examination; assign roles if using teams
4. Require rebuttal of the *strongest* opposing argument, not the easiest — this drives the perspective-taking benefit
5. Debrief: evaluate argument quality against the rubric, surface what changed in learners' thinking, and have learners articulate the strongest point from the *opposing* side
6. Follow with individual synthesis writing to consolidate ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

## Examples
- **Structured academic controversy in environmental science**: teams debate a land-use proposal, switch sides mid-activity, then jointly write a recommendation — the standard Johnson & Johnson classroom format
- **[Moot court](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_students/moot_court/)** in law schools: argued hypothetical cases before a judge panel, with immediate oral feedback — debate with professional stakes
- **Fishbowl debates in large lecture courses**: six students debate while the rest score argument quality on a rubric, then rotate; everyone prepares both sides
