# Allowing Think-Time and Re-Reading

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 9 report an effect size

## Description
Allowing think-time and re-reading means deliberately extending the interval between posing a question (or presenting material) and expecting a response, and permitting learners to revisit text multiple times before responding. This supports deeper comprehension and reduces frustration, particularly for learners who process language or information more slowly.

## Design Implications

Extended wait-time after questions shifts classroom discourse from rapid recall toward more elaborate reasoning: teachers who sustain silence for 3+ seconds after a question get longer, more cognitively complex student responses [~S]. Re-reading (repeated reading) improves fluency and comprehension because each pass frees working-memory resources from decoding, allowing attention to shift to meaning [~M]. Both tactics work only if the time is structured — unstructured silence or aimless re-reading produces little gain.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Flexibility in pacing and scheduling; a supportive, non-pressured classroom climate where silence is normalized
- A clear purpose for the wait: a specific question worth thinking about ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) prompts, [Practice](../elements/practice.md) problems)
- A productive use for re-reading — a goal for the second pass (e.g., "find the claim," "mark what confused you" via [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- A classroom or course norm that silence after a question is productive, not a failure
- Genuinely open questions worth thinking about; wait time after low-level recall questions adds little
- Access to materials for re-reading (texts, recorded explanations, worked problems)
- A follow-up structure that converts thinking into a response ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), written response, or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))

#### Constraints
- Wait-time gains collapse when teachers break the silence after 1–2 seconds, which is the default in most classrooms [~S]
- Re-reading without a purpose or feedback yields diminishing returns; gains plateau when the text is already fully understood [~M]
- Extending time for everyone can slow whole-class pacing; effectiveness depends on learners actually using the time rather than disengaging [-W]
- Timed assessments and rapid-fire questioning formats negate the strategy entirely
- Unstructured extra time invites disengagement; some learners stop attending when nothing is required of them [-M]
- Re-reading familiar text creates fluency-based illusions of knowing — learners mistake ease of re-reading for mastery [Rereading is less effective than retrieval-based study strategies.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024818) [-S]
- Extended time on timed assessments changes what is being measured; accommodations must be deliberate ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md))
- Whole-class wait time can feel uncomfortable for instructors, who tend to cut it short after under one second without training [Wait time improves the quality of classroom discourse.](https://doi.org/10.1177/002248718603700102) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Structured think-pair-share: individual think-time before peer discussion
- Re-reading with varied purpose per pass (first for gist, second for detail, third for evaluation)
- Extended-time accommodations on assessments for identified learners
- Self-paced digital materials ([Adaptive Learning](../principles/adaptive-learning.md)) that let learners control pacing
- **Wait time 1** (after the question) vs. **wait time 2** (after a student responds, before the teacher reacts) — both matter
- Think-pair-share: individual think-time followed by peer rehearsal before public response
- Structured re-reading: second pass with a specific purpose (annotating, answering a guiding question) rather than verbatim repetition
- Extended-time accommodations on assessments for learners with documented processing-speed needs

### Target Learners
- Learners with processing-speed challenges, language-processing difficulties, or reading fluency limitations
- Second-language learners who need additional passes at text
- All learners benefit from wait-time on higher-order questions; the effect is not limited to struggling students [~S]
- Learners with slower processing speed or working-memory constraints, for whom forced-pace questioning produces guessing rather than reasoning [Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- Second-language learners and anyone processing in a non-native language
- Anxious learners, for whom immediate cold-calling suppresses performance
- Less beneficial as unstructured re-reading for learners who already use stronger strategies like self-testing [Rereading is less effective than retrieval-based study strategies.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024818) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension and fluency development
- Higher-order reasoning and elaborated verbal responses during discussion
- Metacognitive monitoring — re-reading teaches learners to detect and repair comprehension failures
- Deeper comprehension and reasoning rather than rapid recall
- Quality of oral and written contributions in discussion
- Self-regulated study habits — knowing when a second pass is useful and how to structure it

### Instructions
1. Pose a question and explicitly announce the think-time ("Take 30 seconds before anyone answers").
2. Enforce silence for 3–5 seconds minimum; count silently if needed [~S].
3. Direct learners to re-read the relevant passage with a specific second-pass goal, using [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) to mark key points or confusions.
4. Invite responses, then extend a second wait-time after a learner answers before evaluating or calling on someone else.
5. Build re-reading into [Practice](../elements/practice.md) cycles rather than treating it as remediation.

## Related Strategies
- [Activating Background Knowledge](activate_background_knowledge.md) — a purposeful first-pass re-read often serves to connect new text to prior knowledge
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — breaking text into segments makes targeted re-reading manageable within working-memory limits [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) — prompts learners to use think-time productively by explaining the text to themselves [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) — think-time gives learners the space to retrieve relevant prior knowledge before new input

## Examples
- **Wait-time in science discourse** — Rowe's classic classroom studies trained teachers to hold 3-second pauses after questions and after student answers, producing longer student utterances and more student-initiated questions.
- **Repeated reading interventions** — Reading fluency programs in which a learner re-reads a short passage aloud several times until reaching a criterion rate, with feedback each pass.
- **UDL-aligned course design** — CAST's UDL Guidelines list "vary the demands and time allowed" as a design feature; instructors post recordings and transcripts so learners can re-watch and re-read at their own pace ([CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org)).
- **[Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md)** — a standard active-learning routine: 30–60 seconds of silent thinking, brief pair discussion, then whole-class sharing; the pair step converts private think-time into a low-stakes rehearsal.
- **Extended-time testing accommodations** — most university disability services grant 1.5× time for documented processing-speed or ADHD-related needs.
- **Purposeful re-reading in reading instruction** — a second read of a science text with the task of marking claims vs. evidence, converting repetition into analysis.

## Description
Sufficient time is allowed for learners to process information and re-read materials if needed. In discourse, this takes the form of deliberate wait time — pausing several seconds after posing a question before calling on anyone. In study contexts, it means permitting and structuring re-reading of texts, problems, and feedback rather than forcing immediate responses.

## Design Implications

Extended wait time after questions increases the length and quality of student responses and the number of voluntary contributions [Wait time improves the quality of classroom discourse.](https://doi.org/10.1177/002248718603700102) [+S]. However, re-reading is one of the *weaker* study strategies on its own: rereading a text produces modest gains compared with retrieval practice or elaborative strategies [Rereading is less effective than retrieval-based study strategies.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024818) [~M]. The strategy therefore works best when think-time is paired with an active task — a prompt to annotate, predict, or formulate an answer — rather than passive repetition [Rereading is less effective than retrieval-based study strategies.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024818) [-M].
