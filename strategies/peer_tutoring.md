# Peer Tutoring

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Peer tutoring arranges learners into structured pairs or small groups in which one student (the tutor) explains material, models procedures, questions, and gives feedback to another (the tutee). Effective implementations are not informal "work with a partner": they specify roles, materials, tutor scripts or prompting routines, and regular role switching, with the teacher training tutors and monitoring pairs.

## Design Implications

Peer tutoring works because both roles learn: tutees receive individualized, immediate explanation and feedback, while tutors consolidate their own understanding through the act of explaining and answering questions [~S]. Effects are consistently positive but depend on structure — unstructured peer help produces far smaller gains than tutoring with trained routines, role scripts, and teacher monitoring [~S]. Reciprocal designs, where partners alternate tutor and tutee roles, spread the benefits of both positions and avoid stigmatizing weaker students as permanent "receivers."

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit training for tutors: how to explain, prompt rather than tell, and give specific feedback ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) routines)
- Structured materials — problem sets, prompt cards, or scripts — so tutors are not improvising content
- Regular role switching or pairing rotation to distribute benefits
- Teacher monitoring and brief check-ins to correct misconceptions before they propagate

#### Constraints
- Tutors with shaky understanding can entrench errors; teacher monitoring and answer keys are essential safeguards [-M]
- Unstructured "help your neighbor" without training, scripts, or monitoring yields minimal gains [-M]
- Fixed tutor/tutee hierarchies can harm the tutee's status and motivation; reciprocal designs mitigate this [-W]
- High-stakes or novel content beyond the tutor's competence is a poor fit; tutoring works best for consolidating material both partners have some grasp of [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal peer tutoring** — partners alternate roles within or across sessions (e.g., Classwide Peer Tutoring)
- **Cross-age tutoring** — older students tutor younger ones, often with strong motivational benefits for the tutor
- **Same-ability dyads** — both partners work at the edge of their competence, reducing status asymmetry
- **Peer-assisted learning strategies (PALS)** — curriculum-embedded routines pairing tutoring with structured reading or math activities

### Target Learners
- Tutees struggling with material the class has already partially covered — individualized explanation at their level [~S]
- Tutors with moderate (not minimal) mastery; explaining consolidates and reveals gaps in their own understanding [~S]
- Less effective when the tutee's needs far exceed the tutor's knowledge, or when either partner lacks the social skills the routine requires [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation and fluency with previously taught skills and concepts
- Verbalization and articulation of reasoning — explaining forces retrieval and organization
- Social and communicative skills: questioning, giving feedback, taking another's perspective

### Instructions
1. Select content both partners have encountered, and prepare structured materials (problem sets, prompt cards, or scripts).
2. Train tutors in a short routine: explain, then prompt with questions rather than telling, then give specific feedback ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. Pair students and assign roles; establish how and when roles switch.
4. Run timed tutoring cycles with the teacher circulating, monitoring accuracy, and correcting misconceptions.
5. Debrief briefly — what explanations worked, what remained confusing — and rotate pairings periodically.

## Related Strategies
- [Cooperative Learning](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md) — the broader family of structured peer interaction; tutoring is its most asymmetric, role-differentiated form
- [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — tutoring typically follows initial teacher-led instruction; peers consolidate rather than introduce content
- [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) — freed class time is a common venue for peer tutoring cycles

## Examples
- **Classwide Peer Tutoring (CWPT)** — a well-researched reciprocal routine in which entire class pairs alternate weekly as tutor and tutee, with points, materials, and switching rules specified by the program; widely used in elementary reading and math.
- **Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS)** — a structured reading and math tutoring curriculum developed at Vanderbilt University ([https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu)), with scripted partner reading and summarization routines.
- **Cross-age math tutoring programs** — middle schoolers tutoring elementary students on arithmetic fluency, with tutor training sessions preceding each cycle.
