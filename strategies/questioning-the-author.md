# Questioning The Author

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Questioning the Author (QtA) is a discussion-based reading comprehension strategy in which the teacher poses open-ended "queries" directed at the author's intentions — e.g., "What is the author trying to tell us here?" or "Does that make sense with what came before?" — rather than asking students to recall text details. Students treat the text as fallible and under construction, working collaboratively to build meaning as they read, and the teacher follows up with "follow-up queries" that probe and connect student responses.

## Design Implications

QtA shifts comprehension instruction from assessing what students extracted from text to actively constructing meaning during reading, positioning students as sense-makers in dialogue with the author [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Because queries are open and interpretive, they generate productive cognitive effort and discussion rather than single-word recall [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The teacher's role is facilitative: initiating queries, marking student ideas as building blocks, and re-voicing them toward the author's message.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text with substantive ideas worth discussing — dense, conceptually rich material where the author's message is not fully transparent
- Teacher facility with open-ended questioning and with responding to unexpected student ideas without funneling toward a predetermined answer
- A discussion norm in which students respond to one another, not only to the teacher ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Planning of "initiating queries" at key idea junctures in the text, prepared in advance

#### Constraints
- Ineffective with simple narrative or highly decodable texts where the author's message is transparent — there is nothing to negotiate
- Requires significant teacher skill; poorly facilitated QtA collapses into guess-what-the-teacher-is-thinking recitation, which reduces the interpretive effort the strategy depends on [~M]
- Works poorly in large groups without discussion norms; students who lack basic decoding fluency may spend capacity on word recognition rather than meaning-making
- Less suitable when the goal is literal recall or discrete skill practice rather than conceptual understanding of text

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full QtA lesson**: extended discussion across an entire text with planned query points
- **Brief QtA inserts**: single queries embedded in an otherwise teacher-led reading of a passage
- **Text Talk variant** (Beck & McKeown): QtA queries applied to read-alouds with young children, with attention to vocabulary developed from the text
- **Content-area adaptation**: queries directed at authors of science or history texts ("Why does the author present this evidence here?"), connecting to [Disciplinary Literacy](../principles/disciplinary-literacy.md)

### Target Learners
- Elementary through secondary students reading expository or conceptually dense text
- Struggling comprehenders who decode adequately but treat reading as retrieval rather than meaning-making
- English learners benefit when queries are paired with attention to [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and interpretation of expository text
- Reasoning about author's purpose, evidence, and text structure
- Collaborative meaning-making and academic discussion skills

### Instructions
1. Select a text with ideas worth building, and identify 3–5 junctures where the author's message needs negotiation; draft an initiating query for each (e.g., "What is the author telling us here?").
2. Read a segment aloud or have students read it, then pose the initiating query — not a detail question.
3. Follow up on student responses with follow-up queries that probe ("What in the text makes you say that?") and connect ideas across students, using [Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) to re-voice and build on contributions.
4. Mark student ideas as "building blocks" toward understanding the author's message, and consolidate at the end: "So what is the author's overall message?"
5. Fade teacher facilitation over time so students generate their own author-directed questions ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Related Strategies
- [Reciprocal Teaching](../elements/reciprocal-teaching.md) — similarly discussion-based comprehension instruction, but organized around student-led application of strategies rather than author-directed queries
- [Socratic Seminar](../strategies/socratic-seminar.md) — shares open questioning, but targets argumentation across a whole text rather than incremental meaning-building during reading
- [Think-Aloud](../strategies/think-aloud-modeling.md) — teacher models the sense-making that QtA queries elicit from students

## Examples
- Beck, McKeown, Hamilton, and Kucan's original work with elementary social studies and science textbooks, where students negotiated the compressed, idea-dense prose of standard texts.
- **Text Talk** (Beck & McKeown) — read-aloud discussions with kindergarten and first-grade students using QtA queries before vocabulary instruction drawn from the text.
- A middle-school history teacher pausing a textbook passage on the American Revolution to ask, "Why do you think the author lists these three causes and not others?"
