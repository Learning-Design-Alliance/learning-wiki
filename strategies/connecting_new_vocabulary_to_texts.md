# Connecting New Vocabulary to Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy embeds vocabulary instruction within the texts learners are actually reading, rather than teaching word lists in isolation. Teachers identify high-leverage words in a text (often Tier Two academic words per Beck et al.'s framework), provide brief student-friendly explanations tied to the text's themes, and connect new terms to concepts learners already hold. The goal is for words to be acquired as tools for understanding the text, not as isolated items to memorize.

## Design Implications

Vocabulary learned in context is more durable because the surrounding text supplies semantic associations and usage constraints that a definition alone cannot [Stahl & Fairbanks' meta-analysis found context-based vocabulary instruction improves both word knowledge and comprehension.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Word selection matters more than word quantity: a small set of words chosen for their centrality to the text's meaning and their general utility outperforms exhaustive pre-teaching [~S]. Because working memory is limited, pre-teaching a handful of key terms before reading reduces the load of decoding unfamiliar words during comprehension [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], and fluent word recognition frees attention for meaning-making [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts rich enough that target words genuinely matter for comprehension
- Teacher preparation to select words by utility and text centrality (Tier Two/Tier Three analysis)
- Brief, learner-friendly explanations plus at least one encounter with the word in meaningful use ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Multiple spaced encounters with each word across the unit — one exposure rarely produces durable learning [~S]

#### Constraints
- Pre-teaching too many words consumes instructional time and overloads learners, leaving insufficient reading time [-M]
- Contextual inference alone is unreliable: learners frequently misinfer word meanings from context, so explicit explanation must accompany contextual exposure [-S]
- Effectiveness drops for learners who lack the background knowledge the text presumes; word explanations cannot substitute for missing concepts [~M]
- For advanced readers, pre-teaching familiar words is redundant and wastes time [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Before reading:** brief pre-teaching of 3–7 critical words with friendly definitions and an example
- **During reading:** [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) target words in context, margin glosses, or embedded definitions
- **After reading:** word discussions anchored to text events, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) linking new words to known concepts, and activities requiring learners to use words in new contexts
- **Embedded media:** narrated glossaries or pop-up definitions in digital texts (e.g., Newsela's leveled articles with embedded vocabulary support)

### Target Learners
- Elementary through secondary readers encountering academic language in content-area texts [+S]
- English language learners, who benefit from explicit explanation paired with rich context rather than inference alone [+M]
- Struggling readers, for whom unfamiliar vocabulary is a primary comprehension bottleneck [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Less beneficial for proficient readers with strong vocabulary, for whom pre-teaching is redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Word knowledge: accurate meaning, usage, and morphological awareness
- Reading comprehension: understanding texts that depend on target vocabulary [+S]
- Academic language development: acquiring generalizable Tier Two terms transferable across disciplines

### Instructions
1. Read the text in advance and select 3–7 words that are central to comprehension and broadly useful ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the selection to a manageable set)
2. Before reading, provide brief, student-friendly explanations with an example and a quick [activation](../principles/activation.md) of related prior knowledge [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
3. During reading, have learners encounter the words in context and mark or discuss them ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
4. After reading, engage learners in using the words — discussion, writing, or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to familiar concepts ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
5. Revisit the words in spaced, varied contexts over subsequent lessons [~S]

## Examples
- Choosing words like *seized* and *sleek* from a text about Hatshepsut and discussing how they shape understanding of her character and Egyptian civilization.
- **[Word Generation](https://wordgeneration.org)** — a middle-school curriculum that teaches academic vocabulary through discussion and reading of engaging texts rather than isolated lists.
- **Newsela** — leveled news articles with embedded vocabulary support, letting learners meet target words in authentic context at adjustable reading levels.
