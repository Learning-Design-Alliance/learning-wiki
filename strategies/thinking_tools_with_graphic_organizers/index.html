Thinking Tools with Graphic Organizers¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 14 studies (6 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 14 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

When teachers deliver content through lectures, readings, or video, they can have students interact with the content by completing a graphic organizer in groups, pairs, or even on their own. The organizer — a concept map, comparison matrix, flowchart, or hierarchical outline — externalizes the structure of the material, requiring learners to select, relate, and arrange key ideas rather than passively receive them. The strategy pairs content delivery with an active processing task, converting a presentation into a learning-by-doing episode.

Design Implications¶

Graphic organizers improve learning when they force generative processing: learners must identify relationships (compare, sequence, categorize) rather than copy text Nesbit & Adesope meta-analysis found learner-generated concept maps outperform reading/listening controls. [+S]. The organizer's structure should match the content's structure — a comparison matrix for contrasting cases, a flowchart for processes — so that completing it builds an accurate schema rather than a generic fill-in exercise Chunking content into an organized visual structure reduces working memory load. [+M]. Supplying a partially completed organizer (skeleton with some nodes filled) outperforms both fully blank and fully completed versions for novices, because it directs attention without eliminating the generative work Emphasis and structure direct learner attention to relevant information. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An organizer whose structure matches the target content's logical structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, cause–effect)
  • Content delivered in chunks that map onto organizer sections (Chunking)
  • A follow-up step where learners explain or apply what they organized (Application), since the organizer alone does not guarantee understanding
  • For group use, roles or prompts that ensure all students contribute to the organizing, not one student filling it in

Constraints¶

  • Fully completed organizers handed to learners produce weak learning; the benefit comes from generating the structure, not viewing it Learner-generated organizers outperform supplied ones. [-M]
  • For learners with high prior knowledge, a heavily scaffolded organizer can be redundant and slow them down Guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners. [~M]
  • If the organizer's categories are imposed and mismatch the content, learners force-fit ideas into the wrong structure, harming schema formation [~W]
  • Copy-and-paste completion (transcribing text into boxes without transformation) yields near-zero generative processing [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • During lecture: pause points where pairs complete one section of the organizer before the next segment
  • With reading or video: organizer as an annotating frame students fill while consuming content
  • Jigsaw variant: groups each complete a different section, then teach it to other groups
  • Compare-and-contrast variant: groups complete organizers independently, then compare results across groups — discrepancies become discussion fodder (Class Discussion)
  • Advance organizer variant: a partially completed organizer presented before the content primes relevant schema (Advance Organizers)

Target Learners¶

  • Novices encountering unfamiliar, content-dense material who need help imposing structure Chunking content into an organized visual structure reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Learners who benefit from dual coding — verbal content re-represented spatially and visually [~M]
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners, for whom a rigid organizer adds busywork without new processing Guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: identifying relationships, similarities, and differences among ideas Learner-generated concept maps improve conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Knowledge organization: building hierarchical or sequential schema rather than isolated facts
  • Abstraction from multiple examples: comparison organizers support noticing shared structure Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Choose an organizer type that matches the content structure (matrix for comparisons, flowchart for processes, hierarchy for taxonomies).
  2. Pre-fill one or two nodes as a model, leaving the generative work to students.
  3. Deliver content in chunks (Chunking), pausing after each for students to complete the corresponding section in pairs or groups.
  4. Circulate and prompt with questions ("How does this connect to what we saw earlier?") rather than supplying answers (Coaching).
  5. Have groups compare completed organizers and resolve discrepancies through Class Discussion.
  6. Close with an application task (Application) that requires using the organized knowledge, not restating it.

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — organizers presented before content to activate prior knowledge; this strategy uses them during and after delivery
  • Comparing Cases — group comparison of completed organizers enacts the same contrasting mechanism
  • Jigsaw — a cooperative structure that distributes organizer sections across groups

Examples¶

  • Comparison matrix in science: after a lecture on cell types, pairs complete a matrix contrasting plant, animal, and bacterial cells across structure, energy, and reproduction; groups then compare matrices and debate disputed cells.
  • Flowchart with video: students watching a process video (e.g., how a bill becomes law) fill a sequential flowchart at pause points, then apply it to a novel scenario.
  • Concept mapping in reading: students map characters, motivations, and conflicts from a novel, then compare maps across groups to surface different interpretations.
