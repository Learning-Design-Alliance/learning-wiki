# Research Reports

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A research report communicates the purpose, methods, findings, and implications of an investigation to an audience that was not present for the work. Unlike a journal article, a learning-oriented research report is a designed artifact: it uses visual structure, plain language, and layered detail so readers can extract the headline finding quickly and drill into evidence as needed. Producing one is itself a learning activity — it requires learners to synthesize, prioritize, and translate findings for an audience.

## Design Implications
Research reports work as both a communication format and a synthesis task. As a format, their effectiveness depends on managing the reader's cognitive load: well-structured sections, informative headings, and purposeful graphics let readers allocate attention to meaning rather than navigation [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. As a task, asking learners to write reports forces integration of evidence into coherent claims, which supports deeper processing than reading or summarizing alone [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear research question stated up front, with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or executive summary that frames the whole document
- Visualizations chosen to match the data structure, not decoration — graphics should carry the argument, with captions that state the takeaway [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M]
- Layered depth: headline → summary → methods → appendix, so different audiences can enter at different levels
- An explicit chain of evidence from data to claim, so readers can evaluate rather than merely accept conclusions

#### Constraints
- Dense, jargon-heavy reports are effectively unread by lay audiences; without [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md), the format fails its core purpose [-M]
- Decorative or dual-encoded-poorly graphics increase extraneous load and depress comprehension, especially for low-prior-knowledge readers [~S]
- Reports that bury limitations or uncertainty produce overconfident readers; omitting caveats is a known failure mode of science communication [-M]
- Long unstructured prose sections defeat the format; readers skim, and key findings get missed [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Poster or infographic variants** compress the report to a single visual surface for conference or community settings
- **Data dashboards** (e.g., interactive reports in Tableau or Observable) replace static figures with explorable data, shifting readers from audience to investigators
- **Structured templates** (executive summary, key findings, evidence, limitations, recommendations) standardize the genre for novice writers
- **Peer review cycles** in which classmates critique drafts for clarity before revision

### Target Learners
- Secondary and postsecondary students conducting inquiry projects, science-fair work, or capstone research [+W]
- Graduate students and professionals learning to communicate technical work to non-technical stakeholders [~M]
- Less suitable as a first exposure to a topic — readers need enough prior knowledge to interpret evidence claims; novices benefit more from [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) with narrative framing [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Synthesis: integrating multiple sources of evidence into a coherent argument
- Science communication: translating technical findings for varied audiences
- Data literacy: interpreting and critiquing visualizations and statistical claims
- Academic writing: mastering the conventions of an evidence-based genre

### Instructions
1. Define the audience and decision the report should support; write the research question at the top of the draft.
2. Draft an executive summary or [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) stating the question, method in one sentence, and headline finding.
3. Organize findings into [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)-sized sections with informative headings that state conclusions, not topics ("Retention dropped 20% after week 6," not "Results").
4. Select or build visualizations that each make one point; write takeaway captions rather than titles that merely name the variables.
5. State methods and limitations explicitly, using [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or sidebar notes to keep caveats visible without cluttering the main text.
6. Close with implications and recommendations tied directly to the evidence, then peer-review drafts for clarity before final revision.

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — reports can present a case's evidence base; cases give reports a narrative anchor
- [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md) — the claim–evidence–reasoning structure of a report is an argument schema applied to empirical data
- [Peer Feedback](../elements/peer-feedback.md) — structured critique of report drafts improves both the draft and the reviewer's evaluative skill

## Examples
- **AAAS Science in the Classroom** ([https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org](https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org)) — published research papers annotated with learning lenses, letting students read authentic reports with scaffolds.
- **Our World in Data** ([https://ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org)) — research communication built entirely on layered, takeaway-titled visualizations with accessible prose; a model of the format for student projects.
- **AP Research / Capstone programs** — students produce a 4,000–5,000-word academic research report defended before a panel, making the report the summative assessment artifact.
