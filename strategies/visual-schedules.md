# Visual Schedules

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A visual schedule is a persistent, at-a-glance display — pictures, icons, written words, or objects — that shows what will happen, in what order, and often what comes after the current activity. Learners check the schedule, complete an activity, mark it done, and move to the next item, making the schedule a self-management tool rather than a teacher-directed prompt.

## Design Implications

Visual schedules externalize temporal structure, reducing the working-memory and attentional demands of tracking multi-step sequences [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Predictability of "what comes next" lowers anxiety and transition resistance, particularly for learners who struggle with unexpected change, and shifts stimulus control from adult prompting to the environment itself — a core mechanism in structured teaching approaches such as TEACCH [~S]. Schedules function as a form of advance organizer for the day or task, orienting attention before instruction begins [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, visible location or personal format (wall chart, binder, tablet app such as [First Then Visual Schedule](https://www.goodkarmaapplications.com) or [Choiceworks](https://beevisual.com))
- Representations matched to learner literacy: objects or photos for pre-readers, icons or text for fluent readers
- A routine for interacting with the schedule — check, complete, mark off, transition — taught explicitly and faded from adult prompting
- Schedule content that matches what actually happens; broken promises erode trust in the tool

#### Constraints
- Overly rigid schedules can increase distress when reality deviates; building in a "change" card or deliberate variation teaches flexibility rather than fragility [~M]
- If an adult continuously directs the learner to the schedule, it remains an adult-controlled prompt and does not build self-management [-M]
- Icon-only schedules can add decoding load for learners who read fluently; text is often the more efficient format [~W]
- For open-ended, inquiry-oriented activities, a fixed sequence can conflict with the goal of learner-driven pacing [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class daily schedule vs. individual mini-schedules for a single task (e.g., steps of a lab report)
- Object schedules (an actual spoon for lunch) for learners with severe disabilities or visual impairment
- Digital schedules with timers and embedded choice points, which support [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) by letting learners control order where sequence permits
- First-then boards: a two-item schedule used to bridge a non-preferred activity to a preferred one

### Target Learners
- Autistic learners and learners with intellectual or developmental disabilities, where visual schedules are an evidence-based practice [~S]
- Young children and emergent readers who cannot yet use written agendas
- Learners with ADHD or executive-function difficulties who lose track of multi-step sequences [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Whole classrooms during transitions, where a shared visual reference reduces repeated verbal directions

### Target Learning Goals
- Executive function: planning, sequencing, and self-monitoring of activity ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))
- Behavioral regulation: independent transitions and reduced off-task behavior
- Routines and independence: performing daily living or classroom procedures without adult prompting

### Instructions
1. Identify the sequence to be represented (day, task, or routine) and break it into discrete, observable activities, applying [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) so no step is lost in the display.
2. Select a representation format matched to the learner's literacy and vision ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)).
3. Introduce the schedule with [Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md): model checking it, completing an item, marking it done, and transitioning.
4. Fade adult prompting systematically so the schedule itself gains stimulus control; use a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) at transitions to confirm the learner is self-managing.
5. Review and revise the schedule with the learner, adding choice points and "change" cards to build flexibility.

## Examples
- **TEACCH Structured Teaching** ([teacch.com](https://teacch.com)) — individual visual schedules are a defining component; learners move a schedule symbol to a matching location, making the environment itself direct behavior.
- **Choiceworks** ([Bee Visual](https://beevisual.com)) — tablet app supporting picture schedules with timers and waiting boards for home and classroom routines.
- **Elementary classroom daily agenda** — a whiteboard icon-and-word sequence reviewed at morning meeting, with a student volunteer moving a marker to the current activity.
