# Separating Effort From Achievement

## Description
Separating effort from achievement means giving learners two distinct channels of recognition: one for the quality of their work against standards (achievement) and one for their persistence, strategy use, and improvement over time (effort). In practice this means effort-based praise and feedback are delivered separately from grades or performance judgments, so learners never interpret effort as a substitute for mastery — or failure as evidence that effort is worthless.

## Design Implications

The strategy targets attribution and motivation: learners who attribute success to controllable causes (effort, strategy) persist longer than those who attribute outcomes to fixed ability [~S]. But effort praise is only effective when it is specific and tied to process — vague praise for "trying hard" can lower achievement by signaling low expectations [Pairing effort praise with strategy instruction outperforms effort praise alone.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. The design goal is not to reward effort *instead of* achievement, but to make both visible on separate scales so a struggling learner sees effort paying off in growth even before performance catches up.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Two distinct feedback channels: a standards-referenced judgment of work quality and a separate process/progress comment ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
- Rubrics or criteria that define achievement independently of time-on-task, so effort never inflates grades
- Specific process feedback naming the strategies that produced improvement, not generic encouragement [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Opportunities for learners to see their own growth over time (portfolios, progress charts, revision cycles)

#### Constraints
- Unqualified effort praise ("good job working hard") after poor performance signals low ability expectations and can reduce achievement [Effort praise without strategy information can backfire.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- If effort is rewarded with the same grades or credentials as achievement, standards erode and high performers disengage
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, added effort scaffolding can be redundant or demotivating [Guidance benefits decline as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Effort framing alone does not overcome systematic skill deficits; it must be paired with strategy instruction and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Grading:** separate "academic achievement" and "learning behaviors" columns on report cards (common in standards-based grading systems)
- **Feedback language:** process-focused comments ("your revision strategy worked here") distinct from person-focused praise ("you're smart"), which undermines persistence after failure [~S]
- **Revision structures:** allow resubmission so effort converts into achievement over time, keeping the two channels connected but not conflated
- **Self-report:** have learners log effort and strategy use separately from self-assessed performance to build metacognitive accuracy

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners who attribute failure to low ability and disengage early [~S]
- Learners recovering from high-confidence errors, who benefit from seeing errors as effort-and-strategy problems rather than ability verdicts [High-confidence errors, when corrected, improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]
- Advanced learners need less effort scaffolding and more challenge; over-praising their effort can feel patronizing [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Sustained engagement and persistence on difficult, multi-step tasks
- Adaptive attribution: linking outcomes to strategy use rather than fixed ability
- Long-term achievement growth that follows from protected motivation — the strategy serves achievement goals indirectly, not by lowering standards

### Instructions
1. Define achievement criteria publicly and independently of effort, using rubrics or success criteria ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. During [Practice](../elements/application.md) and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), give two-part feedback: first the standards-referenced judgment, then a separate process comment naming effective strategies or effort patterns.
3. Replace person praise ("you're talented") with specific process praise ("you checked each step against the criteria") [~S].
4. Build revision cycles so effortful improvement visibly changes achievement outcomes over time.
5. In whole-class discourse, celebrate improvement and strategy use separately from top scores, so multiple learners have legitimate paths to recognition.

## Examples
- **Standards-based grading (e.g., Marzano-style report cards)** — separates "academic achievement" scores from "work habits" scores so a grade of B with high growth is reported differently from a B with coasting.
- **Zearn / Khan Academy progress dashboards** — display effort metrics (problems attempted, minutes of practice, streaks) alongside mastery metrics, keeping the two visually distinct.
- **Writing workshop models (e.g., Lucy Calkins' Units of Study)** — teachers confer on both the draft's quality against rubric criteria and the writer's revision strategies, in separate conversational moves.
