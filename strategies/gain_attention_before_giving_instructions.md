# Gain Attention Before Giving Instructions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The strategy establishes a reliable signal — a clap pattern, chime, raised hand, countdown, or call-and-response — that learners are trained to recognize as a cue to stop, orient, and listen. Instructions are delivered only after the signal has secured attention, ensuring that task directions arrive while learners can actually process them.

## Design Implications

Attention is a prerequisite for instruction: information arriving while learners are mid-task or distracted is unlikely to be encoded, and re-explaining wastes instructional time. Gagné's first event of instruction, "gain attention," exists precisely because reception of information is the gateway to all later processing ([Gagné's 9 Events](../patterns/gagnés-9-events-of-instruction.md)). A consistent signal functions as a conditioned cue through repeated pairing with the expectation of quiet focus, an application of [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) [+M]. Because working memory cannot hold fragmented instructions heard partway through, securing full attention before speaking also allows directions to be [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) into a coherent, receivable unit [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A single, unambiguous signal chosen deliberately for the setting (auditory signals carry across a room; visual signals work in noisy spaces)
- Explicit teaching of the signal's meaning and expected response, followed by brief rehearsal until the routine is automatic
- Consistent use: every instruction, every time, especially while the routine is being established
- Instructions delivered *after* — never during — the transition to attention

#### Constraints
- Signals lose effectiveness when overused or used without follow-through; if the teacher talks over inattention, learners learn the signal predicts nothing [-M]
- Auditory signals degrade in genuinely noisy environments (workshops, transitions, outdoor settings) and need a visual backup [-W]
- A signal secures *orientation*, not *engagement*; attention without relevance decays within seconds, so instructions must follow immediately and connect to the task's purpose [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Reliance on a single teacher-controlled cue can crowd out learners' developing self-regulation of attention; pairing the external cue with an internal self-check ("Am I ready to hear the steps?") mitigates this [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Auditory:** clap patterns, chimes, call-and-response ("Class–Yes" in Whole Brain Teaching)
- **Visual:** raised hand with learners responding in kind, lights flicked, a silent countdown on the board
- **Verbal:** a consistent phrase or a deliberate shift in voice level
- **Digital contexts:** notification pings, a "focus mode" banner, or a facilitator's "eyes on me" message before posting instructions in chat or an LMS
- Signals can be co-designed with learners to increase buy-in and cultural fit

### Target Learners
- Whole-class and large-group settings at any age; most valuable where one instructor addresses many learners simultaneously
- Younger learners and learners with attention difficulties benefit most from an external, predictable cue [+W]
- Adolescents and adults may find gimmicky signals patronizing; a low-key verbal or visual cue preserves dignity while serving the same function [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning goal in itself: this strategy serves *readiness to receive* procedural information, task directions, and safety-critical instructions
- Especially important before multi-step instructions, where missing a single step derails the task

### Instructions
1. Choose one signal appropriate to the environment and learner age.
2. Teach and rehearse the signal explicitly: signal → expected response → brief practice until automatic.
3. When instructions are ready, give the signal and wait for full attention before speaking — do not begin talking over noise.
4. Deliver instructions in short, chunked steps, checking that the first step is understood before adding the next ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) via a quick comprehension check).
5. Follow through consistently; if attention drifts mid-instruction, re-signal rather than continuing.
6. Periodically refresh or rotate the signal if responsiveness declines.

## Related Strategies
- Establishing and teaching classroom routines — the attention signal is one instance of a broader routine system; consistency across routines is what makes any single cue work
- Wait time after questions — like the attention signal, it structures the timing of teacher talk relative to student processing

## Examples
- An elementary teacher claps a rhythm; students echo the clap and go silent, after which the teacher gives two-step directions for a science station rotation.
- A secondary teacher flicks the classroom lights once and stands silent at the front; within seconds students orient, and the teacher delivers lab safety instructions.
- In a synchronous online class, the facilitator posts "🎧 Listening check" in chat and pauses until all participants respond, then shares screen-share instructions.
