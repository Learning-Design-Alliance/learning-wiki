# Peer Review/Peer Editing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Peer review asks students to exchange drafts or completed work and provide structured, criterion-based feedback on each other's performance, typically using a rubric, checklist, or prompt set. Each student plays two roles: reviewer, which requires evaluating work against criteria, and reviewee, which requires interpreting and acting on feedback. Peer editing is the writing-focused variant, emphasizing revision of drafts rather than summative judgment.

## Design Implications

Peer review operationalizes [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): feedback functions formatively when it targets the task and the processes behind it, not the person [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The reviewer role is often the more powerful learning position — evaluating someone else's work against criteria forces students to internalize quality standards and detect flaws, a skill that transfers to revising their own work [~M]. Structure is essential: unstructured "comment on your partner's essay" exchanges produce vague, low-utility feedback, whereas rubric-anchored review produces specific, actionable comments [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear evaluation criteria, ideally a rubric students have practiced applying ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- Anonymized or low-stakes exchange arrangements to reduce social discomfort
- Time allocated for reviewees to interpret feedback and revise, not just receive it
- Instructor modeling of high-quality feedback before students review independently ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

#### Constraints
- Student reviewers often under- or over-rate relative to instructors, and reliability drops when criteria are vague or the work is complex [~M] — peer grades should not carry high-stakes weight without calibration
- Feedback quality suffers when reviewers lack domain knowledge; novices can spot surface errors but miss conceptual flaws [-M]
- Friendship and status effects bias feedback toward leniency unless reviews are anonymized [-M]
- Without a required revision step, students frequently ignore peer comments and learning gains collapse [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Calibrated peer review:** students first rate an instructor-anchored exemplar and receive accuracy feedback before reviewing peers (e.g., Calibrated Peer Review)
- **Double-blind exchange:** anonymized drafts reduce bias and increase critical candor
- **Feedback-only vs. grading:** separating peer comments from grades keeps the activity formative
- **Author response:** requiring reviewees to write a brief response plan converts received comments into revision commitments

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary students in writing-intensive or project-based courses, where drafts and revision cycles are natural [~S]
- Reviewers benefit most when they have enough prior knowledge to recognize quality but are still consolidating their own standards [~M]
- Anxious or low-efficacy students can benefit from the reduced-stakes audience, supporting persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing criteria for quality work
- Revision and self-regulation: diagnosing weaknesses and planning improvements
- Domain concepts: reviewing erroneous work deepens the reviewer's own conceptual understanding [Reviewing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Communication and professional critique skills

### Instructions
1. Set explicit criteria and share the rubric before drafting begins ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Model the review process on an anonymized sample, thinking aloud about how criteria map to specific text features ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. Pair or group students and exchange drafts; require criterion-anchored comments — one strength, one specific issue, one suggested change per criterion.
4. Have reviewees write a short response plan identifying which comments they will act on and why ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. Build in a revision cycle and a brief whole-class debrief on recurring issues ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (CPR)** (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — discipline-neutral web system in which students first calibrate against instructor-scored exemplars, then review three peer texts; widely used in chemistry and writing courses.
- **Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms** — algorithmically distribute submissions so each student reviews several peers and receives aggregated feedback, reducing single-reviewer bias.
- **Writing-across-the-curriculum workshops** — small-group read-aloud protocols in which each draft is heard and discussed against a shared rubric before revision.
