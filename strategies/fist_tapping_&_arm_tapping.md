# Fist Tapping & Arm Tapping

## Description
Fist tapping and arm tapping are multisensory spelling strategies in which learners physically tap out the syllables and sounds of words while saying and spelling them aloud. In fist tapping, learners tap a fist on the desk once per syllable while saying the word; in arm tapping, they tap from shoulder to wrist (or wrist to hand) with two fingers, one tap per sound or syllable, then "sweep" down the arm while saying the whole word. The strategy pairs a motor trace with auditory and verbal encoding, engaging multiple modalities simultaneously — the VAKT (visual-auditory-kinesthetic-tactile) tradition of Orton-Gillingham instruction.

## Design Implications

Tapping converts an abstract orthographic task into a coordinated motor-verbal sequence, giving learners a physical rhythm that segments words into pronounceable units [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. The simultaneous motor, auditory, and verbal channels support encoding through redundant pathways, consistent with dual-coding accounts of memory [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md). The strategy is most defensible as a segmentation and attention-focusing device within explicit phonics instruction, not as a standalone spelling method [Systematic phonics outperforms non-phonics approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A word list matched to taught sound-spelling patterns, not arbitrary vocabulary
- Explicit modeling of the tap-say-spell sequence before independent use ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with narration)
- Consistent tap-to-syllable (or tap-to-phoneme) mapping so the motor pattern becomes automatic
- Follow-up writing or spelling from memory so the motor routine transfers to production

#### Constraints
- Evidence for the *multisensory* component itself is weaker than for the phonics instruction it usually accompanies; motor tracing adds little once sound-spelling knowledge is established [~M]
- Tapping consumes working memory and attention; for learners who have already automatized spelling, it can slow performance and add extraneous load [~M]
- Used with whole words rather than sound-spelling patterns, it degrades into rote motor memorization with poor transfer [-M]
- Not a substitute for instruction in phonemic awareness or orthographic patterns; tapping without explicit pattern teaching yields minimal gains [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Fist tapping** — one desk tap per syllable; suited to longer multisyllabic words
- **Arm tapping** — tap from shoulder to fist per sound, then sweep down while saying the whole word; common in Orton-Gillingham and [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com) routines
- **Simultaneous vs. sequential** — some routines have learners say the whole word while tapping; others say each sound, then blend
- **Faded use** — tapping is typically withdrawn as spelling becomes automatic, consistent with [Fading](../elements/fading.md) of supports

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and spellers still building phonemic segmentation skills [+M]
- Learners with dyslexia or weak phonological processing, for whom Orton-Gillingham-based multisensory routines are widely used [~M] — benefits likely stem from the explicit, structured phonics rather than the motor component alone
- Less useful for fluent spellers, for whom the routine is redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Phonemic awareness: segmenting words into sounds
- Orthographic mapping: connecting sounds to spellings for retention
- Automatic word spelling through repeated, structured practice

### Instructions
1. Model the routine: say the word, tap it once per syllable (fist) or per sound (arm), then spell it aloud letter by letter while tapping.
2. Have learners echo the full tap-say-spell sequence chorally, then individually.
3. Repeat across several days with the same word set, then mix in previously learned words for spaced retrieval ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Ask learners to write the word from memory after tapping, then check against the model ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) of any mismatches).
5. Fade tapping once words are spelled correctly from memory in writing contexts.

## Examples
- **Orton-Gillingham lessons** — arm tapping is a standard spelling procedure in certified OG curricula such as the [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com) and [SPIRE](https://www.schoolspecialty.com/spire).
- **Tier 2 small-group intervention** — a reading specialist has students fist-tap each syllable of multisyllabic words (e.g., "con-stant") before spelling them on whiteboards.
