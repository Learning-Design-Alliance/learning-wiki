# Word Processing for Writing Skills

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Word processing as a writing strategy has learners compose, share, review, and revise text in an editable digital environment such as Google Docs or Microsoft Word. Because the document is a living artifact, writing becomes iterative rather than single-draft: peers and teachers can comment inline, learners can track revisions, and assistive features (text-to-speech, word prediction, voice typing) lower the mechanical barriers of transcription. The strategy treats the tool not as a typewriter but as an environment for [Practice](../elements/practice.md), feedback, and metacognitive reflection on one's own composing process.

## Design Implications

Word processing shifts writing from a product to a process: revision cost drops to near zero, which makes drafting, feedback, and redrafting cycles practical at scale. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that word processing, especially when paired with instruction on its revision features, produces moderate positive effects on writing quality relative to paper-based composing [~M]. The largest gains come not from the tool itself but from the pedagogy built on it — structured peer review and explicit revision expectations — since unstructured access to editing features does not by itself improve writing [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Reliable access to devices with word processing software and, for collaborative features, internet connectivity
- Basic keyboarding and file-management skills, or explicit instruction in them
- A structured revision/feedback routine (e.g., comment prompts, rubrics) so that peer and teacher feedback targets specific writing qualities
- Teacher modeling of how to use revision tools — track changes, comment resolution, version history — rather than assuming learners will discover them

#### Constraints
- Without reliable device or internet access, the strategy widens rather than narrows achievement gaps [-M]
- Learners with weak keyboarding skills may lose composing fluency to transcription effort, offsetting revision benefits [~M]
- Easy deletion and overwriting can erase evidence of the writing process; if version history is not used, teachers lose formative information [-W]
- Unstructured peer commenting frequently produces vague praise ("nice job") that does not improve revision quality [~M] — feedback must be scaffolded to be effective [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- For extended handwritten assessments or contexts where the final performance medium is paper, over-reliance on digital composing may not transfer [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Collaborative co-writing:** shared documents where small groups compose together, making planning and revision decisions visible
- **Assistive configuration:** word prediction, text-to-speech, and voice typing for learners with dyslexia, dysgraphia, or emerging literacy
- **Process portfolios:** version history used as a formative record, with learners annotating what they changed and why to build metacognitive awareness
- **Blended drafting:** planning by hand, drafting and revising digitally, for learners who ideate better on paper

### Target Learners
- Developing writers in upper elementary through adult education, who benefit most from low-cost revision cycles [~M]
- Learners with dysgraphia, dyslexia, or fine-motor difficulties, for whom word prediction and speech-to-text remove transcription barriers and let composing ability show [~M]
- Reluctant writers, for whom the lower stakes of a revisable draft reduce writing avoidance [~W]
- Less beneficial for learners still struggling with basic keyboarding or letter formation, where the interface adds load [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Foundational writing skills: drafting, sentence construction, organization, and conventions
- Revision and editing as distinct composing behaviors, supported by visible change tracking
- Metacognition about writing: evaluating one's own text against criteria and planning revisions
- Peer collaboration and feedback literacy: giving and acting on specific, criterion-referenced comments [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Set the task and criteria.** Provide a rubric or success criteria before drafting so feedback and revision have a target.
2. **Model the process.** Demonstrate drafting, commenting, and revising in a shared document, thinking aloud about revision decisions ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
3. **Draft.** Learners compose in the word processor, using assistive features as needed ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. **Peer review.** Assign comment prompts tied to the rubric (e.g., "identify the claim and one piece of evidence") so feedback is specific rather than evaluative.
5. **Revise and reflect.** Learners act on feedback and briefly annotate their major changes, making revision decisions explicit ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
6. **Review the process.** Use version history to discuss how the text evolved, reinforcing that writing is iterative.

## Related Strategies
- Peer review and structured feedback routines — word processing provides the medium that makes iterative peer feedback logistically feasible
- Process writing workshop approaches — the word processor is the infrastructure for draft–feedback–revision cycles
- Assistive technology accommodations — text-to-speech, word prediction, and voice typing extend the same tool to learners with transcription barriers

## Examples
- **Google Docs in K–12 classrooms** ([https://edu.google.com/workspace-for-education/products/docs/](https://edu.google.com/workspace-for-education/products/docs/)) — students share drafts, comment on peers' work, and use version history to track revision progress; teachers leave inline feedback during drafting rather than after submission.
- **Clicker Docs** ([https://www.cricksoft.com/us/clicker/docs](https://www.cricksoft.com/us/clicker/docs)) — a primary-grades word processor with age-appropriate word banks, speech feedback, and audio note recording, designed for emergent and struggling writers.
- **Microsoft Word with Editor and Dictate** — used in adult and higher-education writing courses for grammar feedback and speech-to-text drafting.
