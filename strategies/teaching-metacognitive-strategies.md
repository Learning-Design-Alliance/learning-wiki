# Teaching Metacognitive Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Teaching metacognitive strategies means explicitly instructing learners in how to plan an approach to a task, monitor their comprehension and progress while working, and evaluate and adjust their strategies afterward. It is carried out through direct explanation of strategies, [demonstration of expert thinking](../elements/think-aloud.md) (e.g., think-aloud modeling), guided practice with prompts and checklists, and structured reflection routines. The goal is to make invisible cognitive processes — self-questioning, strategy selection, error detection — explicit, teachable, and eventually self-initiated.

## Design Implications

Metacognitive strategy instruction reliably improves academic achievement across domains and age groups, with the strongest effects when instruction is embedded in subject-matter tasks rather than taught as a generic stand-alone unit [de Boer et al., 2018 [+S]; Dignath & Büttner, 2008 [+S]]. Effective programs combine three components: explicit strategy knowledge (what the strategy is and why it works), procedural training (how to execute it), and conditional knowledge (when and why to deploy it) — omitting the conditional component produces learners who possess strategies but fail to use them spontaneously [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A named, concrete repertoire of strategies (e.g., self-questioning, summarizing, planning before writing, checking work against criteria)
- Modeling of the strategies by the instructor, ideally via think-alouds that expose expert monitoring and decision-making
- Sustained practice embedded in authentic content tasks, with prompts or checklists that scaffold strategy use until it becomes self-initiated
- Opportunities for structured reflection (e.g., post-task evaluation: "What worked? What would I change?")

#### Constraints
- Generic, decontextualized "learning skills" courses show weak transfer; strategies must be practiced within domain tasks to stick [-S]
- Strategy instruction adds time and cognitive load; for novices juggling heavy intrinsic load, an additional metacognitive routine can overload working memory and depress performance [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S] — introduce one strategy at a time
- Overly rigid strategy checklists can produce mechanical application without comprehension, and can impede learners who already have effective idiosyncratic approaches [~M]
- Effects fade if scaffolds are removed abruptly; prompts must be faded gradually as learners internalize the strategies [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Embedded vs. stand-alone:** integrating strategy talk into regular lessons (reciprocal teaching in reading, "plan-monitor-evaluate" prompts in problem-based learning) versus separate study-skills modules — embedded approaches show stronger transfer
- **Prompt-based vs. modeled:** written prompts and checklists scale cheaply in online environments; live think-aloud modeling is richer but resource-intensive
- **Individual vs. collaborative:** paired or group metacognitive dialogue (e.g., reciprocal teaching roles) distributes monitoring across learners and works well for younger students

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners and low achievers benefit most, partly because they often lack strategies that higher achievers use spontaneously [Dignath & Büttner, 2008 [+S]]
- Primary and secondary students show robust gains; effects for younger children require more scaffolding and simpler strategy repertoires [~S]
- Less beneficial for learners who already use effective strategies — instruction may be redundant or disruptive [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own studying and problem solving
- Comprehension monitoring: detecting breakdowns in understanding while reading or listening
- Transfer and strategy selection: knowing *when* a strategy applies, not just *how* it works
- Long-term retention and study efficiency (e.g., replacing rereading with retrieval practice and spaced review)

### Instructions
1. **Name and explain** the strategy, including why it works — build conditional knowledge, not just procedure.
2. **Model it** with a think-aloud while working a real task, verbalizing monitoring moves ("This doesn't match what I expected — let me reread").
3. **Guide practice** with prompts, checklists, or structured roles (e.g., reciprocal teaching's predict–question–clarify–summarize) embedded in [case-based or authentic tasks](../elements/case-studies.md).
4. **Prompt reflection** after tasks: what strategy was used, how well it worked, what to try next time.
5. **Fade support** gradually — remove prompts as learners self-initiate, and revisit strategy use when task demands change.

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — small-group reading instruction where students rotate through the roles of questioning, clarifying, summarizing, and predicting; large comprehension gains in classroom studies.
- **Cognitive Apprenticeship (reading/writing workshops)** — instructors think aloud while composing or analyzing text, then coach students through the same strategies with fading support.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-tracking dashboards and hint structures prompt learners to monitor their own progress and choose when to seek help.
- **Exam wrappers** (e.g., used in physics courses at the University of Michigan) — post-exam reflection forms asking students to analyze how they prepared and what to change, feeding directly into the next study cycle.
