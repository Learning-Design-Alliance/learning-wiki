# Positive Time Out

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Positive Time Out is a non-punitive alternative to exclusionary time-out in which a learner who is emotionally dysregulated moves to a pre-established "calm-down" space — chosen or co-designed with the learner — to cool off and then rejoin the activity. Unlike punitive time-out, which removes the learner as a consequence for misbehavior, positive time-out is framed as a tool the learner *uses* to build self-regulation, ideally selected by the learner rather than imposed by the teacher [Nelsen, 2006]. It is typically paired with a brief re-entry conversation or [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) before the learner resumes work.

## Design Implications

Positive time-out treats dysregulation as a skill deficit rather than willful defiance, consistent with the view that autonomy and perceived choice sustain motivation and cooperation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on the learner having been explicitly taught calming strategies *before* the space is needed, and on the space being experienced as helpful rather than as banishment.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A designated, comfortable calming space with limited stimulation and optional self-soothing materials
- Prior instruction and rehearsal of calming strategies (breathing, counting, self-talk) so the break is more than mere removal
- Clear, taught procedures for entering and — critically — for re-entering the activity
- A teacher stance that frames the space as support, not punishment; punitive framing converts it into conventional time-out

#### Constraints
- If imposed as a consequence for misbehavior, it functions as standard time-out and loses its self-regulation benefits [-M]
- Learners in acute distress cannot use cognitive calming strategies effectively; instruction during the episode itself is largely wasted [-M]
- Overuse or open-ended "escape" access can become avoidance of demanding tasks [~M] — pair with a defined re-entry routine
- For learners whose behavior is maintained by escape from tasks, any break can inadvertently reinforce avoidance [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual calm-down corners vs. a whole-class reset routine
- Learner-initiated ("I need a break") vs. teacher-suggested ("Would a break help?")
- Physical space vs. portable alternatives (headphones, a signal card) in classrooms without spare space
- Home adaptation: parents co-design a "positive time-out" space with the child, per the Positive Discipline model

### Target Learners
- Young children and learners with developing self-regulation, including many students with ADHD or emotional/behavioral disabilities [+M]
- Learners responsive to autonomy-supportive classroom climates, where choice about the break increases buy-in [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Less appropriate as-is for learners using behavior to escape academic demands without additional supports

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing arousal and independently initiating a calming routine
- Social-emotional learning: naming emotions and selecting coping strategies
- Behavioral readiness: returning to learning tasks quickly after dysregulation

### Instructions
1. Co-design the space with learners so it signals help, not punishment; establish norms for its use.
2. Explicitly teach and rehearse calming strategies during calm moments ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md), role-play), not during incidents.
3. Teach a self-initiation signal or phrase so the learner exercises choice in taking the break.
4. Keep the break brief and time-limited; use a timer or a self-determined "ready" criterion.
5. Re-enter through a brief [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) — name the feeling, confirm the strategy used, restate the task — then return to work.
6. Follow up later with [active-listening](active-listening.md) to solve the recurring trigger, rather than treating the break as the intervention itself.

## Related Strategies
- [Active-listening](active-listening.md) — the follow-up conversation that addresses the trigger behind the dysregulation
- [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — structured re-entry and ongoing emotional monitoring that make the break instructional rather than merely a pause

## Examples
- **Positive Discipline classrooms** ([positivediscipline.com](https://www.positivediscipline.com)) — teachers and parents establish "positive time-out" spaces children help design, explicitly distinguished from punitive time-out.
- **Conscious Discipline** ([consciousdiscipline.com](https://consciousdiscipline.com)) — the "Safe Place" is a classroom calming station with taught breathing and self-regulation routines, functionally equivalent to positive time-out.
- **Responsive Classroom** ([responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)) — "take a break" spots with teacher-taught re-entry routines, used as support rather than consequence.
