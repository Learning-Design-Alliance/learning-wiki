# Student-Created Magazines

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
In a student-created magazine project, learners select topics of personal interest, locate and closely read primary sources (e.g., a *Scientific American* article on gerrymandering or fractals in Jackson Pollock's paintings), and write accessible articles summarizing and explaining the content for an audience of peers. The individual articles are compiled into a shared publication, giving the work an authentic audience and product. In Alessandra King's algebra classroom, students produced a magazine with dozens of articles on real-world applications of mathematics, from gerrymandering to invisibility cloaks.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines generative learning with authentic purpose: summarizing source material in one's own words is among the better-supported study strategies [Dunlosky et al. found practice and summarizing among the more useful techniques.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M], and producing a public artifact shifts students from passive reception to active construction [ICAP framework: constructive activity outperforms passive and active modes.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Choice of topic supports autonomy and interest, which sustains engagement and deeper processing [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S] [Interest development sustains engagement with unfamiliar material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to primary sources — databases, journals, curated reading lists — at readable levels for the age group
- Sufficient time for research, drafting, revision, and editorial collaboration
- Scaffolds for research and writing: source-evaluation checklists, summary templates, exemplar articles
- Teacher guidance on topic selection and on connecting the source content back to course concepts

#### Constraints
- Students with weak research or writing skills can stall at the source-reading stage; without explicit instruction in summarizing, they copy rather than synthesize [-M]
- The authentic-product framing can pull attention toward layout and decoration over conceptual accuracy; assessment must weight mathematical or disciplinary understanding explicitly [-M]
- Time-intensive; the research-and-write cycle can crowd out practice on core procedural skills if used as the primary instructional vehicle [~M]
- Open topic choice without teacher vetting can yield sources above students' reading level or tangential to course goals [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class single-issue magazine vs. small-group themed issues vs. individual article collections
- Print, digital (e.g., a class blog or PDF zine), or mixed-media formats
- Discipline-specific variants: science magazines, history journals, "math in the news" columns
- Scaffolded entry: teacher-provided source sets for struggling readers, open-source hunting for advanced students

### Target Learners
- Secondary students, who have enough reading fluency to tackle popular-science primary sources but benefit from structured writing support [+M]
- Students whose interest in a subject is latent; connecting course content to real-world phenomena builds situational interest that can develop into individual interest [Interest development sustains engagement with unfamiliar material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as-is for novice writers at earlier grade levels without heavy scaffolding of the summarizing and research subskills [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: connecting abstract course content (e.g., geometric series, statistics) to authentic applications
- Research and source-literacy skills: locating, evaluating, and closely reading primary sources
- Academic writing: summarizing, explaining, and writing for a lay audience
- Collaboration and editorial judgment through peer review of drafts

### Instructions
1. **Frame the project.** Present exemplar articles and the publication plan; establish the authentic audience ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).
2. **Select topics.** Students choose real-world applications of course content, with teacher vetting for relevance and readability ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
3. **Read primary sources closely.** Students annotate and extract key claims, evidence, and mathematics from their source ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. **Draft summaries.** Students write accessible articles explaining the source's content and its connection to course concepts, using summary and explanation scaffolds.
5. **Peer review and revise.** Classmates check accuracy of the disciplinary content and clarity for a lay reader; feedback should target the task and process, not just mechanics [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
6. **Assemble and publish.** Compile articles into the magazine; assess on research quality, writing clarity, and accuracy of the underlying concepts ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)).

## Related Strategies
- Jigsaw group projects — the magazine's division of topics into expert articles mirrors the jigsaw structure of distributed expertise and shared assembly
- Learning journals — both use generative writing, but the magazine adds an authentic external audience
- Case-based discussion — magazine articles can serve as entry cases for subsequent class discussion

## Examples
- **Alessandra King's algebra class** (Holton-Arms School, described in *Edutopia*): students produced a magazine of articles on real-world math — gerrymandering, fractals in Jackson Pollock's paintings, invisibility cloaks — each based on a closely read primary source such as a *Scientific American* article (https://www.edutopia.org).
- **Student science zines in secondary biology**: classes compile "current research" issues summarizing recent findings, published to the school community.
