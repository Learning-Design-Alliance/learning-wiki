# Revision And Resubmission

## Description
Revision and resubmission structures assessment so that feedback arrives *before* the grade is final: learners receive specific, actionable feedback on an initial attempt, revise, and resubmit for a new evaluation. The strategy converts feedback from a terminal verdict into an input to further learning, making the feedback–action–improvement cycle the unit of assessment rather than the single submission.

## Design Implications

Feedback only improves achievement when learners actually use it, and resubmission requirements create the structural incentive to do so [Feedback improves learning when learners act on it.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Without a resubmission opportunity, most learners read the grade and ignore the comments; the requirement to revise changes how feedback is attended to and encoded. The cycle also builds self-regulated learning: learners must interpret comments against criteria, plan changes, and evaluate their own revisions [Feedback prompts self-regulation when it addresses the task process, not just the product.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear success criteria or rubrics shared before the first attempt, so feedback references standards learners already know
- Feedback that is specific and actionable — identifying what to change and how, not just what is wrong
- Time and workload capacity for a second round of marking; without it, the cycle collapses
- A grading policy that makes revision genuinely worthwhile (e.g., grade replacement or averaging) rather than symbolic

#### Constraints
- Feedback that is vague, evaluative, or grade-focused produces little revision quality even with resubmission allowed [Feedback focused on grades rather than task improvement has weak effects.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-M]
- If the grade can only improve trivially (or not at all), learners do minimal, cosmetic revision
- Unlimited resubmission without deadlines invites procrastination and offloads learning onto the marker; cap cycles (typically one or two)
- Heavy marking load is a real constraint at scale; strategies like audio comments, whole-class generic feedback, or peer review of drafts mitigate it
- Less effective for tasks where the first attempt is already near mastery — revision yields little and can feel like busywork [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full grade replacement** — the resubmitted grade replaces the original; strongest incentive, highest marking cost
- **Draft-feedback-final** — only the final version is graded; the first attempt is formative
- **Partial revision** — learners revise only sections flagged in feedback, reducing marking load
- **Peer-mediated revision** — structured peer feedback on drafts before instructor review ([Collaborative Evaluation](../patterns/collaborative-evaluation.md))
- **Revision memos** — learners submit a short note explaining what they changed and why, making revision thinking visible

### Target Learners
- Developing writers and novice researchers, whose first drafts are reliably improvable [Strategy instruction plus feedback improves writing quality.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Learners building self-regulation, who benefit from explicit cycles of interpreting feedback and planning revisions
- Less beneficial for learners who treat grades as identity judgments; revision framing must be paired with messages that ability is malleable, or low performers may disengage [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality improvement of complex products: essays, designs, code, arguments
- Self-regulated learning: interpreting criteria, monitoring one's own work, planning improvements
- Internalization of standards: repeated criterion-referenced revision builds durable conceptions of quality

### Instructions
1. Publish the rubric or success criteria before the first attempt ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Collect the first attempt and return specific, task-focused feedback without a final grade, or with a provisional one.
3. Have learners plan revisions, optionally via a revision memo or [Action Planning](../strategies/action_planning.md).
4. Where marking load is a concern, use structured peer feedback on drafts before instructor review.
5. Collect the revision with a deadline; grade against the same criteria.
6. Close the loop with brief whole-class feedback on common patterns, or individual [5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md) for learners who struggle to act on written comments.

## Examples
- **Writing-intensive courses using portfolio drafting** — students submit drafts, receive instructor comments, and revise; only the portfolio grade counts, so every piece goes through at least one revision cycle.
- **Code review workflows** — a pull request is reviewed with line-level comments and must be revised before merging; the resubmission loop is the native workflow of professional software development.
- **[Expeditionary Learning / EL Education](https://eleducation.org)** — K-12 curriculum built around "multiple drafts" and critique protocols (e.g., "kind, specific, helpful" peer feedback) with public exhibition of revised work.
