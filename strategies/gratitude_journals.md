# Gratitude Journals

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A gratitude journal is a recurring writing activity in which learners list or elaborate on things they are thankful for, typically once or twice weekly, using prompts such as "three good things that happened and why they happened." It is carried out as a brief structured routine — usually 5–15 minutes per session over several weeks — rather than as open-ended diary writing.

## Design Implications

Gratitude journaling is one of the most consistently validated positive-psychology interventions: structured gratitude exercises reliably improve self-reported well-being relative to control conditions [Seligman's gratitude visit and three-good-things exercises produced lasting well-being gains.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. In educational settings, the mechanism is motivational and relational rather than directly cognitive: gratitude increases perceived social support and positive affect, which in turn supports engagement and persistence [Gratitude listing improved well-being and reduced somatic complaints compared with hassles- and events-listing controls.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Effects on academic achievement itself are indirect and should not be claimed.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent schedule (weekly or twice-weekly works better than daily, which shows adaptation effects)
- Structured prompts that require *elaboration* — why the good thing happened, who made it possible — not bare listing
- A private or low-stakes sharing format; mandatory public disclosure changes the activity's character
- Sufficient duration (typically 3+ weeks) before effects are measurable

#### Constraints
- Daily prompting produces hedonic adaptation; effects attenuate when the activity becomes routine [-M]
- For learners in acute distress or with depressive symptoms, forced positive reflection can backfire or feel invalidating [-W]
- Effects are strongest on self-reported well-being; claims of direct gains in grades or test performance are not supported [-W]
- Younger children need concrete scaffolding (sentence stems, drawing options) or the writing task itself dominates the experience

#### Implementation Variability
- **Three good things** (Seligman et al., 2005): write three things that went well and why
- **Gratitude letter/visit**: a one-off deep elaboration addressed to a person, read aloud to them
- **Counting kindnesses / gratitude toward teachers**: relational variants that strengthen classroom climate
- **Multimedia variants**: photo-based or audio journals for learners with writing difficulties

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults; effects in children are weaker and require concrete prompts [Gratitude interventions showed smaller and less consistent effects in early-childhood samples than in adolescents and adults.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~W]
- Learners at risk of disengagement or low belonging, where the relational focus of gratitude toward peers and teachers can support connection [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for students at risk of feeling they do not belong.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a universal daily routine; better positioned as a periodic, prompted practice

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: positive affect, well-being, and relationship awareness
- Classroom climate: increasing prosocial orientation and perceived support
- Reflective practice: building a habit of structured self-reflection (see [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) for the broader routine)

### Instructions
1. **Set the schedule and frame.** Introduce the practice briefly and authentically; avoid framing it as an assignment to be graded, since external evaluation can undermine the intrinsic benefit [Autonomy support sustains intrinsic motivation better than controlling structures.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
2. **Provide an elaboration prompt.** E.g., "Write about three things that went well this week and what caused them, including anyone who helped."
3. **Write for 5–15 minutes.** Allow private writing; offer sentence stems for learners who need them.
4. **Optionally share.** Low-stakes voluntary sharing (pair or circle) amplifies the relational effects but must remain optional.
5. **Repeat weekly for 3–6 weeks**, then taper; sustained daily use reduces effectiveness through adaptation.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Seligman et al. (2005)** field exercise: participants wrote and personally delivered a gratitude letter; the "three good things" written exercise produced increased happiness and decreased depressive symptoms at one- and three-month follow-ups.
- **Froh, Sefick & Emmons (2008)**: middle-school students (grades 6–7) who counted blessings weekly for three weeks showed higher optimism, life satisfaction, and school satisfaction than controls.
- **Character Lab "Gratitude" playbooks** (https://characterlab.org): classroom-ready gratitude routines for teachers, including the gratitude letter.
