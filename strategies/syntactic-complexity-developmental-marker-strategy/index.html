Use syntactic complexity as a sensitive developmental marker to monitor children's writing progress in the upper elementary grades¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 2 studies (2 associational), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends a specific assessment practice: "syntactic complexity could be considered an important developmental marker of written language, and thus a sensitive indicator to monitor children's progress in writing." This follows from the finding that syntactic complexity showed a clear developmental progression between fourth and sixth grade even though text length and story content did not, and despite a general lack of syntax-focused instruction in current writing curricula.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Narrative writing samples scored for mean length of a t-unit in words, following the coding scheme used in the study

Constraints¶

  • Validated in this study only for narrative picture-elicitation tasks in grades four to six; the authors note narrative writing does not cover writing-to-learn activities

Target Learners¶

  • children in the upper elementary grades (fourth to sixth grade)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • monitoring growth in written syntactic complexity

Claims¶

  • Sixth-grade syntactic complexity of narrative writing is predicted by fourth-grade oral grammar, inhibition, and planning in addition to initial syntactic complexity [+W]
  • Syntactic complexity of narrative writing increases significantly between fourth and sixth grade, while text length and story content do not [+W]
  • Transcription skills are not longitudinally predictive of syntactic complexity in the upper elementary grades, consistent with their declining influence as handwriting and spelling automatize [+W]
  • Executive functions do not directly predict syntactic complexity or story content once transcription and language skills are controlled [+W]
  • In a cross-sectional path model of fourth graders, handwriting fluency statistically mediated the relation between inhibition/updating and all narrative outcomes (text length, syntactic complexity, story content) [+W]
