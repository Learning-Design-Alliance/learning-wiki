# Direct Instruction Spelling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Direct instruction spelling teaches spelling through explicitly sequenced, teacher-led lessons: the instructor models a spelling pattern or strategy, guides choral or written practice, and immediately corrects errors. Lessons follow a fixed routine — review of previously taught words, introduction of a new pattern with [demonstration](../patterns/direct-instruction.md), guided practice, and a short test or dictation — so that attention goes to the pattern rather than to figuring out the task. The approach contrasts with incidental "catch it as you read" spelling, which leaves generalization to chance.

## Design Implications

Explicit, systematic phonics-based spelling instruction produces stronger generalization to untaught words than whole-word memorization, because learners acquire the underlying orthographic pattern rather than isolated word images [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. Instruction should group words by pattern rather than presenting arbitrary lists, since [chunking](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) words around a shared structure reduces the working-memory load of each item [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Because spelling knowledge is retention-dependent, distributed review of previously taught words outperforms massed practice on the current list [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A scope and sequence ordered from high-frequency, regular patterns to less regular ones
- A fixed lesson routine: review → new pattern → modeled spelling → guided practice → dictation or test
- Immediate error correction with the correct form re-modeled, not just marked wrong
- Cumulative review built into every lesson, not saved for end-of-unit tests

#### Constraints
- Rote memorization of unpatterned word lists yields poor transfer and rapid forgetting [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [-M]
- Whole-class pacing fails learners far above or below the group's level; the scripted uniformity of direct instruction limits responsiveness to individual errors
- Over-drilling irregular words as if they were decodable wastes instructional time; a small set of high-frequency irregular words is better taught as look-say-cover-write-check exceptions
- Correction that only flags errors ("spelling mistakes: −2") without re-modeling the correct form produces little learning [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Morphology emphasis: teaching roots, prefixes, and suffixes for upper-elementary and secondary learners, where most new words are polymorphemic
- Test-study-test vs. study-test format: pretesting words before instruction can focus attention, though it must be followed by explicit teaching of the pattern
- Digital delivery: adaptive spelling platforms (e.g., [Spelling Shed](https://www.spellingshed.com), [Nessy Spelling](https://www.nessy.com)) automate the review schedule and error logging

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and spellers in K–2 acquiring alphabetic and orthographic patterns [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]
- Struggling spellers and learners with dyslexia, who benefit most from explicit structure and cumulative review rather than incidental exposure
- Fluent readers with persistent spelling errors, for whom automatic word recognition has masked weak production-side orthography [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Orthographic pattern knowledge: generalizable spelling rules and letter-sound correspondences
- Automaticity in encoding: spelling high-frequency words without conscious effort, freeing working memory for composition
- Self-monitoring: checking and correcting one's own spelling during writing

### Instructions
1. **Review (2–3 min):** Quick oral or written dictation of 3–5 previously taught words, spaced from earlier lessons [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
2. **Introduce the pattern:** State the rule explicitly and model spelling 2–3 example words aloud, segmenting sounds and articulating choices ("I hear /k/ at the end, so I try *ck* after a short vowel")
3. **Guided practice:** Learners spell new words following the same pattern with immediate teacher feedback and correction at the word and strategy level [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]
4. **Extend to untaught words:** Dictate 1–2 words learners have never seen that follow the pattern, to check generalization rather than list memorization
5. **Apply in writing:** Learners use the words in a sentence or short composition, since spelling knowledge transfers to writing only when practiced in production contexts

## Examples
- **[Direct Instruction programs](https://www.nifdi.org)** — *Spelling Mastery* and *Morphographic Spelling* (SRA/McGraw-Hill) implement this strategy with fully scripted lessons, cumulative review, and morphographic strategies for irregular words.
- **[Spelling Shed](https://www.spellingshed.com)** — Game-based practice organized by phonics and spelling patterns, with adaptive review scheduling.
- **Tier 2 intervention blocks** — Small-group lessons following the review–model–practice–dictate routine, common in MTSS/RTI frameworks for struggling spellers.
