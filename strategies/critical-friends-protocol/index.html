Critical Friends Protocol¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The Critical Friends Protocol (CFP) is a structured peer-review process — most associated with the Annenberg Institute's National School Reform Faculty — in which a presenter shares work-in-progress and a small group of "critical friends" responds through timed, scripted steps: presentation, clarifying questions, warm and cool feedback, presenter reflection, and debrief. A facilitator enforces the norms (time limits, presenter silence during feedback, descriptive rather than evaluative language) so that critique is honest but psychologically safe. The protocol format converts informal, often vague peer feedback into disciplined public reasoning about a shared artifact.

Design Implications¶

CFP works because structure and role separation reduce the social costs of critique: the presenter must listen without defending, and responders must describe before evaluating. This aligns with evidence that feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and process rather than the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. The scripted sequence also functions as contingent scaffolding — support that is gradually released as the group internalizes the norms Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A real, authentic artifact or dilemma presented by a genuine owner who intends to act on the feedback
  • A trained facilitator who enforces timing, turn-taking, and norms
  • Defined roles: presenter, facilitator, responders
  • Explicit norms distinguishing descriptive feedback ("I noticed…") from evaluative judgment ("This is good/bad")
  • Adequate time — most protocols run 45–60 minutes; truncating steps undermines the structure

Constraints¶

  • Without facilitator skill, protocols become ritualistic; groups follow the steps without generating substantive critique [~M]
  • Presenters who are not genuinely stuck or invested treat the session as performance, not inquiry — feedback uptake drops sharply
  • Groups without established trust produce either harsh or hollow feedback; protocols do not substitute for relational groundwork [~M]
  • Repeated use with the same group and no rotation of presenters creates fatigue and diminishing returns [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Tuning Protocol — focuses on examining student work against an agreed standard or rubric
  • Consultancy Protocol — presenter brings a dilemma rather than an artifact; group offers perspectives, presenter reflects
  • Descriptive Review — longer collaborative format (originating from Prospect School) emphasizing narrative observation before interpretation
  • Peer review in coursework — the same structure scaled down for students critiquing each other's drafts, with rubrics replacing facilitator judgment

Target Learners¶

  • Adult professionals (teachers, designers, clinicians) examining practice artifacts; the protocol was designed for and is best evidenced in professional learning communities [~M]
  • Advanced students producing complex work (theses, portfolios, designs) who can give and receive structured critique
  • Less effective for novices who lack the domain knowledge to give substantive feedback — their comments stay at surface features [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving the quality of authentic work products through iterative revision
  • Developing feedback literacy: giving specific, actionable, non-evaluative critique
  • Building norms of collaborative inquiry and public reasoning about practice
  • Metacognitive reflection — the presenter's reflection step makes thinking about one's own work explicit

Instructions¶

  1. Prepare the group — establish norms (presenter silence during feedback, descriptive language, confidentiality) and assign the facilitator role.
  2. Present — the presenter shares the artifact or dilemma with framing questions (5–10 min), activating the group's purpose for looking (Activation).
  3. Clarify — responders ask neutral clarifying questions only; no evaluation yet.
  4. Warm and cool feedback — presenter steps back (physically or verbally); responders offer praise ("warm") and probing critique ("cool"), keeping feedback at the task and process level Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  5. Presenter reflection — the presenter responds to what heard, naming what they will act on; this is where the learning consolidates.
  6. Debrief — the group discusses the process itself, building collective capacity for future rounds (Check-In and open discussion work here).
  7. Iterate — the presenter revises the work and, ideally, returns with it; feedback without revision loops rarely changes practice [-M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer Review — the student-facing generalization of the same structure
  • Action Research — CFP often serves as the collaborative examination step within practitioner inquiry cycles
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — a lighter-weight dyadic alternative for individual feedback
  • Facilitate sessions with a welcoming environment, probing questions, and structured pauses
  • Use a consultancy protocol in which each leader presents a problem of practice for structured peer discovery
  • Use professional learning communities to review student work and give teachers feedback
  • Implement PAIRR through scaffolded steps: AI readings, criteria-based peer review, role-assigned chatbot prompting, and comparative reflection

Examples¶

  • National School Reform Faculty (NSRF) — the originating organization; publishes the Tuning, Consultancy, and other protocols freely at nsrfharmony.org. Widely used in school-based professional learning communities.
  • Critical Friends Groups (CFGs) in schools — small teacher groups meeting monthly to tune lessons and examine student work against standards.
  • Design studio critiques in higher education — architecture and design programs use protocol-like structures (present, clarify, critique, reflect) that predate and parallel CFP.
  • Peer review of writing drafts — composition courses adapt the warm/cool feedback distinction using rubric-anchored response sheets.
