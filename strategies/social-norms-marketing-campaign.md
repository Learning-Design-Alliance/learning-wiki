# Social Norms Marketing Campaign

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), `q3` · 0 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
A social norms marketing campaign surveys a population to establish the *actual* prevalence of a behavior or attitude, then communicates that accurate data back to the population through posters, digital media, orientation sessions, or peer leaders. It targets the gap between perceived norms ("most students binge drink") and actual norms ("most students have 0–4 drinks"), on the theory that people misjudge peer behavior and adjust their own behavior toward the (usually exaggerated) perceived norm. The approach originated in campus alcohol prevention (Perkins & Berkowitz, 1986) and has since been applied to academic engagement, bystander behavior, recycling, and equity/climate interventions.

## Design Implications

The strategy rests on the social-psychological finding that pluralistic ignorance and false consensus drive conformity: learners who overestimate how much peers engage in a risky behavior (or under-engage in a beneficial one) shift toward the misperceived norm, and correcting the misperception removes that pressure [~S]. Effects are consistently positive but typically small-to-moderate, and depend heavily on campaign credibility, message saturation, and the accuracy of the underlying data [~M]. Because the mechanism is normative belonging rather than information transfer, it pairs naturally with [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) supports and social-norms feedback delivered individually.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Valid local survey data on actual attitudes and behaviors — national statistics are less persuasive and can be inaccurate for the specific population
- Identification of the *misperception* (perceived vs. actual gap) before designing messages; a campaign without a real gap has nothing to correct
- Repeated, high-visibility message delivery across multiple channels over at least a semester
- Messages framed positively ("most students do X") rather than negatively ("don't do X"), which can inadvertently normalize the risky behavior
- Local, current data on actual behavior and attitudes within the learner's reference group (survey-based, not national averages)
- Messages framed around the *positive majority* ("most students do X"), never around the problem behavior's prevalence
- A credible, salient reference group the audience identifies with (same campus, same program, same year)
- Repetition and multiple channels; single one-off posters show weak effects

#### Constraints
- Ineffective or counterproductive when actual norms are unfavorable: publicizing that "most students skip class" reinforces the problem [~S] — the strategy only works when the true norm is healthier than the perceived one
- Fails when audiences distrust the data source or perceive messages as institutional propaganda; credibility of the messenger is a gating condition [-M]
- Single-shot or low-intensity campaigns show negligible effects; sustained multi-channel exposure is required [-M]
- Can backfire with "deviance advocacy" — highlighting even a minority behavior draws attention to it (the [focus theory of normative conduct](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2000-00509-004) warns against making undesirable behavior salient) [-W]
- Injunctive messages ("everyone thinks you should…") can trigger reactance when learners feel their autonomy is threatened [~M]
- Weak or implausible data ("98% of students always recycle") is discounted; perceived exaggeration undermines credibility [-M]
- Effects decay without reinforcement; norms must be re-communicated as cohorts change [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Population-level campaigns**: campus-wide media blitzes (the classic alcohol-prevention model, e.g., Northern Illinois University's "Most NIU students make healthy choices")
- **Personalized normative feedback**: individual reports comparing a student's own behavior to actual peer norms (e.g., electronic check-up-to-go / eCHECKUP TO GO), which shows stronger effects than mass media alone [~M]
- **Peer-delivered norms**: student leaders communicate survey results, increasing credibility
- **Academic norms variant**: communicating that "most students in this course value learning and expect to succeed," used in equity-focused course redesigns
- **Campaign posters/media**: campus-wide normative messaging (classic alcohol-education model)
- **Personalized normative feedback**: individual reports comparing one's own behavior to actual peer norms (e.g., electronic screening and brief intervention tools like [eCHECKUP TO GO](https://www.sanctionedprofessor.com/echeckup)) — stronger effects than mass media [+M]
- **Classroom adaptation**: instructors report actual class-wide study habits, attendance, or help-seeking norms rather than relying on students' inflated estimates of how much peers procrastinate
- **Injunctive + descriptive combination**: pairing what peers do with what peers approve of, where descriptive data alone is insufficient [~M]

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults in group settings (residences, cohorts, teams), where peer comparison is most salient [~S]
- New students during transition periods, when norms are still being calibrated and misperceptions are most consequential
- Members of stigmatized or minoritized groups, where correcting misperceived norms about peers' attitudes supports inclusion — related evidence that brief belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and young adults in group settings (secondary, higher education) where peer comparison is salient [+M]
- Learners whose misperceptions are large — those who believe the problematic behavior is far more common than it is
- Less effective for learners whose behavior is driven by factors other than normative perception (e.g., physiological dependence, structural barriers) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral and climate goals: reducing risky behavior (substance use, plagiarism, absenteeism) and increasing beneficial behavior (help-seeking, bystander intervention)
- Attitudinal goals: correcting pluralistic ignorance about peers' values (e.g., most students support inclusive conduct)
- Not a fit for knowledge or skill objectives — the mechanism is normative perception, not instruction
- Behavioral and attitudinal change: reducing risky health behaviors, academic dishonesty, disengagement
- Intergroup attitudes: correcting exaggerated perceptions of prejudice or exclusion among peers [building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+W]

### Instructions
1. **Measure actual norms.** Survey the target population anonymously on behaviors and attitudes; compute true prevalence rates.
2. **Measure perceived norms.** Ask the same population what they believe peers do and think; identify gaps where perception is worse than reality.
3. **Segment and prioritize.** Choose the misperceptions with the largest gap and the strongest behavioral consequence.
4. **Design positive, data-based messages.** Frame as majority behavior ("7 in 10 students protect their passwords"), attribute to a credible local source, and avoid moralizing.
5. **Deliver repeatedly across channels.** Posters, LMS banners, orientation, peer leaders, and course syllabi; sustain for a full term minimum.
6. **Pair with individual feedback where possible.** Add personalized normative feedback tools (e.g., [eCHECKUP TO GO](https://www.echeckup.com)) for higher-impact delivery.
7. **Re-survey and iterate.** Assess whether perceptions and behaviors shifted; retire messages that audiences discount.

## Related Strategies

- Peer Leader Programs — peer messengers increase perceived credibility of normative data

## Examples
- **Northern Illinois University** — the landmark social norms alcohol campaign (1990s) reported sustained reductions in heavy drinking through repeated "most students drink moderately" messaging ([http://www.socialnorms.org](http://www.socialnorms.org), archived).
- **eCHECKUP TO GO** ([https://www.echeckup.com](https://www.echeckup.com)) — web-based personalized normative feedback used at hundreds of campuses; students receive individualized comparisons to actual peer drinking norms.
- **Bystander-intervention campaigns** — programs such as [Bringing in the Bystander](https://www.unh.edu/prevention-innovations-research-center/bringing-bystander) communicate that most students would intervene against sexual violence, correcting underestimates of peer willingness to act.
- **Academic integrity campaigns** — institutions publicize survey results showing most students do *not* cheat, countering inflated perceptions of peer dishonesty.
- **Northern Illinois University social norms campaign** ([https://www.niu.edu/socialnorms/](https://www.niu.edu/socialnorms/)) — a long-running campus campaign reporting actual student drinking norms; associated with sustained reductions in perceived and actual heavy drinking [+M]
- **eCHECKUP TO GO** ([https://www.sanctionedprofessor.com/echeckup](https://www.sanctionedprofessor.com/echeckup)) — web-based personalized normative feedback for alcohol and cannabis used across hundreds of campuses
- **Classroom norm reporting** — instructors sharing survey results that "most students in this course review notes weekly" to counter students' belief that nobody else studies, supporting help-seeking and persistence

## Description
Social norms marketing (also called the social norms approach) corrects widespread misperceptions of what peers actually do and believe. Learners overestimate how many peers engage in risky or disengaged behavior (e.g., heavy drinking, skipping class, cheating) and underestimate protective behaviors; campaigns publicize accurate data — "most students here have 0–4 drinks when they party" — to close the gap between perceived and actual norms. The strategy is carried out through targeted messages delivered via posters, orientation sessions, personalized feedback, or course communications, grounded in locally collected survey data rather than generic statistics.

## Design Implications

The strategy rests on the finding that behavior is strongly regulated by perceived norms, and that these perceptions are systematically distorted [Perceived peer norms predict individual behavior and correcting them shifts behavior.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Because learners infer "what everyone does" from the most visible peers, interventions must supply credible, local, identity-relevant reference groups — a message about "students at your university" outperforms one about "people in general" [+M]. Messages that inadvertently emphasize the prevalence of the undesired behavior (e.g., "most students binge drink — don't be one of them") can backfire by normalizing it [-S].

- Repetition and multiple channels; single one-off posters show weak effects

#### Constraints
- Descriptive messages highlighting high prevalence of the undesired behavior increase it — the "boomerang effect" [Highlighting widespread undesirable behavior normalizes it and can increase its occurrence.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [-S]

### Instructions
1. **Collect local data.** Survey the actual reference group on the target behavior and attitudes; establish the true majority behavior.
2. **Identify the misperception.** Compare perceived norms to actual norms; the gap is the intervention target.
3. **Craft positive-majority messages.** Frame around what most peers *do* do ("7 in 10 students here seek help before the exam"), avoiding any statement of the undesired behavior's prevalence.
4. **Deliver through multiple channels.** Posters, orientation, LMS announcements, and — most effectively — [personalized normative feedback](../elements/feedback.md) comparing the individual to actual peer data.
5. **Pair with supporting elements.** Combine with [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) to surface actual peer behavior visibly, and [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) messaging so learners see the productive majority as their in-group.
6. **Repeat and refresh.** Re-survey and re-communicate each term; norms messaging decays without reinforcement.
