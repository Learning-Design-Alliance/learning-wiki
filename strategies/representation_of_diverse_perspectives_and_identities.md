# Representation of Diverse Perspectives and Identities

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Incorporating multiple and varying perspectives within learning content to honor and value diverse approaches to learning. This involves presenting content that reflects different cultures, identities, and ways of knowing — through examples, case protagonists, authorship, imagery, and framing — rather than defaulting to a single dominant cultural lens. The approach supports learners in making connections between concepts and their own experiences, and signals who belongs in the discipline.

## Design Implications

Representation is not decorative: who appears in examples, whose knowledge is treated as authoritative, and which contexts anchor problems all shape learners' sense of belonging and identification with the subject matter [~M]. Culturally responsive teaching links content to learners' cultural reference points and has been associated with stronger engagement and achievement for students from non-dominant backgrounds [~M]. Representation also functions cognitively: varied, contrasting cases across contexts support abstraction of underlying principles rather than binding concepts to a single surface context [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying concepts.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Audit of existing materials for whose perspectives are present, absent, or stereotyped
- Authentic, non-tokenistic examples — diverse representation woven into the substance of cases and problems, not confined to heritage months or sidebars
- A classroom climate where learners can surface their own perspectives without risk ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) with established norms)
- Instructor knowledge of learners' cultural and linguistic backgrounds ([Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md))

#### Constraints
- Superficial or stereotyped representation can do more harm than omission — it reinforces the very biases it claims to address [-M]
- Poorly facilitated discussion of identity-laden content can trigger stereotype threat, depressing performance for stigmatized learners [~S] — framing tasks as non-diagnostic of group ability mitigates this
- Adding perspectives without restructuring assessment or participation norms produces symbolic inclusion with little learning benefit [-W]
- Learners may feel exposed or uncomfortable when asked to speak for "their" group; representation of perspectives must not be delegated to individual students as spokesperson duty

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content-level:** diversify case protagonists, worked examples, historical narratives, and authorship of assigned readings
- **Pedagogy-level:** invite learners' funds of knowledge into analysis ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) drawn from multiple cultural contexts)
- **Structural-level:** adopt culturally relevant pedagogy as a whole-course design stance, including assessment and classroom discourse norms
- **Discipline-dependent:** in mathematics and science, representation often means diverse problem contexts and histories of discovery; in humanities, it means canon diversification and multiperspectival source analysis

### Target Learners
- Learners from non-dominant cultural, linguistic, or identity groups, who otherwise encounter few signals of belonging in the discipline [~M]
- All learners, who benefit from encountering multiple framings and ways of knowing when forming general concepts [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying concepts.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Learners whose sense of academic self-efficacy is fragile; identity-affirming environments support persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: abstracting principles across varied cultural and contextual instances
- Perspective-taking and empathy: understanding issues from multiple vantage points ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Critical literacy: analyzing how perspective shapes texts, data, and claims
- Belonging and identity formation within a discipline

### Instructions
1. Audit current materials: catalog whose perspectives, identities, and contexts appear in examples, cases, and readings; identify gaps and stereotypes ([Address Biases in the Use of Language and Symbols](address_biases_in_the_use_of_language_and_symbols.md))
2. Redesign core examples and cases so diverse perspectives are integral to the content, using [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) from multiple cultural contexts and contrasting framings of the same phenomenon
3. Connect new content to learners' prior experiences and cultural knowledge ([Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md))
4. Structure discussion so multiple perspectives are elicited and treated as analytically valuable, not as personal testimony ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. Assess with varied contexts and allow multiple valid framings of problems ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)), checking that performance gaps do not track representation of identity in task contexts

## Examples
- **[Facing History and Ourselves](https://www.facinghistory.org)** — history curriculum built on multiperspectival primary sources, asking students to analyze events from multiple actors' viewpoints.
- **Culturally relevant mathematics instruction** — anchoring ratio and proportion problems in contexts drawn from students' communities (e.g., [Real World Math](https://www.realworldmath.org) style tasks) rather than defaulting to a single cultural frame.
- **Open textbook remix projects** — faculty adapting open educational resources (e.g., via the [Open Education Network](https://open.umn.edu/opentextbooks)) to replace examples and imagery with locally and culturally relevant ones.
