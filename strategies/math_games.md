# Math Games

## Description
Math games are structured activities designed to stimulate enthusiasm for math learning by providing opportunities to explore fundamental number concepts, engage in strategic mathematical thinking, and deepen understanding of numbers. Games encourage exploration of number combinations, place value, patterns, and computational strategies, deepening mathematical understanding and reasoning. They also create authentic opportunities for teachers to observe or assess students and to work with individuals or small groups while the rest of the class plays independently.

## Design Implications

Well-designed math games embed [practice](../elements/practice.md) in a goal structure that motivates repeated engagement, and repetition is the mechanism through which fluency develops [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]. The strongest evidence comes from linear number board games: preschoolers from low-income backgrounds who played a linear number board game for four short sessions showed durable gains in numerical magnitude comparison, number line estimation, and counting relative to controls [Ramani & Siegler, 2008] [+S]. Games work best when the mathematical decision-making, not luck or recall speed, determines the outcome, and when play is followed by discussion that makes strategies explicit [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Games whose rules require the target mathematical reasoning (e.g., comparing magnitudes, decomposing numbers), not just arithmetic recall
- Time for repeated play across sessions — a single session rarely changes fluency or strategy use
- Teacher facilitation: pausing play for [discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), asking players to justify moves, and connecting game moves to formal notation
- Debrief structures that surface strategies, since the learning is in the reasoning, not the winning

#### Constraints
- Games used solely to drill number facts may not sustain engagement, and may generate negative feelings when recall speed determines success [-M] — timed-recall games disadvantage students with slower retrieval but sound reasoning, and speed pressure can undermine the [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) that games are meant to build
- If luck dominates skill, the game motivates without teaching; the mathematical content must drive outcomes
- Without a debrief, students may play procedurally and never connect game moves to the underlying concepts [~M]
- Poorly matched difficulty (too easy or too hard) collapses engagement; games need adjustable rules to stay in the productive struggle zone

#### Implementation Variability
- Board games with linear, numbered spaces directly scaffold number line knowledge; the linear spatial structure is the active ingredient, not the game format itself
- Card and dice games (e.g., "Closest to 100") can be tuned by changing targets, operations, or number ranges
- Digital games (e.g., [DreamBox Learning](https://www.dreambox.com), [Prodigy](https://www.prodigygame.com)) can adapt difficulty automatically, enacting [adaptive difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md)
- Whole-class vs. pair formats: pairs generate more mathematical talk; solo digital play trades discussion for volume of practice

### Target Learners
- Elementary students, especially early-numeracy learners; board game interventions show the largest effects for children from low-income backgrounds who enter with less informal number knowledge [Ramani & Siegler, 2008] [+S]
- Students who need many low-stakes repetitions to build fluency without the anxiety of timed testing
- Less effective as-is for advanced learners unless rules are modified to increase strategic depth

### Target Learning Goals
- Computational fluency through repeated, motivated practice
- Number sense: magnitude comparison, estimation, place value, and number combinations
- Strategic thinking: planning, evaluating alternatives, and justifying moves
- Mathematical discourse: articulating and comparing strategies during debriefs

### Instructions
1. Select or design a game whose win condition requires the target reasoning (e.g., comparing sums, decomposing a target number).
2. Demonstrate a round with explicit reasoning, connecting game moves to representations ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) preceded by modeling).
3. Have students play in pairs, rotating roles; circulate to observe strategies and pose questions rather than supplying answers.
4. Pause for a mid-game or post-game debrief: ask players to explain a key decision and compare strategies ([Provide feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) at the strategy level, since process-level feedback is most effective [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]).
5. Record equations or representations produced during play and use them for [assessment](../elements/assess-performance.md) and follow-up instruction.

## Examples
- **The Great Race / linear number board games** — In Ramani and Siegler's studies, preschoolers played a board game with numbered, linearly spaced squares; four 15-minute sessions produced gains in counting, magnitude comparison, and number line estimation that persisted at least nine weeks.
- **"Closest to 100"** — Students draw digit cards and place them to form two two-digit numbers whose sum should approach 100; the win condition forces place-value reasoning and estimation, and rules can be modified (target 1,000; use decimals) to adjust difficulty.
- **[NCTM Illuminations games](https://www.nctm.org/classroom-resources/illuminations/lessons/)** — Published classroom games such as "Deep Sea Duel" pair gameplay with teacher notes on observing student strategies.
