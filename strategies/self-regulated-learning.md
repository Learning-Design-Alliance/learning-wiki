# Self Regulated Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self Regulated Learning (SRL) is the process by which learners take active control of their own learning: setting goals, selecting and adapting strategies, monitoring comprehension, and adjusting effort and approach based on feedback. As an instructional strategy, it means explicitly teaching these planning, monitoring, and evaluation processes — not merely expecting them — so learners internalize a cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection [Zimmerman's cyclical model](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

## Design Implications

SRL instruction works because learners who monitor their understanding and adapt their strategies allocate effort more effectively than those who simply study harder; strategy training alone is insufficient without instruction in when and why to deploy strategies [+S]. Effective programs embed SRL prompts and modeling within subject-matter instruction rather than teaching study skills in isolation [Dignath & Büttner meta-analysis of classroom interventions](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-008-9080-x) [+S]. Because SRL depends on learner autonomy, overly controlling instructional environments suppress the very behaviors the strategy aims to build [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit modeling of planning, monitoring, and self-evaluation ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of metacognitive decisions)
- Structured prompts or templates for goal setting and self-monitoring ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), learning journals, planning sheets)
- Accurate feedback learners can use to calibrate self-assessment ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)) [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Time and tasks complex enough that regulation matters — trivial tasks give learners nothing to regulate

#### Constraints
- Novices often cannot judge their own comprehension accurately; unguided self-assessment produces miscalibration and premature termination of study [Learners frequently hold illusions of knowing when monitoring is untrained](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031313) [-S]
- Strategy instruction delivered as decontextualized "study skills" courses shows weak transfer to real coursework [~M]
- High extraneous load leaves no capacity for monitoring; learners under overload default to passive re-reading [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Over-scripted regulation checklists can displace genuine strategic decision-making, especially for advanced learners [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Embedded prompts (e.g., "What is your goal for this section?") vs. standalone metacognitive instruction
- Scripted vs. emergent: structured SRL cycles ([Experiential Learning Cycle](../patterns/experiential-learning-cycle.md)) vs. teacher-modeled regulation during authentic tasks
- Individual (self-monitoring journals) vs. socially shared regulation (collaborative planning and peer evaluation)

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through adult learners; effects of SRL training are strongest and most durable from late primary onward [+S]
- Struggling learners and low achievers benefit substantially from explicit strategy instruction [Dignath & Büttner (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-008-9080-x) [+S]
- Novices need more external scaffolding of regulation; experts already regulate adaptively and may find prompts redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive goals: accurate self-monitoring, calibration of confidence, strategy selection
- Long-term retention and transfer, where learners must choose among strategies (e.g., [Distributed Practice](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S] vs. rereading)
- Dispositional goals: academic self-efficacy, persistence, and ownership of learning

### Instructions
1. Model the full cycle aloud on an authentic task: set a goal, predict difficulty, monitor comprehension, evaluate the outcome ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. Provide planning scaffolds — goal sheets, task analysis prompts — and [Fading](../elements/fading.md) them as learners internalize the cycle [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].
3. Embed monitoring prompts during [Practice](../elements/practice.md) ("Can you explain this in your own words? What don't you understand yet?").
4. Give learners access to accurate performance data so self-evaluation can calibrate against reality ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
5. Close each cycle with structured reflection: what worked, what to change next time — feeding forward into the next forethought phase.

## Examples
- **Zimmerman & Campillo's cycle** — classroom routines in which students plan before problem solving, self-record progress, and self-evaluate afterward, applied in math and writing.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery dashboards make progress visible, supporting goal setting and self-monitoring; learners choose what to practice next.
- **[Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu)** — iterative project work with natural plan–build–test–revise cycles; teachers add reflection prompts to make regulation explicit.
- **Reciprocal teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — students alternate roles of questioning, clarifying, summarizing, and predicting, internalizing comprehension monitoring through social practice.
