# Anticipation Guides

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An anticipation guide is a series of 3–8 declarative statements — some true, some plausible-but-false — that learners respond to (typically agree/disagree) before reading or instruction. After engaging with the content, learners revisit their initial responses, discuss discrepancies, and revise their answers with justification. The strategy was developed by Readence and colleagues for content-area literacy and is widely used in science, social studies, and language arts.

## Design Implications

Anticipation guides work by activating relevant prior knowledge before new material is encountered, which improves comprehension and integration [Activating prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. They also deliberately surface misconceptions: when learners commit to an answer and then encounter contradictory evidence, the resulting cognitive conflict motivates conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. The post-reading revisit-and-revise step is essential — guides used only as a pre-activity lose most of their benefit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Statements that target the *big ideas* of the text or lesson, not trivia
- At least some statements that learners will answer incorrectly before instruction — the guide must be diagnostic, not just a preview
- A structured post-activity discussion or revision step where initial answers are compared against the text's evidence
- Sufficient prior knowledge to make initial predictions meaningful ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))

#### Constraints
- If all statements are easily answered correctly beforehand, the guide adds little and may function as mere busywork [-M]
- Learners with very low prior knowledge may guess randomly, producing no productive activation or disequilibrium [~M]
- Poorly worded or ambiguous statements can entrench misconceptions if the follow-up discussion never explicitly resolves them [-M]
- Overly long guides (10+ statements) shift attention from ideas to compliance [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Agree/disagree with probability scale** — learners rate confidence (e.g., 1–5), which sharpens the surprise when answers are contradicted
- **Think-pair-share format** — individual responses followed by partner or whole-class discussion before reading ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- **Digital polling** — tools like Mentimeter or Google Forms collect anonymous class-wide response distributions, making misconceptions visible to the whole group
- **Extended guides** — a third column asks learners to cite textual evidence for their revised answer, adding argumentation practice

### Target Learners
- Novices in a content domain who hold common misconceptions (e.g., naive physics or history myths) [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]
- Reluctant readers, who gain a purpose and personal stake in reading the text [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Less useful for learners with strong, accurate prior knowledge, for whom the guide is largely redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect ideas with disciplinary ones
- Reading comprehension: setting a purpose and monitoring for confirmation or disconfirmation
- Metacognition: committing to predictions and evaluating them against evidence

### Instructions
1. Identify 3–8 key ideas from the text, including common misconceptions, and write them as declarative statements.
2. Present the guide and have learners respond individually before any instruction ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame the activity).
3. Briefly discuss initial responses so learners commit publicly to positions.
4. Learners read or engage with the content ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md), lecture, or video).
5. Revisit the guide: learners revise answers and cite evidence for changes; resolve each statement explicitly in discussion.
6. Close by naming the misconceptions that were overturned, connecting them to the target concepts.

## Examples
- **Science misconceptions** — Before a unit on seasons, a guide includes "Summer is warmer because Earth is closer to the Sun" (false); post-reading revision forces confrontation with the tilt explanation.
- **[Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org)** — Provides templates and content-area examples of anticipation guides for K–12 literacy instruction.
- **History class** — A guide for a unit on the Great Depression includes "The stock market crash caused widespread bank failures immediately," prompting learners to read for causal sequence and revise.
