Provide structure, question-writing instruction, and feedback/revision time in game design projects¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 associational, 1 design), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Based on the finding that students wrote mostly recall questions, the author recommends that "more structure be provided with respect to teaching students how to write questions." Students need explicit instruction on writing higher-order questions and on revising knowledge-level questions upward on Bloom's taxonomy, plus teacher feedback on questions and peer revision opportunities. The author also suggests implementing the game as a unit project rather than a review tool, spreading the work across the unit so the game becomes a driving artifact in a project-based science unit.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Time in the unit schedule for question-writing instruction, teacher feedback, and revision

Constraints¶

  • Four days in a computer lab before a test left little time for instruction on question writing or feedback

Target Learners¶

  • secondary school students in science courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • writing higher-order questions
  • improved achievement on unit assessments

Claims¶

  • An earlier analysis found 94% of student game questions were Knowledge-level and none exceeded Application [+W]
  • Students creating homemade PowerPoint games in an environmental chemistry class wrote primarily knowledge-level questions [+W]
  • Students who created games twice did not write significantly more higher-order questions than students who created games once [+W]
  • Student-authored physics questions mostly require application or analysis, with under 5% at the lowest recall level [~W]
