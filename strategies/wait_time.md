# Wait Time

## Description
Wait time is the period of silence between the time a question is asked and the time when one or more learners respond to that question. It involves pausing for a minimum of three seconds after posing a question to allow learners time to process and formulate a response. It also involves waiting after getting a response to allow other learners to process the answer as well. The specific duration of wait time depends on the complexity of the question, the ability of the learners, and the clarity with which the question was asked. Recall and lower-level questions may require 1-3 seconds, calculation questions may take 4-6 seconds, and higher-order questions may require 6-10 seconds.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires conscious effort from the instructor to pause for at least three seconds after asking a question. It may also require adjusting the pace of instruction to accommodate the additional time.
#### Constraints
- Teachers may feel uncomfortable with silence or pressured to cover content quickly, making it difficult to consistently implement wait time. Students accustomed to quick-fire questions and answers may initially resist the change.

### Target Learners

- All learner levels, particularly beneficial in elementary and secondary education

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Improve the quality and length of student responses, involve more class members, elicit better quality answers, encourage students to ask their own questions

### Affordances
- Allows learners time to process information, formulate thoughtful responses, and take intellectual risks. It also provides an opportunity for both the teacher and all students to complete appropriate information processing tasks, feelings, oral responses, and actions.

### Personalization
- Teachers can adjust the wait time based on the complexity of the question, the ability of the students, and the clarity with which the question was asked. Different students may require different amounts of time to process information and formulate a response.

## Assessment Evidence
- Increased length and correctness of student responses, decreased number of no answers or "I don't know" responses, increased number of students volunteering answers, improved academic achievement test scores

## Impact
- Research by Mary Budd Rowe in 1972 and Robert Stahl in the 1990s demonstrated that wait time can lead to positive changes in student and teacher behaviors and attitudes, including improved response quality, increased participation, and enhanced academic achievement.
