Emphasize Time on Task¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Time plus energy equals learning. There is no substitute for time on task: learners need to allocate realistic amounts of time to learning and use that time effectively. This strategy, one of Chickering and Gamson's (1987) Seven Principles for Good Practice in Undergraduate Education, directs instructors and tutors to make time expectations explicit, structure sessions into purposeful segments, and teach learners to manage their own study time. How the tutor or instructor uses session time defines time expectations for the learner and establishes the basis for sustained effort.

Design Implications¶

Time on task is a core variable in classic models of school learning — Carroll (1963) defined degree of learning as the ratio of time actually spent to time needed — and engaged (not merely allocated) time predicts achievement Fredricks et al.'s review links behavioral engagement to achievement. [+M]. But raw time is not sufficient: how time is distributed matters. Spacing study across sessions produces durable retention far better than massing it Distributed practice improves long-term retention relative to cramming. [+S]. The strategy therefore has two faces: ensuring enough time is allocated, and structuring that time so it is genuinely engaged rather than passive seat time.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit communication of expected preparation and study time per session or assignment
  • Structured sessions divided into timed, purposeful segments (Check-In at segment boundaries)
  • A mechanism for monitoring completion (Assessment or progress checks)
  • Referral pathways for learners with persistent time-management difficulties (e.g., a learning strategist or academic skills center)

Constraints¶

  • More time does not compensate for poor instructional quality or unengaging tasks; low-quality time yields low-quality learning [-M]
  • Rigid timed segments can fragment deep work; complex tasks need longer uninterrupted blocks [~M]
  • Emphasizing time alone can promote surface strategies (logging hours rather than learning); pairing with Active Learning mitigates this [-M]
  • Externally imposed time structures can undermine autonomy for self-regulated learners [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Tutoring contexts: divide sessions into timed segments (warm-up, focused work, consolidation) and set explicit preparation expectations
  • Course design: estimate and publish expected hours per credit/assignment; use pacing schedules or milestone deadlines
  • Self-paced/online: embed progress indicators and recommended weekly time budgets; Flipped Classroom structures reallocate contact time to active work
  • Remediation: refer to time-management coaching rather than attempting to teach study skills within content sessions

Target Learners¶

  • Novice learners and tutees in higher education or adult learning programs who have not yet developed self-management routines
  • Learners who underestimate the time required for mastery and overestimate their preparation
  • Less necessary for highly self-regulated learners, who already allocate time effectively [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Study habits and self-regulation: establishing realistic pacing and steady application
  • Procedural and skill goals that require accumulated practice volume
  • Retention of foundational knowledge, where spaced regular work outperforms cramming Distributed practice improves long-term retention relative to cramming. [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. State time expectations explicitly — communicate how much preparation each session or assignment requires, and expect completion on schedule.
  2. Structure session time — divide meetings into timed segments with distinct purposes, opening with a brief Check-In to set the agenda.
  3. Prioritize engaged time — fill segments with Practice and Active Learning activities rather than passive review.
  4. Coach scheduling — underscore regular work, steady application, self-pacing, and spacing of study over time Distributed practice improves long-term retention relative to cramming. [+S].
  5. Monitor and follow up — track assignment completion; refer learners with persistent difficulties to a learning strategist or skills center.

Related Strategies¶

  • Pacing and milestone scheduling — operationalizes time expectations across a course
  • Study-skills coaching — the referral destination when time-management deficits persist
  • Spaced retrieval scheduling — determines how allocated time should be distributed for retention
  • Schedule protected release and preparation time for educators completing pre- and between-session toolkit work

Examples¶

  • A writing center tutor opens each session by stating the session plan in timed segments (10 min diagnosis, 25 min focused revision, 10 min planning next steps) and tells the tutee exactly how long to spend drafting before the next meeting.
  • Carnegie Mellon's Open Learning Initiative courses display estimated time-on-task per unit and log actual time spent, letting learners compare their engagement against design assumptions.
  • A flipped course publishes a weekly time budget (e.g., 90 min video/preparation, 150 min in-class active work) so learners can plan steady, spaced study instead of pre-exam cramming.
