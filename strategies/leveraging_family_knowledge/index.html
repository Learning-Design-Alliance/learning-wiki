Leveraging Family Knowledge¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 review), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Leveraging family knowledge means treating students' households and communities as sources of academic content rather than deficits to be remediated. Teachers identify the knowledge, practices, and skills that exist in students' families — farming, cooking, mechanics, storytelling, multilingualism — and build instruction on that foundation. The approach originates in the funds of knowledge research tradition (Moll et al., 1992), in which teachers conducted household visits and then designed lessons that connected classroom tasks to the competencies they documented.

Design Implications¶

Connecting instruction to students' lived experience raises the perceived value of school tasks, which supports motivation and engagement Connecting content to students' lives increases task value, motivation, and engagement. [+M]. The strategy reframes the teacher's role from content deliverer to ethnographer and curriculum designer: learning about students' families is a prerequisite step, not an afterthought. It also shifts classroom discourse so that students occupy positions of expertise, which redistributes participation and authority and aligns with situated learning — knowledge is learned in and connected to the contexts of its use.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Time and structures for learning about students' families: household visits, family surveys, community walks, or home-language inventories
  • Flexible curriculum with room for student-contributed content and multiple ways of demonstrating knowledge
  • Willingness on the teacher's part to treat family practices as intellectually legitimate, not merely as decorative "culture day" material
  • Sensitivity to families who prefer privacy or whose circumstances make participation burdensome

Constraints¶

  • Asking students to speak for their family or culture can put them "on the spot," especially for students navigating bicultural identities or family circumstances they do not wish to disclose [-W]
  • Superficial implementation — one-off heritage celebrations disconnected from assessment — risks tokenism and stereotype reinforcement rather than deeper learning [-W]
  • Teachers' assumptions about what a family "must" know can reproduce the same deficit views the strategy is meant to counter; documented knowledge, not assumed knowledge, should drive lesson design [-W]
  • Requires sustained effort and institutional support; without it, the practice tends to collapse into occasional anecdotes [-W]
  • If family-derived contexts are poorly matched to the target concept, the extra contextual detail can distract from the mathematics or science being taught rather than support it [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Household ethnography (Moll model): teachers visit homes, document funds of knowledge, and design case studies and lessons around them
  • Student-as-expert formats: students interview family members, then teach the class a skill or practice, using class discussion and demonstration structures
  • Curriculum infusion: existing units are recontextualized — e.g., ratio problems built from family recipes, narrative writing from oral family histories
  • Family as audience: student work is shared back with families through exhibitions and publications, connecting to Authentic Audiences & Purposes

Target Learners¶

  • Students from culturally and linguistically diverse, immigrant, rural, and working-class communities, whose household knowledge is most often invisible in standard curricula [+W]
  • Students whose school engagement is low; connecting tasks to family knowledge raises task value and participation Connecting content to students' lives increases task value, motivation, and engagement. [+M]
  • Multilingual learners, whose home language becomes a resource through activation of prior knowledge rather than an obstacle

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Engagement and motivation: making academic tasks personally meaningful Connecting content to students' lives increases task value, motivation, and engagement. [+M]
  • Conceptual understanding: anchoring abstract content in familiar practices, consistent with activation of prior knowledge
  • Cultural competence and empathy: students encounter peers' communities as knowledge-rich, supporting Building Empathy
  • Identity development: students see their backgrounds as academically valuable

Instructions¶

  1. Learn about families first. Use surveys, home-visit programs, or informal conversations to document household practices and skills before designing lessons; avoid relying on assumptions.
  2. Identify connections to content. Map documented funds of knowledge onto upcoming units, using analogies to bridge family practices and academic concepts.
  3. Activate prior knowledge. Open lessons by eliciting relevant family experience through activating prior knowledge routines, so students enter the task from competence rather than deficit.
  4. Position students as experts. Design tasks — interviews, demonstrations, case studies — in which students teach family-derived knowledge to peers, with the teacher providing coaching on academic framing.
  5. Share and reflect. Use class discussion and Collaborative Learning structures to compare practices across families, then return finished work to families as authentic audiences.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the general cognitive routine this strategy grounds in students' specific family and community experience
  • Accessing Students' Background Knowledge — the broader practice of eliciting what students bring to instruction
  • Structured reflection exercise for educators to situate their family engagement practice within the three engagement approaches
  • Student-designed community walks that flip teacher and learner roles to build cultural humility

Examples¶

  • Moll et al.'s funds of knowledge project (Tucson, AZ): teachers conducted household visits among Mexican-American families and built modules on construction, farming, and economics that fed directly into math and social studies instruction (Moll, Amanti, Neff, & González, 1992)
  • Family Storytelling / oral history units: students interview elders, transcribe narratives, and analyze them as primary sources in history or as mentor texts in writing
  • Culturally responsive mathematics: lessons using family recipes, remittances, or household budgeting as contexts for fractions, ratios, and algebraic reasoning
