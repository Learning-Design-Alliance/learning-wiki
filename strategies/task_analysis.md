# Task Analysis

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Task analysis involves decomposing a complex assignment — most commonly a writing task — into smaller, manageable sub-tasks with clear success criteria for each. Learners read or hear the instructions aloud, restate the task in their own words, and jointly identify and highlight the key requirements before beginning work. The result is a shared, explicit map of what the assignment demands, in what order, and to what standard.

## Design Implications

Task analysis reduces extraneous cognitive load by externalizing the structure of a complex task, freeing working memory for the substantive work rather than for parsing ambiguous instructions [Breaking complex material into manageable parts reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Its signature move is comprehension checking: having learners paraphrase the task and mark its key parts converts passive receipt of instructions into active processing, surfacing misunderstandings before they become wasted effort [Advance organizers improve learning by clarifying task structure in advance.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A complete, accurate analysis of the target task by the instructor — you cannot scaffold what you have not decomposed yourself
- A mechanism for learners to restate the task in their own words ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)), so misconceptions surface early
- Highlighting or annotation of the key requirements in the prompt ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- A visible sequence of sub-tasks learners can check off as they progress

#### Constraints
- Time-consuming; the analysis itself consumes instructional time that competes with content work [~M]
- Over-decomposition can fragment a holistic task (e.g., writing as voice and argument) into mechanical steps, harming quality and motivation [~M]
- Effectiveness depends on learner engagement; a checklist completed without paraphrasing catches nothing [-M]
- Less valuable when the task is simple or the instructions are already transparent — analysis becomes redundant busywork [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-led:** teacher models the decomposition, learners follow — appropriate for novices
- **Collaborative:** pairs or groups analyze the prompt together and compare decompositions
- **Learner-generated:** students produce their own sub-task plan, building transferable planning skill; fade instructor scaffolding over time
- **Domain-general:** the same procedure applies to lab reports, projects, and multi-step problems, not only writing

### Target Learners
- Students with learning and thinking differences who benefit from explicit structure and reduced instruction-processing demands [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Novices facing a genre or task type for the first time
- Less necessary for experienced learners, who can plan independently and may find step-by-step decomposition constraining [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Task comprehension: knowing exactly what the assignment asks
- Procedural planning: sequencing sub-goals toward a finished product
- Self-regulation: monitoring progress against an explicit plan [Self-regulated learning improves academic outcomes.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Present the assignment and state its purpose and final product ([State Objectives](../elements/advance-organizers.md) as an advance organizer for the whole task)
2. Read the instructions aloud together; have learners paraphrase the task in their own words ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
3. Highlight or underline the key requirements in the prompt ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
4. Decompose the task into ordered sub-tasks, chunked so no single step overloads working memory ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
5. Have learners check off sub-tasks as they complete them and compare their work to the stated criteria ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

## Examples
- **Special education writing instruction (e.g., Understood.org guidance):** teacher and student read a writing prompt aloud, underline the directive verbs ("compare," "explain"), and break the essay into prewriting, drafting, and revising sub-tasks with individual due dates. See [https://www.understood.org](https://www.understood.org)
- **SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development):** students analyze a genre task, set sub-goals, and self-monitor against a graphic organizer before writing — a validated writing intervention for grades 2–12
- **4C/ID-based multimedia courses:** whole complex tasks are analyzed into task classes of increasing complexity, with each class scaffolding one additional skill component
