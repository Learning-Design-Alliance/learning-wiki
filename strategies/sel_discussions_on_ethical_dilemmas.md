# SEL Discussions on Ethical Dilemmas

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy involves using age-appropriate, real-life ethical dilemmas as discussion starters to help students practice responsible decision-making. Students identify the problem, analyze the situation from multiple perspectives, propose and defend solutions, and evaluate outcomes against ethical principles — all within a facilitated, low-stakes discussion format. It is typically integrated into social studies, language arts, or advisory periods.

## Design Implications

Structured dilemma discussions work because they require learners to articulate and defend reasoning rather than merely state opinions, activating the social-cognitive processes that moral development research identifies as central to ethical judgment growth [~M]. Well-designed discussions create [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — students encounter perspectives that conflict with their initial intuitions, which prompts deeper reasoning and perspective-taking [Building empathy through perspective-taking activities improves social awareness.](../principles/building-empathy.md) [+M]. The facilitator's role is critical: open-ended questioning and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) produce better reasoning gains than lecture-style moralizing, which students tend to reject as preachy [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of age-appropriate, culturally relevant dilemmas with genuine (not rhetorical) tension between competing values
- A facilitator skilled in open questioning, [Active Listening](active_listening.md), and withholding premature judgment
- Discussion norms that make disagreement safe — ground rules, turn-taking structures, or [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)
- A closure step where students articulate a reasoned position ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)), not just a vote

#### Constraints
- Dilemmas that are fake or trivially resolvable ("should you copy homework?") collapse into one-right-answer recitation and produce no reasoning practice [-M]
- Unfacilitated or teacher-dominated discussions devolve into opinion-swapping; without prompts to justify and evaluate reasons, students rarely advance beyond their initial stance [-M]
- Students with strong prior emotional investment in a dilemma may defend positions defensively rather than reason about them; personalizing dilemmas too close to live conflicts in the room backfires [~W]
- Discussion alone does not build the self-regulation component of SEL — decision-making practiced verbally may not transfer to behavior without follow-up application [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured formats:** [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) or [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) force perspective-taking by requiring students to argue a side they did not choose
- **Written pre-work:** brief individual reasoning before discussion raises the quality of contributions and gives quieter students an entry point
- **Written follow-up:** reflective writing or [Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) after discussion consolidates the reasoning practiced orally
- **Embedded dilemmas:** dilemmas drawn from current literature, history, or science content (e.g., stem-cell policy in biology) integrate SEL with academic goals rather than competing for time

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through high school students; dilemmas must be developmentally scaled (concrete fairness conflicts for younger students, multi-stakeholder social dilemmas for adolescents) [~M]
- Adolescents in particular benefit, as perspective-taking capacity and identity formation make dilemma discussion developmentally well-timed [+W]
- Students with strong verbal skills and classroom trust; students new to a class or with limited discussion experience need scaffolds and norms first

### Target Learning Goals
- Responsible decision-making: analyzing situations, weighing consequences, and evaluating options against ethical principles
- Ethical and moral reasoning: constructing and critiquing arguments grounded in values
- Social awareness and empathy: understanding how the same situation looks from different stakeholders' positions
- Discussion and argumentation skills transferable to academic contexts

### Instructions
1. Select or write a dilemma with genuine tension between two defensible values, matched to students' age and experience ([Case Study](../elements/case-study.md))
2. Establish discussion norms and activate relevant background knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
3. Have students individually identify the core problem and their initial position ([Challenge Identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md), [Decision-Making](../elements/decision-making.md))
4. Facilitate structured discussion with open prompts ("What would you do and why?", "Who else is affected?"), using [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) deliberately when consensus comes too easily
5. Optionally assign students to argue positions they did not choose ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md), [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
6. Close with individual commitment: students state a reasoned decision and one thing that would change their mind ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) → [Act](../elements/act.md))

## Examples
- **[CASEL](https://casel.org)** — the leading SEL framework organization; its responsible decision-making competency is the target of dilemma-based discussion curricula, and its program guides list dilemma discussion among common middle/high school practices
- **Facing History and Ourselves** — published curriculum using historical case dilemmas (e.g., bystander decisions during the Holocaust) followed by structured discussion and reflection; widely implemented in secondary schools
- **Advisory-period dilemma circles** — a common middle school format where a weekly dilemma (e.g., a friendship loyalty conflict) is discussed in a 20-minute circle with talking-piece norms
