Emotion-Focused Pretend Play¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal), q3 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

The teacher or caregiver enters children's pretend play in the dramatic play area and, as a co-player, starts conversations about how the imaginary characters feel — "Why is your baby crying? What will make her happy?" Children are helped to give their characters emotions, act those emotions out, and respond to the emotions of others' characters. The adult's role is that of a coaching co-player who scaffolds emotion vocabulary and perspective-taking within the play itself, rather than directing it.

Design Implications¶

Pretend play provides a psychologically safe, low-stakes context in which children can rehearse emotional experiences at a distance from real consequences, and adult-guided emotion talk during play is associated with gains in emotion knowledge and theory of mind Goldstein & Winner found guided pretend play improved emotion understanding and empathy in young children. [+M]. The strategy enacts Building Empathy by having children adopt and articulate perspectives other than their own, and it works best when the adult follows the children's play agenda rather than hijacking it [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A dedicated pretend play space with props (dolls, costumes, puppets, play kitchen) that invite role enactment
  • An adult who can enter the play as a character and model emotion language in role ("My dog ran away and I feel so sad!")
  • Time — sustained play episodes of 20–40 minutes, not brief transitions
  • A rich emotion vocabulary introduced through Check-Ins and storybook talk so children have words to use in play

Constraints¶

  • Children who have not developed symbolic play skills may not know how to pretend; they need modeling of play scripts before emotion focus can land [-W]
  • Adult over-direction turns play into a lesson, reducing children's engagement and the self-regulatory benefits of play [~M] — the emotion talk must ride on the children's scenario, not replace it
  • Benefits are weaker when play is purely physical or constructional with no role or character element [-W]
  • Requires small groups; emotion talk is rare in large, chaotic pretend play settings [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Puppet-mediated: adult uses a puppet character to ask about feelings, useful for shy children
  • Emotion-provocation: adult introduces a scenario event ("Oh no, the dragon is scared of the storm!") and lets children resolve it
  • Peer coaching: older or more skilled children are paired with younger ones
  • Home extension: families are coached to use the same emotion talk in play at home

Target Learners¶

  • Preschool and early elementary children (roughly ages 3–8), when symbolic play and emotion understanding are developing rapidly [+M]
  • Children with limited emotion vocabulary or delayed theory-of-mind skills, including many children with language delays Dialogic, responsive adult talk improves children's expressive language. [+M]
  • Children who struggle to identify or discuss their own emotions in direct conversation — the fictional frame lowers the personal stakes

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotion knowledge: identifying and labeling feelings in self and others
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: imagining how a character feels and why Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
  • Emotional expression and regulation: enacting, exaggerating, and resolving emotions in a safe context
  • Social narrative skills: sustaining a shared pretend scenario with peers

Instructions¶

  1. Observe the children's play and identify the scenario and roles before entering.
  2. Enter as a character, not as a teacher — ask "Can I be the customer?" rather than directing.
  3. Introduce emotion talk in role: ask characters how they feel, why, and what would help (Act It Out).
  4. Model emotional expression by giving your own character a feeling and acting it out with face, voice, and body.
  5. Extend and connect: prompt children to respond to each other's characters' emotions ("Your friend is sad — what can we do?").
  6. Fade your participation as children begin generating emotion talk themselves (Fading of adult scaffolding).
  7. Debrief briefly after play, connecting character feelings to real experiences during a Check-In.

Related Strategies¶

  • Acting-Role-Play — the broader family of role enactment; this strategy adds a deliberate emotion focus
  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a structured warm-up that builds the emotion vocabulary pretend play then uses
  • Act It Out — physical enactment of emotions that can feed into character play

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the adult's in-role prompting is a form of situated coaching
  • Act It Out — enacting the character's emotion is the core move
  • Check-In — before and after play, surfaces and consolidates emotion language
  • Practice — repeated play episodes provide distributed practice in emotion recognition and expression

Examples¶

  • Tools of the Mind (Vygotskian early childhood curriculum) — children plan their pretend roles in advance ("make a play plan"), and teachers scaffold sustained dramatic play; emotion talk is embedded in role maintenance. https://toolsofthemind.org
  • Head Start social-emotional curricula — teachers join dramatic play centers and coach feeling words as part of programs such as PATHS (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies), which pairs direct emotion lessons with play application. https://www.pathseducation.com
  • Goldstein & Winner's acting studies — children assigned to guided pretend play and acting conditions showed improved understanding of others' emotions compared with visual arts controls.
