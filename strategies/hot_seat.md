# Hot Seat

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
One learner assumes the role of a book character, significant figure in history, or concept (such as a tornado, an animal, or the Titanic). Sitting in front of the rest of the class, the learner responds to classmates' questions while staying in character. Variations include small-group hot seats (one "character" per group of four or five), and panel formats in which several learners play different characters and interact like guests on a talk show.

## Design Implications

Hot seat is a form of structured role-play that converts passive content review into generative retrieval: the learner in the seat must organize and articulate knowledge from within the role's perspective, while questioners must formulate questions that probe the material [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The question-and-answer structure adds accountability — the seated learner cannot succeed without genuine command of the content, and questioners cannot ask good questions without it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A topic or character rich enough to sustain questioning (a character with motives, a figure with decisions, a concept with causal structure)
- Preparation time for the seated learner to research and rehearse the role
- Clear norms for respectful, on-topic questioning; the facilitator models question types early ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- A physical or virtual arrangement where the seated learner is visible and audible to questioners

#### Constraints
- High social exposure can disadvantage shy or anxious learners; forcing participation without preparation or opt-in structures reduces both comfort and learning quality [~M]
- Without content preparation, the activity becomes improvisational entertainment rather than retrieval — surface-level answers go uncorrected
- Risk of stereotyping when portraying historical or cultural figures; requires facilitation to keep portrayals respectful and evidence-based
- Whole-class format gives one learner the floor while many remain passive; small-group or panel variants mitigate this

#### Implementation Variability
- **Panel format:** several learners play different characters and respond to one another, surfacing contrasting perspectives ([Debate](debate.md))
- **Small-group hot seats:** parallel sessions increase per-learner talk time and participation
- **Concept hot seat:** the learner embodies a non-human entity (a volcano, a cell, a supply chain), which is especially useful in science for externalizing causal reasoning
- **Reverse hot seat:** the audience is in role and the seated learner (teacher or expert guest) is questioned as an interviewer

### Target Learners
- Learners who benefit from articulating knowledge verbally rather than only writing it [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Adolescents and adults studying literature, history, or social science, where perspective-taking is central to the content; empathy-oriented role-taking can improve intergroup attitudes when structured around understanding another's perspective [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Reluctant participants need preparation time, choice of role, or small-group formats; avoid cold-calling unprepared learners into the seat

### Target Learning Goals
- Perspective-taking: reasoning from within a character's or figure's viewpoint and constraints
- Active recall and application: retrieving, organizing, and defending knowledge under questioning ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Communication skills: formulating and answering questions in real time
- Conceptual understanding: embodying a system or concept to explain its behavior

### Instructions
1. Select a character, figure, or concept with enough depth for sustained questioning; ensure all learners have the background knowledge needed to question well.
2. Prepare the seated learner(s) with research time and guiding questions; allow notes if retrieval demands are too high initially ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — fade notes as fluency grows).
3. Seat the learner before the class or group; open with facilitator-modeled questions, then hand questioning to peers ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. Debrief out of role: ask the seated learner what was hard to answer, and ask questioners what evidence was missing — this converts performance into reflection ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. Rotate roles across sessions or use small-group/panel variants so many learners experience both roles.

## Examples
- **History:** a learner sits as a figure from a unit on the American Revolution (e.g., a loyalist merchant), fielding questions from classmates about motives and decisions, then debriefs on which questions lacked documentary support.
- **Literature:** after reading a novel, small groups each hot-seat a different character, then panels compare how each character justified the same event.
- **Science:** a learner embodies the Titanic's hull design and must answer engineering questions about why it sank, externalizing causal reasoning about buoyancy and materials.
- **Mantle of the Expert** (Heathcote & Bolton, 1995): a whole-class drama approach in which learners operate as in-role experts questioned by the teacher — hot seat at curriculum scale.
