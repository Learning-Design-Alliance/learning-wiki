# Weekly Student Reflection

## Description
Weekly student reflection is a recurring, low-stakes routine in which students spend 10–15 minutes responding to structured prompts about what they learned, how they learned it, where they struggled, and what they will do next. Prompts are typically short written responses — journals, exit tickets, or LMS forms — that cycle through phases of self-monitoring (What did I do?), self-evaluation (How well did it work?), and strategic planning (What will I change?). When instructors read and respond, the routine doubles as a formative-assessment channel and a vehicle for teacher–student dialogue.

## Design Implications

Weekly reflection operationalizes the self-regulation cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection, giving students recurring practice at monitoring their own understanding and adjusting strategies [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on prompt structure: generic prompts ("How was your week?") produce generic answers, while prompts that require students to connect strategies to outcomes produce measurable gains in self-regulated behavior and achievement [+M]. Because reflection surfaces misconceptions and confusion early, it also functions as [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — but only if the instructor closes the loop by acting on what students report.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A protected, predictable time slot each week; reflection that is "when there's time" gets skipped
- Structured prompts that target specific self-regulation phases (monitoring, evaluation, planning) rather than open-ended venting
- A low-stakes grading stance (completion-based or ungraded) so students write honestly rather than performatively
- An instructor response mechanism — even brief written replies — so students see that reflection feeds instruction [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Unstructured or repetitive prompts quickly degrade into ritual compliance; students write what they think the teacher wants [-M]
- Reflection without follow-through (instructor never reads or acts on it) teaches students that the routine is performative [-M]
- For students with weak metacognitive vocabulary, reflection prompts can exceed their ability to articulate learning states; scaffolds like sentence starters or [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) with selectable options lower this barrier [~M]
- Weekly cadence may be too infrequent to catch confusion in fast-paced courses; pairing with per-session [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) compensates [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Journal format:** running document students revisit, enabling comparison of earlier and later reflections
- **Exit-ticket format:** brief end-of-week form (Google Forms, LMS quiz) aggregated by the instructor
- **Dialogue format:** reflection responses seed brief individual conferences or written exchanges
- **Portfolio format:** reflections attached to artifacts of work, connecting self-evaluation to evidence

### Target Learners
- Adolescents through adults; younger students need heavily scaffolded prompts and oral alternatives
- Students developing self-regulation benefit most from the recurring monitor–evaluate–plan cycle [+M]
- Struggling students gain a private channel to surface confusion they would not raise publicly
- High-achieving students may find routine reflection redundant once self-monitoring is habitual [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition: accurate self-assessment of understanding and strategy use
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and adjusting learning behavior
- Transfer of responsibility: gradually shifting ownership of learning from instructor to student [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Instructor-side goals: diagnosing misconceptions and calibrating pacing

### Instructions
1. **Choose a prompt cycle.** Rotate prompts across self-regulation phases, e.g., "What was the muddiest point this week?", "What strategy did you use, and did it work?", "What is one thing you will do differently next week?"
2. **Model reflection first.** Demonstrate a think-aloud reflection using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) so students see what substantive reflection sounds like rather than one-line answers.
3. **Protect the time.** Schedule 10–15 minutes at a fixed point (end of last class, Friday opening, weekend LMS task).
4. **Keep it low-stakes.** Grade on completion or not at all; honesty requires safety.
5. **Respond and act.** Read responses, reply briefly at the [task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md), and visibly adjust instruction — reteaching a muddy concept, for example — so students see reflection has consequences.
6. **Fade the scaffolds.** Move from provided prompts to student-generated reflection questions as self-monitoring becomes habitual.

## Related Strategies
- [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) — the shorter, higher-frequency cousin; weekly reflection aggregates what daily check-ins sample
- [Exit Tickets](../elements/assessment.md) — a single-session version focused on content rather than self-regulation
- [Learning Journals](../principles/annotating.md) — a longer-format variant integrating reflection with course artifacts

## Examples
- **"Muddiest Point" weekly forms** — a classic Classroom Assessment Technique (Angelo & Cross): students name the least-clear idea of the week; the instructor opens the next class by addressing the most common muddles.
- **Tanner (2012) metacognitive prompts** — published sets of prompts for biology courses ("What was the one most useful thing you learned?"), used as end-of-class or weekly reflections in large enrollment courses.
- **Portfolio-based reflection in writing programs** — first-year composition courses commonly require weekly process memos attached to drafts, connecting reflection to revision decisions.
