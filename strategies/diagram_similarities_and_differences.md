# Diagram Similarities and Differences

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners identify two or more words, concepts, or cases, generate a list of similarities and differences, and represent those relationships in a Venn diagram or comparison matrix. The strategy is one of Robert Marzano's high-yield instructional strategies: identifying similarities and differences is the single largest-effect category in his synthesis of instructional research [Marzano, R. J. (1998) meta-analytic synthesis] [+S]. It is a structured form of [Analogical Reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) in which learners must analyze both items deeply enough to make justified comparisons.

## Design Implications

Comparison forces elaborative processing: to state how two items differ, learners must retrieve the defining features of each and evaluate them against one another, which produces more durable encoding than studying either item alone [Schwartz & Bransford contrasting cases prepare learners for instruction] [+S]. The diagram format adds a spatial-visual representation of the same relationships, supporting dual coding [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]. Critically, the *learner-generated* list matters more than the completed diagram — presenting a filled-in Venn diagram removes the analytical work that produces the benefit [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Two or more items that share enough structure to make comparison meaningful (not arbitrary pairs)
- A graphic organizer — Venn diagram, comparison matrix, or T-chart — provided as a scaffold
- Sufficient background knowledge of both items; pre-teach or allow resource access before comparing
- A follow-up step where learners use the comparison (write a summary sentence, classify a new example) so the analysis is applied ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners with limited background knowledge of either item produce superficial or invented comparisons [~M] — activate prior knowledge first ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
- Comparing items with no shared structure (e.g., a word and a random word) yields busywork rather than analysis [-M]
- Overly open prompts ("compare these two chapters") without a dimension to compare along can overload working memory [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M] — specify the comparison dimensions or provide a matrix with labeled rows
- If the teacher completes the diagram, the strategy reduces to note-taking and loses most of its effect [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher-provided vs. learner-generated lists**: begin with a provided list for novices, then fade to generation ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))
- **Item type**: vocabulary words, historical figures, scientific processes, literary characters, solution methods
- **Organizer type**: Venn diagram for overlapping categories; comparison matrix for multiple dimensions; analogy frames ("X is to Y as ___ is to Z") for relational comparison
- **Social format**: individual reflection followed by pair comparison of lists ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) → [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who know both items at least at a surface level; novices need prior activation or resources first [~M]
- Language learners building disciplinary vocabulary — comparison of near-synonyms (e.g., *observe* vs. *infer*) sharpens word meaning [+M]
- Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge to generate genuine similarities [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual discrimination: distinguishing confusable terms or categories
- Vocabulary depth and disciplinary literacy
- Relational and analogical reasoning: mapping structure from a familiar item to an unfamiliar one
- Preparation for classification and categorization tasks

### Instructions
1. Select two (or three) items with genuine shared structure and display them.
2. Activate knowledge of each item briefly — definition, example, or quick recall prompt ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
3. Learners individually list similarities and differences ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)).
4. Learners transfer the list onto a Venn diagram or comparison matrix, placing shared features in the overlap.
5. Pairs or the class compare lists, resolving disagreements — the negotiation is where discrimination is refined ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
6. Learners apply the result: write a summary sentence, classify a new example, or predict behavior of one item based on the other ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)).

## Examples
- **Vocabulary (ELA/Science)**: Learners compare *mass* and *weight*, listing shared features (both measure "amount," both use units) and differences (scalar vs. location-dependent), then classify new statements as describing one, the other, or both.
- **History**: Comparing two primary-source accounts of the same event in a comparison matrix with rows for author, purpose, and claims — a disciplinary literacy application.
- **Mathematics**: Comparing multiplication and addition solution strategies for the same word problem, then explaining when each is more efficient.
- **ReadWorks and similar ELA platforms** (https://www.readworks.org) include paired-text comparison tasks with Venn diagram printables as a standard comprehension activity type.
