# Graphic Organizers (Math)

## Description
Graphic organizers are structured visual templates — diagrams, mats, charts, and grids — that externalize the structure of a math problem or concept so learners can organize information spatially instead of holding it in working memory. In mathematics they serve two main functions: problem-solving scaffolds (e.g., step-by-step word-problem frames, "break it down" templates) that help students identify what is being asked and which operation to use, and concept-development tools (e.g., the [Frayer model](https://www.theteachertoolkit.com/index.php/tool/frayer-model), Venn diagrams, place-value mats) that map relationships among ideas. Common formats include the Frayer model, step-by-step frames, Venn diagrams, number lines, place-value charts, ten-frames, and coordinate or hundredths grids.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers reduce extraneous cognitive load by offloading intermediate problem states onto paper, freeing working memory for reasoning [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on being paired with explicit instruction in *how* to use them: an organizer handed to students without modeling becomes a form to fill in rather than a thinking tool [~M]. Organizers that encode a problem schema (e.g., schema diagrams distinguishing "combine," "compare," and "change" word problems) produce stronger transfer than generic templates [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A template matched to the problem type or concept structure (a compare-problem frame will not fit a rate problem)
- Instructor modeling of organizer use, ideally with [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) narration of each decision
- A [Fading](../elements/fading.md) plan: moving from completed examples, to partially completed organizers, to student-generated diagrams

#### Constraints
- Generic organizers applied without schema instruction do not improve word-problem performance; the organizer must map the underlying problem structure [~M]
- Over-reliance on a single template can anchor students to one representation and impede flexibility when problems do not fit the frame [-M]
- Benefits shrink for students with strong prior knowledge, who find the external structure redundant [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Poorly designed organizers that duplicate the textbook's own structure add transcription work without adding thinking [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Schema-based organizers** (Jitendra's schema-broadening instruction): diagrams keyed to word-problem types, taught across a family of examples
- **Concept organizers**: Frayer model, Venn diagrams, and concept maps for vocabulary and relationships
- **Structural mats and grids**: place-value charts, ten-frames, hundredths grids for number sense and computation
- **Student-generated diagrams**: learners draw their own organizer, which shifts the activity from filling in to representing — closer to [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and stronger for transfer [+W]

### Target Learners
- Students with learning disabilities or mathematics difficulty, for whom externalizing structure is best supported by the evidence [~S]
- Novices encountering a new problem type or concept who lack a schema for organizing the information
- Less beneficial for advanced students, who should be weaned onto self-generated representations [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Word-problem comprehension: identifying quantities, relationships, and the required operation
- Conceptual structure: comparing and contrasting concepts, building definitions (Frayer model)
- Representational fluency: connecting symbolic, visual, and verbal forms of a concept

### Instructions
1. Select or design an organizer that matches the target problem schema or concept structure.
2. Model its use on a worked problem, narrating each decision ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Work through a second problem collaboratively, gradually transferring control to students ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. Have students apply the organizer to new problems during [Practice](../elements/practice.md), then compare solutions in a [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
5. Fade the organizer: partial templates, then blank paper with a prompt to "draw the structure yourself."

## Related Strategies
- [Schema-Based Instruction](schema-based-instruction.md) — the word-problem variant of math graphic organizers, with the strongest evidence base
- [Worked Examples](worked-examples.md) — a completed organizer on a solved problem functions as a worked example of the representation itself
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — marking up problems shares the goal of making structure visible

## Examples
- **Schema diagrams in Jitendra's word-problem instruction** — students classify a problem as "change," "group," or "compare" and complete the matching diagram before solving; validated across elementary intervention studies.
- **Frayer model for math vocabulary** — a four-quadrant organizer (definition, characteristics, examples, non-examples) used to teach concepts like "prime number" or "slope."
- **[IRIS Center math graphic organizer templates](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu)** — printable place-value mats, sorting mats, and problem-solving frames designed for students with disabilities.
- **Ten-frames and rekenreks in early numeracy curricula** — structural organizers that make the base-ten structure of numbers visible before symbolic work.
