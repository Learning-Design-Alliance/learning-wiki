Dimensions of Critical Evaluation¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 mixed) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal), q3–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy makes the dimensions of critical evaluation explicit and teaches them as a differentiated framework rather than a single "is this credible?" judgment. Learners discuss four dimensions: Relevance (importance to a reading purpose), Accuracy (verifiable, up-to-date facts), Bias/Perspective (the author's position or slant), and Reliability (trustworthiness based on author and publishing body). Comparing the dimensions reveals a structural distinction: relevance and accuracy are judgments about the content itself, while perspective and reliability require examining details about the author and agenda. This gives learners a concrete way to remember that any evaluation must consider both the claims made and the author's expertise to make those claims.

Design Implications¶

Explicitly naming and contrasting the dimensions prevents the common failure mode in which learners collapse all evaluation into a vague credibility gut-check. Direct instruction in evaluation criteria outperforms reliance on checklists alone: students taught to evaluate sources strategically show stronger online reasoning than students given rubric-style heuristics like CRAAP without practice [Breakstone et al. show checklist heuristics transfer poorly without strategy instruction.] [-M]. Encountering sources that conflict on these dimensions can create productive Cognitive Disequilibrium that motivates deeper evaluation Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear definitions and worked examples for each dimension, with Case Studies of sources that vary on one dimension at a time
  • Comparative analysis activities — sources that are accurate but biased, relevant but unreliable — so learners must discriminate rather than apply a single test
  • Authentic evaluation tasks with real web sources, news articles, or disciplinary texts
  • Ongoing reinforcement across the course; a one-off lesson does not establish durable evaluation habits

Constraints¶

  • Checklist-style application (e.g., CRAAP tests) can produce surface compliance without genuine judgment; learners tick boxes rather than reason [Checklist heuristics without strategy instruction transfer poorly to novel sources.] [-M]
  • Novices may lack the domain knowledge to judge accuracy at all, defaulting to surface features like website design or author credentials [Novices rely on surface features when they lack domain knowledge for content evaluation.] [~M]
  • Less effective for younger learners without substantial scaffolding and simplified source sets
  • Requires repeated practice with feedback; dimensions taught once and never revisited decay quickly

Implementation Variability¶

  • Lateral reading adaptation: teach learners to leave the source and check what others say about it (the approach used by professional fact-checkers), rather than reading vertically within the source
  • Disciplinary embedding: apply the dimensions to peer-reviewed literature in a discipline (reliability = peer review and journal reputation) versus popular media
  • Contrast sets: present source pairs differing on exactly one dimension to sharpen discrimination

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents and adult learners with basic reading fluency who encounter unvetted information independently
  • Students beginning research projects who must select sources for the first time
  • Less effective for learners who lack the background knowledge to assess accuracy claims; pair with domain instruction first

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Differentiating evaluation dimensions: knowing that relevance and accuracy concern content, while perspective and reliability concern the author and agenda
  • Media and information literacy: making reasoned judgments about source quality
  • Epistemic cognition: understanding that credibility judgments are multi-dimensional and evidence-based, not binary

Instructions¶

  1. Define each dimension with Direct Instruction, giving a one-sentence definition and a concrete example of a source that scores high or low on that dimension alone.
  2. Contrast content-based and source-based judgments — have learners sort the four dimensions into "about the content" (relevance, accuracy) and "about the author/agenda" (bias, reliability), discussing why the distinction matters.
  3. Analyze contrast cases using Case Studies: pairs of sources that differ on a single dimension, so learners must isolate what changed.
  4. Discuss in groups via Class Discussion or structured Argumentation: learners defend an evaluation of a real source, citing specific evidence for each dimension.
  5. Apply to authentic tasks — learners evaluate and select sources for an actual research or writing assignment, then justify their selections using the framework.
  6. Revisit and fade support across subsequent assignments, shifting from guided analysis to independent evaluation.

Related Strategies¶

  • Lateral Reading — the professional fact-checker technique that operationalizes the reliability dimension by checking external sources
  • Sourcing — attending to author and publication details, the core of the bias/perspective and reliability dimensions
  • Corroboration — cross-checking claims across sources to evaluate accuracy

Examples¶

  • A media literacy class analyzes news articles on the same event from outlets with different editorial positions, using the four dimensions to explain why an article can be accurate yet biased, or relevant yet unreliable.
  • A first-year composition course has students evaluate candidate sources for a research paper, writing a one-paragraph justification per source that addresses all four dimensions before inclusion.
  • A science methods course applies the dimensions to conflicting web pages about climate data, prompting learners to notice that accuracy judgments require domain knowledge while reliability judgments can be made from source features.
