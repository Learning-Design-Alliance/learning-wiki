# Modeling Revision

## Description
Modeling revision is an instructional strategy in which the teacher explicitly demonstrates the process of revising — not just the final product — by working through a draft (their own or a student sample) while verbalizing the decisions involved: rereading for problems, diagnosing what is unclear or ineffective, considering alternatives, and making and evaluating changes. Because revision is a largely invisible, self-directed process, modeling converts tacit expert routines into observable steps learners can imitate. It is typically carried out as a live or recorded [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) over a shared text, often followed by guided or independent revision practice.

## Design Implications

Revision is a metacognitively demanding activity: writers must detect mismatches between their intentions and their text, then translate diagnoses into concrete edits. Novices rarely do this spontaneously, and simply telling them to "revise" produces cosmetic changes (spelling, word swaps) rather than substantive restructuring [~M]. Modeling the *process* — including false starts, hesitation, and self-questioning — gives learners both a procedure and a disposition: that revision is normal, iterative, and evaluative rather than punitive. The demonstration should show diagnosis and decision-making, not just before/after text, so learners can transfer the reasoning to their own drafts.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An authentic draft (teacher-written or anonymized student work) with genuine problems worth revising
- Verbalized reasoning that makes evaluation criteria explicit — why a sentence is unclear, what the writer is trying to achieve ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- A revision routine learners can internalize (e.g., reread → diagnose → consider options → revise → reread)
- Follow-up opportunities for learners to apply the routine to their own or peers' drafts with feedback ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Modeling on polished, problem-free text teaches nothing; the draft must contain authentic, diagnosable issues
- If the model shows only expert fluency without visible self-questioning, learners conclude revision is a talent rather than a procedure [-M]
- Whole-class modeling on one text can become passive watching; without immediate application to learners' own writing, gains fade [~M]
- Over-modeled revision (teacher supplies every diagnosis) can create dependence; the demonstration must fade toward learner-directed diagnosis [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Live vs. recorded:** live modeling allows responsive pauses and student suggestions; recorded modeling (e.g., screencasts) lets learners replay the reasoning
- **Teacher text vs. student sample:** revising anonymized student work raises relevance and normalizes imperfection; revising the teacher's own draft models vulnerability and authentic struggle
- **Interactive modeling:** pausing to ask the class "What's wrong here? What are my options?" turns the demonstration into guided diagnosis before the teacher resolves it
- **Gradual release:** full teacher modeling → shared/interactive revision → peer revision → independent self-revision

### Target Learners
- Novice writers (upper elementary through adult) who treat "revising" as proofreading or who skip it entirely
- Learners who can produce drafts but cannot evaluate them against quality criteria
- Less effective as repeated whole-class modeling for experienced writers, who benefit more from targeted feedback and self-set revision goals [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural knowledge of a revision routine (reread, diagnose, revise, verify)
- Metacognitive monitoring: detecting gaps between intention and text
- Dispositional change: framing writing as iterative rather than single-draft
- Application of genre and quality criteria to one's own work

### Instructions
1. Select or write a draft with authentic, discussable problems at the target level (sentence, paragraph, argument, or organization).
2. Model a full revision pass as a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): reread aloud, verbalize the evaluation ("This doesn't show *why* — my reader can't follow the claim"), generate options, choose one, and make the edit visibly.
3. Name the routine explicitly so learners can reuse it (e.g., "Read like a reader, not a writer → mark what confuses you → fix the biggest problem first").
4. Move to shared revision: present a new draft and have the class diagnose it before you revise, then to [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) as students revise their own drafts with conferring or peer review.
5. Fade the support over successive writing tasks, shifting from teacher-diagnosed problems to student-identified goals.

## Examples
**[Calkins' Units of Study](https://www.heinemann.com/unitsofstudy/)** (Teachers College Reading & Writing Project) — writing workshop mini-lessons built around the teacher "demonstrating" revision on their own draft, then sending students off to try the same move in their writing.

**Screencast revision modeling in composition courses** — instructors record themselves revising a student paragraph in Word or Google Docs, narrating each diagnosis; students then submit a revision memo applying the same routine to their own draft.

**[Graham & Perin's](https://doi.org/10.1177/002205740818600104) "modeling" recommendation** — in their meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction, structured collaborative and modeled writing episodes were among the elements with the largest effect sizes on writing quality.
