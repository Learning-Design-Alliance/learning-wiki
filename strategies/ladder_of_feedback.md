# Ladder of Feedback

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Ladder of Feedback is a discussion protocol developed by David Perkins and colleagues at Harvard Project Zero for structuring response to work-in-progress. It sequences feedback into four rungs, climbed in order: (1) **clarify** — ask neutral questions to understand the work before judging it; (2) **value** — name what is genuinely strong or promising; (3) **express concerns** — raise problems as personal, tentative statements ("I worry that…"); and (4) **suggest** — offer concrete alternatives, ideally framed as possibilities rather than prescriptions. The ordering is the design: understanding precedes evaluation, and affirmation precedes critique, so that concerns land within a context of demonstrated attention to the work.

## Design Implications

The protocol operationalizes research showing that feedback improves learning most reliably when it addresses the task and the processes behind it, rather than the person, and when vague praise or global judgment is avoided [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The clarify rung forces receivers to articulate intent and forces givers to understand before evaluating, which reduces feedback built on misreadings. The value rung is not mere politeness: specific identification of strengths directs the learner's attention to what to keep doing, and preserves the receptivity needed for concerns to be heard [~M]. Framing concerns and suggestions tentatively keeps the work's ownership with the creator, supporting the autonomy that sustains revision effort [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Work that is genuinely in progress — the protocol is designed for revisable drafts, not finished products
- A facilitator or written protocol that enforces the rung order and prevents jumping straight to concerns
- Enough time for every participant to speak on each rung, or a structured turn-taking format
- A culture of psychological safety; the "concerns as personal statements" framing only works if status costs of critique are low

#### Constraints
- The value rung degrades into empty praise when participants are rushed or unfamiliar with the work, and empty praise carries no informational benefit [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- The full four-rung sequence is slow; applying it to low-stakes or trivially revisable work wastes time and breeds protocol fatigue
- With unequal status in the room (instructor present, expert novice gap), participants may skip genuine concerns or defer to the authority figure, hollowing out the protocol
- For novices who cannot yet discriminate good from weak work, peer "values" and "concerns" may be inaccurate; the protocol structures *delivery* of feedback but does not by itself build evaluative skill [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written variant**: participants post sticky notes or comments per rung (e.g., in a shared doc), useful for asynchronous or large classes
- **Compressed variant**: collapse clarify and value into a single "appreciate and inquire" rung for shorter cycles
- **Recursive use**: run the ladder on successive drafts, so suggestions from round one become the clarify targets of round two
- **Role assignment**: designate one participant per rung type to guarantee coverage in quiet groups

### Target Learners
- Students in studio, writing, design, or project-based courses where drafts are regularly shared and revised
- Adolescents and adults; younger learners can use a simplified two-rung version (questions and suggestions) but often struggle to hold the full sequence
- Groups with established norms; introducing the ladder mid-conflict or in a low-trust group rarely works [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improving the quality of work-in-progress through iterative revision
- Building evaluative judgment: learners internalize the criteria they apply to peers' work and apply them to their own drafts [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Developing discourse norms for critique — a social and metacognitive goal as much as an academic one

### Instructions
1. **Present the work** briefly; the creator states what kind of feedback would be most useful (link to [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
2. **Clarifying questions**: the group asks neutral questions to understand intent — no judgments, no embedded advice. This rung enforces [Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) before evaluation.
3. **Values**: each participant names something specific and genuinely valuable in the work, with reasons.
4. **Concerns**: participants voice worries in the first person and tentatively ("I wonder whether…"), keeping ownership with the creator.
5. **Suggestions**: concrete alternatives are offered as options, not directives; the creator chooses what to take up (link to [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
6. **Close**: the creator responds briefly, naming what they will revise — no defense or debate required (link to [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) norms).

## Examples
- **Project Zero / Harvard Graduate School of Education** — the protocol's origin; documented in Perkins (2003) and used widely in Project Zero institutes and the [Visible Thinking](http://www.pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines) routine collection.
- **Architectural and design studio crits** — schools of architecture use ladder-style sequencing to replace the traditional "crit," in which unstructured expert judgment often dominated.
- **National School Reform Faculty Critical Friends Groups** — educators use the ladder within CFG protocols to give feedback on one another's student work and lesson drafts ([nsrfharmony.org](https://www.nsrfharmony.org)).
