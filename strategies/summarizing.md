# Summarizing

## Description
Summarizing asks learners to integrate the most important information from a text, lesson, or problem into a concise overview in their own words. In problem-solving contexts, it extends beyond restating content: learners justify their answer, evaluate whether their solution plan succeeded, and articulate the strategies they used. The act of selecting, condensing, and reorganizing forces deep processing of the material rather than surface reproduction.

## Design Implications

Summarizing works because it requires learners to reconstruct meaning — identifying what matters, dropping what does not, and building connections — which produces better comprehension and retention than rereading or passive review [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Summaries function as a form of generative processing: the learner must do the cognitive work of organizing information into a coherent structure, which consolidates it for later retrieval. In mathematics, the strategy doubles as a metacognitive checkpoint — justifying an answer and evaluating the plan exposes gaps in reasoning that would otherwise go unnoticed.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A source text, problem, or lesson with identifiable key information
- Learners who can distinguish central ideas from supporting detail; where they cannot, scaffolds such as [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) help them locate what matters
- A clear summary format or length constraint (e.g., "three sentences," "one paragraph," "explain your strategy in two steps") — constraints prevent verbatim copying and force selection
- Feedback on summary quality, since learners often omit critical content or retain trivia without guidance ([Provide Feedback](../elements/assessment.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners with weak topic knowledge struggle to identify key information and may produce summaries that are lists of surface features rather than integrated overviews [-M] — these learners need modeling of expert summarizing before summarizing independently
- Poorly constrained summaries can devolve into transcription; copying without selection produces little generative processing [-M]
- For novices, summarizing a complex text can add extraneous load before a coherent schema exists [~M] — sequencing matters: summarize after comprehension support, not as a substitute for it
- Effectiveness declines when summaries are graded on length or polish rather than accuracy of key ideas [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written summaries** — the standard form; a sentence-length limit forces prioritization
- **Oral summaries** — partner retells or "sum it up in 30 seconds" routines; lower barrier for learners with writing difficulties
- **Structured frames** — fill-in templates ("The problem was about… The strategy I used was… because…") that scaffold the justification component in math
- **Collaborative summarizing** — pairs negotiate a joint summary, surfacing disagreements about what is important
- **Summary as assessment** — one-sentence summaries or "tweet-length" recaps as quick formative checks

### Target Learners
- All learners, but strongest effects for those who otherwise use passive study strategies like rereading [+M]
- Learners who benefit from structured approaches; frames and templates make the strategy accessible to those who cannot yet self-select key ideas
- Less beneficial without support for learners with limited vocabulary or background knowledge, who cannot yet judge relevance [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: building a coherent mental model of a text
- Retention and consolidation: strengthening memory for key content [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Metacognition and mathematical reasoning: justifying answers and evaluating solution strategies
- Study skills: replacing rereading with a generative review strategy

### Instructions
1. Present the text, problem, or completed solution, ensuring learners have first comprehended the material (support with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) if needed).
2. Model expert summarizing once — think aloud while selecting key ideas and discarding detail, so learners see the selection criteria.
3. Ask learners to produce a constrained summary in their own words (length or format limit), integrating main points rather than listing them.
4. In problem-solving contexts, extend the summary to justification: state the answer, explain why the strategy was chosen, and evaluate whether the plan worked.
5. Provide [feedback](../elements/assessment.md) focused on whether key ideas were captured and connections made, not on style.
6. Follow with [practice](../elements/practice.md) — apply the summarized understanding to a new problem or discussion, converting the summary into use.

## Related Strategies
- **Self-Explanation** — the underlying mechanism; summarizing is self-explanation applied to whole texts and solution processes
- **Annotating** — a precursor skill; margin notes and highlighting identify the material summaries will draw from
- **Retrieval Practice** — summarizing from memory combines generation with retrieval for stronger retention
- **Concept Mapping** — an alternative synthesis format that makes relationships explicit rather than prose-based

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — small groups take turns summarizing a text segment as one of four strategies, with the teacher modeling and fading support over sessions.
- **Math journaling** — after solving a multi-step problem, students write "My answer is… I solved it by… This worked because…" — the justification and strategy-evaluation form of summarizing.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — lesson wrap-ups and unit summaries model concise overviews that students can emulate in their own notes.
- **One-sentence summaries in AP courses** — students compress a reading into a single grammatical sentence using a "somebody wanted but so" frame, forcing selection and integration.
