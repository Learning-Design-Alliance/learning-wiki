# Music and Emotions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners listen to selected musical excerpts and identify, name, and discuss the emotions the music evokes, then connect those responses to their own experiences. Music is a reliable elicitor of emotion — listeners consistently report felt emotions matching the perceived emotional character of a piece [Juslin & Västfjäll's BRECVEMA framework describes seven mechanisms through which music arouses emotion.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08004795) [+S] — which makes it a low-stakes, concrete stimulus for practicing emotional identification and expression.

## Design Implications

Music provides an affective stimulus that is shared but personally interpreted, so discussion of it naturally surfaces the distinction between feeling, perception, and expression. Naming emotions in response to music builds emotional granularity, which supports self-regulation and empathy [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. The activity works best when facilitators treat all responses as legitimate — there is no correct emotion to report.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to a music player and curated excerpts spanning contrasting emotional characters (e.g., major/minor, fast/slow, different genres and cultures)
- A facilitator to guide discussion and ensure respectful, non-judgmental exchanges
- An emotional vocabulary scaffold (word bank or [Feelings Vocabulary](../elements/feelings-vocabulary.md) chart) so learners can move beyond "good/bad" labels

#### Constraints
- Requires learners to be respectful of differing musical tastes; ridicule of responses shuts down disclosure [-M]
- Individual responses to music vary with personal history, culture, and associations [Music-evoked emotion depends heavily on listener context, memory, and cultural background.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08004795) [~S] — facilitators should not treat any mapping of music-to-emotion as universal
- Learners processing trauma or acute distress may find unstructured emotional disclosure destabilizing; participation in sharing should be optional [-W]
- Background music played *during* other learning tasks can impair comprehension and memory for verbal material [Background music harms reading comprehension and verbal memory.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.22.2.381) [-S] — music should be the object of attention, not accompaniment

#### Implementation Variability
- **Listen-and-label**: brief excerpt, then individual emotion naming before group discussion
- **Music journaling**: learners write freely in response to longer pieces, then share selectively
- **Learner-curated playlists**: students bring music that expresses an emotion they have felt, deepening ownership and cultural representation
- **Composition/creation**: learners create short soundscapes to represent an emotion, moving from recognition to expression
- **Cross-cultural comparison**: the same excerpt discussed across classrooms to highlight cultural variation in emotional interpretation

### Target Learners
- All ages; particularly valuable for learners who struggle to identify or verbalize emotions verbally (young children, learners with language processing difficulties) [~M]
- Groups building cohesion — shared musical experience supports belonging [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a *diagnostic* tool: a learner's stated emotion reflects interpretation, not a stable trait

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional awareness and granularity: expanding the vocabulary and discrimination of felt emotions
- Empathy and perspective-taking: hearing that others respond differently to the same stimulus [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Emotional self-regulation: recognizing music as a strategy for mood management [Adolescents use music deliberately for emotional self-regulation.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024094) [+M]
- Positive associations with emotional expression: normalizing talk about feelings

### Instructions
1. **Set the frame** with a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md): establish that all emotional responses are valid and sharing is optional.
2. **Play a short excerpt** (30–90 seconds) with full attention — no other task, since divided attention degrades both listening and emotional response [-S].
3. **Elicit individual responses** through [Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md) ("What did you feel? Where in your body? When have you felt this before?"), optionally using [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) for younger learners.
4. **Facilitate [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)** comparing responses, explicitly surfacing differences as evidence that emotional responses are personal, not right or wrong.
5. **Close with a naming step**: learners label the emotion precisely using a vocabulary scaffold, connecting the felt experience to language.
6. **Repeat across contrasting excerpts** so learners discriminate among related emotions (e.g., sadness vs. melancholy vs. nostalgia).

## Examples
- **Social-emotional learning curricula**: SEL programs use music listening and discussion as a standard medium for emotion identification and mood management instruction (see NAfME, [Music as a Medium for SEL](https://www.nafme.org/2023/10/music-as-a-medium-for-sel/)).
- **Music therapy practice**: guided music listening is used clinically to support emotional awareness and regulation, with clients selecting and discussing music tied to personal experience [Music listening reduces physiological stress response.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070156) [+M]
- **Language classrooms**: teachers play excerpts from target-culture music and discuss the emotions expressed, combining emotional vocabulary with cultural learning.
