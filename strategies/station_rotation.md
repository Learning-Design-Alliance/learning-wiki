# Station Rotation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Station rotation divides a class into small groups that move through a set sequence of stations on a schedule, each with a distinct activity modality: a teacher-led station for targeted instruction, a collaborative station for group work, and an independent or digital station for practice. Unlike [Lab Rotation](lab-rotation.md) or [Flipped Classroom](flipped-classroom.md) models, rotation happens *within* the classroom, keeping the teacher central to every student's learning time. It is the most common blended-learning model in K–12 classrooms (Horn & Staker, 2015).

## Design Implications

Station rotation operationalizes [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) by shrinking group size and increasing per-student interaction time — a teacher working with 8 students delivers more responsive instruction than one lecturing 32 [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The model's power comes from differentiation: the teacher-led station can target each group's current level, while digital stations deliver adaptive practice [Adaptive learning improves outcomes when content adjusts to learner level.](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]. Success depends on stations being genuinely interdependent parts of one lesson arc, not disconnected activities.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A predictable rotation schedule (timer, visual board) so transitions cost minutes, not learning
- Tasks at each station that students can attempt with minimal adult support, since the teacher is committed to one group
- Digitally or physically self-contained materials at independent stations, with built-in [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) or self-checking
- Established routines and norms for movement, voice level, and asking for help

#### Constraints
- Transition time and off-task behavior erode gains when routines are weak, especially with younger learners [~M]
- Digital stations that are mere video-watching or worksheet substitution add no value over whole-class instruction [-W]
- Fixed equal-time rotations ignore pacing differences; fast finishers idle while strugglers are cut off mid-task [~M]
- Requires more upfront planning than whole-class teaching; poorly designed stations increase rather than manage load [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class rotation** (all students rotate together) vs. **exit-ticket tiering** (only students needing reteach rotate to the teacher station while others continue)
- Two-, three-, or four-station configurations; two-station "dip" models reduce transition overhead
- Anchor activities (extension tasks) absorb pacing differences at independent stations
- [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) can replace fixed sequences to add learner autonomy

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, who benefit from short activity segments and varied modalities [+M]
- Students below grade level, who get small-group reteach at the teacher station [+M]
- Less suitable for very large classes without co-teaching support, or for learners who cannot yet self-manage at independent stations [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency and [Practice](../elements/practice.md) at independent stations
- Conceptual understanding via targeted small-group instruction
- Collaboration and discourse at the collaborative station [Collaborative learning improves achievement when structured with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Define one lesson objective and map how each station contributes a different route to it.
2. Design the teacher-led station first — use [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) data to target the specific gap each group needs addressed.
3. Design the collaborative station around structured interdependence, not just "work together."
4. Design the independent station with self-checking materials or adaptive software; avoid passive content consumption.
5. Teach and rehearse rotation routines before using stations for high-stakes content.
6. Run 10–15 minute rotations with a visible timer; use [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) or exit tickets to regroup students between lessons.

## Examples
- **Rocketship Public Schools** (https://www.rocketshipschools.org) — early K–12 blended-learning network built around daily learning-lab and station rotations.
- **Khan Academy** (https://www.khanacademy.org) — commonly used as the digital station, with mastery-based practice providing feedback without teacher presence.
- **Zearn** (https://www.zearn.org) — publishes station-rotation lesson templates pairing its digital math lessons with teacher-led small-group instruction.
