    Critique Protocols¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Critique protocols are structured routines — often with prescribed sentence stems, role assignments, and turn-taking rules — through which learners examine one another's drafts or performances and offer feedback. The protocol constrains the interaction so that critique targets the work rather than the person, is specific enough to act on, and follows a predictable sequence (e.g., warm observations, then questions, then suggestions).

    Design Implications¶

    Structured critique converts feedback from an evaluative judgment into an instructional event: learners analyze criteria, compare work against them, and articulate improvements, which deepens their own understanding of quality Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, and what next?" drives achievement gains. [+S]. Giving feedback is often as beneficial as receiving it — the reviewer must judge work against criteria, which is a form of elaborative practice Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when criteria are clear. [+M]. Protocols matter because unstructured peer feedback tends toward vague praise or personal comments; explicit structures and rubrics raise the specificity and usefulness of peer comments Peer assessment quality improves when structured criteria or rubrics are provided. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A visible model of quality work (exemplar or Non-Examples) so reviewers share a standard
    • Clear criteria or a rubric that the critique references
    • Work-in-progress rather than finished work, so feedback can still change the outcome
    • A taught protocol with sentence stems and time limits, rehearsed before it carries stakes

    Constraints¶

    • Without trained criteria, peer feedback drifts to vague praise ("I like it") or personal remarks, producing no actionable guidance Checklist-style evaluation without criteria training is ineffective in online settings. [-M]
    • Low-trust classrooms suppress honest critique; learners hedge to protect relationships, and feedback quality drops [-M]
    • Novice reviewers can transmit misconceptions; instructor moderation or spot-checking is needed in early iterations [-W]
    • Critique of highly subjective or idiosyncratic work (personal narrative, artistic voice) can flatten expression if criteria over-specify style [~W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Gallery critique: whole-group examination of one work, modeled by the instructor (Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" routine)
    • Critical friends / tuning protocols: small groups follow timed steps — presenter describes, group questions, group discusses while presenter listens, presenter responds
    • Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise–Question–Suggest: lightweight stems for younger or newer critics
    • Studio walk / design crit: rotating stations with written sticky-note feedback, common in art and design education
    • Asynchronous critique: structured comment threads in platforms like Peergrade or Perusall, using the same stems

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners old enough to articulate observations against criteria (upper elementary through adult) Peer assessment works across grade levels but needs more scaffolding for younger students. [+M]
    • Learners producing iterative artifacts (writing, design, code, proofs) where revision is expected
    • Reviewers benefit even when their own work is not critiqued — judging others' work builds evaluative judgment [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Quality criteria internalization: understanding what makes work good, not just producing work
    • Revision and iteration: treating drafts as improvable Authentic audiences and iterative critique improve the quality of student work. [+M]
    • Evaluative judgment and self-assessment: the capacity to critique one's own work independently
    • Discourse and argumentation: justifying observations with evidence from the work Structured argumentation improves reasoning quality. [+M]

    Instructions¶

    1. Establish criteria: co-construct or present a rubric; anchor it with exemplars and Non-Examples so reviewers share a standard.
    2. Model a critique: the instructor critiques a sample work aloud using the protocol, making the moves visible (Think-Aloud).
    3. Run the protocol: pairs or small groups exchange drafts; each critique follows the fixed sequence — describe what you see, ask questions, offer specific suggestions — with sentence stems visible.
    4. Author response: the author paraphrases the feedback and states one revision they will make, closing the loop (Articulation).
    5. Revise and re-critique: authors revise; a second round shows whether feedback was actionable and builds the revision habit.
    6. Debrief the protocol itself: briefly discuss which feedback was most useful and why, improving future critique quality.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Peer Feedback — the broader practice; critique protocols are the structured form that makes it reliable
    • Revision Cycles — critique is the feedback event inside an iteration loop
    • Rubric Design — supplies the criteria that anchor critique

    Examples¶

    • Ron Berger's EL Education critique lessons (eleducation.org) — the "Austin's Butterfly" routine: kindergarten students give kind, specific, helpful feedback across multiple drafts of a scientific drawing, demonstrating critique with young learners.
    • Harvard Project Zero "Tuning Protocol" (pz.harvard.edu) — a timed critical-friends structure for examining student or adult work in professional learning communities.
    • Design studio crits in architecture and art education — the original model: public examination of work-in-progress against disciplinary standards, with the instructor moderating.
    • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — asynchronous structured peer review with rubric-anchored comment prompts, used in MOOCs and university courses at scale.
