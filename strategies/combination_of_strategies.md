# Combination of Strategies

## Description
Combining two or more calming sensory strategies to create a more comprehensive and effective approach to self-regulation. Examples include digging in a tactile bin while listening to white noise, sitting with a weighted blanket while looking at sensory bottles, or chewing gum while working on a fine motor task. The rationale is that learners with multiple sensory needs — for example, proprioceptive seeking alongside auditory sensitivity — are better served by addressing those needs simultaneously than by a single isolated tool.

## Design Implications

Multi-component self-regulation approaches align with the broader finding that interventions targeting multiple channels of regulation (attention, arousal, emotion) tend to outperform single-component ones [Diamond & Lee, 2011 [+S]. However, the specific evidence for *stacking* sensory strategies is weaker than for structured multi-component programs: most support comes from single-subject designs and occupational therapy case studies rather than controlled comparisons of combined versus single strategies [~W]. The practical design question is not whether to combine, but how to help learners identify which combination serves which need — making the strategy selection itself a teachable, self-monitored skill.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A collection of sensory tools and activities spanning different sensory modalities (tactile, proprioceptive, auditory, visual, oral)
- A designated space or routine for implementing these strategies
- Clear guidelines for participation, so the combination does not become a distraction from the primary task
- Observation or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines to evaluate whether a given combination is actually regulating (calming and organizing) rather than over- or under-stimulating

#### Constraints
- Combining strategies multiplies the variables to monitor; a combination that helps one learner may overstimulate another, and effects can shift day to day [~W]
- Without individualized guidance, learners may default to preferred (but not regulating) combinations, or use combinations as avoidance rather than re-engagement
- Requires additional resources, space, and adult support; poorly resourced implementations tend to collapse into unstructured "sensory break" time with no regulatory purpose [-W]
- Evidence for weighted blankets, sensory bottles, and similar tools in isolation is limited and mixed; combining them does not compound weak evidence into strong evidence [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Simultaneous combination** — two strategies used at once (e.g., weighted lap pad + white noise during seatwork)
- **Sequenced combination** — strategies ordered as a regulation routine (e.g., heavy work first, then quiet visual focus tool, then return to task)
- **Learner-selected vs. adult-prescribed** — older or more self-aware learners can experiment and self-select; younger learners typically need an adult to model and narrow options
- **Embedded vs. dedicated** — combinations can be embedded in the ongoing activity (chewing gum while writing) or delivered in a separate calming space before returning

### Target Learners
- Learners with sensory processing differences, including many autistic learners and learners with ADHD, who show heterogeneous sensory profiles that a single strategy cannot address (plain text — no claim page yet)
- Learners who require a more comprehensive approach to self-regulation because one modality alone does not shift their arousal state
- Less appropriate as a universal strategy: for most learners, a single well-chosen strategy plus [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in using it is sufficient, and added components add management cost without benefit

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing arousal states and selecting tools to reach an optimal state for learning
- Metacognitive awareness: learning *which* strategies work for *which* states, building a personalized regulation repertoire
- Behavioral readiness: returning to instructional tasks after dysregulation with less lost learning time

### Instructions
1. Observe and document the learner's sensory responses to identify which needs a combination should address (e.g., seeking proprioceptive input, avoiding auditory noise).
2. Introduce one strategy at a time, using [Practice](../elements/practice.md) so the learner experiences each tool's effect in isolation before combining.
3. Pair strategies across modalities so they serve complementary functions — one input-seeking (e.g., heavy work, chewing) and one input-reducing (e.g., noise-reducing headphones, dimmed light).
4. Model the combination explicitly with [Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md), narrating when and why to use it ("Your body is wiggly and the room is loud — let's do wall pushes, then put on headphones").
5. Build in a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) after use: did the combination help the learner return to the task? Adjust the combination based on the answer.
6. Fade adult prompting toward a learner-managed plan, using [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) to let learners select from pre-vetted combinations independently.

## Related Strategies
- Sensory breaks and individual calming strategies — the components that this strategy combines; combination is the next step once single strategies have been trialed
- [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — the adult support structure through which combinations are modeled, monitored, and faded

## Examples
- An occupational therapist designs a regulation plan for an autistic second-grader: five minutes of heavy work (carrying books, wall pushes) followed by sitting on a wiggle cushion with noise-reducing headphones during independent reading, with a visual [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) card afterward.
- A middle school resource room offers a "regulation menu" [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md) where students select a paired combination (e.g., kinetic sand + instrumental music) and log its effectiveness, building a personal evidence base over the term.
- The Zones of Regulation curriculum (Kuypers, 2011) teaches learners to match tool combinations to identified emotional/arousal "zones" — a widely used structured implementation of this strategy.
