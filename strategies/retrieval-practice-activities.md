# Retrieval Practice Activities

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Retrieval practice activities require learners to pull information out of memory — via free recall, short-answer questions, flashcards, low-stakes quizzes, or brain dumps — rather than reviewing material again. The act of successful retrieval itself modifies memory, making the retrieved knowledge more accessible later. Activities range from ungraded "warm-up" recalls to spaced quizzing embedded across a course.

## Design Implications

Retrieval practice produces substantially larger and more durable learning gains than restudying, with effects that grow over retention intervals [Testing produces stronger long-term retention than restudying.](../claims/testing-effect-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Its benefits extend beyond memorization: retrieval can also strengthen inference and application when questions target those processes [Retrieval practice can improve transfer as well as factual recall.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-transfer.md) [+M]. Because learners systematically misjudge fluency — re-reading feels productive while retrieval feels effortful — activities should be framed so learners do not abandon them [Learners often fail to predict the benefit of retrieval practice.](../claims/learners-misjudge-retrieval-benefit.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions or prompts that can actually be answered from memory (not recognition-only formats exclusively)
- Feedback or access to correct answers after retrieval, so errors are corrected rather than consolidated [Feedback following retrieval enhances its benefits.](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+M]
- Distribution across time; a single massed retrieval session forfeits most of the benefit ([Spaced Practice](../principles/spacing.md))
- Low-stakes framing so retrieval functions as learning, not merely assessment ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))

#### Constraints
- Retrieval of poorly encoded material yields little benefit and can entrench errors when learners guess and receive no feedback [Retrieval practice is less effective when material was never adequately encoded.](../claims/retrieval-fails-without-encoding.md) [-M]
- High-stakes framing converts retrieval into test anxiety, reducing its learning benefit for anxious learners [~M]
- Simple factual recall questions build retention of facts but transfer poorly if the target goal is application or problem-solving [~S]
- Benefits diminish for complex materials when retrieval itself overloads working memory; partial notes or scaffolds can offset this [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Free recall / brain dumps**: learners write everything they remember; cheap and format-agnostic
- **Low-stakes quizzing**: short ungraded or lightly graded quizzes at the start or end of class
- **[Flashcards](flashcards.md) with spaced repetition**: individual study, ideally with expanding intervals ([Spaced Repetition](../principles/spaced-practice.md))
- **Retrieval-based discussion**: pairs reconstruct material from memory before consulting notes
- **Interleaved retrieval**: mixing question types or topics, which adds discrimination benefits [Interleaving improves discrimination between problem types.](../claims/interleaving-improves-discrimination.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Learners at nearly all levels benefit, though effects are often larger for those with weaker prior knowledge [~M]
- Learners prone to illusions of competence from fluent re-reading — most learners [Learners often fail to predict the benefit of retrieval practice.](../claims/learners-misjudge-retrieval-benefit.md) [+M]
- Younger learners may need more scaffolding of the retrieval process itself (prompts, partial cues) [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Long-term retention of facts, concepts, and procedures
- Fluency and automaticity in foundational knowledge ([Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md))
- Transfer and application, when retrieval questions themselves require application [Retrieval practice can improve transfer as well as factual recall.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-transfer.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Encode first.** Ensure material has been taught adequately ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)); retrieval is a consolidation activity, not a first exposure.
2. **Design retrieval prompts** that match the target goal — recall prompts for retention, application prompts for transfer.
3. **Run the retrieval activity** with materials put away: brain dump, quiz, or pair-reconstruction. Keep it low-stakes.
4. **Provide feedback** immediately after retrieval, correcting errors and filling gaps.
5. **Schedule repeated retrievals** at spaced intervals, expanding the gap as mastery grows ([Spaced Practice](../principles/spacing.md)).
6. **Interleave** topics or problem types once basic mastery is reached.
7. **Explain the effect to learners** so they persist through the effortful feeling rather than reverting to re-reading.

## Examples
- **[Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net)** — spaced-repetition flashcard system implementing expanding retrieval intervals; widely used in medical education.
- **[Retrieval practice guides by Agarwal & Bain](https://www.retrievalpractice.org)** — classroom routines (brain dumps, "two things," mini-quizzes) designed for K–12 teachers.
- **Medical school curriculum quizzing** — many US medical programs embed weekly low-stakes case-based quizzes; research in this setting shows testing outperforming repeated study for retention of clinical material [+S].
- **Course warm-up recalls** — opening a lecture with a 3-minute written recall of the previous session before new content.
