# Playworks TeamUp Recess Program

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
TeamUp is a variant of the [Playworks](https://www.playworks.org) program model in which school staff — rather than a full-time Playworks coach — run a structured recess. Schools receive training, a game guide, and equipment, then treat recess like an instructional period: games are planned in advance, taught explicitly, and led by rotating adults or older students. The design goal is to convert unstructured playground time into a predictable social-learning environment that builds cooperation, conflict resolution, and physical activity while reducing bullying and post-recess classroom disruption.

## Design Implications

TeamUp applies the logic of [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) to a non-classroom setting: rather than hoping social skills emerge from free play, it models and rehearses them through repeated, structured game routines. The evidence base for recess-based structured interventions is generally positive — playground interventions reliably increase moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and can reduce antisocial behavior [~M] — and the Mathematica randomized controlled trial of Playworks found improvements in recess safety, student inclusion, and teachers' reports of students' conflict-resolution abilities, with weaker and less consistent effects on academic outcomes [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Trained adults (teachers, paraprofessionals, or parent volunteers) who are present and leading, not merely supervising
- A small repertoire of well-taught games with explicit rules, so disputes have an agreed reference point ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
- A conflict-resolution routine (e.g., rock-paper-scissors as the Playworks "junior referee" mechanism) that students learn once and apply independently
- Equipment and a game plan prepared in advance — recess treated like a [lesson plan](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md), not a break in instruction

#### Constraints
- Effects depend on implementation fidelity; schools where staff turnover is high or training is superficial see the program degrade into ordinary supervision [~M]
- Structured games can crowd out the developmental benefits of genuinely free, self-directed play — creativity, self-organized negotiation, and autonomy — if every recess minute is scripted [~M]
- Gains in recess climate do not reliably transfer to classroom behavior or achievement; the RCT found academic and school-climate effects were smaller and less consistent than recess-level effects [~M]
- Requires buy-in from playground staff; where adults see recess as "their break," leadership routines collapse

#### Implementation Variability
- **TeamUp (school-led)** vs. **full Playworks coach model**: the coach model embeds a dedicated site coordinator; TeamUp trades intensity for cost and sustainability
- Junior coach / peer-leader variants: older students run games, adding a [learning-by-teaching](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M] benefit for the leaders themselves
- Partial adoption: schools may implement only the game library and conflict-resolution routine without full scheduling

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (K–6), the population in which nearly all recess-intervention evidence has been gathered
- Students who struggle with unstructured social settings — the predictable rules and adult facilitation lower the social-executive demands of the playground [~M]
- Older students serving as game leaders, who benefit from leadership responsibility and modeling roles [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional skills: turn-taking, inclusion, and peer conflict resolution
- Behavioral regulation: returning to class settled and ready to learn
- Physical health: increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity during the school day
- Procedural fluency in a shared game culture that reduces rule disputes

### Instructions
1. Train all recess staff in the game library and the conflict-resolution routine before launch ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
2. Publish a weekly recess plan — which games, which zones, which adults — like a lesson plan ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
3. Teach each new game explicitly during the first days: model it, play a demonstration round, then release students ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Establish a universal dispute-resolution move (e.g., rock-paper-scissors) so students resolve conflicts without adult adjudication ([Fading](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]).
5. Rotate student leaders into game-leadership roles to build ownership and reduce adult dependence.
6. Check in with teachers weekly about post-recess classroom behavior and adjust the game plan ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).

## Examples
- **Playworks TeamUp** ([playworks.org](https://www.playworks.org)) — the program itself: training, game guides, and equipment kits enabling schools to run structured recess without a full-time coach.
- **Ready for Recess (Omaha, NE)** — a similar structured-recess intervention using activity zones and staff training, which increased children's physical activity at recess (Huberty et al., 2013).
- **SPARK Active Recreation** ([sparkpe.org](https://sparkpe.org)) — a commercial structured-recess and physical-education program with a comparable game-library model.
