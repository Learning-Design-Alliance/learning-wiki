# Rubric Based Self Assessment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Rubric based self assessment asks learners to judge their own draft or performance against explicit, criterion-referenced descriptors before (or instead of) receiving instructor evaluation. Learners rate their work on each criterion, cite evidence for the rating, and identify revisions. The rubric converts tacit quality standards into visible, actionable criteria, making the learner an active participant in evaluation rather than a passive recipient of grades.

## Design Implications

Self assessment works when learners have accurate standards to judge against; rubrics supply those standards and reduce the idiosyncratic leniency and drift that plague unstructured self-evaluation [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The act of comparing one's work to criteria also builds the evaluative judgment needed for self-regulated learning — learners internalize what "good" looks like rather than depending on external verdicts. Simple checklists, by contrast, tend to be applied mechanically and add little [Checklist-style self-evaluation is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]; rubrics with qualitative descriptors outperform binary checklists because they require judgment, not box-ticking.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rubric with clear, observable criteria and graduated performance descriptors, written in language learners can understand ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- A structured self-rating activity (rating plus evidence, not just a score)
- An opportunity to revise based on the self-assessment, so the judgment feeds action ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- Calibration experience: seeing exemplars scored against the rubric before rating their own work

#### Constraints
- Novices systematically overestimate their performance; uncalibrated self-assessment can reinforce inaccurate self-views [-M] — pairing with exemplar comparison or instructor moderation mitigates this
- Vague or jargon-heavy rubrics produce ratings uncorrelated with actual quality [-M]
- When self-assessment counts toward grades, learners inflate ratings; keep it low-stakes or ungraded [~S]
- Rubric focus can narrow attention to listed criteria at the expense of holistic quality or creativity [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Draft-stage self-check**: learners rate a draft against the rubric and revise before submission
- **Calibrated peer review**: learners first score anonymized exemplars, receive feedback on their scoring accuracy, then score their own work (e.g., the Calibrated Peer Review process)
- **Rubric co-construction**: learners help draft criteria, deepening ownership and understanding of quality standards
- **Self- vs. peer-assessment pairing**: learners self-rate, then compare with a peer's rating of the same work, surfacing perception gaps

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to apply criteria meaningfully; pure novices lack the knowledge to judge their own work accurately [-M]
- Learners developing self-regulation and revision habits [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Advanced learners refining evaluative judgment toward professional standards

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality criteria knowledge: understanding what distinguishes strong from weak performance
- Self-regulated learning: monitoring and evaluating one's own work against standards
- Revision skill: translating evaluative judgment into concrete improvements

### Instructions
1. Introduce the rubric and walk through exemplars at different performance levels, scoring them together so learners see how descriptors map to real work.
2. Have learners score an anonymized sample and compare ratings with peers or the instructor to calibrate judgment.
3. Learners rate their own draft on each criterion, citing specific evidence from the work for each rating.
4. Learners identify the two lowest-rated criteria and plan concrete revisions ([Action-Oriented Feedback](action-oriented_feedback.md) applies to self-generated feedback too).
5. Learners revise and resubmit with the completed self-assessment attached; instructor grades against the same rubric and notes where self-ratings diverged.

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (CPR)** (https://cpr.molsci.org) — discipline-agnostic system where students score exemplars for accuracy before evaluating their own and peers' work against a rubric.
- **Writing portfolios in the National Writing Project model** — students assess drafts against shared criteria and write reflective cover letters justifying their ratings before teacher evaluation.
- **AP Studio Art portfolio scoring** — students self-evaluate their portfolios against the published College Board rubric before submission, using the same descriptors the readers will apply.
