# Reciprocal Peer Feedback

## Description
Reciprocal peer feedback arranges learners into pairs or small groups who review one another's work against shared criteria, then revise in light of what they receive. Each learner plays both roles — producing feedback and receiving it — which distinguishes reciprocity from one-way peer review. The exchange is typically structured with [rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md), sentence stems, or protocols so that comments are specific and actionable rather than evaluative.

## Design Implications

Peer feedback works because both roles generate learning: reviewing exposes learners to alternative approaches and forces them to internalize quality criteria, while receiving feedback supplies timely, multiple perspectives that a single instructor cannot match [Assessment for learning improves achievement when learners understand criteria and act on feedback.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The reviewing role is often the more powerful one — evaluating someone else's work against criteria builds the evaluative judgment learners need to improve their own work. Reciprocity also builds a sense of audience and accountability that raises the quality of the work being reviewed [Authentic audiences improve student work quality.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Shared, explicit criteria ([rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) or checklists) so reviewers judge the work, not personal taste
- Training or modeling of feedback quality before the first exchange ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of good and weak feedback comments)
- Time and structure for reviewees to act on the feedback — feedback that is received but never used produces little learning
- A low-stakes draft stage; feedback works best on improvable work, not final products

#### Constraints
- Untrained reviewers default to vague praise ("good job!") or personal comments, which do not improve work [Checklist-style evaluation without guidance is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Feedback quality drops when reviewers lack sufficient domain knowledge to detect problems — in highly technical domains, peers may validate errors [~M]
- Power and relationship dynamics suppress candor: friends soften criticism, and unequal status (e.g., mixed-ability pairing without structure) can make feedback feel like judgment rather than help [-M]
- If grades are attached to peer scores, reviewers inflate ratings and the formative function collapses [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Calibrated peer review**: reviewers first score an instructor-anchored exemplar and receive accuracy feedback before reviewing peers (used by [Calibrated Peer Review](https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) and [Peergrade](https://www.peergrade.io)/[Peerceptiv](https://peerceptiv.com))
- **Written asynchronous exchange** (discussion boards, document comments) vs. **synchronous talk-based protocols** (e.g., gallery walk with sticky-note critique)
- **Reciprocal roles over time**: rotating reviewer assignments across multiple drafts so every learner experiences both roles repeatedly
- **Anonymous vs. identified review**: anonymity increases critical candor but removes the accountability and dialogue benefits of identified exchange [~M]

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary learners in writing-intensive or project-based courses, where multiple feedback cycles exceed what one instructor can provide [Collaborative writing improves text quality when peers review drafts.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Learners developing evaluative judgment — the capacity to judge quality in their own work — which reviewing others' work develops more directly than receiving feedback alone [+M]
- Less suitable for complete novices in technical domains, who cannot yet detect errors in peers' work [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improving product quality through revision (writing, design, code, arguments)
- Internalizing quality criteria and standards ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Communication and professional critique skills — giving specific, respectful, actionable feedback
- Self-regulation: using external feedback to plan one's own revisions

### Instructions
1. Publish the task and the [rubric](../elements/rubrics.md) before work begins, so criteria shape production, not just review.
2. Have learners produce a low-stakes draft ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. Train reviewers: show exemplar work and exemplar feedback comments, and have learners critique the feedback itself ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)).
4. Pair or group learners and exchange drafts; each reviews using the rubric and sentence stems ("One strength is… One change that would most improve this is…").
5. Deliver feedback, then require a revision plan or revised draft — closing the loop is where the learning gains occur.
6. Rotate roles across cycles so every learner reviews and is reviewed; debrief on feedback quality, not just work quality.

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (CPR)** — a web-based system in which students complete calibration exercises against instructor-anchored exemplars before reviewing three peers' work; widely used in STEM writing assignments. [https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu](https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu)
- **Peerceptiv** — a peer assessment platform that assigns multiple anonymous reviewers per submission and grades reviewers on the accuracy and helpfulness of their feedback, making reviewing itself an assessed learning activity. [https://peerceptiv.com](https://peerceptiv.com)
- **Writing-across-the-curriculum peer review workshops** — small-group draft exchanges with rubric-guided comment sheets, a standard structure in composition courses since Gere's work on writing groups.
