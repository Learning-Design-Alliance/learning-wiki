# Vocabulary Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Vocabulary instruction deliberately selects high-value words and teaches them directly through clear definitions, examples, context, and repeated opportunities to use them. It typically includes pre-teaching challenging words before reading a text so students do not stall on unfamiliar terms, and it can be embedded in any content area. Effective programs go beyond dictionary definitions to build rich word knowledge — multiple meanings, morphological relatives, and contextual usage.

## Design Implications

Explicit vocabulary instruction reliably improves word learning and, more modestly, reading comprehension [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension of texts containing taught words.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. Pre-teaching key terms reduces the working-memory burden of decoding meaning and content simultaneously [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], freeing attention for comprehension. Depth matters more than breadth: a few words taught richly (multiple encounters, varied contexts, active use) outperform long lists of definitions [Rich, contextualized vocabulary instruction outperforms definition-only approaches.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identification of the key vocabulary: prioritize Tier Two words (high-frequency academic words like *analyze*, *reluctant*) over rare technical terms or common words students already know (Beck et al., 2013)
- Clear, concise, student-friendly definitions — not dictionary paraphrases
- Multiple encounters with each word across contexts, including [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) and [Lectures](../elements/lectures.md)
- Time for spaced practice and active use in writing and speaking

#### Constraints
- Students may memorize definitions without genuine understanding if instruction is definition-only [Definition memorization without contextual use produces shallow word knowledge.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [-M]
- Time-consuming; covering too many words dilutes depth of learning for all of them
- Does not remediate underlying decoding or fluency problems — students who cannot read the words on the page need [phonics or fluency support](../strategies/accessible_syntax.md), not more word lists
- Gains on taught words do not automatically transfer to general comprehension of untaught vocabulary [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-teaching before reading**: brief front-loading of 3–8 critical terms before a text
- **Rich instruction**: [Frayer-type four-square work](../strategies/acronyms_and_acrostics.md), [analogies](../elements/analogies.md), semantic maps, and [act-it-out](../elements/act-it-out.md) dramatization
- **Morphological analysis**: teaching roots, prefixes, and suffixes so students can unlock untaught words
- **Multimodal formats**: definitions delivered as text, [visuals](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md), and audio to support diverse learners
- **Incidental + embedded**: word walls, classroom discourse norms, and teacher modeling of academic language

### Target Learners
- All grade levels; particularly beneficial for struggling readers and students with limited vocabulary or English learners [Explicit vocabulary instruction disproportionately benefits lower-vocabulary learners.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Students encountering dense academic texts who would otherwise disengage or become frustrated
- Less necessary for students who already know the target words — pre-assessment should guide selection [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Word knowledge: accurate meaning, usage, and multiple senses of target words
- Reading comprehension: understanding challenging texts containing taught vocabulary
- Academic language development: using disciplinary vocabulary productively in writing and discussion
- Word-learning strategies: independent use of context clues and morphology

### Instructions
1. **Select** 5–10 high-utility words from the upcoming text or unit, prioritizing Tier Two academic words and essential disciplinary terms.
2. **Pre-teach** with student-friendly definitions, an example, and a non-example; connect to prior knowledge using [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) and [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md).
3. **Deepen** through an activity requiring active processing — semantic mapping, sorting, or [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) — rather than copying definitions.
4. **Read and apply** the words in context via [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md), with [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) to mark word use in the text.
5. **Use productively**: require the words in writing assignments and discussion, with [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) followed by guided practice.
6. **Review spacedly** with games, flashcards, or quick [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) across subsequent days and weeks.

## Examples
- **Pre-teaching before a science text**: before reading about ecosystems, the teacher defines *producer*, *consumer*, and *decomposer* with visuals and a quick sorting game, then students find each word in the text and explain its use.
- **Word of the week routines**: many schools use structured academic-vocabulary routines (e.g., from [Bringing Words to Life](https://www.guilford.com/books/Bringing-Words-to-Life/Beck-McKeown-Kucan/9781462508167)) in which a small set of words is introduced, acted out, used in sentence frames, and revisited daily.
- **Quizlet and flashcard games**: [Quizlet](https://quizlet.com) and similar tools support spaced retrieval practice of definitions, though they work best as review after rich instruction rather than as the sole method.
