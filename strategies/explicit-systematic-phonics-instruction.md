# Explicit Systematic Phonics Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Explicit systematic phonics instruction teaches grapheme–phoneme correspondences directly and in a planned sequence — typically from high-utility, simple correspondences to more complex ones — with teacher modeling of blending and segmenting, immediate application to decodable text, and cumulative review. "Explicit" means the correspondences and the blending procedure are taught directly rather than inferred from exposure to text; "systematic" means the sequence is pre-planned by instructional design rather than driven opportunistically by the texts children happen to encounter.

## Design Implications

Phonics instruction is one of the most consistently supported findings in educational research: systematic phonics programs outperform unsystematic or no-phonics comparison conditions on word reading, and the advantage for decoding underpins later comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. The mechanism is [Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md): when decoding becomes effortless, working memory is released for meaning-making, consistent with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). Effectiveness depends on the instruction actually being explicit and systematic — teaching a few correspondences per lesson, modeling blending, and giving immediate practice with words containing only taught patterns.

### Instructions
1. Introduce 1–3 new grapheme–phoneme correspondences per lesson, each with a keyword image and articulation cue.
2. Model blending aloud (e.g., /m/–/a/–/p/ → *map*), then have students blend with you, then independently — a gradual release consistent with [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md).
3. Practice phonemic awareness skills in isolation from print (oral blending and segmenting) before connecting them to letters.
4. Have students read words, sentences, and then decodable texts containing only taught patterns, with immediate corrective feedback.
5. Dictate words and sentences for spelling, reversing the mapping (phonemes → graphemes).
6. Cumulatively review prior correspondences in each lesson and reteach any that fall below mastery on checks.
