Responding with "Yet"¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Adding the word "yet" to responses when learners express frustration or inability (e.g., "I can't do this") reframes the statement as temporary rather than fixed: "You can't do this yet." The move is a linguistic enactment of growth mindset theory — it signals that current performance is a stage in development, not a verdict on ability, and implies that continued effort and appropriate support will produce improvement Dweck's mindset framework links beliefs about malleability to persistence and response to setbacks. [+M]

Design Implications¶

"Yet" works as a low-cost framing device that shifts learner attributions from stable inability to improvable performance, which supports persistence after difficulty. Its effect depends on the surrounding feedback ecology: the word alone is a cue, but it changes beliefs only when paired with process-focused feedback and concrete routes forward Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and the process rather than the person. [+S]

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Consistent use by the instructor across repeated encounters with learner frustration; sporadic use reads as a slogan rather than a belief
  • Pairing with process-focused feedback that names what the learner did and what to try next, so "yet" points to an actual path
  • Instructor modeling of the same framing in self-talk ("I haven't figured this out yet"), so learners see the stance demonstrated rather than merely prescribed (Modeling)
  • Task design that makes improvement genuinely attainable — appropriately challenging tasks with support (Scaffolding)

Constraints¶

  • Standalone "yet" without concrete support is dismissed as empty encouragement and can degrade instructor credibility [-W]
  • Mindset interventions overall show small and heterogeneous effects; benefits concentrate on at-risk or low-achieving students and shrink for students already confident or high-achieving [~S] (Sisk et al., 2018)
  • Applied indiscriminately, "yet" can imply that all failure is a matter of insufficient effort, blaming learners for structural barriers or genuine aptitude limits [-W]
  • If effort is praised without progress, learners may conclude that effort signals low ability, reversing the intended effect [~S] (Mueller & Dweck, 1998)

Implementation Variability¶

  • Verbal responses in one-on-one feedback, written comments on work, and rubric language ("not yet meeting criterion" instead of "failing")
  • Whole-class norm-setting: introducing "the power of yet" as shared language early in a course
  • System-level adaptations: mastery-based platforms (e.g., Khan Academy's mastery levels) encode "not yet" structurally by marking skills as "attempted" or "familiar" rather than failed

Target Learners¶

  • Learners experiencing early failure or frustration in a new domain, who are most likely to form fixed-ability attributions [~M]
  • Low-achieving or at-risk students, for whom mindset interventions show the largest (though still modest) effects [~S]
  • Less relevant for learners with strong self-efficacy, who already interpret difficulty as part of learning [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Dispositional: persistence, resilience after setbacks, willingness to attempt difficult tasks
  • Metacognitive: interpreting errors as information rather than verdicts
  • Motivational: building self-efficacy through visible incremental progress Growth-oriented attributions support persistence. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Notice fixed-ability statements ("I'm bad at math," "I can't write") and respond by appending "yet" while restating the goal as reachable: "You haven't found the strategy that works for you yet."
  2. Follow immediately with process guidance — name one specific next step or strategy (Provide Feedback) — so "yet" is backed by a route forward.
  3. Model the same language in your own self-talk when demonstrating tasks (Modeling), e.g., "I don't see the pattern yet — let me try another case."
  4. Reinforce the framing during direct instruction by normalizing errors as expected stages ("This is the point where everyone gets confused — you just haven't consolidated it yet").
  5. Track progress visibly so learners can see the "yet" resolving into mastery over time.

Related Strategies¶

  • Process-focused praise — the feedback companion to "yet"; both redirect attention from the person to the process
  • Normalizing error — shares the goal of reframing mistakes as part of learning rather than evidence of inability
  • Mastery-based progression — structural version of the same idea, where "not yet" is built into assessment states

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) — mastery tracking marks skills as "attempted," "familiar," or "proficient," encoding a not-yet progression rather than pass/fail.
  • PERTS growth mindset programs (https://www.perts.net) — scalable classroom interventions grounded in Dweck's research; teacher-response practices including "yet" framing are part of the toolkit.
  • Elementary mathematics classrooms — teacher responds to "I can't do fractions" with "You can't compare fractions yet — today we're going to learn the first step," then demonstrates that step.
