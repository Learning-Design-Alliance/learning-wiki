# Concept Mapping

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Concept maps are visual tools that help learners organize and articulate what they know about a given concept or topic. Important concepts are enclosed in a circle or box and connected by labeled links that specify the relationship (e.g., "begins with," "includes," "aids"), forming a network of propositions rather than a linear outline. Because learners must decide which concepts matter and how they relate, mapping forces generative processing of the material instead of passive rereading.

## Design Implications

Concept mapping works because it requires learners to actively construct relationships between ideas, which supports integration of new material with prior knowledge [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows that concept mapping activities generally outperform reading, lecturing, and list-making conditions for retention and transfer [Nesbit & Adesope meta-analysis.](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430629001413) [+M]. However, the labeled links are essential: maps without relational phrases degrade into clustering exercises, and the generative value comes from articulating *why* two concepts connect, not from drawing boxes.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A focused focus question or domain ("How does X affect Y?") — maps built without a question tend to be unordered brain dumps
- Structured prompts and guidelines for link labels; the instructor must model map construction before learners map independently
- Instructor feedback on the accuracy of relationships, not just completeness ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
- Sufficient working-memory support: chunking source material before mapping prevents overload [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Concept mapping is not uniformly superior to other generative activities: retrieval practice produced better delayed retention than concept mapping in direct comparison [Karpicke & Blunt retrieval-practice comparison.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327) [-S] — mapping is a construction activity, not a substitute for retrieval
- Novices can produce maps that encode misconceptions; without corrective feedback, errors become consolidated in the visual model
- Effectiveness drops when learners copy or rearrange provided terms rather than generating relationships themselves
- High element interactivity material can overload working memory during map construction, particularly for learners with low prior knowledge [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Fill-in maps**: learners complete partially built maps (links or concepts omitted) — lower load, suitable for novices
- **Collaborative mapping**: pairs negotiate link labels, exposing divergent understandings for discussion
- **Expert maps as advance organizers**: a completed map presented before instruction previews the domain structure ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- **Digital tools** (CmapTools, Miro, Lucidchart) enable revision and link annotation; paper works equally well for one-off tasks

### Target Learners
- Benefits learners across K–12, higher education, and professional training, with meta-analytic support across levels [Nesbit & Adesope meta-analysis.](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430629001413) [+M]
- Particularly valuable for learners with some prior knowledge who can generate meaningful links; complete novices may need fill-in or expert-map scaffolds first
- Less effective as a standalone study method for learners who already organize material well — retrieval practice may serve them better [Karpicke & Blunt retrieval-practice comparison.](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327) [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Relational knowledge: understanding how concepts within a domain connect, not just what they are
- Knowledge assessment: maps make learners' mental models visible, revealing misconceptions for diagnosis
- Knowledge integration: consolidating material from multiple sources into a coherent structure

### Instructions
1. Pose a focus question and, if learners are new to mapping, show a worked example map with labeled links.
2. Have learners brainstorm and list the key concepts for the topic ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Learners arrange concepts hierarchically or as a network, drawing links and writing a relational label on every link.
4. Learners explain their map aloud or in writing, articulating the propositions ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. Provide feedback on relationship accuracy and have learners revise; revisit the map after subsequent instruction ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).

## Examples
- **IHMC CmapTools** (https://cmap.ihmc.us) — free software developed by Novak's group at the Institute for Human and Machine Cognition; supports collaborative map building and proposition-level linking.
- **Science education curricula** using "expert skeleton maps" — Novak and Cañas's approach gives learners a partially completed expert map to extend, reducing initial load while preserving generative processing.
- **Medical education** — concept mapping of pathophysiology (e.g., mapping disease mechanisms in problem-based learning tutorials) to expose students' causal reasoning about patient cases.
