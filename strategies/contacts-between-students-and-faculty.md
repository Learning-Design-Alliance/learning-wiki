# Contacts Between Students And Faculty

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Contact between students and faculty is the first of Chickering and Gamson's Seven Principles for Good Practice in Undergraduate Education: frequent student–faculty interaction in and out of class, in person and online. It is carried out through responsive teaching presence — timely feedback, accessible office hours, active facilitation of discussion, informal check-ins, and mentorship — rather than through mere availability.

## Design Implications

Interaction quality matters more than quantity: contacts that convey personal interest and intellectual investment predict motivation, persistence, and achievement, while perfunctory contact does not [Astin's longitudinal data show student–faculty interaction strongly predicts satisfaction and retention.](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/What+Matters+in+College-p-9781555423041) [+S]. Frequent low-stakes contact also builds students' sense of belonging, which mediates persistence, especially for first-generation and underrepresented students [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. In online settings, instructor presence is the strongest lever: teaching presence (facilitation, direct instruction, prompt feedback) is the CoI dimension most consistently associated with perceived learning [Teaching presence in online courses supports perceived learning and satisfaction.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2007.04.001) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Multiple, low-barrier channels for contact: office hours (in person and virtual), email norms with stated response times, discussion forums with instructor participation
- Instructor behaviors that signal approachability: learning names, early-semester [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md), personalized feedback
- Structured occasions for interaction: [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), conferences, mentoring meetings
- Institutional support: reasonable advising loads, class sizes that permit individual contact

#### Constraints
- Large-enrollment courses make individualized contact impractical; scaling via TAs or automated messaging dilutes the effect unless messages are personalized [~M]
- Mandatory or performative contact (e.g., required office-hour visits with no purpose) can feel surveilling and reduce trust [-W]
- Instructor responsiveness that is slow or inconsistent is worse for belonging than no promised channel at all — unmet expectations of contact damage perceived care [-M]
- Contact alone, without intellectual substance (feedback on ideas, challenge, mentorship), does not improve outcomes; social familiarity is not pedagogy [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **In-class:** cold-calling with warmth, mid-lecture questions, one-minute papers responded to publicly
- **One-on-one:** early-semester intake meetings, feedback conferences, [5-minute writing conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)
- **Online:** video announcements, active discussion facilitation, personalized audio/video feedback, predictable response-time commitments
- **Structural:** undergraduate research advising, first-year seminars, learning-community faculty affiliation

### Target Learners
- First-year and first-generation students, who benefit most from normalizing faculty contact and building belonging early [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- At-risk or struggling students, for whom early contact enables timely intervention
- Online learners, who otherwise experience isolation and attrition at higher rates [Teaching presence in online courses supports perceived learning and satisfaction.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2007.04.001) [+M]
- Advanced students seeking mentorship, research opportunities, and career guidance

### Target Learning Goals
- Persistence, motivation, and engagement outcomes more than discrete content objectives
- Academic socialization: learning disciplinary norms and expectations through dialogue
- Feedback-responsive revision and self-regulated learning

### Instructions
1. Establish contact norms in the syllabus: response times, office-hour availability, and preferred channels.
2. Run an early-semester [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) with each student or a sample of the class to open a channel before problems arise.
3. Facilitate [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) actively rather than lecturing monologically; use student names and reference their prior contributions.
4. Return feedback quickly and personally; where volume prevents individual comments, use whole-class synthesized feedback plus targeted individual notes.
5. Maintain a visible online teaching presence (announcements, forum participation) so asynchronous students experience equivalent contact [Teaching presence in online courses supports perceived learning and satisfaction.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2007.04.001) [+M].
6. Escalate to individual outreach when engagement drops — a brief personal message outperforms an automated warning.

## Examples
- **First-year seminars** (e.g., the [AAC&U High-Impact Practices](https://www.aacu.org/trending-topics/high-impact) framework) — small classes with intensive faculty interaction are among the best-documented retention interventions.
- **Online discussion facilitation** in Canvas or Moodle courses — instructors who post weekly synthesis messages and reply substantively to students sustain teaching presence at scale.
- **Writing conferences** — brief one-on-one meetings on drafts, which convert grading into dialogue and model revision thinking.
