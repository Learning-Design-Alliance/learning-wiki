Use validated EEG and eye-tracking models to provide objective, expert-free performance and learning-rate feedback in surgical residency training¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 associational), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article proposes that its EEG and eye-tracking regression models, once validated, let surgical residency programs evaluate trainee performance and learning rate objectively without an expert RAS surgeon present during practice. The authors state the models "provide objective, unbiased assessments of RAS trainees' performance without needing an expert RAS surgeon's presence during practice sessions", reducing costs, enabling immediate feedback, and allowing first-attempt data to predict learning rates so programs can plan strategies for slower learners.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Validation with a broader sample size, more diverse subjects across hospitals and specialties, and additional surgical tasks, as the authors state

Constraints¶

  • The study was conducted within a single U.S. health system, so findings may not apply to other institutions, specialties, or countries
  • Moderate R2 values of the learning-rate models (0.64, 0.71, 0.69) are attributed to limited sample sizes
  • Practical application of EEG and sensor-based techniques is limited by difficulty of use and potential equipment disruptions

Target Learners¶

  • RAS surgical trainees (residents, fellows, surgeons in training)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Robot-assisted surgical psychomotor skills (tissue manipulation, needle driving, dexterity, precision) and their rate of acquisition

Claims¶

  • EEG-derived temporal flexibility and search information in specific Brodmann areas and frequency bands are associated with simulator task performance [+W]
  • First-attempt EEG features and initial performance score predict learning rate, with higher initial scores associated with lower learning rates [+W]
  • In the Tubes task, larger average pupil diameter of the nondominant eye and higher rate of saccade are associated with lower RAS simulator performance [+W]
  • In the Dots and Needles task, higher entropy of the nondominant eye's pupil diameter is associated with lower performance [+W]
  • No significant correlation between hours of RAS practice and simulator task performance [+W]
  • No significant correlation between simulator performance and self-reported mental workload (SURG-TLX) [+W]
