# Case Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (6 for) · 12 studies (6 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 12 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Case Based Learning (CBL) places learners in the role of analyst or decision-maker confronting a realistic scenario — a patient history, a business dilemma, a legal dispute, an engineering failure. Learners examine the case's details, identify the central problem, apply relevant concepts, and defend a course of action, typically through structured [discussion](../elements/discussion.md) and [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md). Unlike [Problem-Based Learning](../patterns/problem-based-learning.md), CBL usually supplies a fuller, pre-constructed narrative rather than an open problem statement.

## Design Implications

CBL works because it situates abstract concepts in the messy, contextualized conditions under which they must later be applied, supporting transfer and retention [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the case being *analyzed*, not merely read: structured prompts, small-group discussion, and required justifications convert a story into reasoning practice [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Multiple varied cases are more effective than a single exemplar for building flexible, adaptable knowledge structures [Multiple varied cases support flexible knowledge under Cognitive Flexibility Theory.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-written case with authentic complexity, ambiguity, and enough data to support multiple defensible positions
- Explicit analysis scaffolds: guiding questions, [advance organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), or an analysis framework (e.g., issue → evidence → options → recommendation)
- A facilitation structure — small-group work followed by whole-class debrief — so learners compare reasoning rather than only conclusions
- Clear linkage between the case and the target concepts; cases chosen for *conceptual fit*, not just narrative appeal
- A well-documented, realistic case with genuine decision points and no obvious single answer
- Preparation materials that establish the prerequisite concepts before analysis begins
- Structured discussion protocols or analysis frameworks (e.g., issue → analysis → recommendation)
- A facilitator skilled at probing reasoning rather than lecturing ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Closure: an expert debrief or model analysis so learners can calibrate their reasoning
- A well-written case with genuine decision points, not a thinly disguised illustration of a textbook answer
- Preparation structures (pre-reading questions, response submissions) that ensure learners arrive having committed to an analysis
- A facilitation plan: probing questions, board plans, and willingness to let discussion go somewhere unplanned
- Explicit connection back to course concepts during or after discussion ([Debrief](../elements/debrief.md))

#### Constraints
- Cases that are engaging but conceptually thin produce lively discussion with little durable learning; alignment to learning goals must be explicit
- Without structure, novices flounder in case complexity and extract surface stories rather than underlying principles [~M] — scaffolds should [fade](../elements/fading.md) as analytical skill grows
- Large-enrollment settings struggle with the discussion-intensive format; written case analyses or structured online threads are partial substitutes [~W]
- Learners with minimal prior knowledge may lack the domain schema to interpret the case at all; brief pre-reading or [activation](../elements/activation.md) activities are needed first [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Cases without closure or expert comparison leave novices with unresolved misconceptions — learners may encode flawed reasoning as readily as sound reasoning [-M]
- Novices without sufficient domain knowledge can flounder in unstructured case discussion, engaging in trial-and-error search rather than principled analysis [~M]
- A single memorable case can induce overgeneralization; learners may treat one context's solution as universal [-W]
- Group discussion dominated by a few voices reduces the retrieval practice that drives the effect [-M]
- Cases with an obvious single "right answer" collapse into recall exercises and forfeit the judgment-building benefit [-M]
- Novices without adequate domain knowledge can flounder in open discussion, encoding surface details rather than principles [~M] — some prior instruction or scaffolding is usually needed first
- Large classes without discussion infrastructure reduce the method to lecture with a story attached [-M]
- Poorly facilitated discussion can entrench misconceptions if errors go unchallenged [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Harvard method**: cold-call-driven whole-class case discussion typical of business and law schools — see [Case-Based Learning: Harvard Method](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)
- **Small-group CBL**: used extensively in medical education, often with a facilitator per group (Thistlethwaite et al., 2012)
- **Trigger-short cases**: brief vignettes that launch discussion, blending CBL with problem-based formats
- **Asynchronous online CBL**: cases posted with staged releases of information and structured discussion boards
- **Progressive disclosure** (Harvard method): information is released in stages; learners must commit to decisions before seeing consequences
- **Contrasting case sets**: several short cases varying one dimension, supporting abstraction of underlying principles
- **Erroneous or flawed cases**: learners diagnose what went wrong, leveraging error analysis
- **Student-authored cases**: learners construct cases from their own experience, shifting the goal toward synthesis
- **Discussion-first (Harvard method):** cold-calling and board-driven whole-class analysis of a case learners have prepared in advance
- **Small-group analysis:** teams work a case then report out; scales better and adds peer explanation
- **Progressive disclosure:** the case unfolds in stages and learners commit to decisions before seeing consequences, mirroring real decision pressure
- **Multimedia/anchored cases:** video-based cases (e.g., the Jasper Woodbury series) embed data in rich narrative contexts

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied reasoning: connecting principles to situated decisions
- Diagnostic and judgment skills: weighing incomplete, conflicting evidence
- Argumentation and justification: defending and revising positions under peer scrutiny
- Flexible knowledge structures: seeing one concept across many contexts [Multiple varied cases support flexible knowledge under Cognitive Flexibility Theory.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M]
- Applied reasoning and diagnosis: connecting principles to ambiguous situations
- Decision-making under uncertainty: weighing trade-offs and justifying choices
- Professional identity and judgment: practicing the role, not just the content
- Applied reasoning: using concepts to diagnose, decide, and justify
- Professional judgment and argumentation: weighing competing values and evidence
- Perspective-taking: understanding stakeholder positions within a situation

### Instructions
1. Select or write a case aligned to target concepts, with genuine ambiguity and decision points ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. Activate relevant prior knowledge with a short pre-brief or framing question ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
3. Have learners analyze the case in small groups using an explicit framework, producing a position with justification ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md))
4. Debrief in whole class, contrasting groups' reasoning and surfacing the underlying principles ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md))
5. Follow with a second, structurally different case so learners must adapt rather than repeat their first solution [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
