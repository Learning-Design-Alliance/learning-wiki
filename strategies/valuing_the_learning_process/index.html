Valuing the Learning Process¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Valuing the learning process means structuring instruction, feedback, and evaluation so that learners experience understanding, exploration, and productive struggle as the point of the activity — not merely the route to a grade. In practice this involves framing tasks as opportunities to grow, rewarding strategy use and revision, and de-emphasizing social comparison and normative evaluation. The goal is to shift learners toward a mastery (task-involved) goal orientation rather than a performance (ego-involved) one.

Design Implications¶

Mastery-oriented classroom climates produce higher engagement, persistence after difficulty, and deeper strategy use than performance-oriented climates Ames, 1992 [+S]. When evaluation emphasizes progress and understanding rather than ability ranking, learners are more willing to attempt challenging tasks and to treat errors as information rather than as verdicts on their worth Butler, 1988 [+M]. Autonomy-supportive framing — offering choice and rationale rather than controlling evaluation — sustains intrinsic motivation that grades alone tend to undermine Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Feedback that targets the task and the process (strategy, effort, revision) rather than the person Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Opportunities to revise and resubmit work, so improvement is visibly valued over first-attempt performance
  • Public framing of errors as normal and informative — e.g., analyzing Erroneous Examples or discussing high-confidence misconceptions High-confidence errors improve retention. [+M]
  • Assessment practices that report growth and criterion mastery, not just scores (Assessment for Learning)

Constraints¶

  • Grading on a curve and public ranking directly contradict this strategy; normative evaluation reliably shifts learners toward performance goals Butler, 1988 [-S]
  • Praise for ability ("you're so smart") reinforces a fixed view and reduces persistence after failure; process praise is required instead Dweck, 1986 [-M]
  • In high-stakes credentialing contexts, learners' attention follows what is graded; ungraded process work is deprioritized unless it carries visible weight [~M]
  • Vague exhortations to "value the journey" without structural change (revisable work, criterion-referenced grading) produce no measurable shift in orientation [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Revision cycles: draft–feedback–revise structures in writing and design courses
  • Mastery grading: standards-based or competency-based grading that reports criterion attainment rather than averaged points
  • Error-positive routines: "favorite mistake" discussions, error analysis tasks, normalized Check-Ins on confusion
  • Process portfolios: documenting drafts, reflections, and strategy changes as assessable artifacts

Target Learners¶

  • Learners who have experienced repeated failure or grade anxiety, for whom performance framing triggers avoidance Dweck, 1986 [+M]
  • Struggling learners who disengage when errors are publicly penalized
  • High-achievers in competitive climates, who often avoid challenge to protect perceived ability [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Deep conceptual understanding and transfer, rather than recall for a test
  • Self-regulated learning: monitoring, strategy selection, and revision habits Self-Regulated Learning
  • Persistence and productive struggle on challenging, open-ended tasks

Instructions¶

  1. Frame each task in terms of what learners will come to understand, not what score they will earn; provide a rationale for the activity (Activation)
  2. Design at least one low-stakes attempt before any graded evaluation, and give Feedback at the task and process levels Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  3. Build in structured revision so improvement is rewarded; use criterion-referenced Assessment rather than curves or rankings
  4. Normalize errors by analyzing flawed worked examples or common misconceptions as class content Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M]
  5. Praise strategy use, effort, and progress specifically — never ability in the abstract Dweck, 1986 [+M]
  6. Offer meaningful choices in topic, method, or pacing to support autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]

Related Strategies¶

  • Formative Assessment — the assessment infrastructure that makes process-focused evaluation possible
  • Competency-Based Learning — grading on criterion mastery rather than averages enacts process valuation structurally
  • Coaching — ongoing process-level feedback during work, not just after it

Examples¶

  • Standards-based grading in K-12 mathematics (e.g., the practices popularized by Shawn Cornally's Think Thank Thunk and embedded in many SBG implementations): grades report mastery of specific standards, and reassessment is always available, so a low score signals "not yet" rather than failure.
  • Writing process portfolios in composition courses (following NWP approaches): drafts, peer feedback, and revision memos are assessed alongside the final piece, making the process itself the object of evaluation.
  • Khan Academy — mastery-based progress tracking with unlimited retries on exercises; the interface frames struggle as normal ("keep practicing") rather than as failure, and hints are always available without penalty.
