# Improve simulation-based learning through curriculum integration, increased capacity with low-cost local materials, open access, and a designated simulation coordinator

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article's participants and authors propose concrete improvements for simulation-based medical education in Rwanda: integrate simulation into the curriculum with clear learning goals and early, regular skills training; schedule multiple smaller-group sessions throughout clinical rotations; and expand access so facilities can be used like a library. Students praised innovative low-cost approaches, reporting "We used papayas and tree tomatoes to simulate the uterus, and I learned a lot from that training." The authors add that "a designated training staff member should be assigned to coordinate simulation-based learning activities to ensure students have opportunities for repeated practice and feedback."

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Better curriculum design, pedagogical principles, and local innovative solutions rather than necessarily more scarce resources
- Well-defined, clear learning goals with consistency and regularity throughout training
- Trained simulationists to support both group and individual practice
#### Constraints
- Resource-limited setting with shortages of staff, space, and equipment; findings come from a single institution with 15 participants

### Target Learners
- undergraduate medical students in low-income or resource-limited settings

### Target Learning Goals
- procedural skills acquisition, confidence, and readiness to practice
