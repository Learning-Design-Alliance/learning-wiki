Sentence Expansion¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (5 causal, 3 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Sentence expansion gives learners a short, often kernel sentence (e.g., "The boy ran.") and asks them to elaborate it by answering question prompts — who, what, when, where, why, how — or by embedding appositives, subordinating conjunctions, and adverbial phrases. It is carried out as a brief, frequent activity, often at the start of a lesson, and is a core activity in The Writing Revolution method (Hochman & Wexler, 2017). Because the content is supplied, learners can devote full attention to syntax rather than invention.

Design Implications¶

Sentence expansion isolates sentence-level syntax as the unit of practice, reducing the competing demands of idea generation, organization, and transcription that normally consume working memory during writing Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+S]. By supplying the content, the activity functions as a constrained practice task in which learners manipulate grammatical structures deliberately rather than incidentally. Its effectiveness depends on connecting the sentences to curriculum content, so that syntactic practice simultaneously deepens comprehension of the subject matter Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A base sentence drawn from the current lesson's content, not from decontextualized grammar exercises
  • Explicit teaching of the target structures (subordinators, appositives, question words) before learners are asked to use them
  • Brief, frequent practice cycles rather than long isolated sessions; sentence-level work compounds over time like other forms of distributed practice [+S]
  • Feedback that names the structural move made ("You added a because clause — that shows cause")

Constraints¶

  • Expansion without a target structure devolves into word-stuffing; learners add adjectives rather than practicing subordinate clauses [-M]
  • Decontextualized sentences (unrelated to lesson content) forfeit the comprehension benefit and reduce transfer to actual writing [-M]
  • Over-reliance on formulaic prompts can produce stilted, template-driven prose if expansion is never faded into free composition [~M]
  • Less valuable for learners who already write complex sentences fluently; the exercise becomes busywork [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Question-prompt expansion: learners answer who/what/when/where/why/how prompts to grow the sentence
  • Sentence combining: learners merge two or more short sentences into one, practicing embedding and coordination (Saddler & Graham, 2005)
  • Because/But/So frames: learners complete sentence stems with each conjunction, forcing distinct logical relationships with the same content
  • Appositive and subordinator insertion: more advanced variants targeting specific grammatical moves
  • Oral-first expansion: learners expand sentences aloud before writing, useful for emerging writers

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and adolescent writers who produce short, simple sentences Sentence-combining instruction improves writing quality for less skilled writers. [~W]
  • English language learners, for whom supplied content lowers the language-production burden while syntax is practiced explicitly
  • Struggling writers; benefits diminish for proficient writers who already embed clauses routinely [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Syntactic fluency: producing grammatically complex sentences on demand
  • Reading comprehension: unpacking how sentence structure encodes relationships (cause, contrast, sequence)
  • Content knowledge: elaborating lesson concepts through restatement and expansion
  • Logical relationships: distinguishing cause (because), contrast (but), and consequence (so)

Instructions¶

  1. Select or co-construct a kernel sentence from the lesson content (e.g., "The Romans built roads.").
  2. Model one expansion aloud, naming the structural move (adding a when clause), consistent with direct instruction.
  3. Have learners expand the sentence using a specified prompt or conjunction frame — one target structure at a time.
  4. Compare two or three learner expansions as a class, discussing how each changes meaning.
  5. Fade the scaffolds: move from prompted expansion to free expansion, then require complex sentences in authentic writing tasks.

Related Strategies¶

  • Sentence combining — the merging variant of the same sentence-level practice
  • Accessible syntax — teaching sentence structures learners can then deploy in expansion
  • Activate background knowledge — expansion works best when the base sentence taps content learners already understand
  • Use sentence construction activities to teach compound, complex, and compound-complex sentence types

Examples¶

  • The Writing Revolution — Hochman & Wexler's method embeds sentence expansion, because/but/so frames, and sentence combining into content instruction across grade levels.
  • Middle-school history lesson — students expand "The Silk Road connected civilizations." using because, but, and so stems, practicing causal reasoning about trade while rehearsing subordination.
  • Saddler & Graham (2005) — fourth-grade students received peer-assisted sentence-combining instruction; less skilled writers produced significantly longer, higher-quality compositions.
