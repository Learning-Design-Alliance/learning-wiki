# Creating a Culture of Error

## Description
Creating a culture of error means deliberately establishing norms — through instructor modeling, feedback practices, and task design — in which errors are expected, surfaced, and analyzed rather than hidden or punished. Learners are asked to attempt work publicly, share incorrect reasoning, and examine why errors occur. The strategy draws on research showing that errors, when engaged with productively, deepen conceptual understanding and that fear of failure suppresses the very behaviors (questioning, risk-taking, help-seeking) that drive learning.

## Design Implications

A culture of error changes what learners do with mistakes: instead of concealing them, learners expose them for analysis, which supports error-based learning and self-correction. Structured engagement with errors — such as analyzing flawed worked examples — builds conceptual knowledge more effectively than error-free instruction alone [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. The social dimension matters: learners must believe they will not be embarrassed or penalized for errors, which depends on belonging and psychological safety [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Feedback within this culture should target the task and the reasoning process, not the person, so that error discussion stays diagnostic rather than evaluative [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructor modeling: the teacher openly analyzes their own errors and treats student errors as interesting data ("What does this mistake teach us?") rather than as deficiencies
- Norms that decouple error from status — e.g., no ridicule, credit for productive revision, public celebration of "favorite mistakes"
- Tasks with genuine room for error: low-stakes attempts, drafts, and [erroneous examples](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) to analyze
- Feedback routines that are specific, process-focused, and actionable [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Growth-mindset and error-tolerance messaging alone, without changed grading and task structures, produces weak or no effects [Growth mindset interventions show small, context-dependent effects.](../claims/growth-mindset-interventions-small-effects.md) [~M] — exhortation cannot substitute for structural safety
- In high-stakes, grade-dominated environments, learners rationally conceal errors; a culture of error cannot coexist with punitive error accounting [-M]
- Excessive tolerance for error without correction can entrench misconceptions; errors must be surfaced *and* resolved, not merely accepted [-M]
- Learners with histories of failure may initially resist public error disclosure; trust builds gradually and cannot be demanded on day one [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Anonymous error analysis**: collect common errors and discuss them without attribution — lowers social risk while preserving the learning value
- **Erroneous-example routines**: present a flawed solution and ask learners to find, diagnose, and repair the error
- **Productive failure sequencing**: let learners attempt problems before instruction, then compare their failed approaches to canonical solutions [Productive failure improves conceptual learning.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]
- **Digital environments**: platforms with immediate, encouraging feedback (e.g., low-stakes quizzing with retry) normalize error at scale

### Target Learners
- Learners with low prior knowledge, who generate the most errors and benefit most from error-based analysis [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Students at risk of disengagement, for whom belonging and safety signals are especially consequential [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- High-achieving learners accustomed to error-free performance may need the most explicit norm-setting, as error threatens their identity [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: diagnosing *why* an error occurs requires engaging with underlying structure
- Self-regulation and self-correction: learners develop error-monitoring habits
- Risk-taking and persistence: willingness to attempt difficult problems without certainty
- Help-seeking and questioning: norms that make confusion discussable

### Instructions
1. **Set norms explicitly.** On day one, name the expectation that errors are data; model it by analyzing your own mistakes aloud.
2. **Design low-stakes attempts.** Use drafts, retries, and ungraded problem attempts so errors carry no grade penalty.
3. **Surface errors systematically.** Collect and anonymize common errors, or present prepared erroneous examples for diagnosis [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M].
4. **Give process-focused feedback.** Respond to errors by pointing at reasoning and strategy, not ability or effort platitudes [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. **Require revision.** Close the loop: learners correct and resubmit, making error resolution part of the workflow.
6. **Protect belonging.** Monitor for ridicule or status loss; intervene immediately, since a single public shaming can undo weeks of norm-building [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

## Related Strategies
- **Formative assessment** — error surfacing is the raw material of formative feedback loops
- **Productive failure** — a task-sequencing variant in which errors precede instruction by design
- **Growth mindset framing** — the belief component; effective only when paired with structural changes described here

## Examples
- **My Favorite Mistake routine** (widely used in math classrooms, popularized by Teach Like a Champion practice): the teacher selects an anonymous student error and leads the class in analyzing what the error reveals and how to fix it, positioning the error-maker as having contributed something valuable.
- **Productive failure lessons** (Kapur's published studies in Singapore secondary math classrooms): students attempt novel problems in small groups before any instruction, then compare their invented (often wrong) methods with the canonical solution.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-based practice with unlimited retries and hint scaffolds; the interface treats incorrect answers as steps in a retry loop rather than terminal judgments.
- **Code review culture in CS courses** — peer review routines that frame bugs as normal artifacts of programming, with explicit norms against blaming the author.
