# E.S.C.A.P.E. Junk News

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
E.S.C.A.P.E. is a six-part heuristic developed by the Newseum (NewseumEd) for evaluating the credibility of news and online information. Each letter names a lens: **E**vidence, **S**ource, **C**ontext, **A**udience, **P**urpose, **E**xecution. Considering even one of the six concepts can help determine whether information is reliable; together they form a reusable checklist students can apply to any story.

## Design Implications

The strategy converts expert source-evaluation into a compact, memorable schema, reducing the working-memory burden of open-ended judgment [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on pairing the checklist with authentic practice on real stories: research on civic online reasoning shows that students taught to evaluate sources through repeated application of concrete criteria substantially outperform peers who receive no such instruction, while most students otherwise fail to evaluate even obviously unreliable content [Students' civic online reasoning improves with explicit instruction and practice.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]. The checklist is a starting frame, not a substitute for the deeper move of leaving the page to verify claims elsewhere (lateral reading).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to real news stories, social media posts, and websites for analysis
- A worked demonstration of the evaluation process before independent application ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of an expert evaluating a story)
- Follow-up practice with feedback on judgments, not just completion of the checklist

#### Constraints
- Requires critical thinking skills and attention to detail; students with weak background knowledge may apply the criteria superficially, ticking boxes without genuine judgment
- Checklist-style evaluation alone is weaker than lateral-reading strategies in which students leave the page to check the source; heuristic application without verification can produce false confidence [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when students only evaluate obviously fake examples — they then fail to transfer scrutiny to plausible-but-biased sources [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Introduce one letter at a time across a unit rather than all six at once, using [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) for each
- Adapt vocabulary and complexity for grade level (picture-based "evidence" hunts for younger students; full source-tracing for older ones)
- Combine with [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) tasks: students must justify a credibility verdict using ESCAPE criteria as evidence

### Target Learners
- Grades 6–12 and college/university students encountering news and social media information daily
- Students with limited media-literacy background benefit most from the explicit structure; advanced students may find the checklist redundant and should move to open-ended source investigation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluate news stories for credibility and trustworthiness
- Identify evidence, purpose, and audience in persuasive media
- Transfer evaluative habits to independent information use outside class

### Instructions
1. Select a current, authentic story (mix of reliable, biased, and fabricated sources).
2. Model the full evaluation aloud, walking through each ESCAPE lens on one story ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Have students evaluate a second story in pairs, applying each criterion and recording evidence for their judgments ([Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md): "Who is the audience, and how does that shape the presentation?").
4. Compare verdicts across groups and resolve disagreements by returning to the text — disagreements create productive [cognitive disequilibrium](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M].
5. Fade the checklist: students evaluate new stories without the scaffold, then justify their reasoning in writing.

## Examples
- **[NewseumEd](https://newseumed.org)** — the original home of E.S.C.A.P.E. Junk News, with free classroom posters, worksheets, and curated "junk news" story sets for grades 6–12.
- **[Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — free curriculum and assessments teaching lateral reading and source evaluation; its assessments show most students struggle to evaluate sources without such instruction.
- A civics teacher gives pairs three stories on the same event (wire service, partisan blog, fabricated site) and asks them to rank credibility using ESCAPE, then defend the ranking in a short written verdict.
