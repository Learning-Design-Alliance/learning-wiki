# Dolch Word List - Word Search Puzzles

## Description
This strategy uses word search puzzles whose target words are drawn from the Dolch Word List — the 220 high-frequency service words compiled by Edward W. Dolch (1936) that account for a large share of running text in early reading materials. Learners scan letter grids to locate sight words, providing repeated visual exposure intended to build automatic word recognition. The puzzles function as low-stakes, self-checkable practice rather than primary instruction.

## Design Implications

The rationale is that automatic recognition of high-frequency words frees working memory and attention for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Word searches supply repeated visual exposure, but exposure alone is weak for building orthographic representations; recognition is strengthened when visual scanning is paired with reading, writing, and spelling the same words [~M]. Because the Dolch list is fixed and finite, puzzles can be sequenced by frequency and revisited over time, which aligns with spaced practice [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated subset of the Dolch list appropriate to the learner's current level (pre-primer through grade 3 bands)
- Grids where target words are visually salient and distractor letters do not accidentally form other words
- A follow-on activity requiring learners to *read or write* the located words, not just circle them ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Prior or concurrent explicit instruction on the words — puzzles reinforce, they do not teach ([Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md))

#### Constraints
- Word searches require letter-by-letter scanning, which can encourage decoding-like attention to letters rather than whole-word recognition — the opposite of the fluency goal [~W]
- Sight-word drill without phonics support limits generalization to novel words; phonics-based approaches outperform whole-word methods for transfer [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [-S]
- As a game-like activity, word searches can consume instructional time with low learning yield relative to reading connected text or spelling the same words [-M]
- Learners who have already achieved automaticity gain little from further puzzle exposure; the benefit is confined to words not yet recognized instantly [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Word-shape variants:** grids that preserve word shape (ascenders/descenders) to support orthographic mapping
- **Read-then-find:** learners read the word aloud before searching, pairing phonological form with visual form
- **Write-after-find:** learners write each located word from memory after circling it, adding a retrieval component
- **Sentence context:** located words are immediately used in a dictated sentence, connecting sight words to meaning

### Target Learners
- Early readers (K–2) still building automatic recognition of high-frequency words [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Struggling readers who need low-anxiety, self-checkable practice with irregular or high-frequency words
- Less appropriate for learners already reading fluently, for whom the activity adds no new learning [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Automatic word recognition: instant identification of high-frequency words without decoding
- Orthographic mapping: linking spelling patterns to pronunciations for irregular words
- Reading fluency: reducing pauses at common words in connected text

### Instructions
1. Select 5–8 Dolch words from the learner's current band; pre-teach each word explicitly ([Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md)).
2. Have learners read each target word aloud before searching ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Learners locate the words in the grid ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Learners write each found word from memory, then read it in a short sentence ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
5. Reintroduce the same words in new puzzles across days rather than in one session ([Spaced Repetition](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md)).
