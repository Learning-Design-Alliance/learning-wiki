# Math Games and Movement Breaks

## Description
Short physical math games and movement breaks are inserted into lessons when learners become restless or attention wanes. They serve two functions at once: restoring attention through brief physical activity, and giving learners a second, embodied encounter with the math concept being taught. Games range from teacher-led whole-class activities (stand up/sit down, finger speed-sums, group formation games) to student-pair games, and require no special materials.

## Design Implications

Brief bouts of physical activity during academic time are associated with improved attention and on-task behavior in children [Hillman et al. found acute exercise improves executive attention in children.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0812640106) [+M], and embedding activity into academic content preserves instructional time rather than competing with it [Donnelly & Lambourne found classroom-based physical activity supports or improves achievement.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2011.02.007) [+M]. The math content in the game should be retrieval practice for recently taught material, not new instruction — the break refreshes attention while the game itself strengthens recall [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A small repertoire of no-materials games the teacher can launch in under 30 seconds
- Games that rehearse already-taught content (e.g., speed-sums, skip counting, number-bond games), not new concepts
- Clear start/stop signals so the break stays short and returns cleanly to instruction
- Teacher enthusiasm and participation — the energy of the activity depends on the teacher modeling it

#### Constraints
- Games lose their attention-restoring effect if they run too long or become routine; novelty and brevity are essential [~M]
- Poorly matched game difficulty (too easy or too hard) turns the break into off-task time rather than practice [~M]
- Student-pair games require an even number of students or a teacher-participant fallback
- Games that reward speed can disadvantage anxious learners and reinforce the belief that math is about fast answers [~M] — use games where accuracy, not speed, determines success when math anxiety is a concern
- High-energy games immediately before tasks requiring sustained quiet focus can leave some classes dysregulated rather than refreshed [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher-led whole-class games** (stand up/sit down for true-false equations, group formation by number properties) — lowest setup cost, works with any class size
- **Student-pair games** (finger speed-sums, "war" variants with operations) — increase [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) and self-correction
- **Pure movement breaks** with no math content — still restore attention, but forfeit the retrieval-practice benefit
- Games can be adapted by students themselves, adding ownership and variation

### Target Learners
- K–4 students, whose attention spans and developmental need for movement make seated instruction for long blocks counterproductive [Hillman et al. found acute exercise improves executive attention in children.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0812640106) [+M]
- Restless or low-attention learners, for whom the break is a regulation tool as much as a learning activity
- Learners building fluency with basic facts, who benefit from repeated low-stakes retrieval [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Less suited to older learners working on complex multi-step problems, where the interruption costs more than the refresh gains [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Basic-fact fluency and automaticity through repeated retrieval
- Attention restoration and readiness for subsequent instruction
- Positive affect toward mathematics — associating math with play rather than threat
- Embodied understanding of number concepts (grouping, magnitude, parity)

### Instructions
1. Watch for attention signals (fidgeting, off-task talk) indicating a break is needed — typically after 15–25 minutes of seated work.
2. Launch a no-materials game that rehearses recently taught content, e.g., stand up/sit down for true-false equations or finger speed-sums in pairs.
3. Keep the game to 2–5 minutes with a clear stop signal.
4. Have students briefly explain or justify one answer aloud during the game ([Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md)) so the game includes reasoning, not just recall.
5. Return directly to instruction, ideally at a natural transition point, and follow with targeted [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on the same content.

## Examples
- **Stand up/sit down**: teacher displays an equation (e.g., 7 × 8 = 54); students stand if true, sit if false — whole-class, no materials, immediate feedback visible to the teacher.
- **Finger speed-sums**: pairs flash fingers 1–5 each; first to call the sum wins the round — builds addition fluency with built-in [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md).
- **Group formation**: teacher calls "groups of 4" or "groups with an even number of people"; students form groups — embodied practice with divisibility and number properties.
- **GoNoodle** (https://www.gonoodle.com) — commercial movement-break videos; use the math-content channels or pair a generic break with a quick oral math game to retain the retrieval benefit.
