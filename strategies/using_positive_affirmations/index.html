Using Positive Affirmations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Using positive affirmations is a strategy in which learners identify negative messages from their inner voice and deliberately replace them with positive statements of self-worth, capability, or growth ("I can improve with practice," "I belong in this class"). Affirmations may be self-generated or drawn from pre-made lists, and are typically rehearsed at regular intervals — at the start of a day, before a challenging task, or during reflection. The strategy draws on self-affirmation theory, which holds that affirming core personal values buffers defensive responses to threat and supports openness to learning Cohen & Sherman, 2014 [+M].

Design Implications¶

Affirmations work by protecting a learner's sense of self-integrity, which reduces the threat response that otherwise consumes working memory and attention during difficult tasks Cohen & Sherman, 2014 [+M]. Value-affirmation interventions have improved academic outcomes for students under stereotype threat or identity threat Sherman et al., 2009 [+S]. However, the strategy is fragile: generic positive self-statements can backfire for learners with low self-esteem, who experience them as unbelievable and contradictory to their self-view Wood, Perunovic, & Lee, 2009 [-M]. Effective implementation therefore favors process-focused, believable statements over grandiose ones Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Time for learners to identify their own negative self-talk before replacing it — the contrast is what makes the affirmation meaningful
  • Affirmations that are specific, plausible, and tied to effort or process rather than fixed outcomes
  • Regular, repeated use; one-off affirmation activities show weak or no lasting effects Cohen & Sherman, 2014 [~M]
  • A supportive classroom climate so affirmations do not feel forced or performative

Constraints¶

  • Generic positive self-statements ("I am lovable") can worsen mood and self-evaluation for learners with low self-esteem, who reject them as false Wood, Perunovic, & Lee, 2009 [-M]
  • Affirmations without accompanying skill-building or practice can produce confidence without competence, and the mismatch eventually undermines credibility [-M]
  • Effects are strongest under conditions of threat or adversity; for learners facing no self-relevant threat, affirmation activities add little Cohen & Sherman, 2014 [~M]
  • Benefits fade over time without periodic re-administration; single-session interventions show diminishing returns [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Value affirmations — learners write about a core personal value (family, creativity, relationships) rather than a self-statement; this is the form with the strongest experimental support Sherman et al., 2009 [+S]
  • Growth-mindset affirmations — statements emphasizing malleability of ability, often paired with explicit mindset instruction Blackwell, Trzesniewski, & Dweck, 2007 [+M]
  • Written journals vs. oral routines — writing produces more elaboration; oral group routines are faster but risk insincerity
  • Embedded prompts — affirmations delivered just before high-stakes tasks (tests, presentations) rather than as standalone routines

Target Learners¶

  • Students experiencing academic or identity threat, for whom value affirmation buffers performance costs Sherman et al., 2009 [+S]
  • Learners whose self-efficacy is fragile; affirmations support persistence when paired with genuine skill development Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less suitable, as generic self-statements, for learners with low self-esteem, who may respond negatively Wood, Perunovic, & Lee, 2009 [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and motivational goals: self-efficacy, resilience, and willingness to persist through difficulty Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Self-regulation: noticing and restructuring negative self-talk is a form of metacognitive monitoring Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Not a substitute for cognitive goals — affirmations support the conditions for learning, not learning itself

Instructions¶

  1. Model the contrast. Use direct instruction to show what negative self-talk sounds like and how it affects effort and attention.
  2. Identify negative messages. Learners reflect individually on recurring negative thoughts, using individual reflection or a journal.
  3. Craft replacements. Learners write specific, believable, process-focused affirmations ("I can solve this if I break it into steps") rather than absolute claims.
  4. Rehearse regularly. Build a brief daily or pre-task routine; repetition is required for effects to emerge Cohen & Sherman, 2014 [+M].
  5. Connect to action. Pair each affirmation with an immediate learning behavior — attempting the task, using a strategy — so the statement is validated by experience practice.
  6. Reflect on impact. Periodically revisit journals to notice changes in self-talk and persistence.

Related Strategies¶

  • Growth mindset framing — affirmations about malleable ability are a delivery vehicle for mindset messages
  • Journaling — the written medium through which value affirmations are most often administered
  • Self-monitoring — noticing negative self-talk is the prerequisite step

Examples¶

  • Value-affirmation writing before assessments — In the Sherman et al. (2009) studies, middle-school students wrote a few sentences about a personally important value at the start of the term; the intervention narrowed the racial achievement gap over two years.
  • Morning affirmation routines in elementary classrooms — Teachers open the day with students choosing or composing one affirmation, then revisiting it before challenging work.
  • Pre-test self-talk scripts — Students prepare a short script of process-focused statements ("I've practiced this; I'll work through it step by step") to read before exams.
