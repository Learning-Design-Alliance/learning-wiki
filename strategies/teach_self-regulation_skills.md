# Teach Self-Regulation Skills

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy makes the processes of self-regulation — goal setting, strategy use, self-monitoring, and self-evaluation — an explicit object of instruction rather than an assumed prerequisite. Learners are taught concrete tactics (planning before working, checking comprehension while reading, revising after feedback) and given structured opportunities to apply and reflect on them. Instruction typically follows a cycle of modeling, guided practice, and independent application with fading support.

## Design Implications

Self-regulation instruction works best when strategies are taught within subject-matter tasks rather than as generic study skills; embedded instruction produces larger and more durable gains than decontextualized training [Self-regulation strategy instruction improves academic performance, especially when embedded in content.](../claims/self-regulation-strategy-instruction-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Teachers should model the strategies aloud — making monitoring and decision-making visible — then progressively hand responsibility to learners [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit modeling of strategies, ideally via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) so learners hear the internal monitoring dialogue
- Structured tools that externalize regulation: goal sheets, planning templates, self-check rubrics, progress trackers
- Regular [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines that prompt learners to assess their own state and progress
- Feedback that targets processes and self-regulation, not just the task outcome [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Generic "learning to learn" courses detached from content show weak transfer; strategies must be practiced in the domain where they are needed [-M]
- Over-scaffolding self-monitoring (excessive checklists, constant teacher prompting) can displace learners' own regulation and create dependence [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~M]
- Young learners and novices often cannot accurately self-assess; unguided self-evaluation can reinforce miscalibration [-M]
- Regulation demands consume working memory; introducing new strategies during high-load tasks can impair performance [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Cognitive regulation: planning, monitoring comprehension, strategy selection during academic tasks
- Behavioral regulation: attention management, persistence, help-seeking routines
- Emotional regulation: recognizing frustration, reappraisal, and recovery strategies before re-engaging
- Social regulation: co-regulation in [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md), where groups negotiate goals and monitor shared progress

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners and students with attention or executive-function challenges, who benefit most from explicit strategy instruction [Self-regulation strategy instruction improves academic performance, especially when embedded in content.](../claims/self-regulation-strategy-instruction-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Adolescents and adults developing independent study habits; younger children need more external structure and co-regulation before self-regulation is realistic
- Learners whose motivation is undermined by external control benefit when strategy instruction is paired with choice [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive goals: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning
- Procedural goals: acquiring and flexibly deploying study and problem-solving strategies
- Dispositional goals: persistence, help-seeking, and adaptive response to difficulty and failure

### Instructions
1. **Model the strategy.** Demonstrate planning, monitoring, and revision via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) on an authentic task, naming each regulatory move.
2. **Guided practice.** Work through tasks with [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md) — prompts, templates, and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — while learners begin applying the strategy themselves.
3. **Prompt self-monitoring.** Insert [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) points where learners compare their progress against their plan and adjust.
4. **Provide process feedback.** Give feedback on strategy use and self-regulation, not just correctness [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. **Fade support.** Remove prompts and templates as learners demonstrate independent use [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
6. **Reflect.** Close with structured reflection on what strategies helped and when to reuse them.

## Examples
**Zimmerman and colleagues' self-regulation training cycle** — Learners are taught a forethought → performance → self-reflection cycle applied to writing and studying tasks, with teachers modeling each phase and fading prompts over successive tasks.

**[Tools of the Mind](https://toolsofthemind.org)** — Early-childhood curriculum in which children write play plans and self-check against them, building behavioral regulation through structured planning artifacts.

**[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Mastery trackers and goal-setting features externalize progress monitoring, prompting learners to plan and evaluate their own practice sequences.
