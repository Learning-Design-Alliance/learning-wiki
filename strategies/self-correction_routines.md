# Self Correction_Routines

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self correction routines are repeatable procedures that prompt learners to evaluate their own performance against explicit criteria, locate errors, and revise before external feedback arrives. Typical moves include checking work against a rubric or answer key, error analysis ("find and classify your mistake"), and structured revision passes. The routine shifts the act of correction from the teacher or system to the learner, making error detection itself a practiced skill.

## Design Implications

Self correction works because it activates self-monitoring during learning: learners who evaluate their own work against criteria develop the internal standards needed to regulate performance without support [Self-regulated learning improves achievement.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. Feedback is most effective when it tells learners *where they are relative to a goal* and *what to do next* — a routine that has learners locate their own discrepancies against criteria delivers exactly this information, with the added benefit that generating it strengthens monitoring skill [Feedback that addresses the task and process improves learning more than feedback about the self.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The routine must be taught and rehearsed; simply telling learners to "check your work" produces little change without a concrete procedure and criteria.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit success criteria, rubrics, or worked solutions learners can compare against
- A concrete, rehearsed procedure (e.g., a checklist, error taxonomy, or revision protocol)
- Time built into the task cycle for checking and revising, not just producing
- An error-tolerant classroom climate where finding one's own mistakes is rewarded rather than penalized

#### Constraints
- Learners cannot self-correct errors they do not recognize; without an answer key, rubric, or criteria, weak-knowledge learners confirm their own mistakes [-M]
- Self-correction consumes time and working memory; for novices, checking can overload the very resources needed for the task itself [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- If the routine becomes mechanical box-ticking, learners verify completion rather than quality, and monitoring gains disappear [-W]
- Over-reliance on answer keys can encourage answer-matching rather than reasoning about why a response is wrong [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Checklist protocols**: learners verify each criterion in turn (e.g., writing checklists for claims, evidence, reasoning)
- **Error classification**: learners label each mistake by type (conceptual, procedural, careless) before revising, which targets the revision and builds error-pattern awareness
- **Delayed self-grading**: learners solve, then compare against a fully worked solution, annotating where and why their approach diverged
- **Peer-mediated correction**: pairs exchange work and apply the same routine, which adds the benefit of seeing another's errors before checking one's own
- **Faded routines**: the checklist or protocol is progressively removed as learners internalize the monitoring moves

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough knowledge to recognize discrepancies between their work and the criteria — complete novices typically lack the knowledge to detect their own errors [~M]
- Learners developing independent study skills and exam preparation habits [+M]
- Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who need [worked examples](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and external correction first before self-monitoring is feasible [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural accuracy: catching and fixing computational, grammatical, or code errors
- Self-regulated learning: building the monitor-and-revise cycle learners apply without prompting [Self-regulated learning improves achievement.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]
- Quality standards internalization: learning what "good" looks like in a domain by applying criteria repeatedly
- Metacognitive accuracy: calibrating judgments of one's own understanding against actual performance

### Instructions
1. **Set criteria first.** Before the task, give learners the rubric, checklist, or exemplar they will later check against — criteria known in advance shape the work itself, not just the review.
2. **Complete the task without interruption.** Learners produce a full draft or attempt before any checking begins, so the routine reviews a complete performance.
3. **Run the check.** Learners apply the routine: compare against the key or rubric, mark discrepancies, and classify each error by type.
4. **Revise.** Learners correct identified errors and, where the routine includes it, explain *why* each original answer was wrong — the explanation, not the marking, is where learning occurs.
5. **Log error patterns.** Learners record recurring error types across tasks, turning individual corrections into a personal error profile that guides future practice.
6. **Fade the scaffold.** Reduce the checklist to a brief self-prompt ("Did I check units? Did I answer the question asked?") as monitoring becomes habitual.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Writing revision checklists** — Students use a COPS-style checklist (Capitalization, Organization, Punctuation, Spelling) to self-edit drafts before peer or teacher review; the routine is taught with modeled think-alouds first.
- **Math error analysis journals** — After receiving a marked problem set, students sort their errors into conceptual vs. procedural vs. careless categories, re-solve each missed problem, and log patterns in a journal.
- **Programming test-driven workflows** — Platforms like [Codecademy](https://www.codecademy.com) and [Exercism](https://exercism.org) have learners run automated tests against their own code, read the failure output, and revise — a self correction loop with the test suite as criteria.
- **Exam wrapper routines** — Post-exam reflection forms (used widely in higher education, e.g., Carnegie Mellon's [Eberly Center exam wrappers](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html)) walk students through diagnosing which question types they missed and why.
