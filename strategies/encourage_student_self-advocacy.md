# Encourage Student Self-Advocacy

## Description
Self-advocacy instruction teaches students to understand their own strengths, challenges, and learning needs, and to communicate those needs effectively to others. It is carried out through explicit instruction in self-knowledge, knowledge of rights and available supports, and communication skills, combined with authentic opportunities for students to make decisions and request help within the classroom. The instructor's role shifts from sole provider of support to coach of a skill students will need when the instructor is no longer present.

## Design Implications

Self-advocacy is a component of self-determination, and interventions that build it improve academic and transition outcomes, particularly for students with disabilities [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on students first developing accurate self-knowledge — learners who misjudge their own understanding rarely seek help when they need it — and on a classroom climate where asking for help is normalized rather than stigmatized. Because self-advocacy requires students to monitor their own comprehension and take ownership of goals, it draws directly on [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) and [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md): autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, which self-advocacy both requires and reinforces [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Structured opportunities for students to identify and articulate their strengths and challenges ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- A classroom culture where help-seeking is expected and low-stakes; [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and anonymous question channels lower the social cost of asking
- Explicit instruction in communication scripts (whom to ask, what to say, what to request) — self-advocacy does not emerge from exhortation alone
- Authentic decision points where student requests actually change something (seating, pacing, format of materials), so advocacy is reinforced by results

#### Constraints
- Students with low self-efficacy may avoid help-seeking even when instruction is available, because asking appears to confirm inadequacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [~M] — normalize help-seeking before expecting it
- Advocacy without response is worse than no advocacy: if requests are ignored or punished, students learn that speaking up is futile
- Requires sustained instructional time; one-off lessons on "knowing your learning style" produce little durable change
- Overemphasis on fixed "learning styles" as the content of self-knowledge is counterproductive [X] — focus instead on task demands, strategies, and evidence of what works for the individual

#### Implementation Variability
- Scripted curricula (e.g., the I-Plan and SLiCK self-advocacy strategies) versus embedded approaches woven into ordinary lessons
- Anonymous channels (question boxes, digital backchannels) as a scaffold toward public self-advocacy
- IEP/504 meetings attended and eventually led by the student — a high-fidelity, high-stakes application

### Target Learners
- Students with disabilities and those receiving accommodations, for whom self-advocacy predicts post-secondary success [~M]
- Adolescents and young adults approaching transitions (middle school, high school, college, workplace), where support structures become the student's responsibility to initiate
- Reluctant help-seekers of any profile; structured help-seeking instruction benefits high-achieving students who under-use support [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-knowledge: accurate awareness of strengths, challenges, and effective strategies
- Communication: requesting clarification, accommodations, and help appropriately
- Self-regulation: monitoring comprehension and acting on it [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Goal-setting: framing process goals the student can own and pursue [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — formative assessment gives students the evidence they need to know *what* to advocate for; without feedback on their own performance, self-advocacy requests are guesses
- [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — self-advocacy is a performance skill; coaching during real requests (e.g., emailing a teacher, speaking in an IEP meeting) builds it far better than discussion alone
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — modeling and articulation make the hidden moves of self-advocacy (noticing confusion, choosing words, following up) observable and learnable

### Instructions
1. Build self-knowledge: guide students through [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) on a recent task — what was hard, what helped, what they would do differently.
2. Model the skill: demonstrate a help-seeking or accommodation request aloud, including the internal decision-making ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
3. Rehearse: students practice requests through [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) or role-play with scripts before using them in real contexts.
4. Create low-stakes channels: use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and anonymous question tools so help-seeking has minimal social cost initially.
5. Act on requests: visibly honor student advocacy where reasonable, and explain decisions when declining — responsiveness is what makes the skill stick.
6. Transfer responsibility: fade teacher prompting and move students toward leading their own conferences and accommodation conversations.

## Examples
- **Student-led IEP meetings** — programs such as the Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction (SDLMI) have students with disabilities prepare, present, and increasingly lead their own IEP meetings, with documented gains in self-determination and goal attainment.
- **[Understood.org](https://www.understood.org)** — publishes student-facing self-advocacy toolkits and scripts for requesting accommodations, used by families and classrooms.
- **Anonymous backchannels** — a teacher uses a digital question queue (e.g., Google Forms or Padlet) so students can flag confusion without public exposure, then gradually transitions students to asking directly.
