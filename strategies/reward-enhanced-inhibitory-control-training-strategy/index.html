Incorporate reward prospect into inhibitory control training interventions to strengthen behaviour change effects¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 quant-synthesis), q4 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Given that reward prospect significantly improves IC, the authors propose using rewards within inhibitory control training (ICT): "Reward may be used to increase the value of health-related cues (e.g., healthy foods) or devalue unhealthy behaviour-related cues". The opportunity to gain rewards for avoiding health risk may improve associative learning and strengthen intervention effects, offering a route to more prolonged behavioural change.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Reward must be contingent on successful inhibition within the training task

Constraints¶

  • The article notes future research is needed; the meta-analysis measured transient task-level effects, not long-term training outcomes

Target Learners¶

  • Adults in health-behaviour change contexts
  • Clinical populations targeted by inhibitory control training

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Strengthening self-control and health-related inhibitory behaviour change

Affordances¶

  • Inhibitory Control Transient Motivation Sensitive State

Claims¶

  • The effect of reward on inhibitory control is not moderated by participant age (children vs adults) [+W]
  • The effect of reward on inhibitory control is not moderated by clinical status, including ADHD and substance use disorder samples [+W]
  • The prospect of reward for successful inhibition significantly improves inhibitory control (SMD = 0.429) compared with no-reward conditions [+W]
  • The effect of reward on inhibitory control is not moderated by reward type (monetary vs points vs other) [+W]
  • The reward effect on inhibitory control is significant across all six IC task types, but task type significantly moderates the effect, with the largest effect in Stroop tasks [+W]
