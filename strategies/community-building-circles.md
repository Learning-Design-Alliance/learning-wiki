# Community Building Circles

## Description
Community building circles are a structured dialogue format — adapted from restorative practices and Indigenous talking-circle traditions — in which participants sit in a circle and take turns responding to an open prompt, often passing a talking piece that signals whose turn it is to speak. Circles are used at the start of a course or day (check-ins), before difficult content, or after conflict, with the explicit goal of building relational trust and a sense of belonging rather than covering academic content.

## Design Implications

Circles work by giving every learner equal, protected airtime and public acknowledgment of their membership in the group, which supports belonging — a known lever for engagement and persistence [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The equal-turn structure also lowers the social risk of speaking: because everyone speaks and no one is called on, quieter learners participate who would not volunteer in open discussion [~M]. Circles are relational infrastructure, not content instruction — their academic payoff is indirect, operating through climate, trust, and willingness to take intellectual risks later.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear, low-stakes opening prompt (e.g., "share one thing that went well this week") that every learner can answer
- Agreed norms: speak from your own experience, listen without interrupting, the right to pass
- A talking piece or equivalent turn-taking mechanism to slow and equalize participation
- Consistency — circles build trust only when repeated regularly, not run once as an event

#### Constraints
- Time-intensive: a circle of 25 learners at 30–60 seconds each consumes most of a period, crowding out content instruction [-M]
- Forced personal disclosure can harm learners who feel unsafe or exposed; prompts must be low-risk and passing must be genuinely consequence-free [-M]
- Poorly facilitated circles can surface conflict or inequity without resolution, damaging rather than building trust [-W]
- Effects on achievement are indirect and mediated by climate; circles alone do not improve learning outcomes [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Check-in circles** (5–10 minutes) at the start of class, with brief prompts
- **Academic circles** applying the format to content discussion, using text-based prompts
- **Restorative circles** after conflict, focused on harm and repair rather than community building
- **Literature circles / seminar variants** that combine round-robin structure with role assignments

### Target Learners
- Learners in new groupings (start of year, new course, merged cohorts) where relationships are unformed [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Learners from marginalized groups for whom belonging uncertainty is a barrier to participation [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less effective for mature, self-organizing groups that already have established norms and may find the ritual redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: empathy, perspective-taking, and listening skills [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Classroom climate: psychological safety and willingness to take intellectual risks
- Metacognitive and self-regulatory habits through regular structured reflection

### Instructions
1. Arrange seating in a circle with no barriers (desks moved, no "back rows"); the physical geometry signals equal status and supports [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md).
2. Open with a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md): state the prompt, model a short answer yourself, and restate norms (right to pass, confidentiality, listening).
3. Run the round with a talking piece; enforce equal airtime so dominant voices do not capture the space — consider [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) or rotating who speaks first to disrupt status hierarchies.
4. Close with a brief synthesis or "one word" round that marks the circle as complete and transitions to the next activity.
5. Repeat on a predictable schedule; trust accumulates across repetitions, not within a single session.

## Examples
- **Restorative Practices in Pittsburgh Public Schools** (International Institute for Restorative Practices) — whole-school implementation of community and repair circles; district-wide evaluation found improved climate and reduced suspension days ([iirp.edu](https://www.iirp.edu)).
- **Morningside Center for Teaching Social Responsibility** — publishes classroom circle protocols used in NYC public schools for community building and difficult-current-events discussions ([morningsidecenter.org](https://www.morningsidecenter.org)).
- **Advisory programs in EL Education schools** — weekly crew circles with consistent prompts across a school year, tying circle routines to character and belonging goals ([eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)).
