# Spark Engagement with Student-Generated Questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students formulate their own questions about a topic and pursue investigations to answer them, rather than responding only to teacher-posed prompts. The strategy converts curiosity into an instructional engine: question generation precedes and drives inquiry, giving students ownership over both the direction and the purpose of their learning.

## Design Implications

Student question generation increases engagement and investment because learners pursue questions they find personally meaningful, and autonomy over learning goals supports intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. The quality of student questions is itself learnable — explicit instruction in question formulation (open vs. closed, testable vs. speculative) markedly improves the questions students produce [~M]. Question generation also primes productive cognitive conflict: a question students cannot yet answer creates a knowledge gap that inquiry then resolves [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom norm that treats student questions as legitimate curriculum material, not interruptions
- Explicit scaffolds for question formulation (e.g., the Question Formulation Technique: produce, improve, prioritize questions) — unstructured "ask anything" prompts yield low-quality questions [~M]
- Curricular flexibility to follow promising questions, or a deliberate mapping of student questions onto required content
- A mechanism for students to pursue answers: research time, data collection, or structured [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)

#### Constraints
- Unguided inquiry from student questions can overload novices, who lack the background knowledge to formulate productive or answerable questions [~S] — minimal-guidance findings suggest novices need question scaffolds, not free exploration
- Student questions may diverge from mandated curriculum goals; forcing alignment can drain the autonomy that makes the strategy motivating [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]
- Time-intensive: supporting diverse student investigations requires more class time and teacher facilitation skill than teacher-directed instruction
- Students accustomed to answer-oriented schooling may initially generate shallow or test-anticipating questions; the skill develops over repeated cycles

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full inquiry projects**: student questions define multi-week investigations (project- or problem-based formats)
- **Lesson-level hooks**: students generate questions at the start of a unit; the teacher uses them to structure subsequent instruction
- **Question Formulation Technique (QFT)**: a bounded 30–50 minute routine for producing, refining, and prioritizing questions without requiring full inquiry follow-through
- **Discussion seeding**: student questions replace teacher questions as the agenda for [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) activities

### Target Learners
- Effective across K–12 and higher education; particularly valuable for students who have experienced disempowerment in teacher-directed settings, since valuing their questions signals epistemic authority and supports belonging [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students with some topical background knowledge — they generate more productive questions than complete novices [~M]
- Reluctant learners, for whom personally chosen questions provide a reason to engage that teacher-assigned tasks may lack [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Engagement and motivation: building investment in learning through personally meaningful purposes
- Inquiry and research skills: formulating, refining, and investigating answerable questions
- Critical thinking and epistemic practices: evaluating what makes a question testable or productive
- Deeper conceptual understanding when questions drive [active learning](../principles/active-learning.md) investigations [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Prompt a topic or phenomenon** — present an artifact, event, or problem connected to the unit, and activate what students already know ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
2. **Generate questions** — students produce as many questions as possible without evaluation, then improve them (convert closed to open, make them testable) and prioritize a shortlist.
3. **Plan investigation** — map prioritized questions to available resources and methods; where questions exceed the unit's scope, name that explicitly and connect them to future learning.
4. **Investigate** — students pursue answers through research, experiments, text study, or structured [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), with the teacher providing scaffolds and [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) checkpoints rather than answers.
5. **Report and reflect** — students share findings, evaluate which questions were answered and which remain open, and generate new questions, making the cycle recursive.

## Examples
- **Question Formulation Technique** ([Right Question Institute](https://rightquestion.org)) — a published routine in which students produce, improve, and prioritize questions on a "QFocus" prompt; used across K–12 and higher education to shift question-asking from teacher to student.
- **Driving questions in project-based learning** — e.g., in [Expeditionary Learning](https://elschools.org) (EL Education) schools, students help shape the guiding questions that anchor multi-week expeditions, then investigate them through fieldwork and expert interviews.
- **Science classrooms** — students generate testable questions about a local environmental issue (e.g., water quality in a nearby stream) and design investigations to answer them, connecting inquiry to [authentic audiences](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md).
