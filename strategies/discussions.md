# Discussions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Discussions engage learners in structured, text-based conversation about what they read, with the teacher establishing routines, norms, and facilitation moves that keep talk productive. Activating background knowledge, checking for understanding, and fostering student-to-student conversation are the core components. The goal is comprehension through oral language: learners articulate interpretations, respond to others' reasoning, and negotiate meaning from the text.

## Design Implications

Structured discussion improves comprehension and literacy outcomes when it goes beyond recitation to genuine student-to-student exchange [~S]. The critical variable is discourse quality: classrooms where teachers use authentic questions and uptake (building responses into follow-up questions) produce measurably better comprehension than those dominated by known-answer questioning [~S]. Discussion works because articulating ideas aloud prompts elaboration and self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M], and because hearing peers' interpretations exposes learners to multiple readings of the same text.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared text worth discussing, with enough interpretive space for multiple defensible readings
- Established routines and norms for productive, respectful talk (turn-taking, evidence-based claims, listening)
- Teacher facilitation skill: open-ended questioning, revoicing, and steering without dominating
- Activation of background knowledge before discussion begins ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))

#### Constraints
- Unfacilitated discussion degrades into opinion exchange unanchored in the text; teacher facilitation is necessary, not optional [-M]
- Recitation-style IRE questioning (Initiate–Respond–Evaluate) suppresses the student reasoning that makes discussion valuable [-S]
- Dominant voices and status differences can silence English language learners and lower-status students unless structures (turns, roles, sentence starters) redistribute airtime [-M]
- Discussions consume substantial instructional time relative to the content covered; inefficient for transmitting large bodies of factual material [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Small-group [Peer Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) versus whole-class sharing; small groups increase participation density but need norms and reporting structures
- Talk moves frameworks (e.g., Accountable Talk) that script teacher facilitation moves such as revoicing, prompting for reasoning, and challenging claims
- Sentence starters and structured roles for English language learners and novice discussants
- Text-based protocols (seminar, jigsaw, debate) that vary the discussion format while keeping the text at the center

### Target Learners
- All learners, with particular benefit for English language learners, who gain oral rehearsal of academic vocabulary before writing [~M]
- Learners with some familiarity with the text or topic — discussion requires something to talk about; students with no prior exposure benefit from [Activation](../elements/activation.md) first
- Less effective for learners who lack the vocabulary to enter the conversation without scaffolds such as sentence starters or pre-taught terms

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension and interpretation of complex texts
- Vocabulary development through oral use of academic language
- Communication skills: articulating claims, citing evidence, responding to others' reasoning
- Critical thinking: evaluating competing interpretations of a text

### Instructions
1. Select a text with genuine interpretive demand and prepare 2–3 open, text-dependent questions using [Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md).
2. Activate background knowledge and pre-teach key vocabulary ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)) so all learners can enter the discussion.
3. Establish or review discussion norms: cite the text, build on or challenge ideas, ensure every voice is heard.
4. Launch [Peer Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) in small groups, providing sentence starters for learners who need them.
5. Facilitate with talk moves — revoice, ask for evidence, invite disagreement — rather than evaluating answers.
6. Consolidate through [Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/class-discussion.md), where groups report and the teacher highlights strong textual reasoning.
7. Follow with writing that extends the discussion, so oral language feeds into written composition.

## Examples
- **Accountable Talk** (Institute for Learning, University of Pittsburgh) — a talk-moves framework in which teachers use revoicing, reasoning prompts, and challenges to keep whole-class discussion accountable to the text, the reasoning community, and standards of rigor.
- **Reciprocal Teaching** — small-group discussion routines in which learners take turns leading dialogue about a text using prediction, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing.
- **Socratic Seminar** — students discuss an open-ended question about a text in inner/outer circle formats, with the teacher facilitating rather than evaluating.
