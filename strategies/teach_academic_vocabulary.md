# Teach Academic Vocabulary

## Description
Teaching academic vocabulary means identifying the discipline-specific and high-utility words a text, task, or project depends on, then providing explicit instruction on their meanings and uses rather than leaving learners to infer them from context. Instruction typically includes student-friendly definitions, multiple encounters across contexts, and active use of the words in speaking and writing. While all students benefit, English language learners often need more targeted and extended support.

## Design Implications

Vocabulary knowledge is one of the strongest predictors of reading comprehension, and explicit instruction on selected words reliably improves both word learning and comprehension of texts containing those words [Stahl & Fairbanks' meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056001071) [+S]. Depth of word knowledge matters more than sheer number of words taught: instructional routines that require learners to manipulate word meanings — comparing, contrasting, justifying — produce richer, more durable knowledge than definitional instruction alone [Nagy & Townsend characterize academic vocabulary learning as tool-building rather than label-learning.](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.011) [+M]. Because working memory is limited, pre-teaching key terms reduces the load of decoding unfamiliar language during content tasks, freeing attention for the actual learning goal [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]; fluent word recognition likewise frees cognitive resources for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Careful selection of a small set of high-utility terms (Tier 2 general academic words and Tier 3 domain-specific words), rather than exhaustive lists
- Student-friendly definitions and examples, ideally contrasted with non-examples and near-neighbors
- Multiple, spaced encounters with each word across the unit [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Activities requiring active use — [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) texts with word meanings, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) linking new words to known concepts, discussion and writing tasks that require the target terms

#### Constraints
- Instruction on too many words dilutes learning; depth beats breadth, and exhaustive pre-teaching crowds out content work [Nagy & Townsend argue for depth of word knowledge over word counts.](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.011) [~M]
- Definitional or dictionary-copy instruction alone produces shallow knowledge that does not transfer to comprehension [Stahl & Fairbanks found definitional-only methods weaker than rich instruction.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056001071) [-S]
- Contextual inference from reading alone is unreliable for unfamiliar academic words, especially for ELLs — words needed for comprehension must be taught, not discovered
- Time-intensive; requires front-loading during unit planning

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-teaching** before reading or project launch versus **embedded teaching** at the moment of encounter; pre-teaching suits dense informational texts, embedded teaching suits discussion-driven work
- **Morphological analysis** (teaching roots, prefixes, suffixes) as a generative strategy that extends beyond the words directly taught
- **Word walls, glossaries, and frayer models** as persistent reference supports versus **interactive routines** (e.g., Beck's Text Talk questioning) as the primary mechanism
- **Tiered instruction**: shared core instruction with additional [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) — cognate awareness, first-language supports, visuals — for English language learners

### Target Learners
- All learners encountering new disciplinary language, since academic vocabulary is a gatekeeper for content comprehension [Nagy & Townsend.](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.011) [+M]
- English language learners, who often lack the conversational exposure to academic words that native speakers accrue incidentally; they benefit disproportionately from explicit, contextualized instruction [Stahl & Fairbanks.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056001071) [+M]
- Struggling readers, for whom unfamiliar vocabulary compounds decoding demands and consumes working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of complex academic and informational texts
- Academic language production: using disciplinary terms accurately in discussion and writing
- Conceptual understanding of content, since many academic words encode the discipline's core concepts [Nagy & Townsend.](https://doi.org/10.1002/rrq.011) [+M]
- Word-learning strategies (morphology, context use) that support independent vocabulary growth

### Instructions
1. During unit planning, identify the words essential to the texts and tasks; prioritize Tier 2 academic words and Tier 3 terms central to the content, limiting direct instruction to roughly 8–10 words per week.
2. Pre-teach the most critical terms using [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md): student-friendly definition, example, non-example, and a quick check for understanding.
3. Have learners process meanings actively — [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) connecting new words to known concepts, sorting and comparing tasks, and [Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) of why a word fits or does not fit a context [+S].
4. Embed the words throughout the unit: require their use in discussion prompts, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) tasks, and project deliverables so each word is encountered and produced multiple times across spaced intervals [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
5. Maintain a visible reference (word wall, personal glossary) and revisit words in later weeks to consolidate retention.
6. Provide tiered supports: cognates, visuals, and L1 glosses for ELLs; extension into etymology and morphological analysis for advanced learners.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Beck, McKeown, & Kucan's robust vocabulary instruction** (*Bringing Words to Life*, Guilford Press) — Tier 2 words taught through friendly definitions, multiple contexts, and interactive questioning such as "Would you be *reluctant* to eat a sandwich you loved?"
- **Word Generation** (SERP Institute, https://www.serpinstitute.org/wordgeneration) — a free middle-school curriculum that teaches high-utility academic words through weekly debates, reading, and writing across subject areas.
- **Project-based learning launch** — before a science project on watersheds, the teacher pre-teaches *runoff*, *permeable*, and *erosion* with diagrams and non-examples, then requires each term in the team's project proposal and final presentation.
