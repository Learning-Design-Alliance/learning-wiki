# Room Positioning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Room positioning is the deliberate arrangement of seats, tables, and the instructor's location to shape sightlines, proximity, and interaction patterns. It is carried out by choosing a layout (rows, horseshoe, clusters, cabaret) and positioning the instructor relative to learners before and during a session, adjusting as activities change.

## Design Implications

Physical layout is not neutral: seating position and room design measurably affect engagement and achievement [Classroom design and layout affect learning progress.](../claims/classroom-design-affects-learning-progress.md) [+M]. Layout determines which interaction structures are physically possible — rows support attention to a single presenter, clusters support [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md), and a horseshoe supports discussion — so positioning should be chosen from the intended activity, not habit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear decision about the dominant interaction mode of the session (presentation, discussion, group work)
- Movable furniture, or at minimum a layout chosen to fit the fixed furniture
- Instructor movement paths that allow proximity to all learners, not just the front rows

#### Constraints
- Fixed, forward-facing rows suppress peer interaction and make discussion physically awkward [-M]; lecture halls designed this way push sessions toward transmission even when the plan is interactive
- Students seated at the back and sides participate less and are attended to less, an effect that compounds over a term [~M] — proximity effects mean back-row learners receive less instructor eye contact and fewer questions
- Overly novel or cluttered layouts can distract; irrelevant visual stimulation hurts learning [Irrelevant or incoherent material reduces learning.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [~M]
- Frequent mid-session rearrangement consumes time and can disrupt momentum [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Rows / theater**: for presentations and demonstrations where attention should converge on one focal point
- **Horseshoe / U-shape**: for discussion-based sessions; everyone can see everyone, instructor can approach each seat
- **Clusters / pods of 3–6**: for group work and [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md); rotate membership, not just seats
- **Cabaret (clusters facing a front edge)**: hybrid supporting both group work and whole-class plenary
- **Instructor positioning**: moving away from the lectern and into the room increases perceived immediacy and on-task behavior [~W]; standing near disruptive students reduces off-task behavior without verbal correction [-W direction: distance reduces the need for correction]

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit from sightlines to displays and the instructor; learners with hearing or visual impairments depend on it [Classroom design and layout affect learning progress.](../claims/classroom-design-affects-learning-progress.md) [+M]
- Anxious or marginalized learners benefit from positioning that avoids a "spotlight" front-and-center seat while still enabling participation [~W]
- Young learners and learners with attention difficulties are most sensitive to distraction from windows, doorways, and high-traffic zones [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral: attention management and on-task engagement during instruction
- Interactive: discussion, peer feedback, and collaborative knowledge building
- Affective: belonging and inclusion through layouts where no learner is structurally peripheral

### Instructions
1. Decide the session's dominant interaction mode and select the matching layout (rows for presentation, horseshoe for discussion, clusters for group work).
2. Position displays and the instructor so every seat has an unobstructed sightline and audible path.
3. Place high-distraction zones (doors, windows) away from the instructional focal point, or seat easily distracted learners away from them.
4. Plan your own movement: enter the seating area during practice and discussion rather than staying at the front ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Reconfigure between activities, not mid-activity; use a consistent signal or routine for transitions.
6. For group phases, position yourself at the cluster with the greatest need, using proximity as an implicit support ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md)).

## Examples
- A university flips a fixed lecture course into a **TEAL-style (Technology-Enabled Active Learning) room** — round tables of nine with instructor stations at the edges — as in MIT's TEAL physics program (https://web.mit.edu/edtech/teal.html), enabling in-class group problem solving.
- A K–12 teacher uses a horseshoe for seminar discussion of a text, then has students turn their desks into pods of four for peer review, repositioning between the two activities.
- An online instructor applies the same logic virtually by using breakout rooms for small groups and spotlighting speakers in plenary, mirroring physical positioning decisions.
