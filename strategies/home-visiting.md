# Home Visiting

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Home visiting delivers instruction and support in the learner's own home rather than in a classroom or center. Trained visitors — nurses, early childhood educators, parent educators, or paraprofessionals — work one-on-one with families, typically modeling practices (e.g., responsive caregiving, shared book reading), observing the parent or learner apply them, and providing feedback in context. The approach is most prominent in early childhood and family literacy programs, where the home is the primary learning environment.

## Design Implications

Home visiting works because instruction is situated in the environment where learning behaviors actually occur, allowing modeling and coaching to be immediately contextualized rather than transferred from a distant setting [~M]. Effects depend heavily on program quality and intensity: meta-analytic evidence shows modest average effects on child outcomes, with stronger results for programs using professional staff, structured curricula, and sufficient dosage [~M]. Because the visitor works with a family rather than a class, the strategy functions as intensive individualized [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) embedded in daily routines.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A structured curriculum or visit protocol so visits are consistent and goal-directed, not unstructured social calls
- Visitors trained in both content (e.g., child development, health) and relational skills (building trust with families under stress)
- Sufficient duration and frequency — effects generally require sustained contact over months or years, not one-off visits
- Attention to the parent as the primary agent of change: the goal is building caregiver capability, not delivering services *to* the child

#### Constraints
- Effects on child cognitive outcomes are typically small and fade without ongoing dosage; short-term or low-intensity programs show near-zero impacts [-M]
- High attrition is common among the highest-risk families — those who most need the service are least likely to sustain participation [-M]
- Quality varies widely with visitor skill; poorly trained visitors drift toward generic advice or checklist compliance, undermining the modeling-and-feedback cycle [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when visits ignore family context (culture, literacy, housing stress) and deliver a fixed script regardless of circumstances [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Professional vs. paraprofessional visitors:** nurse-delivered programs (e.g., Nurse-Family Partnership) show the most consistent long-term effects; paraprofessional models are cheaper but more variable in impact
- **Curriculum-based vs. responsive models:** structured curricula (Parents as Teachers, Home Instruction for Parents of Preschool Youngsters) versus more flexible, family-driven approaches
- **Hybrid delivery:** visits combined with center-based programming or group meetings tend to outperform visits alone

### Target Learners
- Expectant parents and families with infants and toddlers, particularly first-time, low-income, or adolescent mothers — the population with the strongest evidence base [~M]
- Families in rural or underserved areas where center-based services are inaccessible
- Caregivers learning to support school readiness and early literacy at home; effects are strongest when visitors model and coach the practice in the moment rather than only describing it [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Parenting knowledge and skill: responsive caregiving, discipline strategies, health practices
- Home learning environment: shared reading, language-rich interaction, play routines
- Family well-being outcomes that indirectly support learning: maternal education, employment, reduced child maltreatment

### Instructions
1. **Assess family context and goals** — begin with the family's own priorities to build trust and relevance before introducing curriculum content.
2. **Model in context** — demonstrate the target practice (e.g., a dialogic reading routine) using the family's actual materials and setting, narrating the reasoning behind each move.
3. **Coach guided practice** — have the parent perform the practice during the visit while the visitor observes and gives immediate, specific feedback.
4. **Plan between-visit practice** — agree on a small, concrete routine to try before the next visit; review it at the start of the following session.
5. **Connect and fade** — link the family to community resources and progressively shift responsibility to the parent as competence grows, mirroring [Fading](../principles/scaffolding.md) in one-on-one form.

## Examples
- **[Nurse-Family Partnership](https://www.nursefamilypartnership.org)** — nurse home visiting for first-time low-income mothers from pregnancy through age 2; the most rigorously evaluated home visiting model, with randomized-trial evidence of effects on child maltreatment, maternal life course, and later child outcomes.
- **[Parents as Teachers](https://parentsasteachers.org)** — parent educators deliver a structured curriculum through home visits and group connections, focused on developmental milestones and school readiness.
- **[Early Head Start Home-Based Option](https://www.acf.hhs.gov/ohs)** — weekly home visits plus group socializations as one delivery option within the federal Early Head Start program; the national evaluation found modest but meaningful gains for home-visited families.
