# Simplify Grading Scales

## Description
Simplify Grading Scales replaces the conventional 0–100 percentage scale with a coarse ordinal scale — typically 0–4 or a small set of performance levels — often anchored to rubric descriptors. The core rationale is mathematical: on a 100-point scale, a zero for a missed assignment is catastrophically far from the next lowest grade (typically 50–60), so a single missing or failed task can mathematically dominate a term grade regardless of later learning. Coarse scales compress this range, keep grades tied to demonstrated performance levels, and make grade meaning transparent to students and families.

## Design Implications

Coarse, criterion-referenced scales shift grades from averaging behavior over time toward describing current proficiency, which aligns grading with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [Assessment for learning practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Because each scale point maps to a described performance level, grades become interpretable feedback rather than opaque point totals, and students can see what the next level requires. The strategy is most powerful when combined with [Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) that define each level and with policies that let later evidence replace earlier evidence (revision, retakes, most-recent-over-mean).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A small set of clearly described performance levels (e.g., 4 = exceeds, 3 = meets, 2 = approaching, 1 = beginning, 0 = no evidence)
- Rubrics or descriptors that make each level observable, so the scale is criterion-referenced rather than a compressed percentage ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
- A policy decision about missing work: either a separate reporting category (e.g., "incomplete") or a floor grade, rather than a zero on the achievement scale
- Communication to students and families explaining what the new scale means

#### Constraints
- A zero on a 100-point scale is not equidistant from other grades; averaging it in punishes a single missing assignment out of proportion to its learning significance [-M] — this is the arithmetic problem the strategy exists to solve, but it only disappears if the zero is removed or redefined, not if the 100-point scale is merely relabeled
- Coarse scales reduce discrimination between adjacent performances; in selective or high-stakes contexts this can be perceived as a loss of information [~W]
- If scale points are not anchored to described criteria, a 0–4 scale is just a smaller 0–100 scale and inherits the same problems of subjectivity and grade inflation
- Transcript and reporting-system compatibility can constrain adoption; converting 0–4 back to letter grades can reintroduce the distortions the change was meant to remove

#### Implementation Variability
- **0–4 mastery scale** with rubric descriptors per level (most common in standards-based grading)
- **Minimum grade floors** (e.g., 50% as the lowest reportable score) as a lighter-touch fix that preserves the 100-point scale
- **Most-recent-evidence / replace-the-grade** policies, where later performance supersedes earlier scores
- **Separating academic achievement from behavior, effort, and timeliness**, reporting the latter separately rather than blending them into one grade
- **Single-point or binary scales** (meets / not yet) for specific skills, paired with narrative feedback

### Target Learners
- Students who miss assignments due to external factors (work, caregiving, instability), for whom a single zero can make a passing grade mathematically unreachable [-M]
- Students recovering from a weak start; coarse scales plus revision policies keep early failure from capping final achievement [Assessment for learning practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Younger learners and multilingual families, for whom a 4-level scale with plain-language descriptors is more interpretable than a percentage

### Target Learning Goals
- Accurate communication of current proficiency rather than accumulation of points
- Motivation to revise and re-attempt: when grades describe levels, improving the work visibly moves the grade
- Separation of achievement from compliance behaviors, so grades reflect learning goals

### Instructions
1. Audit current grading practices: identify where zeros, averaging, and blended non-academic factors distort the meaning of grades.
2. Define 4–5 performance levels with observable descriptors for each major learning goal, using [Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) so each scale point is criterion-anchored.
3. Decide the missing-work policy: report incompleteness separately or use a floor, and never average a zero into an achievement grade.
4. Align [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) tasks to the scale, and score against the rubric rather than deducting points.
5. Pair every scale judgment with [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) that names what the student did and what the next level requires — the scale communicates *where*, the feedback communicates *how*.
6. Pilot in one course or department, compare grade distributions and student understanding, then iterate before school-wide adoption.

## Examples
- **Standards-based grading in districts such as Mineola Union Free School District (NY)** — K–12 reporting on 4-level scales with published descriptors for each standard ([example overview](https://www.mineola.k12.ny.us)).
- **Marzano's proficiency scales** — Robert Marzano's scale templates (0.0–4.0 with half-step scores) used widely in competency-based schools ([Marzano Resources](https://www.marzanoresources.com)).
- **Grading for Equity pilots** — schools implementing Feldman's three pillars (accuracy, bias-resistance, motivation), of which a 0–4 scale with a floor grade is one component.
