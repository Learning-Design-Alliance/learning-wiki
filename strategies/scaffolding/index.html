Scaffolding¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 8 claims (7 for, 1 mixed) · 19 studies (6 causal, 5 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 1 of 19 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Scaffolding is the provision of temporary, adaptive support that enables learners to complete tasks they could not yet perform alone, with support gradually withdrawn as competence develops. The term originates in Wood, Bruner, and Ross's (1976) analysis of adult tutoring, building on Vygotsky's constructivism and the zone of proximal development. Scaffolds can be cognitive (prompts, worked examples, task decomposition), metacognitive (self-regulation checklists), or emotional (sentence stems for articulating feelings, low-stakes practice before high-stakes performance).

Design Implications¶

Scaffolding works because it keeps task demands within working memory limits while learners build the schemas needed to manage the full task unaided Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Its defining feature is contingency: support must be calibrated to the learner's current understanding and adjusted in response to their performance, not fixed in advance Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+S]. Equally important is fading — responsibility for the task must transfer to the learner, since permanent support prevents the development of independent capability Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Diagnosis of the learner's current capability and, for emotional scaffolding, their needs and triggers
  • A support structure that simplifies the task without simplifying the goal (e.g., chunking, prompts, coaching)
  • Ongoing observation or assessment to decide when and how much support to withdraw
  • A safe environment in which attempting the task without full competence carries low cost

Constraints¶

  • Scaffolds that are never faded produce dependence; learners continue to rely on prompts they no longer need Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [-M]
  • Over-support for learners with substantial prior knowledge is counterproductive — the expertise reversal effect means guidance beyond need actively depresses performance Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~S]
  • Non-contingent support (too much or too little relative to the learner's actual state) yields little benefit; the effect depends on the tutor or system responding to learner signals Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [~M]
  • In whole-class settings, one scaffold cannot be contingent for all learners simultaneously; differentiation or adaptive technology is needed

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static vs. adaptive: fixed supports (sentence stems, checklists) vs. dynamically adjusted support based on learner responses; adaptive versions show larger effects Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]
  • Human vs. tool-based: teacher tutoring, peer collaboration, or software hints and adaptive hints (e.g., ASSISTments)
  • Cognitive vs. affective: decomposing academic tasks vs. supporting emotional self-regulation through prompts, modeling, and gradual release

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who would otherwise engage in unproductive search or be overwhelmed by task complexity Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Learners struggling with self-regulation, particularly those highly reactive to new or challenging situations
  • Less benefit — and possible harm — for advanced learners, for whom added structure is redundant or disruptive Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural and whole-task competence on tasks beyond unaided ability Whole-task performance improves transfer. [+M]
  • Self-regulation: internalizing prompts and strategies until they operate independently Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Conceptual understanding when scaffolds prompt learners to articulate reasoning Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Diagnose what the learner can already do and where the task exceeds current capability.
  2. Decompose the task or provide a support structure — chunking, sentence stems, worked examples, or cognitive load management techniques.
  3. Provide guidance through modeling and coaching, making expert reasoning visible.
  4. Have the learner practice the task with the scaffold in place, observing performance closely.
  5. Fade support progressively — reduce prompt specificity, then frequency, then remove entirely — as independent success is demonstrated Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].
  6. Assess independent performance without the scaffold before declaring the goal met.

Related Strategies¶

  • Worked examples — a scaffold in problem-solving contexts; the example-to-problem sequence is a fading schedule Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Formative assessment — supplies the ongoing diagnosis that makes scaffolding contingent
  • Direct instruction — a highly structured, teacher-led form of scaffolding suited to novice learners

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the interactive, contingent form of scaffolding during performance
  • Practice — the arena in which scaffolds are applied and faded
  • Cognitive load management — the mechanism by which scaffolds make difficult tasks learnable

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — scaffolding and fading are core teaching methods alongside modeling and coaching
  • Four-Component Instructional Design — supportive information and procedural information function as embedded scaffolds within learning tasks
  • Cognitive Load Reduction (CLT scaffolding approach) — scaffolding as the primary technique for managing intrinsic load

Examples¶

  • A teacher provides sentence stems to help a student articulate their feelings in a journal, then gradually removes the stems as the student becomes comfortable self-regulating. (Learner Variability Project, Digital Promise)
  • ASSISTments — math homework platform that delivers adaptive hints and scaffolding questions when students err, with support intensity tied to student responses.
  • Reciprocal Teaching — teachers scaffold comprehension strategies (predicting, questioning, clarifying, summarizing) through modeling and guided group practice, then transfer the "teacher" role to students.
  • Khan Academy — hint ladders on practice problems act as on-demand scaffolds, from a nudge to a fully worked step.
