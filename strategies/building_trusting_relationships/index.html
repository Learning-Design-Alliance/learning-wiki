Building Trusting Relationships¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Building trusting relationships means deliberately establishing warmth, credibility, and mutual regard between instructor and learners so that learners feel safe to take intellectual risks, seek help, and persist through difficulty. It is carried out through everyday practices — greeting learners, learning names, Check-Ins, active listening, transparent expectations, and showing appropriate vulnerability — rather than through a single activity.

Design Implications¶

Relationship quality is one of the most consistent correlates of achievement and engagement across grade levels: teacher–student relationship quality predicts engagement and achievement even after controlling for prior attainment Teacher–student relationships predict engagement and achievement. [+S]. Trust lowers the social cost of error, which makes learners more willing to attempt tasks, ask questions, and expose misconceptions — the raw material for Formative Assessment and Coaching. The mechanism is motivational as much as affective: perceived belonging and relatedness support intrinsic motivation and autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dedicated, protected time for interaction (Check-Ins, brief individual conferences)
  • Genuine intent to know learners; perfunctory rapport-building reads as insincere and can backfire
  • Consistency and follow-through — trust is built by reliability over time, not one-off gestures
  • Conveying high expectations alongside warmth; care without challenge reads as low regard

Constraints¶

  • Large class sizes and time pressure limit individualized relationship-building; scalable substitutes (names, brief written check-ins) only partially compensate [~M]
  • Performed intimacy or forced self-disclosure can feel intrusive and reduce trust [-W]
  • Warm relationships without accountability can reduce productive struggle; effects on achievement are mediated by engagement, not direct [~M]
  • Cultural mismatch: displays of warmth and personal disclosure are interpreted differently across cultures; what builds trust in one context may discomfort learners in another [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Brief empathic interventions: short, structured messages conveying that the instructor values and respects learners measurably improve outcomes, including reduced suspensions in disciplinary contexts Brief empathic discipline interventions cut suspensions. [+S]
  • Belonging framing: explicitly communicating that all learners belong and can succeed buffers early setbacks, especially in gatekeeper courses Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+S]
  • Online adaptation: personalized video/audio feedback, discussion presence, and rapid response times substitute for physical presence

Target Learners¶

  • Learners at risk of disengagement or marginalization benefit most; relationship quality is a stronger predictor for struggling students than for high achievers Teacher–student relationships predict engagement and achievement. [+M]
  • Newcomers to a setting (first-year, first-generation, transition-year students) who lack established belonging
  • Learners who have experienced negative prior schooling relationships may need extended, consistent effort before trust forms

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and dispositional goals: motivation, persistence, willingness to seek help
  • Enabling goals for any objective requiring risk-taking: discussion, Argumentation, revision, Practice with feedback
  • Not itself a content goal — it functions as the relational substrate for other goals

Instructions¶

  1. Learn and use every learner's name from the first session; greet learners individually as they arrive.
  2. Open sessions with brief Check-Ins (one-word temperature checks, entry tickets) that signal attention to the person, not just the content.
  3. Model appropriate vulnerability — share your own errors, uncertainties, and learning process — to normalize struggle.
  4. Pair warmth with high, explicit expectations; communicate that you expect success and will support the path to it.
  5. Use Active Listening in conferences and feedback conversations: restate the learner's point before responding.
  6. Follow through visibly on commitments (returning work when promised, acting on feedback surveys) — reliability is the core of trust.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Listening — the conversational skill through which trust is communicated moment to moment
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — a scalable structure for individual relationship-building contact
  • Check-Ins — the recurring ritual that maintains relational connection over a course
  • Create a safe space in arts instruction so students can take productive risks and grow emotionally
  • Form design teams by building trust and relationships and prioritizing meaningful representation before project kick-off.

Examples¶

  • Restorative practice circles (used across many US districts): structured community circles in which teachers and students share perspectives, building relational trust that underpins classroom management.
  • Walton & Cohen social-belonging intervention — a brief reading-and-reflection activity conveying that worries about belonging are common and temporary improved GPA among marginalized college students.
  • First-day name cards plus weekly entry tickets in a large lecture: low-cost adaptations when individual contact time is scarce.
