# Growth Mindset Development

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Growth mindset development cultivates the belief that abilities and intelligence are malleable — that they can be developed through effort, effective strategy use, and help-seeking — rather than fixed traits. It is carried out through process-focused feedback, explicit teaching about neuroplasticity, reframing errors as information, and designing tasks where improvement is visible. The canonical intervention teaches learners that the brain grows new connections with effortful practice, then links that belief to concrete study strategies.

## Design Implications

Growth mindset interventions produce small but reliable gains in achievement for at-risk and struggling learners, with effects concentrated among students facing stereotype threat or academic difficulty [~S]. The mechanism is motivational: mindset shifts persistence, strategy use, and recovery from setbacks rather than directly increasing capacity. Effects are strongest when the mindset message is integrated into ongoing instruction and assessment rather than delivered as a one-off workshop [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Process-focused praise and feedback that names specific strategies and progress, not generic "you're smart" or even generic "you worked hard" [Mueller & Dweck: effort praise without strategy content can teach that performance requires visible struggle.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33) [+M]
- Instructor modeling: teachers who themselves frame errors as diagnostic information and normalize struggle
- Tasks calibrated so effortful improvement is actually achievable — a growth mindset message in a task where effort never pays off is disconfirmed by experience
- Alignment with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) so grades and feedback communicate progress, not fixed verdicts

#### Constraints
- Meta-analytic evidence shows overall effects on achievement are small and highly variable; interventions rarely benefit students who already hold growth beliefs or who are performing well [~S]
- In low-support environments, a mindset message alone cannot overcome structural barriers; effects were strongest for lower-achieving and low-SES students in supportive school contexts [~M]
- Effort praise delivered as pressure ("just try harder") without strategy instruction can reduce performance and teach learners that struggle signals low ability [-M]
- High-stakes, norm-referenced grading environments undermine the message; learners infer mindsets from how failure is treated, not from what is said [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Brief single-session interventions (e.g., the National Study of Learning Mindsets online modules) vs. embedded multi-week curricula
- Teacher-delivered vs. researcher-delivered vs. self-administered online formats
- Content focus: neuroscience of plasticity, "not yet" framing, error analysis routines, or attribution retraining

### Target Learners
- Struggling or at-risk students transitioning to challenging new environments (middle school, first-year college), where effects are largest [~S]
- Students experiencing stereotype threat, for whom mindset messages buffer against disengagement [~M]
- Least effective for high achievers and students who already endorse malleable beliefs [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic resilience: persistence and strategy adjustment after setbacks
- Metacognitive strategy adoption: linking effort to *effective* methods, not just time on task
- Belonging and engagement in threatening or competitive environments [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Teach the malleability concept explicitly — brain plasticity, "not yet" framing — via [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md), keeping it short and concrete.
2. Restructure feedback so it names strategies and progress: [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) with process-focused, strategy-linked comments rather than person-focused praise.
3. Build structured [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) routines where learners analyze errors and select a new strategy, converting setbacks into diagnostic information.
4. Model the mindset yourself — narrate your own learning struggles and recovery, as in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) modeling.
5. Support autonomy in strategy choice so learners experience ownership of improvement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], consistent with [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md).

## Related Strategies
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — makes effort pay off by keeping tasks within reach, which validates the mindset message
- [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — reframes errors as feedback rather than verdicts
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — mindset supplies the motivation; SRL supplies the strategies

## Examples
- **[PERTS / Mindset Works](https://www.mindsetworks.com)** — The Brainology curriculum teaches neuroplasticity through interactive modules; the National Study of Learning Mindsets (Yeager et al., 2019) delivered two 25-minute online sessions to ~12,000 ninth-graders and raised GPA among lower-achieving students.
- **"The Power of Yet" framing** — Replacing failing grades with "Not Yet" status so learners see current performance as a point on a trajectory, not a terminal judgment.
- **Error-analysis journals in mathematics** — Learners document a mistake, identify the faulty strategy, and record the corrected approach, operationalizing "mistakes are information."
