# Design Thinking

## Description
Design Thinking organizes learning around a structured innovation process: empathizing with users, defining a problem, ideating solutions, prototyping, and testing. Learners work on authentic, open-ended problems where user needs — not textbook answers — drive the solution. The process is carried out in teams through rapid, iterative cycles in which early ideas are externalized as sketches or prototypes and revised based on user feedback.

## Design Implications

Design Thinking situates learning in ill-structured, authentic problems, which supports transfer better than well-structured exercises when learners have adequate support [~M]. Its power comes from iteration: prototypes function as externalized thinking that can be critiqued and revised, converting abstract concepts into concrete artifacts. Because the problem space is open-ended, learning depends on encountering multiple cases and perspectives rather than a single correct path [Multiple varied cases support learning in ill-structured domains.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A real or realistic problem with identifiable users whose needs can be investigated
- Time and materials for rapid prototyping and at least one full test-and-revise cycle
- Structured empathy activities (interviews, observation) so the "define" stage rests on evidence, not assumption
- Critique protocols that make feedback specific and actionable [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Weak for novices in the underlying domain: open-ended exploration without domain knowledge imposes heavy search costs [Guidance becomes more necessary as task complexity and learner inexperience increase.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [-S] — minimally guided versions of design tasks can overload novices
- The "empathize" stage often produces shallow user insight when learners lack interview and observation skills
- Process can become ritualized: teams follow the five stages superficially without genuine iteration, yielding engaging activity but little conceptual learning [-M]
- Time-intensive; the full cycle rarely fits a single lesson and is often truncated to ideation-only, which removes the testing feedback loop where most learning occurs

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full cycle vs. sprint:** multi-week projects vs. compressed design sprints (e.g., a 90-minute "wallet exercise"); sprints trade depth of empathy work for exposure to the whole process
- **Domain-embedded vs. process-focused:** using design thinking to teach engineering or biology content vs. teaching the design process itself as the learning goal
- **Physical vs. digital prototyping:** paper mockups, role-played services, or clickable wireframes depending on the problem
- **Depth of user contact:** direct field interviews vs. personas and secondary research

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to generate and evaluate ideas meaningfully; pure novices need more structure first [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Learners developing collaboration, communication, and tolerance for ambiguity
- Students who benefit from seeing knowledge applied to human situations rather than abstract exercises

### Target Learning Goals
- Ill-structured problem solving: framing problems before solving them
- Empathy and perspective-taking: understanding stakeholder needs [Empathy-building activities improve understanding of others' perspectives.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Iterative improvement: learning from failure and revision
- Creative confidence and collaboration

### Instructions
1. **Frame the challenge** — present an authentic problem and audience; use [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) to map the process stages before beginning
2. **Empathize** — learners conduct user interviews or observations; provide interview protocols and model questioning through [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)
3. **Define** — synthesize findings into a point-of-view statement; use [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) to compare how experts frame similar problems
4. **Ideate** — brainstorm with deferred judgment; encourage [Analogical Reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) by asking "where else is this problem solved?"
5. **Prototype and test** — build low-fidelity artifacts, gather user feedback, iterate; structure critique with task- and process-level feedback prompts
6. **Reflect** — debrief on both the solution and the process, connecting experience to underlying concepts

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — like design thinking, grounds learning in authentic problems, but analyzes existing cases rather than generating new solutions
- [Project-Based Learning](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — design thinking supplies a repeatable process structure for the open-ended inquiry at the heart of project work

## Examples
- **Stanford d.school K12 Lab** (https://dschool.stanford.edu) — publishes design challenge curricula and the "wallet project" sprint used widely in schools and universities
- **IDEO design kits** (https://www.designkit.org) — Field Guide to Human-Centered Design used in classrooms and community programs to structure empathy and prototyping activities
- **Engineering capstone courses** — Dym et al. describe design thinking as the organizing framework for undergraduate engineering education, with students iterating on client-driven projects
