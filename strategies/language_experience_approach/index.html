Language Experience Approach¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The language experience approach (LEA) is a whole language method that integrates the development of reading, writing, listening, and speaking skills through the use of learners' experiences. Learners share an experience (an outing, a science demonstration, a personal story), and the instructor records their dictation verbatim on a chart or board. The resulting text — written in the learner's own words — then becomes the material for reading, rereading, word study, and writing activities.

Design Implications¶

LEA works because it makes text personally meaningful and fully decodable within the learner's oral vocabulary: the learner already knows what the sentence says, so instructional attention can go to how print maps onto known speech Activation of prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]. Because the text is generated from the learner's own language, it naturally satisfies Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax — there is no vocabulary gap between reader and text. Repeated rereading of the dictated chart supports fluency growth, and fluency in turn frees cognitive resources for comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+M]. For young children, the shared book and dictation routine resembles the interactive reading that builds oral language Dialogic reading improves expressive language. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A shared experience worth talking about (a class event, experiment, walk, or a learner's own story)
  • Instructor skill in recording dictation verbatim without "correcting" the learner's language mid-flow
  • Chart paper, board, or digital display; follow-up materials for word work and rereading
  • A routine for revisiting the text — rereading, illustrating, sentence cut-ups, word hunts

Constraints¶

  • Time-intensive relative to scripted programs; dictation and transcription consume instructional minutes [-W]
  • Effectiveness depends on the richness of the shared experience; a thin experience yields thin text [-W]
  • Verbatim transcription preserves learner errors (e.g., nonstandard grammar); instructors must decide whether and when to address these without discouraging contribution [~W]
  • As a whole-language-derived method, LEA alone does not systematically teach phonemic awareness or decoding; learners with weak alphabetic skills need explicit code instruction alongside it [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class chart: one shared experience dictated by the group, used for shared reading
  • Individual dictation: one-to-one, common in adult literacy and ESL tutoring (e.g., the "experience story" in volunteer literacy programs)
  • LEA with content areas: students dictate observations after a science experiment or field trip, integrating literacy with content learning
  • Digital adaptation: dictation typed live on a projector, with text later printed for take-home rereading

Target Learners¶

  • Beginning readers in early elementary settings, for whom personally dictated text guarantees comprehension of the "reading"
  • English language learners — the approach validates home language and builds literacy on oral proficiency Activation of prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]
  • Adult literacy and low-literacy learners, whose life experience supplies rich content that commercial primers lack
  • Less suitable as a stand-alone method for learners who need systematic, explicit phonics instruction [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Early reading: connecting speech to print, concepts of print, sight-word growth
  • Oral language development: narrating and sequencing experience
  • Writing: seeing one's own speech become text; later, moving from dictation to independent composition
  • Motivation and identity: experiencing oneself as an author and reader

Instructions¶

  1. Share an experience — conduct or recall a common experience; activate relevant language and vocabulary (Activation)
  2. Dictate and record — learners talk; instructor writes their words verbatim, reading back each sentence as it is written
  3. Read together — choral and individual rereading of the chart, with instructor pointing to track print (Practice)
  4. Word and sentence work — cut-up sentences, word hunts, illustrating the text (Annotating)
  5. Extend to independent writing — learners write their own version or a follow-up sentence (Application)

Related Strategies¶

  • Shared Reading — pairs naturally with LEA; big-book shared reading models what LEA charts then personalize
  • Dialogic Reading — the questioning routine that deepens the oral language LEA builds on
  • Dictated Writing / Interactive Writing — adjacent methods that shift more of the pen to the learner
  • Integrate oracy and literacy in meaningful activities rather than treating the four modes as separate

Examples¶

  • A kindergarten class visits a pumpkin patch, then dictates "We saw big pumpkins. Sam found a tiny one." onto a language experience chart; the class rereads it daily, hunts for the word pumpkin, and each child illustrates and copies one sentence.
  • An adult ESL tutor records a learner's account of immigrating as an "experience story," which becomes the learner's first sustained English text and the basis for vocabulary and grammar study.
  • Reading Recovery lessons incorporate writing and rereading of child-composed text, applying the same principle of literacy built on the child's own language.
