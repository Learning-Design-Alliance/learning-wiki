# Text To Speech Technology

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Text-to-speech (TTS) technology converts written text into synthesized spoken audio, allowing learners to listen to text rather than — or alongside — reading it. Modern implementations range from built-in screen readers and browser extensions (e.g., Microsoft Immersive Reader, Read&Write, NaturalReader) to embedded TTS in learning platforms and e-books. TTS is typically used as an [accommodation](../elements/accommodations.md) for learners with reading difficulties, as a universal design for learning (UDL) option for all learners, or as a fluency support when paired with highlighted text.

## Design Implications

TTS reduces the decoding burden of reading, which matters because struggling decoders spend so many working-memory resources on word recognition that comprehension suffers [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. By offloading decoding to audio, TTS gives learners access to grade-level content they could not yet read independently — but it supports *comprehension of content* more than it *teaches reading*. Evidence for comprehension gains is strongest when TTS is combined with synchronized text highlighting (bimodal presentation) and when learners actively engage with the text rather than passively listening [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Accurate, natural-sounding synthesis; robotic or mispronounced output adds extraneous load [Coherence principle: irrelevant or poorly integrated material hurts learning.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [~M]
- Synchronized highlighting of the word or sentence being spoken, so audio and visual input are integrated rather than competing
- Adjustable speed, voice, and voice pitch to match learner preference and processing pace
- A purpose for listening: comprehension questions, discussion, or note-taking that keeps learners active

#### Constraints
- TTS does not improve decoding or fluency by itself; learners who need to *build* word-recognition skill can bypass the very practice that would develop it [-M]
- Passive listening without a task produces shallow processing, similar to hearing background audio [-W]
- For proficient readers, TTS often adds no benefit and can slow reading or split attention between channels [~W]
- Poorly synchronized or word-by-word highlighting can fragment processing and hurt comprehension of longer passages [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Accommodation mode**: TTS for assessments and content access (e.g., read-aloud accommodations on state tests), independent of reading instruction
- **Bimodal reading**: text and audio simultaneously with highlighting, used during literacy instruction
- **Proofreading support**: learners use TTS to hear their own writing, catching errors the eye skips
- **Second-language support**: hearing pronunciation of unfamiliar words in context

### Target Learners
- Learners with dyslexia, low vision, or other print disabilities, for whom TTS is a well-established access accommodation [+M]
- Struggling decoders who need access to age-appropriate content and vocabulary beyond their independent reading level [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]
- English learners who benefit from hearing pronunciation alongside text
- Proficient readers gain little; TTS may even reduce efficiency for them [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Content-area comprehension: accessing science, social studies, and other expository text
- Vocabulary development: connecting written and spoken forms of new words
- Self-regulated learning: independent review and proofreading of one's own work

### Instructions
1. Select a TTS tool appropriate to the context (built-in OS screen reader, [Microsoft Immersive Reader](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/products/immersive-reader), [Read&Write](https://www.texthelp.com/products/readwrite/), or platform-embedded audio).
2. Configure voice, speed, and highlighting so audio and text are synchronized and intelligible.
3. Set a purpose for listening — comprehension questions, annotation, or discussion — so learners engage actively rather than passively.
4. Pair with comprehension supports such as [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of text into manageable segments and [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) while listening.
5. Monitor whether the learner is using TTS for *access* (appropriate) or *avoiding* decoding practice they still need (adjust accordingly).

## Examples
- **[Microsoft Immersive Reader](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/products/immersive-reader)** — free TTS with syllable splitting, picture dictionary, and synchronized highlighting, embedded across Word, Teams, and Edge.
- **[Read&Write (Texthelp)](https://www.texthelp.com/products/readwrite/)** — toolbar-based TTS with dictionary and study tools, widely used in K–12 special education.
- **[Learning Ally](https://learningally.org)** — human-narrated audiobook library for students with print disabilities, often paired with TTS tools.
- **Read-aloud test accommodations** — many U.S. state assessments permit TTS for non-reading sections, decoupling content access from decoding skill.
