# Varying Levels of Choice in Assessments

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Varying levels of choice in assessments means deliberately calibrating how much decision-making authority students have over what they are assessed on — from fully assigned topics, to constrained choice among options, to open-ended self-selected topics. The strategy is carried out by structuring assessment prompts with tiered options (e.g., choice of topic, choice of format, choice of sources) while holding the assessed criteria constant.

## Design Implications

Choice in assessments supports motivation primarily by satisfying the need for autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], but the motivational benefit depends on the options being meaningful and manageable rather than overwhelming [~M]. The design problem is calibration: too little choice removes ownership; too much choice increases decision load and can depress performance, especially for novices [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A stable set of assessment criteria or [Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) that apply across all options, so comparability and fairness are preserved
- Options that are genuinely distinct in content or angle, not cosmetic variations
- Scaffolding for the decision itself — e.g., [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md), proposal checkpoints, or topic-approval steps
- Alignment between each option and the target learning goals of the [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)

#### Constraints
- Excessive or poorly structured choice increases cognitive load and decision fatigue, reducing quality of work for learners with low prior knowledge [~M] — novices often lack the knowledge to discriminate between options
- Choice of *format* (e.g., video vs. essay) can undermine validity if formats differ in the constructs they measure; comparability of evidence must be checked, not assumed [~M]
- Choice works best when options are relevant to learners' interests and values; irrelevant or trivial choice produces no motivational gain and can add noise [~S]
- In high-stakes or standardized contexts, unconstrained choice can compromise score comparability and reliability [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Topic choice within a domain** — students select the subject of a paper or project; lowest-risk, preserves construct validity
- **Constrained menus** — 3–6 curated options (e.g., [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)); balances autonomy with decision support
- **Format choice** — students demonstrate the same competency through different products; requires careful rubric design
- **Progressive choice** — full assignment early in a course, expanding choice as expertise grows, consistent with the [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)

### Target Learners
- Learners with sufficient prior knowledge to evaluate and select among options meaningfully [~M]
- Adolescents and adults, for whom autonomy is a particularly strong motivational lever [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Disengaged or low-motivation students, for whom relevance-enhancing choice can increase effort and persistence [~M]
- Less beneficial for complete novices, who benefit more from constrained or assigned tasks until they can judge option quality [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Deep, self-directed engagement with content: choice increases ownership of inquiry and argument
- Transferable self-regulation: selecting, justifying, and managing one's own assessment path builds planning skills
- Not well suited to narrow skill fluency goals, where uniform tasks and [Practice](../elements/practice.md) are more efficient

### Instructions
1. Define the competencies and criteria all options must demonstrate; write one shared [Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md).
2. Decide the *level* of choice (topic, question, sources, format) and offer a bounded menu — typically 3–6 options — rather than unlimited choice.
3. Present options via a [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) or equivalent structure so students can compare options easily.
4. Add a low-stakes commitment step (proposal, outline, or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) so misfit is caught before high-stakes work begins.
5. Keep a default or assigned option available for students who prefer not to choose; opting out of choice is itself an autonomy support [~M].
6. After the assessment, have students briefly justify how their choice served the learning goals, connecting choice to [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md).

## Examples
- A history course assigns one essay on "causes of social change" but lets students choose among four case periods; all essays are scored on the same argumentation rubric.
- A programming capstone offers a menu of project briefs (data tool, game, accessibility app) with a shared code-review rubric and a required proposal checkpoint.
- **AP Capstone / performance-based assessment programs** (e.g., [College Board AP Seminar](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-seminar)) allow student-selected research topics within a fixed task structure and common scoring rubrics — a large-scale model of constrained choice.
