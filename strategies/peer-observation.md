# Peer Observation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), `q4` · 2 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Peer observation is a structured, reciprocal process in which colleagues observe each other's teaching to provide constructive, non-judgmental feedback and stimulate reflection. It typically follows a three-stage cycle: a pre-observation meeting to agree on focus and goals, the observation itself (often using a shared protocol or rubric), and a post-observation debrief for feedback and joint reflection. Unlike administrative evaluation, its purpose is developmental — building shared inquiry and a collaborative improvement culture rather than judging performance.

## Design Implications

Peer observation works by making the normally private, tacit dimensions of teaching visible for analysis, converting individual expertise into a shared resource. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the feedback conversation: feedback that targets specific teaching processes rather than global judgments is what drives improvement [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because the observer role draws on social modeling, observing a competent colleague enact a technique functions much like expert demonstration for adult learners [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Institutional commitment and protected time for the full cycle (pre-brief, observation, debrief)
- A culture of trust and psychological safety; observation must be clearly separated from performance evaluation
- A shared observation protocol or focus question so feedback is specific rather than impressionistic
- Careful pairing of colleagues — complementary but non-threatening matches (e.g., same department, different experience levels)
- A shared observation protocol or rubric so both parties attend to the same dimensions of practice
- A pre-observation conversation to establish focus (e.g., questioning technique, pacing, clarity of explanations)
- A post-observation debrief in which the observee self-assesses first, then the observer offers descriptive, non-judgmental evidence
- Institutional or group norms protecting the formative, confidential status of observations

#### Constraints
- Time constraints are the most common failure point; without scheduled release time, the cycle collapses into informal drop-ins with no feedback
- Where observation is linked to appraisal or evaluation, teachers become defensive and feedback loses honesty [-M]
- Poorly matched pairs (large status or expertise gaps) can reproduce hierarchy rather than collegial exchange [~M]
- Unstructured observations produce vague, non-actionable feedback; without a focus, debriefs default to pleasantries [-M]
- When tied to performance evaluation or promotion, observations produce defensive, polished-but-inauthentic teaching rather than improvement [-M]
- Unstructured observation ("just come watch my class") yields vague, low-utility feedback [-W]
- Observers without relevant expertise may anchor on superficial features (room setup, slide design) rather than learning-relevant behavior [~M]
- Reciprocity can collapse into mutual politeness ("you scratch my back…") when the same small group observes repeatedly [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal pairs**: colleagues alternate observer/observee roles across a term
- **Lesson study**: a team jointly plans, observes, and revises a single research lesson — observation embedded in collaborative design
- **Video-based observation**: recorded lessons allow repeated analysis and lower the threat of live observation
- **Departmental rounds**: small groups visit multiple classrooms around a shared instructional focus (e.g., questioning techniques)
- **Reciprocal pairs** — colleagues alternate observer/observee roles across a semester
- **Triads with rotation** — three colleagues cycle roles, broadening the range of models observed
- **Video-mediated observation** — the observee records themselves and selects clips, adding self-observation to peer feedback
- **Peer observation among students** — structured peer review of presentations, writing, or code, using the same protocol logic at learner level
- **Subject-specific vs. cross-disciplinary pairing** — the former gives deeper content insight; the latter surfaces generic pedagogical patterns invisible to insiders

### Target Learners
- Teachers at all career stages; early-career teachers gain concrete models of practice, while experienced teachers gain fresh perspectives on entrenched routines [+M]
- Whole departments seeking instructional coherence — repeated cycles build collective teacher efficacy, which is strongly associated with student achievement gains [+M]
- Less effective where participants are involuntary or where results feed into personnel decisions [~M]
- Instructors, especially early-career faculty and graduate teaching assistants, who lack a reference model of what experienced teaching looks like in practice [+M]
- Pre-service teachers during practicum, where observation plus debrief accelerates professional vision
- Students giving and receiving peer feedback on performances, presentations, or drafts — the act of judging peers' work improves their own subsequent performance [+M]
- Less useful for experts who already have accurate self-monitoring; the added perspective yields diminishing returns [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improvement of specific instructional techniques (questioning, wait time, formative checking)
- Development of reflective practice and analytic language for teaching
- Transfer of effective practices across classrooms through shared observation and debrief
- Professional vision: learning to notice and interpret significant features of practice
- Reflective practice: connecting observed behavior to underlying decisions
- Feedback literacy: both giving and receiving actionable, evidence-based feedback
- Schema building for quality: internalizing criteria through evaluating others' work

### Instructions
1. Agree on the purpose and ground rules with all participants; confirm the observation is developmental and non-evaluative.
2. Hold a pre-observation meeting to set a specific focus question (e.g., "How do I distribute questioning across the room?").
3. Observe using a shared protocol, recording descriptive evidence rather than judgments ([Observation/Shadowing](../elements/observation-shadowing.md) if available; otherwise a structured note template).
4. Debrief promptly using a feedback structure that leads with evidence and invites the observee's own reflection first ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)) — process-level, evidence-anchored feedback drives change [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
5. Agree one concrete adjustment to try before the next cycle, and schedule the reciprocal observation.

## Examples
- A mathematics department runs reciprocal observations focused on questioning techniques; each teacher is observed twice per term with a shared protocol, and debriefs feed into department meetings.
- A team uses [lesson study](../strategies/lesson-study.md) to refine a single lesson: joint planning, live observation of the research lesson, and revision based on detailed observational evidence.
- University teaching development programs (e.g., UK Higher Education Academy schemes) use reciprocal peer observation pairs as a requirement of teaching accreditation.
- **UK PSF-aligned peer observation schemes** — Most UK universities run reciprocal peer observation of teaching as part of Fellowship of the Higher Education Academy pathways; Gosling's models (evaluative, developmental, collaborative) are widely used to frame these schemes.
- **Peer review of presentations** — Students use a shared rubric to observe classmates' conference-style talks, then debrief; observing peers' varied approaches builds criteria for their own next presentation.
- **Video clubs for teachers** — Groups of mathematics teachers (e.g., in the work of the QUASAR project) meet to analyze video clips of one another's lessons, developing shared professional vision of student thinking.

## Description
Peer observation is a reciprocal, non-evaluative process in which colleagues (or peers in a classroom) observe one another's practice using a shared protocol, then meet to discuss what was observed. It converts teaching — or any performable skill — into an object of shared scrutiny, making tacit decisions visible and open to formative feedback rather than summative judgment.

## Design Implications

Peer observation works because observation of others' performance builds the observer's own schema for quality while giving the observed party an external perspective they cannot generate alone [Social modeling and observation underpin skill acquisition through attention, retention, and reproduction.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the feedback conversation: feedback that addresses task and process levels improves learning, while feedback aimed at the person does not [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Structured protocols and a formative (non-evaluative) framing distinguish productive peer observation from surveillance, which suppresses the risk-taking that improvement requires.
