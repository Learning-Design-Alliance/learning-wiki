# Retrieval Practice Openers

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Retrieval practice openers begin a lesson with a brief activity in which learners recall previously learned material from memory — a short quiz, free recall prompt, or two or three questions — before new content is introduced. The act of retrieval itself strengthens and stabilizes memory, and the recalled material is then available to be connected to the day's new content.

## Design Implications

Retrieval practice is one of the most robust findings in learning science: attempting to recall information produces substantially better long-term retention than rereading or reviewing it [+S]. Placing retrieval at the *start* of a lesson serves double duty — it exploits the retrieval-strength benefit while simultaneously activating prior knowledge that new material can attach to [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Openers should target material from prior sessions spaced days or weeks back, not just the immediately preceding lesson, to gain the spacing benefit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions or prompts that target previously taught, retrievable content — not new material
- Low-stakes or no-stakes framing so retrieval is treated as learning, not assessment
- A mechanism for learners to get feedback on their answers (self-check, peer check, or instructor review)
- Brief duration (3–7 minutes) so the opener does not consume instructional time

#### Constraints
- Retrieval of poorly learned or never-encoded material produces errors that can be relearned if not corrected [-M] — feedback after the opener is not optional
- Retrieving the *same* fact in the *same* format every time encourages rote cue-response habits rather than flexible knowledge [~M]; vary question formats and contexts
- If the opener becomes graded or high-stakes, it shifts from a learning event to an assessment event and can raise anxiety without adding retention benefit [~M]
- Openers on material learners have fully mastered waste time with minimal additional benefit [~W] — target partially learned content

#### Implementation Variability
- **Free recall ("brain dump")**: learners write everything they remember about a topic; low preparation, works across subjects
- **Two-to-four question quiz**: paper, oral, or digital (e.g., a short [Kahoot](https://kahoot.com) or Google Forms quiz); faster scoring, more targeted
- **Cumulative starters**: a recurring daily routine mixing questions from the whole course so far (common in UK "Do Now" routines)
- **Retrieval + application**: recall a prior concept, then immediately apply it to a new problem, bridging to the day's content

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit from retrieval practice, but gains are largest for those who would otherwise study by rereading [+S]
- Learners with weaker prior knowledge need more feedback support after retrieval, since they generate more errors
- Learners with test anxiety benefit from consistently low-stakes framing; the routine normalizes recall attempts as safe

### Target Learning Goals
- Long-term retention of declarative and procedural knowledge [+S]
- Fluency and automaticity with foundational facts and procedures needed for new learning
- Activation of prior knowledge as a bridge to new content [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Select 2–4 retrieval prompts targeting material from prior sessions, prioritizing content spaced furthest back and content needed for today's lesson.
2. Present the prompts at the very start of the session, before any review or re-teaching — the struggle to recall is the mechanism, so do not pre-teach first.
3. Give learners a fixed, short time to respond individually from memory (no notes).
4. Provide immediate feedback on correct answers, and briefly re-teach anything that was widely missed.
5. Connect the retrieved content to the new lesson's material, using it as the activation step ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or an [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) bridge can serve this link).
6. Rotate the same content back into future openers on an expanding schedule to combine retrieval with spacing.

## Examples
- A high-school biology teacher opens each class with a four-question "Do Now" quiz mixing last week's and last month's content; students self-grade from a projected key before the new topic begins.
- **Retrieval Practice Guide** ([retrievalpractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org)) — Agarwal and Bain's practitioner resource with downloadable opener routines ("Brain Dump", "Two Things") used across K–12 classrooms.
- A university statistics course begins each lecture with two clicker questions on the previous lecture plus one on material from three weeks earlier, followed by instructor feedback before new content.
