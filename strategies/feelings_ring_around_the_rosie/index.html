Feelings Ring Around the Rosie¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

During recess or circle time, learners play "Ring Around the Rosie" while acting out a different emotion each round. When the group falls down, the facilitator calls an emotion (happy, sad, frustrated, surprised), and learners rise and move through the next round while expressing that emotion with face, posture, and movement. The familiar song structure provides a low-stakes, repetitive frame in which emotional expression becomes playful rather than evaluative.

Design Implications¶

The strategy pairs embodied, playful repetition with emotion labeling — a combination consistent with evidence that pretend and physical play support young children's emotional competence [~M] and that emotion knowledge in early childhood predicts later social adjustment [+M]. Because the game is highly structured and socially shared, it lowers the risk of self-consciousness that can accompany direct emotion expression, while the facilitator's labels build the vocabulary learners need to identify feelings in themselves and others.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An open space where a circle of learners can move safely
  • A facilitator to lead the song, call the emotion each round, and model the expression (Demonstration)
  • A small repertoire of emotions matched to learners' vocabulary level, introduced a few at a time

Constraints¶

  • Requires physical mobility; learners with motor impairments need an adapted role (e.g., facial expression only, or leading the emotion call)
  • Some learners find public emotional expression uncomfortable or overstimulating; forced performance can backfire [~W] — participation should remain invitational, with an observer role available
  • The game rehearses expressing emotions, not regulating them; it does not by itself teach coping strategies and should not substitute for explicit emotion-regulation instruction

Implementation Variability¶

  • Swap the falling-down action for a freeze pose so learners hold the emotion expression for observation and discussion
  • Let learners take turns calling the emotion, shifting them from performers to directors
  • Add a brief Check-In afterward ("Which feeling was hardest to show?") to consolidate labeling
  • Extend to matching emotions to situations ("When would you feel this?") during Class Discussion

Target Learners¶

  • Early childhood and early elementary students (roughly ages 3–8), for whom play is a primary context for social-emotional learning [~M]
  • Learners building emotion vocabulary or who benefit from kinesthetic, embodied encoding
  • Less suitable without adaptation for older students, who may find the song infantilizing; the emotion-charades structure transfers better than the song itself

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotion identification and labeling (recognizing and naming feelings)
  • Nonverbal expression and reading of facial and bodily cues in peers
  • Social participation: turn-taking, shared attention, group coordination

Instructions¶

  1. Teach the game in its plain form first so the song and circle mechanics are automatic and do not compete with the emotional content for attention.
  2. Demonstration: facilitator models one emotion with exaggerated face and body, naming it ("This is frustrated — see my tight fists?").
  3. Play a round; on "we all fall down," the facilitator calls the next emotion.
  4. Learners rise and act the emotion while circling (Act It Out); the facilitator coaches and narrates ("I see lots of surprised faces — wide eyes!").
  5. After 3–5 rounds, close with a short Practice round of guessing: one learner expresses, others name the feeling.
  6. Debrief briefly with a Check-In to connect the play to real situations.

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a related emotion-identification game with a check-in frame

Related Elements¶

  • Act It Out — the embodied expression component
  • Demonstration — facilitator modeling of each emotion before learners attempt it
  • Check-In — the reflection step that consolidates labeling
  • Coaching — in-the-moment narration and feedback during play

Examples¶

  • A preschool teacher runs the game during outdoor play, rotating through four "feelings of the week" so expressions align with the classroom's emotion-vocabulary curriculum (e.g., a Second Step-style feelings unit).
  • A kindergarten inclusion classroom adapts the game so a learner using a wheelchair leads the emotion calls, keeping every child in a valued role.
