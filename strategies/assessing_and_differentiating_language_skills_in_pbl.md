# Assessing and Differentiating Language Skills in PBL

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Assessing and differentiating language skills in project-based learning (PBL) means treating academic language as an explicit outcome of the project, not a hidden prerequisite. Educators assess vocabulary, discourse structures, and language functions alongside content, then use the results to differentiate products (what students create), processes (how they work), and supports (what scaffolds are provided). The cycle is continuous: assessment informs differentiation, and differentiated work generates new evidence of language growth.

## Design Implications

Language demands in PBL are high because projects require students to read, discuss, and produce discipline-specific texts while managing open-ended work. Embedding language assessment within the project — rather than as a separate event — makes it formative and actionable [Formative feedback at task and process levels improves learning more than grade-level feedback.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Differentiation based on that evidence is most effective when scaffolds are adjusted responsively to what learners actually demonstrate [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Ongoing assessment of language skills (vocabulary, syntax, discourse functions) embedded in project checkpoints, not only at final presentation
- Rubrics that separate content criteria from language criteria so both can be assessed and fed back on independently
- Flexible instructional strategies and a commitment to adjusting [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md) as proficiency data accumulates
- Accessible task design consistent with [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) so assessment measures language growth rather than uncontrolled reading difficulty

#### Constraints
- Requires ongoing assessment and adjustment; without dedicated time for checkpoints, language needs surface too late to address [-W]
- Differentiation can lower expectations for ELLs if language supports substitute for, rather than accompany, grade-level content tasks [~M]
- Assessing language during collaborative work is difficult — individual contributions to group talk require structured observation protocols, and group products can mask individual proficiency [-W]
- Additional resources or co-planning time (e.g., with ESL specialists) are often needed to differentiate effectively

#### Implementation Variability
- **Product differentiation:** varied output formats (oral presentation, annotated diagram, written report) at equivalent language demands
- **Process differentiation:** tiered texts, sentence frames, structured [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) roles, or pre-teaching of key vocabulary before project launches
- **Assessment variability:** portfolios, language-focused rubrics, observational checklists during project work, and self-assessment against language targets

### Target Learners
- English Language Learners (ELLs) in project-based environments, who must acquire academic language while simultaneously learning content [~M]
- All students benefit when language criteria are explicit; novices to a discipline's discourse gain the most from vocabulary and structure scaffolds [+W]
- Students with low academic self-efficacy benefit from visible language progress, which supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic language proficiency: vocabulary, syntax, and discourse functions of the discipline
- Transferable communication skills: presenting, arguing, and writing for authentic audiences ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))
- Metalinguistic awareness: students monitoring and self-assessing their own language use

### Instructions
1. Identify the language demands of the project (key vocabulary, text types, discourse functions) during planning, using [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) to preview them with students.
2. Establish baseline proficiency through an early low-stakes [Formative Assessment](../elements/formative-assessment.md) task embedded in the project launch.
3. Use language-specific rubrics at project checkpoints to [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md), separating content mastery from language proficiency.
4. Differentiate processes and products based on results: tiered texts, sentence frames, or extended [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with target language functions for developing learners; extension tasks for advanced ones.
5. Provide targeted feedback on language at task and process level [Formative feedback at task and process levels improves learning more than grade-level feedback.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S], and have students self-assess language growth ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) as [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
6. Adjust scaffolds responsively — fading supports as proficiency is demonstrated [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

## Examples
- A middle-school science PBL unit on water quality pre-teaches disciplinary vocabulary (e.g., *turbidity*, *hypothesis*), assesses its use in lab notebooks at mid-project checkpoints, and offers sentence frames for the final presentation to students still developing proficiency — while advanced students draft the written report.
- ELL students in a project-based history course choose between an oral narrated slideshow and a written exhibit label as their product, with both assessed against the same language-function rubric (cause–effect language, evidence citation).
