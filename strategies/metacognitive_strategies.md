# Metacognitive Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Metacognitive strategies teach students to think about their thinking: to plan an approach before a task, monitor comprehension and progress during it, and evaluate outcomes and strategy effectiveness afterward. They range from embedded prompts and checklists to explicit instruction in self-regulation routines, and they extend to managing motivation and effort, not just cognition.

## Design Implications

Metacognitive instruction reliably improves academic performance when it is integrated into subject-matter teaching rather than delivered as a generic study-skills add-on [Teaching metacognitive strategies embedded in content improves achievement.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220973.1996.9943463) [+S]. The mechanism is giving learners control over processes they would otherwise leave implicit: planning, monitoring, and revision. Because learners are poor judges of their own comprehension, external checks — self-testing, [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), progress monitoring — are needed to make monitoring accurate rather than merely confident [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit modeling of metacognitive moves by the instructor, typically via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), so learners can observe planning and monitoring, not just outcomes
- Structured prompts at each phase — plan ("What is the goal? What approach fits?"), monitor ("Do I understand? Is this working?"), evaluate ("What worked? What would I change?")
- Opportunities to apply and adapt strategies within real content tasks, with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) or feedback on strategy use, not just task answers [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Sustained practice over time; brief one-off workshops produce little durable change

#### Constraints
- Generic strategy instruction detached from content ("learn these study tips") shows weak and inconsistent effects; strategies must be taught and practiced within specific subject domains [Metacognitive instruction works best embedded in subject content.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220973.1996.9943463) [~S]
- Novices often lack the domain knowledge to monitor accurately — they do not know what they do not know — so early monitoring prompts can produce miscalibration [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]
- Reflection activities that are unstructured or graded on completion alone can become ritualistic compliance with little cognitive engagement
- Young learners and novices have limited capacity to simultaneously execute a task and evaluate their execution; prompts must be sequenced, not simultaneous

#### Implementation Variability
- Embedded prompts and checklists within tasks (low cost, scalable)
- [Reciprocal-style](../elements/coaching.md) routines where learners alternate roles of questioner and solver
- Portfolio or journal-based reflection with structured templates
- Strategy instruction curricula (e.g., reciprocal teaching for reading) delivered over multiple weeks

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners and low achievers benefit most; strategy instruction narrows gaps by compensating for underdeveloped self-regulation [Metacognitive strategy training shows the largest gains for lower-achieving students.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220973.1996.9943463) [+M]
- Adolescents and adults, who have the cognitive capacity to reflect on their own cognition; benefits for young children require heavy scaffolding
- Less effective for experts, who already deploy domain-appropriate strategies automatically [Expertise reverses the benefit of added instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own study and problem solving
- Strategy selection: matching approaches to task demands and revising failing approaches
- Transfer: learners who can articulate *when and why* a strategy works are more likely to apply it in new contexts [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Long-term retention through self-testing and spaced review decisions [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Model the full cycle with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): set a goal, choose a strategy, monitor comprehension aloud, notice and repair a breakdown.
2. Introduce a simple planning frame before tasks — goal, approach, anticipated difficulty — using [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) where helpful.
3. Embed monitoring prompts during tasks (comprehension checks, self-testing, [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)) rather than only after completion.
4. Provide [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and process-level feedback on strategy use, not just correctness [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. Close with structured evaluation — what worked, what did not, what to try next time — and gradually [fade](../elements/fading.md) the prompts as learners internalize the cycle [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Related Strategies
- [Self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) — a monitoring strategy that surfaces gaps in understanding during learning
- [Spaced repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md) — a planning strategy learners should be taught to schedule deliberately
- [Formative assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) — external monitoring that scaffolds the internal monitoring learners must eventually do themselves

## Examples
**Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — Small groups alternate roles of summarizer, questioner, clarifier, and predictor while reading; explicit modeling and role rotation teach monitoring and repair strategies, with documented comprehension gains.

**[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Mastery-tracking dashboards make progress visible, prompting learners to plan what to practice next and evaluate their own readiness before assessments.

**Exam wrappers (e.g., widely used in university STEM courses)** — Post-exam reflection forms ask students how they studied, what predicted their errors, and what they will change; a lightweight, embedded evaluation routine.
