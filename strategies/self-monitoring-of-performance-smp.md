# Self-Monitoring of Performance (SMP)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
Self-monitoring of performance is a strategy in which learners observe, record, and evaluate an aspect of their own academic performance — for example, the number of math problems completed correctly per minute, pages read, or accuracy on written work. The learner typically uses a simple recording tool (tally sheet, graph, checklist) against a clear performance standard. Unlike self-monitoring of *attention* or *behavior*, SMP targets the academic product itself, making it a core component of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [Self-monitoring is one of the most consistently effective self-regulation subprocesses for academic outcomes.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M].

## Design Implications

SMP works because it converts an invisible performance stream into visible, immediate feedback, which both informs the learner and functions as a reactive intervention — the act of recording alone often improves the monitored behavior [Self-monitoring produces reactive effects on the monitored behavior.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]. It is most effective when the monitored dimension is one the learner can actually control (rate, accuracy, persistence) and when records are compared against an explicit criterion rather than left uninterpreted.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clearly defined, observable academic behavior or product to monitor (e.g., problems correct per minute)
- A low-effort recording method matched to the learner's age and skill (tally marks, graph, digital counter)
- An explicit performance standard or goal so the record can be evaluated, not just collected
- Learner training in how to count and record accurately before independent use
- A visible, specific performance standard: rubric, worked model, target metric, or answer key ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) provides the model to monitor against)
- A lightweight recording mechanism — checklist, log, or self-scoring form — that does not itself consume working memory [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Opportunities to act on what is monitored; monitoring must be followed by strategy adjustment or practice, not just recording
- Periodic external calibration (instructor feedback, peer check) to correct drifting self-judgments

#### Constraints
- SMP improves the *application* of existing skills rather than teaching new ones [-M] — a learner who cannot do the work will not benefit from tracking it
- Effectiveness drops when the monitored dimension is not under the learner's control or the standard is vague [-M]
- Recording adds working memory and time demands during tasks; for novices the monitoring burden can compete with the learning task itself [~W]
- Accuracy of self-recording degrades without periodic checks; inflated self-records are common when no verification exists [-W]
- Monitoring accuracy is poor for novices: they lack the knowledge to detect their own errors and often judge fluency as mastery [-M]
- Monitoring effort or time-on-task rather than quality of performance can reinforce unproductive persistence [~M]
- Over-monitoring (excessive check-ins, logging overhead) shifts attention from the task to the self and can undermine motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Self-monitoring of accuracy** vs. **rate** — accuracy suits quality goals; rate suits fluency goals
- **Teacher- vs. learner-set goals** — learner-set goals add motivation benefits but require goal-setting training
- **Graphing** — having learners plot their own data strengthens the feedback loop and sustains engagement
- **Fading** — recording prompts can be systematically withdrawn once target performance is stable (McDougall & Brady, 1998)
- **Process monitoring** (Did I follow the steps? Am I confused?) vs. **outcome monitoring** (Is the answer correct? Does the draft meet the rubric?) — process monitoring suits skill acquisition, outcome monitoring suits revision and study regulation
- **Frequency**: continuous (error detection during performance), interval (end of each work session), or milestone-based (end of each task stage)
- **Recording mode**: tally sheets and timers (behavioral), self-scoring against keys (cognitive), structured reflection prompts (metacognitive)
- **Social forms**: peer monitoring pairs and [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) add external accountability and calibration

### Target Learners
- Learners with underdeveloped self-regulation, including students with ADHD or learning disabilities [Self-management interventions reliably improve academic performance for students with disabilities.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Elementary through adult learners; procedures must be adapted to developmental level
- Less appropriate for learners who lack the target skill entirely — teach the skill first, then use SMP for fluency and consistency
- Adolescents and adults with enough metacognitive development to judge quality against a standard [+M]
- Novices benefit only when external standards (models, keys, rubrics) substitute for their missing internal expertise [-M]
- Advanced learners benefit most, since they can detect subtle discrepancies between their performance and expert standards [Self-monitoring accuracy increases with domain expertise.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Fluency building: increasing rate of accurate responding on practiced skills
- Work completion and academic engaged time
- Metacognitive habit formation: establishing the monitor-and-evaluate cycle of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Skill refinement: identifying and correcting specific performance errors
- Study regulation: allocating time and effort to the material most in need of work
- Metacognitive development: building durable habits of judging one's own understanding
- Transfer of self-regulation to independent, unsupervised performance

### Instructions
1. Define the target performance and a measurable indicator (rate, accuracy, or completion).
2. Set an explicit performance criterion with the learner, ideally collaboratively.
3. Teach the recording procedure and have the learner [Practice](../elements/practice.md) it until records are accurate.
4. Have the learner record performance during work sessions and compare results to the criterion.
5. [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) periodically — verify self-records against teacher counts and adjust the criterion upward as performance improves.
6. Fade the recording supports once target performance is maintained independently.

## Examples
- A student tallies the number of math facts answered correctly during a two-minute timing each day and graphs the results against a personal best (McDougall & Brady, 1998).
- A middle schooler records pages read during silent study periods and compares weekly totals to a negotiated goal.
- **[ClassDojo](https://www.classdojo.com)** and similar behavior-tracking apps digitize the recording step, though SMP requires the *learner*, not the teacher, to do the recording for the self-regulation benefit to accrue.
- **Self-scoring math practice**: students check each problem against an answer key immediately, log error types, and choose the next problem set based on which error type dominates — immediate self-scoring improves subsequent study decisions [+M]
- **Writing revision with rubrics**: students score their own draft against a six-trait rubric before peer review, then revise the two lowest-scoring traits
- **Reading fluency graphs**: students chart words-correct-per-minute across repeated readings, making progress visible and motivating further practice [+M]
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery progress bars and skill dashboards externalize performance monitoring, showing learners exactly which skills remain below mastery

## Description
Self-monitoring of performance is a [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) strategy in which learners deliberately observe, record, and evaluate their own progress against a standard — a goal, a rubric, an expert model, or a prior best. It is carried out through mechanisms such as progress checklists, accuracy self-scoring, timing logs, or structured reflection prompts, and it feeds the monitoring–control loop: what learners notice about their performance shapes what they do next [Self-monitoring is the pivotal process linking strategy use to strategy adjustment.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M].

## Design Implications

Self-monitoring converts practice from repetition into feedback-driven adjustment; learners who accurately judge their own performance can target weaknesses rather than rehearsing what they already do well [Monitoring accuracy is necessary for effective self-regulated study decisions.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. The design task is to supply clear standards and low-effort recording structures, because monitoring without a reference standard produces inflated judgments and misdirected study [Learners' self-assessments are often inaccurate without external criteria.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M].

### Instructions
1. Establish the standard: give learners a rubric, target metric, or expert [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) that defines what good performance looks like.
2. Model the monitoring itself: think aloud while checking work against the standard, naming the discrepancies you notice.
3. Provide a recording structure — checklist, self-scoring sheet, or [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) prompt — and have learners monitor at defined breakpoints, not mid-task.
4. Require an adjustment decision: after each monitoring episode, learners select one concrete change to strategy or effort before continuing.
5. Calibrate externally: compare self-scores against instructor or peer judgments periodically and discuss discrepancies.
6. Fade the scaffolds: shift from provided checklists to learner-generated criteria as monitoring accuracy improves [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].
