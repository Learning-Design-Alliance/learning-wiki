# Tier Two Vocabulary Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Tier Two vocabulary instruction targets high-frequency, general-academic words — such as *coincide*, *reluctant*, *analyze*, and *fortunate* — that appear across many contexts and content areas but are unlikely to be learned incidentally by struggling readers (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan's three-tier framework). Instruction is carried out through explicit, friendly definitions, multiple encounters in varied contexts, active processing such as word manipulation and comparison, and repeated spaced review, rather than dictionary copying or isolated memorization.

## Design Implications

Tier Two words offer the highest instructional yield because they are frequent enough to repay teaching yet not so common that most learners already know them; vocabulary instruction produces reliable gains in word learning and comprehension when it goes beyond definitional teaching [Stahl & Fairbanks meta-analysis on vocabulary instruction and comprehension.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. Effective programs distribute word learning across rich, varied encounters rather than a single exposure, since word knowledge is incremental and multidimensional (Nagy & Scott, 2000).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A principled selection process: words must be high-frequency, useful across domains, and known to mature language users but not all students
- Student-friendly explanations in everyday language, not dictionary definitions
- Multiple, varied encounters (typically 4+ exposures in different contexts) with active processing — comparing words, judging when they fit, rewriting sentences
- Spaced review over days and weeks to consolidate word knowledge

#### Constraints
- Teaching too many words at once dilutes depth; 8–10 words per week is a practical ceiling for deep instruction [-M]
- Definitional-only instruction (look it up, write the sentence) produces weak, fragile word knowledge [-S]
- Tier Two selection assumes a monolingual, school-English baseline; for multilingual learners the same word may be Tier Two for one student and a new Tier One label for another [~M]
- Gains in taught-word knowledge do not automatically transfer to general reading comprehension; effects on comprehension are smaller and depend on volume of words taught [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Text-based selection**: pull words from an upcoming reading so instruction supports comprehension of that text
- **Robust vocabulary instruction**: Beck & McKeown's approach emphasizing interactive, playful word use ("Is this a good example of *reluctant*?")
- **Morphological extension**: teach word families (*analyze, analysis, analytical*) to multiply yield per word
- **Frayer model / semantic mapping**: organize new words against known concepts and [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)

### Target Learners
- Students with limited school-relevant vocabulary, including those from low-income households who hear fewer Tier Two words in home discourse [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from explicit instruction because incidental acquisition from text is slower for them [~S]
- Less valuable for advanced readers who already know most high-frequency academic words and need Tier Three (domain-specific) instruction instead

### Target Learning Goals
- Receptive and expressive word knowledge (meaning, use, connotation, collocation)
- Reading comprehension of academic and informational text
- General academic language proficiency that transfers across subjects

### Instructions
1. **Select** 8–10 Tier Two words from an upcoming text or unit, prioritizing words that appear across many contexts and are central to the text's meaning.
2. **Define friendly**: introduce each word with a plain-language explanation and an example, activating related knowledge ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
3. **Process actively**: have students judge examples and non-examples, complete sentence frames, and compare the new word to synonyms — contrasting cases sharpen meaning ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)) [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of word meaning.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].
4. **Extend across the week**: revisit words in new contexts, in discussion, and in writing, spacing encounters over days.
5. **Review and assess**: use quick spaced checks (word walls, exit questions) rather than single end-of-unit tests.

## Examples
- **Robust Vocabulary Instruction (Beck & McKeown)** — kindergarten and primary classrooms teaching words like *mercy* and *tolerate* from read-alouds through interactive questioning and word play; documented gains in expressive vocabulary.
- **Word Generation (SERP Institute)** — a free middle-school program teaching five Tier Two words per week across all subject areas via discussion, debate, and writing (https://www.serpinstitute.org/word-generation).
- **Bringing Words to Life routines** — teachers ask students to choose which of two scenarios "shows *reluctance*," forcing application rather than recitation.
