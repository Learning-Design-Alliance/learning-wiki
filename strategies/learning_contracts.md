# Learning Contracts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A learning contract is a formal, typically written agreement in which a learner commits to specific learning objectives, the strategies and resources they will use to pursue them, a timeline, and the criteria or evidence by which achievement will be judged. The instructor's role shifts from content deliverer to negotiator and evaluator: they help shape feasible goals, approve the plan, and assess against the agreed evidence. Contracts are the signature structural device of self-directed learning in adult education, developed most fully by Malcolm Knowles for andragogical settings.

## Design Implications

Learning contracts operationalize learner autonomy, and autonomy is a reliable lever for intrinsic motivation and engagement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. The act of drafting, monitoring, and evaluating a contract also builds self-regulatory skill, since learners must plan, track progress, and judge their own performance against explicit criteria [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Contracts work best when goals are framed as processes and competencies rather than grades or outcomes, particularly for less experienced learners [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A structured template: objectives, learning activities/resources, timeline, evidence of accomplishment, and evaluation criteria
- Instructor availability for negotiation and [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) during the contract period, not just at signing and grading
- Clear alignment between contract objectives and course or program outcomes so contracts do not drift into busywork
- Learner preparation in goal-setting and self-assessment; students cannot negotiate objectives they have never articulated

#### Constraints
- Contracts impose high planning and self-regulatory demands; novices given full freedom often set vague, unmeasurable, or trivially easy objectives [-M]
- Without scheduled checkpoints, contracts degrade into end-of-term paperwork with little effect on study behavior [-M]
- Instructors accustomed to content control may convert "negotiation" into rubber-stamping, undermining the autonomy that makes contracts motivating [-W]
- Poor fit for tightly sequenced foundational curricula where all learners must reach identical objectives on the same schedule [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full contracts**: learners propose objectives, activities, and evidence from scratch (typical in independent study, fieldwork, capstones)
- **Partial or structured contracts**: instructor supplies the objectives; learners negotiate only activities, resources, and evidence — a common entry point in undergraduate courses
- **Graded vs. pass/fail evaluation**: pass/fail or competency-verified contracts reduce grade pressure and encourage risk-taking
- **Group contracts**: teams negotiate shared goals and individual role commitments, adding accountability through peer expectation

### Target Learners
- Adult and professional learners with clear purposes for learning, for whom self-direction aligns with workplace autonomy [+M]
- Graduate students in independent study, practica, or thesis work
- Undergraduates benefit most when contracts are heavily scaffolded and instructor-negotiated; full autonomy contracts can overwhelm students with weak self-regulation [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]
- Learners with low academic self-efficacy may need early, achievable contract milestones to build persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-directed learning skill: goal-setting, planning, monitoring, and self-evaluation
- Applied and professional competencies where learners bring different prior experience and need different paths
- Affective and dispositional goals (responsibility, ownership) that uniform instruction addresses poorly

### Instructions
1. Diagnose needs: learners assess current competence against course or program outcomes, with instructor [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) to surface gaps.
2. Draft objectives: learners write 2–5 specific, evidence-verifiable objectives; the instructor negotiates scope and rigor.
3. Specify activities and resources: learners select the [application](../elements/application.md) activities, readings, and experiences that will move them toward each objective.
4. Set evidence and criteria: agree in advance on what artifacts or performances will demonstrate attainment and how they will be judged.
5. Establish checkpoints: schedule periodic [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) for progress monitoring and contract amendment.
6. Evaluate: learner self-assesses against the agreed evidence first; instructor evaluates and provides [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) feedback, closing the loop for the next contract cycle.

## Examples
- **Knowles' andragogical practice** — Knowles used contracts as the organizing structure for graduate courses in adult education at Boston University and the Fielding Institute; students negotiated individualized objectives within a shared course competency framework (Knowles, 1986).
- **Nursing and clinical education** — preceptorship programs in nursing and medicine commonly use learning contracts to individualize clinical placement goals, activities, and competency evidence negotiated between student, preceptor, and faculty.
- **University honors and capstone contracts** — many honors programs (e.g., honors contract courses at large US public universities) let students convert a standard course into an enriched one via a contract specifying an extended project and its assessment criteria.
