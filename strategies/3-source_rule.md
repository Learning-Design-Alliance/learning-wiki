# 3-Source Rule

## Description
When students conduct online research, the teacher enforces a rule that any factual claim must be confirmed across three different websites before it can be used. The rule operationalizes *corroboration* — one of the core historical-thinking heuristics alongside sourcing and contextualization — by forcing students to seek independent confirmation rather than trusting the first result. The classic test case is the "tree octopus" hoax site: a claim can appear on three sites and still be false if all three copy the same misinformation, which is precisely why the rule must be paired with source-quality evaluation.

## Design Implications

Corroboration tasks push students beyond single-source reading and toward evaluating the *ecosystem* of information around a claim, which is a foundation of online reasoning [~M]. However, research on professional fact-checkers shows that experts rarely corroborate by reading multiple pages deeply; instead they read *laterally* — leaving a page immediately to check what other sources say about the site itself — and outperform even PhD historians on credibility tasks [~M]. The 3-source rule is therefore best treated as a scaffold that should evolve toward lateral-reading habits rather than a terminal procedure.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A research task with genuine factual claims to verify (not purely interpretive questions)
- Access to multiple, genuinely independent sources — students need guidance on what "independent" means (different publishers, not three sites citing the same wire story)
- A record-keeping structure (annotated bibliography, verification log) so corroboration is visible and assessable
- Instruction in source evaluation ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md), checking authorship, date, and purpose) so the rule is not satisfied by quantity alone

#### Constraints
- Three agreeing sources do not establish truth: misinformation propagates across sites, so agreement can reflect copying rather than confirmation [-M]
- The rule can teach a "count the sources" heuristic that students apply mechanically, without evaluating source quality or independence [-M]
- It is slower and less effective than lateral reading for evaluating unfamiliar websites; experts leave the page rather than corroborating within it [~M]
- Impractical for claims in domains where only one or two authoritative sources exist (primary data, niche scholarship)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Threshold variation:** two sources for low-stakes claims, three for claims that will anchor an argument
- **Independence requirement:** require sources from different publishers, authors, or countries
- **Lateral-reading upgrade:** replace "find three sites agreeing" with "find three sites *about* the original source" (Wineburg & McGrew's fact-checker protocol)
- **Role variation:** assign different students different sources, then have them reconcile discrepancies in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)

### Target Learners
- Middle and secondary students beginning independent online research, who default to trusting top search results [~M]
- Students in history, science, and media literacy courses where sourcing norms are explicit learning goals
- Less useful for advanced learners, who should move to lateral reading and full source appraisal rather than a fixed count

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: evaluating credibility and independence of sources
- Epistemic cognition: understanding that claims require justification and that agreement is evidence only when sources are independent
- Argumentation: building claims on verified evidence ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))

### Instructions
1. Pose a research question with checkable factual claims.
2. Model the rule with a live demonstration, including a hoax site (e.g., the tree octopus) where three sites agree and are all wrong — show that agreement alone fails.
3. Have students gather candidate sources and record each claim with its sources in a verification log ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. Require students to classify each source (author, publisher, purpose, date) before counting it toward the three.
5. Where sources disagree, require students to explain the discrepancy and judge which source is more reliable ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) work well for this).
6. Fade the rule: replace the count with a lateral-reading check — "What do other sites say about *this* source?" — as students gain experience.

## Examples
- **Stanford History Education Group** ([sheg.stanford.edu](https://sheg.stanford.edu)) — published the "tree octopus" assessment and the lateral-reading research that motivates corroboration tasks; its civic online reasoning curriculum includes corroboration lessons.
- **News Literacy Project** ([newslit.org](https://newslit.org)) — Checkology lessons require students to corroborate claims across independent outlets before judging them.
- A history class verifying a claim about a historical event must find three sources from different publishers (textbook, archive, news report), not three pages citing the same encyclopedia entry.
