De-emphasize Answers, Emphasize Reasoning¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (5 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy shifts classroom attention from what the answer is to why it works and how it was reached. Teachers present worked examples — including deliberately flawed ones — and ask learners to find and explain the error, or restructure participation routines so that learners must articulate their thinking process before, or instead of, stating a final answer. The answer becomes evidence in an argument about reasoning, not the endpoint of the activity.

Design Implications¶

Requiring learners to explain and justify their steps triggers self-explanation, which improves conceptual understanding beyond what answer-checking alone produces Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. Studying erroneous examples — worked solutions containing a plausible mistake — builds conceptual knowledge and error-detection skill better than studying only correct solutions Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M]. The strategy also reframes evaluation around process rather than outcome, which benefits novices who lack the knowledge to reason from outcome goals Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A classroom culture in which reasoning is publicly valued and errors are treated as informative, not shameful — this must be established before learners will expose partial thinking
  • Teachers skilled at facilitating discussion of strategies, including responding to wrong reasoning without simply supplying the answer
  • Tasks with reasoning worth examining: multi-step problems, contrasting solution methods, or cases where plausible errors occur
  • Time: reasoning-focused discussion is slower than answer-checking; fewer problems must be covered
  • Tasks with multiple defensible solution paths or interpretable errors, not single-step recall items
  • A classroom culture where errors are treated as information; teachers must respond to wrong answers by probing the reasoning rather than immediately correcting
  • Assessment and grading practices that reward explanation and justification, or the strategy will be undermined by what students perceive as counting
  • Teacher facility with facilitating discussion and with Coaching moves that press on thinking ("Why did you choose that operation?")

Constraints¶

  • Difficult to implement in answer-focused settings where high-stakes assessment rewards speed and correctness; learners may perceive reasoning talk as off-task [-M]
  • For learners with very low prior knowledge, open reasoning discussion can exceed working memory capacity and produce unproductive search; some structure (worked examples, Fading) is needed first [~M]
  • If facilitation is weak, discussion can drift to a single confident voice or degenerate into unfocused talk without consolidation of the target concept [-M]
  • Undermined when high-stakes assessments reward speed and correct answers only — students rationally optimize for what is graded [-M]
  • Time-intensive: examining several reasoning paths takes longer than confirming one answer, creating pressure to revert to answer-checking under pacing constraints [-M]
  • Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who may lack the schema needed to evaluate reasoning at all and benefit more from direct modeling first [~M]
  • Can confuse novices if multiple student strategies are compared without teacher synthesis; unstructured sharing of flawed reasoning can propagate misconceptions [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Error-finding tasks: present a worked solution with a planted error; learners locate, explain, and repair it
  • Answer-last routines: learners explain their approach before revealing the answer (Think-Aloud, Articulation)
  • Multiple methods: compare two or more valid solution paths and discuss why each works
  • Think-pair-share: pairs negotiate reasoning before whole-class discussion, lowering the risk of public error
  • Erroneous examples: present a fully worked but incorrect solution; students find and fix the error, then explain why it was wrong
  • Answer-delayed discussion: collect answers to a conceptual question, have students defend or revise them in Class Discussion before the teacher confirms
  • Reasoning-first norms: teacher opens with "Tell me the first thing you thought about," explicitly banning immediate answer-giving
  • Two approaches compared: display two student solutions side by side and ask which is more efficient or generalizable — a Comparing Cases move that supports abstraction Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and learners who struggle with mathematics or lack confidence in problem-solving; de-emphasizing the single correct answer reduces anxiety and legitimizes partial thinking [~M]
  • Learners with some prior knowledge, who benefit most from explaining and comparing methods Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]
  • Less effective for complete beginners without worked examples to anchor the discussion [~M]
  • Students who experience mathematics as answer-production and have developed unproductive beliefs about speed and correctness; the strategy broadens participation beyond fast answerers
  • Novices with minimal prior knowledge need worked modeling before error analysis becomes productive [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: why procedures work, not just how to execute them
  • Error detection and self-monitoring: recognizing when a solution path has gone wrong
  • Mathematical discourse and argumentation: justifying, critiquing, and comparing reasoning
  • Metacognitive monitoring: evaluating one's own solution path for validity
  • Mathematical argumentation and justification

Instructions¶

  1. Select a problem with rich reasoning and plausible errors; solve it (or present a prepared solution) containing a deliberate mistake
  2. Ask learners to work through the solution and identify where and why it fails, using Non-Examples to sharpen discrimination
  3. Have learners Articulation their own reasoning aloud or in writing before stating an answer; the teacher probes with "why?" and "how do you know?" rather than confirming correctness
  4. Facilitate Class Discussion comparing different solution strategies, treating each as a candidate argument to evaluate
  5. Consolidate: name the key idea the reasoning revealed, and follow with Practice that requires the same reasoning on new problems

Related Strategies¶

  • Productive Failure — lets learners generate and discuss flawed solutions before canonical instruction, using the same reasoning-first logic
  • Think-Pair-Share — a participation structure that makes reasoning discussion low-risk
  • Teach students to intentionally choose from alternative algebraic strategies when solving problems, after they have procedural fluency
  • Emphasizing reasoning pairs naturally with error-based learning: diagnosing incorrect worked examples is its most structured enactment
  • Works alongside formative assessment practices, since eliciting reasoning is formative evidence gathering
  • Question students about why a correct answer was chosen, rather than why an error was made
  • Error-injection exercises: diagnosing subtly wrong simulations as graded practice
  • Teacher follows learners' reasoning path and poses questions that alert learners to their geometric met-befores
  • Have learners develop their own problem-solving templates instead of replicating instructor templates

Related Elements¶

  • Articulation — the mechanism by which reasoning becomes visible and discussable
  • Think-Aloud — models expert reasoning so learners have a target for their own explanations
  • Non-Examples — erroneous solutions function as non-examples that sharpen conceptual boundaries
  • Class Discussion — the forum in which competing reasoning is compared and evaluated
  • Practice — consolidation; reasoning talk without subsequent application rarely transfers

Examples¶

  • Error-finding in algebra: present a worked equation-solving sequence with a sign error; learners locate the step, explain why it is wrong, and correct it — a form of erroneous-example learning shown to build conceptual knowledge Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge. [+M]
  • Number talks (K–6 mathematics): a short daily routine in which learners solve a computation mentally and the teacher records and compares multiple strategies, with the class discussing why each works; the answer is noted but never the focus
  • Kapur's Productive Failure: students attempt novel problems in groups, generating reasoning that is often wrong, before receiving canonical instruction — outperforming direct instruction followed by practice on conceptual and transfer measures [+S]
  • Illustrative Mathematics curriculum: tasks are structured around comparing student solution strategies and explaining reasoning, with answers treated as claims to justify
  • Erroneous-example routine: a teacher projects a solved equation with a sign error and asks, "Find the step where this went wrong, and convince your neighbor." Students must locate and justify the error before any correct solution appears.
  • "My favorite no": a widely used middle-school routine (popularized by Levi & Teaching Channel) in which the teacher collects anonymous wrong answers, displays one, and has the class identify what is right about the reasoning before fixing the error.
  • Cognitively Guided Instruction — teachers elicit and build instruction around students' own solution strategies rather than demonstrating the canonical algorithm first.
  • Case-Based Learning — in professional education, discussion of reasoning and diagnosis is the point; the "answer" is often deliberately withheld or contested.
