# Establish Ground Rules for Peer Critique

## Description
Establishing ground rules for peer critique means co-constructing or explicitly teaching shared norms that govern how learners give, receive, and act on feedback from peers before critique activities begin. Typical rules cover specificity ("critique the work, not the person"), balance ("name one strength and one improvement"), actionability ("offer a suggestion the author can act on"), and reception norms ("ask clarifying questions before defending choices"). The rules are usually posted, revisited, and sometimes paired with sentence stems or critique protocols.

## Design Implications

Peer feedback improves achievement most when it is structured and criterion-referenced rather than spontaneous and evaluative [Peer assessment with structured protocols improves learning outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2019.1669318) [+S]. Ground rules supply that structure: they shift feedback from vague praise or harsh judgment toward task- and process-level comments, which are the feedback levels most strongly associated with learning gains [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Norms also protect the psychological conditions — perceived safety and self-efficacy — that determine whether learners engage with critique at all [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared, visible set of 3–6 norms, ideally co-constructed with learners to build ownership
- A critique protocol or rubric that operationalizes the norms (e.g., "I like / I wonder / I suggest," gallery walk protocols, TAG feedback)
- Modeling of both effective and ineffective feedback before learners critique each other
- Time to rehearse the norms on low-stakes or anonymous work before formal critique

#### Constraints
- Without modeling, learners default to generic praise ("good job") or personal judgment, and ground rules alone do not fix this [-M]
- Overly rigid rules can suppress the honest, critical comments that make critique valuable; norms must explicitly license disagreement [~M]
- Power and status differences in groups can silence quieter learners even with norms in place; anonymous or written-first formats mitigate this [-M]
- Imposing rules without learner buy-in produces compliance, not culture; co-construction matters [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Co-constructed norms**: class generates rules from an analysis of sample feedback; strongest ownership
- **Protocol-embedded norms**: rules are baked into a structured protocol (e.g., Critical Friends' "warm" and "cool" feedback phases) so learners follow them by following the protocol
- **Sentence stems**: "I noticed…," "Have you considered…," "What would happen if…" scaffold norm-compliant language for younger or less experienced critiquers
- **Anonymous first pass**: written, anonymized critique lowers social risk before face-to-face rounds

### Target Learners
- Novices who have never given or received academic critique and lack scripts for doing so [+M]
- Adolescents and adults; younger learners need heavier scaffolding via stems and protocols
- Learners with fragile confidence, for whom unstructured peer judgment can be demotivating [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality improvement of artifacts: drafts, designs, writing, code, performances
- Feedback literacy: learning to interpret and act on evaluative information
- Collaborative and communication skills: giving specific, respectful, actionable input [Small-group learning improves achievement when individual accountability and structured interaction are in place.](../claims/small-group-learning-improves-stem-achievement.md) [+M]
- Self-regulated revision: using external feedback to plan improvements

### Instructions
1. Introduce the purpose of critique with a rationale and, where possible, an authentic audience ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).
2. Show examples of unhelpful vs. helpful feedback and have learners name the difference ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
3. Co-construct 3–6 norms and post them visibly; convert each norm into a concrete behavior or sentence stem ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. Model a full critique round on anonymous sample work, thinking aloud about how the norms guide each comment ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Run a low-stakes critique round using a protocol ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)); debrief which norms held and which need tightening ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
6. Revisit and revise the norms periodically as critique becomes routine.

## Examples
- **Ron Berger's Expeditionary Learning critique culture** — elementary students use "kind, specific, helpful" norms and multiple-draft critique; documented in *An Ethic of Excellence* (2003).
- **Critical Friends Groups (National School Reform Faculty)** — adult professional learning communities use timed protocols with warm/cool feedback phases that embed ground rules structurally ([https://www.nsrfharmony.org](https://www.nsrfharmony.org)).
- **High Tech High project critiques** — students run gallery-walk critique rounds with posted norms ("be kind, be specific, be helpful") before public exhibitions of project work.
