# Know Your Own Perspectives

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reflect deeply on your own attitudes and beliefs and how they may impact your opinions and actions. Understand how your views affect the way you interpret what students say and do. Cultural background influences how we interpret situations and communicate, as well as the choices we make. An important element of culturally responsive communication is understanding how your views affect the way you interpret what students say and do.

## Design Implications

This strategy treats the educator's self-knowledge as a prerequisite for equitable teaching: interpretation of student behavior is filtered through cultural assumptions, and unexamined assumptions produce differential expectations and responses. Structured reflection and perspective-taking exercises can shift teachers' attributions of student misbehavior toward more empathic interpretations [Brief intervention empathic discipline cuts suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S], and empathy-building activities improve intergroup attitudes more broadly [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. The mechanism is attributional: when educators reframe "he doesn't care" as "he may be responding to a context I don't see," their day-to-day interactions change.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Time for self-reflection, possibly guided reflection prompts or discussion questions
- Structured prompts that surface specific interpretations of real classroom incidents, not abstract attitude surveys
- A psychologically safe setting ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) norms or [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) relationships) so honesty does not carry professional risk
- Follow-through structures ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines) that revisit initial reflections over time

#### Constraints
- One-shot implicit-bias awareness training produces attitude changes that fade quickly and rarely alter behavior [Meta-analysis finds implicit bias change procedures yield small, unstable effects on behavior.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~M] — reflection must be sustained and tied to concrete practice
- Mandatory bias training can trigger defensiveness and reactance, especially when framed as accusation [-M]
- Introspection alone can *increase* confidence in one's own objectivity rather than reduce bias [-W]; reflection must be paired with external feedback or contrasting perspectives
- Requires honesty and willingness to confront personal biases; may be challenging for individuals resistant to self-reflection

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual reflective journaling vs. facilitated group inquiry ([Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md))
- Incident-based analysis (re-examining a specific misinterpretation) vs. general cultural self-inventory
- Embedded in teacher preparation coursework vs. ongoing professional learning communities

### Target Learners
- Educators and instructors across all levels (K-12, higher education, professional training)
- Teacher candidates in preparation programs, where reflective habits can be formed before entry into the classroom
- Mentors and coaches working across cultural difference with youth or adult learners

### Target Learning Goals
- Increase self-awareness, improve culturally responsive communication, mitigate implicit bias
- Attributional reframing: interpreting ambiguous student behavior through multiple cultural lenses
- Dispositional growth: openness, humility, and comfort with [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) as an instructional stance

### Instructions
1. Identify a recent incident where you interpreted a student's words or actions quickly and confidently; write down the interpretation and the evidence you actually had ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
2. Generate at least two alternative interpretations grounded in different cultural frames or contexts.
3. Compare interpretations with peers in structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), attending to where colleagues from different backgrounds read the same incident differently.
4. Identify one concrete communication or response pattern you will change, and set a follow-up [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) to review whether the change occurred.
5. Repeat the cycle with new incidents; treat the reflection as ongoing practice rather than a completed exercise.

## Examples
- **Teacher incident journals**: Candidates in culturally responsive teacher-preparation coursework (e.g., Gay's framework as implemented in urban teacher residencies) keep journals analyzing one misinterpretation per week, then discuss them in cohort seminars.
- **[Teaching Tolerance / Learning for Justice](https://www.learningforjustice.org)** professional development modules use identity-reflection windows and "critical self-reflection" prompts before introducing classroom practices.
- **Empathic discipline exercises**: Okonofua, Paunesku, and Walton's "wise feedback" teacher activity — reading research on adolescent psychology and writing about how it applies to one's own students — changed discipline behavior in real schools.
