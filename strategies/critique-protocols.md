# Critique Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q3` · 3 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
Critique protocols are structured routines — often with scripted sentence stems, timed rounds, and defined roles — that govern how learners examine one another's drafts or performances and offer feedback. Well-known formulations include Ron Berger's norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and the [Critical Friends](https://nsrfharmony.org/) protocol suite from the National School Reform Faculty. The protocol, not the teacher's discretion, carries the structure: it specifies what is looked at, in what order, and what kind of comments are permitted.

## Design Implications

Structured critique converts the diffuse social risk of "peer feedback" into a bounded, low-threat routine, which increases both participation and the specificity of comments [Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when structured with clear criteria.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]. Because learners must analyze someone else's work against criteria, the protocol doubles as an [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) learning activity: evaluating examples builds the evaluative judgment learners then apply to their own work. Critique also leverages [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) — good feedback requires stating a claim about the work and supporting it with evidence from the work itself [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A draft or incomplete work product — critique targets improvable work, not finished pieces
- Explicit criteria or a rubric so comments anchor to qualities of the work, not personal taste
- A protocol script: norms, sentence stems, time limits, and turn-taking structure
- Time built in for revision after critique; feedback without a revision cycle is inert [Feedback must be acted on to affect learning.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- A visible model of quality work (exemplar or [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)) so reviewers share a standard
- Clear criteria or a rubric that the critique references
- Work-in-progress rather than finished work, so feedback can still change the outcome
- A taught protocol with sentence stems and time limits, rehearsed before it carries stakes

#### Constraints
- Unstructured peer feedback ("I liked it") produces negligible learning gains; without criteria and stems, critique degrades into social pleasantries [Checklist-style evaluation without criteria is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Critique before learners have enough domain knowledge to judge quality yields confident but inaccurate feedback [~M] — early-stage novices need models of quality first
- Public critique can threaten belonging for anxious or minoritized learners unless norms are actively enforced and drafts are explicitly framed as unfinished
- If the same few students always present, the protocol becomes performance rather than feedback
- Low-trust classrooms suppress honest critique; learners hedge to protect relationships, and feedback quality drops [-M]
- Novice reviewers can transmit misconceptions; instructor moderation or spot-checking is needed in early iterations [-W]
- Critique of highly subjective or idiosyncratic work (personal narrative, artistic voice) can flatten expression if criteria over-specify style [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gallery critique**: work is posted; peers leave written comments on sticky notes (low social risk, asynchronous)
- **Author-driven rounds**: the presenter asks the group a specific question ("Does my evidence support my claim?"), focusing attention
- **Warm/cool feedback**: timed rounds of affirmations then suggestions, common in design studio practice
- **Teacher-modeled critique**: the instructor critiques a sample (or their own) work first, demonstrating the moves before students try ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) style)
- **Gallery critique**: whole-group examination of one work, modeled by the instructor (Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" routine)
- **Critical friends / tuning protocols**: small groups follow timed steps — presenter describes, group questions, group discusses while presenter listens, presenter responds
- **Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise–Question–Suggest**: lightweight stems for younger or newer critics
- **Studio walk / design crit**: rotating stations with written sticky-note feedback, common in art and design education
- **Asynchronous critique**: structured comment threads in platforms like Peergrade or Perusall, using the same stems

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have seen models of quality but not yet internalized criteria [~M]
- Learners in iterative production contexts: writing, design, code, studio arts, research
- Less suitable for absolute novices, who lack the evaluative schema to say anything useful; pair with [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or worked models first
- Learners old enough to articulate observations against criteria (upper elementary through adult) [Peer assessment works across grade levels but needs more scaffolding for younger students.](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]
- Learners producing iterative artifacts (writing, design, code, proofs) where revision is expected
- Reviewers benefit even when their own work is not critiqued — judging others' work builds evaluative judgment [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing what makes work good, not just producing work [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Revision as a habit: normalizing iteration and treating drafts as improvable
- Communication: articulating specific, evidence-based observations [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Quality criteria internalization: understanding what makes work good, not just producing work
- Revision and iteration: treating drafts as improvable [Authentic audiences and iterative critique improve the quality of student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]
- Evaluative judgment and self-assessment: the capacity to critique one's own work independently

### Instructions
1. Establish norms with students: kind, specific, helpful; critique the work, never the person.
2. Share exemplars and non-exemplars and critique one together as a whole group, modeling the language ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)).
3. Distribute drafts and the criteria or rubric; have authors pose a specific question about their work.
4. Run the protocol: timed observation, then structured feedback using sentence stems ("I noticed…", "Have you considered…").
5. Authors respond with clarifying questions only — no defending — then plan revisions.
6. Build in time to revise and, ideally, a second critique round to close the loop.

## Examples
**EL Education (formerly Expeditionary Learning)** — [EL Education's](https://eleducation.org) curriculum embeds Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" critique and multiple revision drafts as a standard literacy routine; students critique models before drafting.

**High Tech High (San Diego)** — project-based school where public critique of drafts is a routine before exhibition; protocols are taught explicitly in advisory.

**Design studio education (architecture, product design)** — the traditional "crit" is a critique protocol in institutionalized form; recent practice adds written and round-robin variants to reduce the well-documented harshness of open-floor critique.
- **Ron Berger's EL Education critique lessons** ([eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)) — the "Austin's Butterfly" routine: kindergarten students give kind, specific, helpful feedback across multiple drafts of a scientific drawing, demonstrating critique with young learners.
- **Harvard Project Zero "Tuning Protocol"** ([pz.harvard.edu](https://pz.harvard.edu/resources/tuning-protocol)) — a timed critical-friends structure for examining student or adult work in professional learning communities.
- **Design studio crits** in architecture and art education — the original model: public examination of work-in-progress against disciplinary standards, with the instructor moderating.
- **Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms** — asynchronous structured peer review with rubric-anchored comment prompts, used in MOOCs and university courses at scale.

## Description
Critique protocols are structured routines — often with prescribed sentence stems, role assignments, and turn-taking rules — through which learners examine one another's drafts or performances and offer feedback. The protocol constrains the interaction so that critique targets the work rather than the person, is specific enough to act on, and follows a predictable sequence (e.g., warm observations, then questions, then suggestions).

## Design Implications

Structured critique converts feedback from an evaluative judgment into an instructional event: learners analyze criteria, compare work against them, and articulate improvements, which deepens their own understanding of quality [Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, and what next?" drives achievement gains.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Protocols matter because unstructured peer feedback tends toward vague praise or personal comments; explicit structures and rubrics raise the specificity and usefulness of peer comments [Peer assessment quality improves when structured criteria or rubrics are provided.](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M].

### Instructions
1. **Establish criteria**: co-construct or present a rubric; anchor it with exemplars and [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) so reviewers share a standard.
2. **Model a critique**: the instructor critiques a sample work aloud using the protocol, making the moves visible ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. **Run the protocol**: pairs or small groups exchange drafts; each critique follows the fixed sequence — describe what you see, ask questions, offer specific suggestions — with sentence stems visible.
4. **Author response**: the author paraphrases the feedback and states one revision they will make, closing the loop ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. **Revise and re-critique**: authors revise; a second round shows whether feedback was actionable and builds the revision habit.
6. **Debrief the protocol itself**: briefly discuss which feedback was most useful and why, improving future critique quality.
