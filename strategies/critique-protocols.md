# Critique Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Critique protocols are structured routines — often with scripted sentence stems, timed rounds, and defined roles — that govern how learners examine one another's drafts or performances and offer feedback. Well-known formulations include Ron Berger's norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and the [Critical Friends](https://nsrfharmony.org/) protocol suite from the National School Reform Faculty. The protocol, not the teacher's discretion, carries the structure: it specifies what is looked at, in what order, and what kind of comments are permitted.

## Design Implications

Structured critique converts the diffuse social risk of "peer feedback" into a bounded, low-threat routine, which increases both participation and the specificity of comments [Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when structured with clear criteria.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]. Because learners must analyze someone else's work against criteria, the protocol doubles as an [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) learning activity: evaluating examples builds the evaluative judgment learners then apply to their own work. Critique also leverages [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) — good feedback requires stating a claim about the work and supporting it with evidence from the work itself [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A draft or incomplete work product — critique targets improvable work, not finished pieces
- Explicit criteria or a rubric so comments anchor to qualities of the work, not personal taste
- A protocol script: norms, sentence stems, time limits, and turn-taking structure
- Time built in for revision after critique; feedback without a revision cycle is inert [Feedback must be acted on to affect learning.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Unstructured peer feedback ("I liked it") produces negligible learning gains; without criteria and stems, critique degrades into social pleasantries [Checklist-style evaluation without criteria is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Critique before learners have enough domain knowledge to judge quality yields confident but inaccurate feedback [~M] — early-stage novices need models of quality first
- Public critique can threaten belonging for anxious or minoritized learners unless norms are actively enforced and drafts are explicitly framed as unfinished
- If the same few students always present, the protocol becomes performance rather than feedback

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gallery critique**: work is posted; peers leave written comments on sticky notes (low social risk, asynchronous)
- **Author-driven rounds**: the presenter asks the group a specific question ("Does my evidence support my claim?"), focusing attention
- **Warm/cool feedback**: timed rounds of affirmations then suggestions, common in design studio practice
- **Teacher-modeled critique**: the instructor critiques a sample (or their own) work first, demonstrating the moves before students try ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) style)

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have seen models of quality but not yet internalized criteria [~M]
- Learners in iterative production contexts: writing, design, code, studio arts, research
- Less suitable for absolute novices, who lack the evaluative schema to say anything useful; pair with [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or worked models first

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing what makes work good, not just producing work [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Revision as a habit: normalizing iteration and treating drafts as improvable
- Communication: articulating specific, evidence-based observations [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish norms with students: kind, specific, helpful; critique the work, never the person.
2. Share exemplars and non-exemplars and critique one together as a whole group, modeling the language ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)).
3. Distribute drafts and the criteria or rubric; have authors pose a specific question about their work.
4. Run the protocol: timed observation, then structured feedback using sentence stems ("I noticed…", "Have you considered…").
5. Authors respond with clarifying questions only — no defending — then plan revisions.
6. Build in time to revise and, ideally, a second critique round to close the loop.

## Examples
**EL Education (formerly Expeditionary Learning)** — [EL Education's](https://eleducation.org) curriculum embeds Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" critique and multiple revision drafts as a standard literacy routine; students critique models before drafting.

**High Tech High (San Diego)** — project-based school where public critique of drafts is a routine before exhibition; protocols are taught explicitly in advisory.

**Design studio education (architecture, product design)** — the traditional "crit" is a critique protocol in institutionalized form; recent practice adds written and round-robin variants to reduce the well-documented harshness of open-floor critique.
