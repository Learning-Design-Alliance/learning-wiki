# Thinking About Emotions in Advance

## Description
This strategy opens a lesson or school day with a brief, structured prompt asking students to identify how they are feeling or what might be bothering them — through a check-in question, mood scale, emotion vocabulary card sort, or quick written reflection. The goal is to surface emotional states *before* instruction begins, so that affect does not operate as unexamined background noise during learning. Naming emotions converts diffuse arousal into identifiable, manageable information, and gives the teacher immediate diagnostic data about the class's readiness to learn.

## Design Implications

Emotions direct attention and consume working-memory resources; unacknowledged anxiety or frustration competes with instructional content for processing capacity [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). Labeling affect ("affect labeling") reduces amygdala reactivity and improves self-regulation, so a brief naming routine functions as a low-cost emotional regulation intervention [+M]. Check-ins also strengthen teacher–student relationships, which are themselves reliably associated with improved engagement and achievement [Learner-centered teacher-student relationships are effective.](../claims/learner-centered-teacher-student-relationships-are-effective.md) [+S]. The strategy works best when the teacher visibly responds to what surfaces — a check-in that is collected but never acknowledged teaches students that the routine is performative [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, low-stakes routine (same time, same format) so participation becomes automatic and does not itself add load
- An emotion vocabulary students can actually use — younger learners or second-language learners may need word banks, faces, or color scales ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- A teacher response loop: acknowledgment, brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) follow-up with individual students, or adjustment of the day's plan
- Psychological safety — students must trust that disclosure will not be punished or ridiculed

#### Constraints
- Forced or public emotional disclosure can increase anxiety and harm students processing trauma; participation should be invitable, not compulsory [-M]
- If the routine consumes substantial time or becomes ritualistic without follow-through, it erodes instructional time and credibility [-W]
- Poorly facilitated sharing can escalate rumination: dwelling on negative affect without a regulation or reframing step can worsen mood rather than clear it [~M]
- Students may learn to give socially desirable answers ("fine") once the routine loses novelty, yielding no diagnostic value [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Nonverbal scales** (color zones, 1–5 finger ratings, mood meters like the RULER approach) — fast, low-exposure, suitable for daily use
- **Written private check-ins** (index cards, journals, digital forms) — allow candor without public exposure
- **Circle dialogue** — verbal sharing in a structured format, higher relational payoff but higher time cost and exposure risk
- **Teacher-modeled check-in** — the adult names their own state first, normalizing the practice and modeling emotion vocabulary

### Target Learners
- Students experiencing transitions (new school, post-break, adolescence) where emotional load is elevated [~M]
- Learners with weak self-regulation or limited emotion vocabulary, for whom naming is the skill being built [+W]
- Students from backgrounds where a warm, predictable teacher relationship is a key engagement lever [Learner-centered teacher-student relationships are effective.](../claims/learner-centered-teacher-student-relationships-are-effective.md) [+S]
- Less suitable as a whole-class requirement for students with trauma histories who need control over disclosure; offer private or opt-out channels [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and labeling one's own emotional states as a precondition for managing them
- Social-emotional learning: building shared emotion vocabulary and empathy for peers ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Readiness for academic content: clearing affective interference so [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of prior knowledge can proceed on a stable footing

### Instructions
1. **Choose a lightweight format** — a mood scale, one-word check-out, or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) question posted as students arrive; keep it under five minutes.
2. **Model first** — the teacher names their own state honestly ("I'm a bit scattered this morning"), demonstrating that the routine is genuine.
3. **Collect responses** — via private written form, quick nonverbal signal, or brief [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) for volunteers only.
4. **Acknowledge and adjust** — name what the class is carrying ("I see several of us are tired after the game"), and adapt pacing, grouping, or task difficulty accordingly; where individual disclosures warrant it, arrange a private follow-up conversation.
5. **Bridge to regulation, not just identification** — pair naming with a brief reset (breathing, goal-setting, choice of entry task) so surfacing emotion leads somewhere ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) can give students autonomy over their re-entry into work, supporting the need for control [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]).

## Examples
- **RULER / Mood Meter (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — students plot their state on a two-axis energy/pleasantness grid at the start of class; the shared vocabulary enables later self-regulation conversations. https://ei.yale.edu
- **Responsive Classroom Morning Meeting** — a structured daily opening with greeting and sharing that surfaces student states before academic work. https://www.responsiveclassroom.org
- **Advisory check-in forms** — many secondary schools use a two-question digital form (How are you arriving today? Anything the class should know?) that gives the teacher a private dashboard before first period.
