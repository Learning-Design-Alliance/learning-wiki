# Mindfulness Practice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 unmarked) · 5 studies (3 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
Mindfulness practice involves short, structured exercises — focused breathing, body scans, mindful observation of thoughts — in which learners deliberately attend to present-moment experience and return attention when it wanders. In instructional settings it is typically delivered as brief (3–10 minute) practices at the start of class or before demanding tasks, rather than as a standalone curriculum.

## Design Implications

Mindfulness training targets the attentional control system: regular practice strengthens the ability to notice mind-wandering and re-orient, which supports working-memory-dependent learning [Mindfulness training improves working memory capacity and reading comprehension through reduced mind-wandering.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612459659) [+M]. Effects on attention and executive function are most reliable when practice is repeated over multiple sessions rather than delivered once [Mindfulness meditation improves executive attention and self-regulation.](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3916) [+M]. It functions as a *readiness* strategy — preparing learners to engage — rather than directly teaching content.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, predictable routine (same time, same brief format) so practice becomes automatic
- A facilitator with some personal practice experience; scripted delivery without familiarity reduces fidelity
- A quiet-enough environment and a norm that stillness is expected, not optional or performative
- Voluntary framing for older learners; coerced practice undermines the self-regulation it aims to build
- Brief, predictable routines (2–10 minutes) integrated at consistent points in the schedule, not one-off events
- Facilitators with their own practice or training; poorly prepared delivery undermines credibility and uptake
- Voluntary, low-pressure participation norms; framing as attention training rather than discipline management

#### Constraints
- Single-session or very brief interventions show weak or null effects on academic outcomes [School-based mindfulness programs show small, inconsistent effects on academic achievement.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-014-0265-1) [~M]
- Learners experiencing acute trauma or anxiety can be *harmed* by interoceptive attention (body scans can trigger distress); trauma-sensitive adaptations (eyes open, external anchors) are required [-M]
- Effects fade without continued practice; benefits decay within weeks of stopping [Mindfulness meditation improves executive attention and self-regulation.](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3916) [~M]
- Poorly implemented programs (untrained teachers, no dosage) can produce null or slightly negative effects, as in the large MYRIAD school trial [-S]
- Mandated or punitive use (e.g., as a consequence for misbehavior) reduces buy-in and can be counterproductive [-W]
- Learners with trauma histories may find closed-eye or body-focused exercises distressing; trauma-sensitive adaptations (eyes open, choice of posture, opt-out) are necessary [-M]
- Effects fade without sustained practice; meta-analytic evidence shows weaker and more variable outcomes for one-off or very short programs [~M]
- Time spent on mindfulness competes with instructional time; the tradeoff is only justified when attention and regulation problems are actually limiting learning [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Opening routine:** 2–3 minute breath focus before a demanding task ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) variant)
- **Embedded practice:** mindful pauses before assessments or after transitions
- **Full curriculum:** multi-week programs such as MBSR (adults) or .b / MindUP (schools)
- **Trauma-sensitive variants:** external-anchor focus (sounds, objects) instead of body awareness
- Focused-attention breathing at lesson openings; transition resets between activities; body scans or mindful movement (yoga, stretching) for younger learners
- Digital supports such as [Headspace](https://www.headspace.com) or [Calm](https://www.calm.com) for independent practice
- Educator-facing mindfulness training (e.g., [CARE for Teachers](https://www.care4teachers.com)) targeting teacher stress, which indirectly improves classroom climate

### Target Learners
- Learners with high mind-wandering or attentional difficulties benefit most from attention-training effects [+M]
- Adolescents and adults can self-report and self-direct practice; young children need concrete anchors (breathing buddy, chime) and shorter durations
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for learners with trauma histories or acute anxiety [~M]
- Learners with high stress, anxiety, or attentional difficulties, who show the largest gains in regulation and working memory [Mrazek et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535801) [+M]
- Adolescents facing high-stakes assessment periods, where stress management protects performance [~M]
- Less effective as a substitute for structured behavioral support for learners with severe self-regulation challenges [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention regulation and on-task behavior (proximal goal)
- Self-regulation and emotion management under evaluative stress
- Not a substitute for content instruction — no reliable direct gains in domain knowledge [~M]
- Self-regulation: noticing and redirecting attention and emotion ([Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md)) [+M]
- Attention management: building the executive control that underlies sustained [Practice](../elements/practice.md)
- Stress reduction: lowering arousal that would otherwise consume working memory resources ([Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)) [~W]

### Instructions
1. **Establish the routine.** Fix a consistent trigger (start of class, before tests). Predictability lowers the working-memory cost of the practice itself.
2. **Deliver a brief practice (2–5 minutes).** Guide attention to a single anchor (breath, sound); instruct learners to notice wandering and return — the noticing-and-returning *is* the training repetition.
3. **Debrief briefly.** One-line reflection ("What did you notice?") builds metacognitive awareness without turning practice into discussion.
4. **Connect to the task.** Transition immediately into demanding work ([Attention](../elements/attention.md)) while attentional state is regulated.
5. **Repeat across weeks.** Effects on attention and stress accumulate with dosage; single sessions do not produce durable change [+M].

## Examples
- **MindUP** (https://mindup.org) — evidence-informed school curriculum with daily 3-minute "brain breaks" embedded in the school day.
- **.b** (https://mindfulnessinschools.org) — 10-lesson secondary-school mindfulness curriculum from the Mindfulness in Schools Project.
- **MBSR** (https://www.umassmemorialhealthcare.org/umass-center-mindfulness) — Kabat-Zinn's 8-week protocol; the adult model from which most classroom adaptations derive.
- **Mrazek et al. (2013)** — university students completed 2 weeks of mindfulness training and showed improved GRE reading comprehension scores and reduced mind-wandering.
- **[MindUP](https://mindup.org)** — published PreK–8 curriculum pairing daily "brain breaks" (breathing-focused core practice) with social-emotional lessons.
- **[Learning to BREATHE](https://learning2breathe.org)** — mindfulness curriculum designed for adolescents, typically delivered in 6–18 sessions.
- **Mrazek et al. (2014)** — two weeks of daily mindfulness training improved GRE reading comprehension scores and working memory in college students, demonstrating academic transfer.

1. Establish a consistent cue and duration (e.g., three minutes after transitions), framed as attention training, not discipline.
2. Model the practice yourself and guide it actively — brief facilitator [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) in the technique precedes independent use.
3. Start with short, structured exercises (breath counting, sound focus); keep eyes-open options available for trauma-sensitive participation.
4. Connect the practice to academic work: after the exercise, name what attention feels like and set a focus goal for the task ahead.
5. Use brief [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to let learners report focus and arousal levels, building metacognitive awareness of their own states.
6. Fade facilitator guidance over weeks so learners can initiate practice independently, consistent with [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).
