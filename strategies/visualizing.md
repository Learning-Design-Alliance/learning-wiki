# Visualizing

## Description
Visualizing asks learners to construct a mental image or external representation of a problem, text, or system — a diagram, sketch, table, concept map, or mental picture — and to use that representation as a working tool for reasoning. It converts verbal or symbolic material into spatial-visual form, engaging [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md): information encoded both verbally and visually is better remembered and better understood than information encoded in a single channel.

## Design Implications

Visualizing improves comprehension and problem solving because external representations offload working memory and make relational structure visible [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. The *type* of representation matters: schematic diagrams that encode the problem's relational structure support solution far better than decorative or literal pictures of surface features [~S]. Prompting learners to explain their own representations deepens the effect [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tools for creating representations: paper and pencil, whiteboards, or software (diagramming tools, graphing calculators, concept-mapping apps)
- Tasks whose structure benefits from spatial or relational depiction (systems, processes, quantitative relationships, narratives)
- Instruction in *what* to represent — learners need to know which features of a problem the diagram must capture, not merely be told to "draw it"

#### Constraints
- Learners who draw literal, pictorial images of surface details rather than schematic representations of relations show no benefit and sometimes perform worse [~S] — young children and low-prior-knowledge learners are especially prone to this
- Visualizing abstract concepts with no natural spatial structure can force misleading metaphors and impair reasoning
- Producing a representation adds production load; for novices, poorly scaffolded drawing tasks can consume capacity needed for the underlying reasoning [-M]
- Benefits diminish for learners with strong prior knowledge who already hold efficient internal representations [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Learner-generated (drawing, mapping) versus instructor-provided (worked diagrams, animations) representations; learner-generated generally produces deeper processing but requires more scaffolding
- Mental visualization (imagery prompts) versus external drawing; external products are easier to assess and revise
- Domain-specific formats: force diagrams in physics, tape diagrams in mathematics, story maps in reading, concept maps in biology

### Target Learners
- Novices and low-prior-knowledge learners, for whom an external representation substitutes for a still-unformed internal model [+M]
- Learners solving multi-step quantitative problems, where diagrams reduce transcription and search errors
- Less beneficial for experts, who may find mandated drawing redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of texts, systems, and processes with relational structure
- Problem solving: representing a problem correctly is the strongest predictor of solving it correctly
- Retention, via dual verbal–visual encoding

### Instructions
1. Model the construction of a representation for a worked problem, narrating which features you encode and why — [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)
2. Present a similar problem and prompt learners to build their own representation before computing or writing an answer — [Practice](../elements/practice.md)
3. Have learners compare representations against a target schematic and explain differences — [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) combined with self-explanation
4. Fade the prompting as learners adopt visualization spontaneously

## Examples
- **Singapore Mathematics** (https://www.moe.gov.sg) — the bar-model method has students draw rectangular bars representing quantities and relationships before solving word problems; the drawing is the solution strategy, not decoration.
- **Physics education "sketch the situation" prompts** — standard practice in curricula such as *Tutorials in Introductory Physics* (McDermott & Shaffer, University of Washington), where force and motion diagrams precede equations.
- **Reading comprehension story maps** — learners diagram setting, characters, and causal chains while reading narrative text.
