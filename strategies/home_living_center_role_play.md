# Home Living Center Role Play

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners visit a home living center — a play area provisioned with domestic props (kitchen set, dolls, furniture, dress-up clothing) — and take on family or household characters, explicitly assigning emotions to those characters and acting them out. The facilitator frames scenarios, prompts emotional vocabulary ("How does the baby feel when…?"), and debriefs afterward so that enacted feelings become named, discussable constructs.

## Design Implications

Role play in a familiar, realistic setting lets learners externalize and rehearse emotional understanding at a safe psychological distance — the *character* feels the emotion, not the learner [~M]. Because the scenario is enacted rather than described, learners must coordinate language, gesture, and social sequencing, which supports both emotional vocabulary and perspective-taking [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. The home context matters: domestic scripts are among the earliest and most universally shared schemas young learners possess, minimizing the background knowledge needed to enter the play.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A physically provisioned center with props that suggest domestic scripts (kitchen, dolls, phone, dress-up items)
- A facilitator who sets up scenarios, models emotional vocabulary, and coaches during play ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- Explicit emotional vocabulary introduced before or during play, so learners have words to assign to characters
- A debrief or reflection in which learners name the emotions they enacted and connect them to real experiences ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

#### Constraints
- Requires learners to be willing to perform in front of others; naturally introverted learners may disengage or comply minimally [~W] — offering observer, narrator, or puppeteer roles mitigates this
- Without facilitator prompting, play can remain at the level of script rehearsal (stirring, feeding) with no emotional content at all [-W]
- Overly prescriptive facilitation ("Now act sad") can collapse the play into compliance and reduce the spontaneous engagement that makes role play effective [~M]
- The center must be refreshed with new props and scenarios; a static center loses its draw and its scenario variety within weeks [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Learners choose their own characters and emotions ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) or draw scenario/emotion cards for a structured variant
- Puppets or stuffed animals can stand in for self-conscious learners, providing distance while preserving enactment
- Older learners and adults can use the same center structure for second-language practice, caregiving training, or counseling role play with more complex scenarios
- A "hot-seating" variant pauses the play and asks the group to question a character about their feelings

### Target Learners
- Preschool and early elementary learners, for whom pretend play is a primary context for developing emotion understanding and social competence [~M]
- Second-language learners, who rehearse high-frequency domestic vocabulary in a meaningful context
- Learners building empathy and perspective-taking [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Adult learners in caregiving, counseling, or hospitality training, where domestic scenarios map directly onto professional situations

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: identifying, naming, and expressing emotions
- Perspective-taking and empathy: inhabiting a character whose feelings differ from one's own
- Social skill rehearsal: negotiation, turn-taking, and cooperative scripting with peers
- Oral language development: narrative sequencing and vocabulary use in context

### Instructions
1. Set up the home living center with props suggesting a scenario (new baby, lost pet, family dinner) and briefly introduce or review target emotion words.
2. Assign or let learners choose characters and emotions; use [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) when a scenario needs specific roles filled.
3. Have learners [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) — enact the domestic scene while visibly expressing their character's emotion.
4. Coach in the moment: pause to ask "What is your character feeling right now? How can you show it?" ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
5. Close with a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) and individual reflection: learners name the emotions they portrayed, what caused them, and what they noticed about others' characters ([Application](../elements/application.md), [Practice](../elements/practice.md)).

## Examples
- A preschool classroom introduces a "new baby" scenario; children take turns as the older sibling, the tired parent, and the crying baby, then debrief on how each character felt and why.
- A kindergarten teacher adds emotion cards (happy, frustrated, worried) to the center; children draw a card and must show, not tell, the emotion through their character's actions.
- An adult ESL class uses a home living center to rehearse family-visit dialogues, with assigned emotional states (nervous, delighted, annoyed) to practice tone and register.
