# Exit Ticket

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An exit ticket is a brief assessment — typically one to three questions or a short prompt — completed by learners in the final minutes of a lesson and submitted before they leave. Responses are reviewed by the instructor, who uses them to gauge understanding, surface misconceptions, and adjust the next lesson. Because it doubles as retrieval practice and as formative assessment data, it serves both learner and instructor simultaneously.

## Design Implications

Exit tickets operationalize [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): they generate fine-grained evidence of learning at the point of instruction, when it can still change teaching [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The retrieval demand also strengthens memory — a low-stakes recall prompt at the end of a lesson acts as a spaced, effortful retrieval event rather than a passive wrap-up. The value depends critically on the instructor's follow-through: tickets that are collected but never read or acted upon lose their formative function and become mere compliance rituals.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prompt tightly aligned to the lesson's core objective, not general "how did it go" feedback
- Low enough stakes that responses reflect genuine understanding, not performance anxiety
- A routine for reviewing responses before the next session and a plan for acting on them (reteach, regroup, or advance)
- Minimal logistical friction — paper slips, a digital form, or an LMS quiz — so the ticket takes under five minutes

#### Constraints
- A single snapshot at one time point is noisy; a struggling response may reflect fatigue or time pressure rather than a learning gap [~M]
- If prompts target surface recall only, the ticket reinforces shallow processing and misses conceptual understanding
- Tickets collected but not acted on provide no formative benefit and consume instructional time [-M]
- End-of-class timing means learners who need help most may rush or disengage; a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) mid-lesson catches problems earlier

#### Implementation Variability
- **3–2–1 format**: three things learned, two questions, one muddiest point ([3–2–1 Reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md))
- **Predict-and-verify**: learners predict the next lesson's application of today's concept, priming [Activation](../principles/activation.md)
- **Digital instant-polling**: tools like Google Forms, Socrative, or Poll Everywhere aggregate responses in real time, letting the instructor reteach immediately
- **Entry-ticket inversion**: the same artifact used at the start of the *next* class as a spaced-retrieval warm-up

### Target Learners
- All ages; the format scales from elementary classrooms to university lectures by adjusting prompt complexity
- Instructors benefit most when class sizes make individual diagnosis impractical — the ticket is a scalable diagnostic
- Anxious or low-confidence learners benefit from the private, written format, which surfaces confusion they would not voice publicly

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation of the day's core concept through retrieval
- Metacognitive awareness: "muddiest point" prompts require learners to locate their own gaps
- Instructional diagnosis: identifying which sub-skills need reteaching before the gap compounds

### Instructions
1. Identify the single most important idea of the lesson and write a prompt that cannot be answered without it — a worked problem, an application to a new case, or a "explain why" question
2. Reserve the final 3–5 minutes; state that the ticket is ungraded and diagnostic
3. Collect responses (paper, form, or LMS) and sort them quickly into "got it / partial / missing" piles
4. Open the next lesson by addressing the most common misconception, citing (anonymously) real ticket responses
5. Vary the format periodically — [3–2–1 Reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md), muddiest point, or one problem — to prevent the routine from becoming mechanical

## Examples
- **Elementary math**: students solve one word problem using the day's strategy on a sticky note posted to the door; the teacher sorts notes into mastery groups for tomorrow's centers
- **University lecture (200+ students)**: a two-question Socrative quiz in the last five minutes; the histogram of responses determines whether the next lecture opens with reteaching or new material
- **Professional development**: participants write one commitment and one lingering question on a card; facilitators use the questions to open day two
