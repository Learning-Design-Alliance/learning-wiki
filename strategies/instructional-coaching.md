# Instructional Coaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Instructional coaching is a form of job-embedded professional development in which a coach partners with a teacher over an extended period (typically a semester or more) to set an instructional goal, observe practice, provide non-evaluative feedback, and support repeated cycles of refinement. Unlike one-shot workshops, coaching embeds learning in the teacher's actual classroom, combining modeling, co-planning, observation, and structured debriefs. Prominent models include Jim Knight's [Impact Cycle](https://www.instructionalcoaching.com) and Cognitive Coaching.

## Design Implications

Coaching applies the same learning science that governs novice skill acquisition to adult learners: teachers, like students, benefit from observation of expert models, deliberate practice with feedback, and gradual transfer of ownership. Meta-analytic evidence shows that professional development incorporating coaching produces substantially larger improvements in both instruction and student outcomes than workshop-only formats [~S]. The mechanism is deliberate practice: a specific goal, repeated enactment, immediate feedback, and revision — conditions rarely available in traditional PD.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained coach with both pedagogical expertise and coaching-specific skills (questioning, listening, non-evaluative stance)
- Sustained time allocation — effective implementations typically involve 10+ contact hours per teacher, often weekly cycles
- A clear instructional target agreed with the teacher, not imposed by the coach
- Trust and confidentiality; coaching must be separated from formal evaluation to elicit honest practice
- Video or live observation as a shared evidence base for debrief conversations

#### Constraints
- Effects shrink or reverse when coaching is coupled to evaluation or high-stakes accountability — teachers perform for the coach rather than disclose problems [-M]
- Large-scale implementations dilute intensity; coaches with caseloads above ~20–25 teachers per cycle deliver less individualized support and weaker effects [~M]
- Coaching a specific practice does not automatically generalize to other practices; transfer requires explicit bridging conversations [~M]
- Poorly specified goals ("be more engaging") produce unfocused cycles with minimal change; goals must be observable and student-centered

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructional coaching** (Knight): goal → learn a research-based strategy → model → observe → refine
- **Cognitive Coaching** (Costa & Garmston): focuses on the teacher's thinking and self-directedness rather than prescribing strategies
- **Video-based coaching** (e.g., [MyTeachingPartner](https://curry.virginia.edu/myteachingpartner), Edthena): asynchronous or synchronous video analysis reduces scheduling burden and increases teacher self-observation
- **Peer coaching**: teachers coach each other; lower cost but requires structures to sustain reciprocity
- **Mathematical Quality of Instruction / content-focused coaching**: coaching embedded in subject-specific routines (e.g., lesson study cycles)

### Target Learners
- Early-career teachers, who show the largest gains from coaching-intensive PD [~S]
- Teachers adopting a specific new practice (a curriculum, a discourse routine, formative assessment) where enactment skill, not knowledge, is the bottleneck
- Experienced teachers benefit less from generic coaching and more from content-focused or peer formats [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Enactment fidelity: translating declarative knowledge of a strategy into fluent classroom performance
- Analytic skill: learning to notice and interpret classroom events (video review builds this)
- Self-regulated professional growth: internalizing the goal–act–reflect cycle so improvement continues after coaching ends

### Instructions
1. **Establish partnership** — agree on a non-evaluative, teacher-driven relationship and confidentiality norms.
2. **Set a measurable goal** — identify one observable instructional target, ideally framed by its effect on students.
3. **Learn the strategy** — the coach introduces the target practice with explanation and [demonstration](../elements/practice.md), often via modeling in the teacher's own classroom or video exemplars.
4. **Model and co-teach** — the coach demonstrates the strategy; the teacher observes or co-enacts before leading.
5. **Observe and collect evidence** — the coach observes enactment, using video or a structured observation tool to capture objective data.
6. **Debrief and refine** — a structured conversation reviews evidence against the goal; the teacher, not the coach, names adjustments.
7. **Repeat the cycle** — iterate until the practice is fluent, then fade support, mirroring [fading](../elements/fading.md) in scaffolded instruction.

## Related Strategies
- Lesson Study — a group-based variant of the same observe–refine cycle, trading individualization for collective analysis
- Video-Based Reflection — the evidence-gathering component of coaching, usable standalone
- Professional Learning Communities — sustained collaborative PD, but peer-run rather than expert-guided

## Examples
- **[MyTeachingPartner](https://curry.virginia.edu/myteachingpartner)** (University of Virginia) — web-mediated coaching in which teachers submit classroom video and receive consultant feedback on teacher–student interactions; replicated RCTs show gains in interaction quality and student achievement.
- **[Instructional Coaching Group](https://www.instructionalcoaching.com)** — Jim Knight's Impact Cycle, widely adopted in U.S. districts; the coach–teacher partnership agreement and "identify–learn–improve" cycle are the operational core.
- **Chicago's Teacher Instructional Coaching Program** — district-scale coaching studied by Kraft, Blazar, and colleagues, illustrating both the promise and the scale-related dilution of effects.
