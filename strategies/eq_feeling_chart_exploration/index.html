EQ Feeling Chart Exploration¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 theoretical), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners use the EQ Feeling Chart — a visual display of emotion vocabulary organized by intensity and valence — to identify and name their current feelings. In pairs, they discuss what they feel and where the feeling comes from, then share observations with the whole class. The strategy converts diffuse internal states into precise, communicable language, building emotional literacy through structured Peer Discussion and Whole-Class Sharing.

Design Implications¶

Naming an emotional state (affect labeling) measurably reduces its intensity and improves regulation, making the act of precise identification itself a self-regulation intervention [~M]. The chart functions as an advance organizer for emotional vocabulary: learners who would otherwise say "bad" or "fine" are prompted toward finer-grained distinctions, and richer emotion differentiation is associated with better emotion regulation and fewer maladaptive coping behaviors [+W]. Partner discussion adds a social-comparison layer — hearing how a peer labels the same situation broadens learners' own emotional vocabulary and builds Building Empathy.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An EQ Feeling Chart (or equivalent emotion-vocabulary display) visible to all learners
  • Discussion prompts that push beyond identification to origins ("What happened that made you feel this way?")
  • Protected time for both partner exchange and whole-class synthesis
  • Norms of psychological safety; sharing must be voluntary, never coerced

Constraints¶

  • Learners with limited emotion vocabulary or alexithymia may find the chart overwhelming rather than supportive; a smaller starter set of feelings reduces load [~M]
  • Compulsory public sharing of feelings can produce performative or defensive responses and damage trust [-M] — keep whole-class sharing at the level of observations, not disclosures
  • Cultural display rules vary: in some contexts naming negative emotions publicly is discouraged; the chart must be adapted rather than assumed universal [~W]
  • Without follow-up, the activity remains a one-off vocabulary exercise; benefits depend on repeated use embedded in routines such as Check-Ins [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Silent entry point: learners point to or mark the chart privately before any discussion, lowering the barrier for reluctant sharers
  • Fictional framing: learners identify a character's feelings from a story or case before their own, using Case Studies as a safe proxy
  • Intensity scaling: learners rate the selected feeling on a 1–5 scale, connecting the chart to self-monitoring of escalation
  • Age adaptation: younger learners use faces and colors; older learners use nuanced vocabulary (e.g., distinguishing frustration from disappointment from resentment)

Target Learners¶

  • All ages, but especially children and adolescents still building emotion vocabulary
  • Learners with weak interoceptive awareness or those who default to undifferentiated labels ("fine," "bad") [+W]
  • Groups with recurring interpersonal friction, where shared vocabulary enables de-escalation
  • Less immediately useful for learners already fluent in emotion language, for whom the chart may feel redundant [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: identifying and precisely naming internal states
  • Self-awareness and Self-Regulated Learning: recognizing feelings as data that influence attention and behavior Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: understanding that others experience and label feelings differently
  • Communication: articulating causes and sources of emotional states to a listener

Instructions¶

  1. Display the EQ Feeling Chart and briefly model its use, naming a feeling of your own and its origin (a brief Demonstration of the target behavior).
  2. Learners privately identify their feeling on the chart, optionally noting intensity.
  3. In pairs, learners share their feeling and discuss its origin using structured prompts; partners practice Active Listening — reflecting back what they heard rather than advising.
  4. Pairs share observations (not disclosures) with the whole class in a facilitated Class Discussion: "What feelings showed up in our room today? What patterns did you notice?"
  5. Close by connecting feelings to learning: "How might this feeling affect your work today?" — linking the activity to ongoing Check-Ins.

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a gamified variant of the same emotion-identification goal
  • Active Listening — the partner skill that makes the discussion phase productive
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — an individualized follow-up channel for learners reluctant to share publicly

Related Elements¶

  • Peer Discussion — the core interaction structure of the partner phase
  • Whole-Class Sharing — the synthesis phase that surfaces group patterns
  • Coaching — teachers can coach precise labeling during circulation
  • Collaboration — shared emotional vocabulary is a precondition for productive group work

Tools¶

  • EQ Feeling Chart (Six Seconds) — the original research-informed feeling chart used in EQ programs: https://www.6seconds.org
  • Mood Meter (Yale RULER) — a color/pleasantness-energy grid for locating feelings, from the RULER approach: https://moodmeterapp.com
  • Zones of Regulation — a four-zone framework widely used in K–12 settings: https://zonesofregulation.com

Examples¶

  • A fifth-grade teacher opens each morning with a two-minute chart check-in; over a term, students' written reflections shift from "I'm mad" to differentiated labels, and conflicts in group work increasingly reference feelings by name.
  • A secondary advisory program uses the Mood Meter before exams; students plot their state, discuss with a partner how that state affects study strategy, and set one regulation move for the session.
  • Six Seconds' EQ curricula use the Feeling Chart as the entry activity in units on self-awareness, followed by role-play and empathy-building exercises.
