# Reading Online Strategy — Evaluating Online Information

## Description
This strategy teaches learners to treat online texts as claims requiring verification rather than as information to absorb. It includes checking who is behind a source, reading laterally (leaving the page to investigate it), corroborating claims across independent sources, and attending to evidence quality. Instruction is typically delivered through authentic web tasks with explicit modeling of expert evaluation moves.

## Design Implications

Explicit instruction in online evaluation produces large gains in students' ability to judge source credibility, far exceeding what students acquire incidentally through general internet use [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. Most students — including high-achieving college students — default to reading vertically (staying on the page) and judging by surface features such as design polish or domain suffix, which professional fact-checkers explicitly avoid [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. Effective instruction makes expert evaluation moves visible through [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling on real websites, then fades to independent practice.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic online tasks using live web content, not sanitized or fictionalized texts
- Explicit modeling of expert moves (lateral reading, source tracing, corroboration), ideally via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration
- A small set of transferable heuristics (e.g., "read laterally first," "find the original source") rather than long checklists
- Repeated practice across varied content types — news, social media, advocacy sites, data visualizations

#### Constraints
- Surface-feature heuristics taught in isolation (checklists like "look for .org" or "check the date") do not transfer and can be gamed by sophisticated misinformation [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~M]
- Evaluation skills do not generalize without practice across multiple content domains; single-lesson interventions show weak durability [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [-W]
- Learners with low prior knowledge of a topic struggle to corroborate claims and may over-rely on source reputation cues [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~M]
- Heavy evaluation demands can impose extraneous load and disrupt comprehension of the content itself; sequencing matters [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Lateral-reading-first approaches (fact-checker style) versus checklist-based approaches — the former shows stronger transfer
- Embedded across disciplines (science, history, civics) versus taught as a standalone media-literacy unit
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) sources with credibility judgments as a lightweight in-text variant

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults who regularly consume news and social media but lack evaluation strategies [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Historically effective even for high-performing students, who often perform poorly on evaluation tasks without instruction [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Younger learners need simplified heuristics and more scaffolding before independent lateral reading

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical evaluation: judging source credibility, bias, and evidence quality
- Information literacy: locating original sources and corroborating claims
- Civic reasoning: making informed judgments about public claims and media

### Instructions
1. Model expert evaluation on a live webpage using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): open a new tab, investigate who is behind the source, and verbalize the decision.
2. Contrast expert moves with novice moves (reading vertically, trusting design cues) using paired examples.
3. Have learners practice lateral reading and source tracing on curated real-world tasks, using a resource like [A Finder's Guide to Facts](../strategies/a_finder's_guide_to_facts.md).
4. Require corroboration across independent sources before accepting a claim, e.g., via the [3-Source Rule](../strategies/3-source_rule.md).
5. Have learners [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) sources with credibility judgments and justifications, then discuss disagreements in a [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
6. Fade support: move from modeled demonstrations to independent evaluation of unfamiliar sources, with periodic check-ins.

## Examples
- **Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning curriculum** (https://cor.stanford.edu) — free lessons and assessments built on fact-checker strategies; classroom studies show substantial pre-post gains in evaluation ability.
- **News Literacy Project — Checkology** (https://checkology.org) — interactive lessons on source evaluation, misinformation, and lateral reading for middle and high school students.
- **History class source tracing** — students given a viral image or claim locate its original context (reverse image search, finding the first publication), mirroring professional fact-checking practice.
