# Complex Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Complex Instruction (CI), developed by Elizabeth Cohen and colleagues at Stanford, is a cooperative learning pedagogy built on three interlocking tenets: rich, open-ended tasks with multiple ability entry points; structured group roles and norms that make learners interdependent; and active "assigning competence" — the teacher publicly names a low-status learner's intellectual contribution to disrupt status hierarchies. Groups of four work on a single task; each learner holds a role (team captain, resource manager, recorder-reporter, facilitator) while the teacher circulates, observes group dynamics, and intervenes on status rather than content.

## Design Implications

CI treats unequal participation as a status problem to be engineered away, not a motivation problem to be remediated. Structured interdependence plus public recognition of competence raises the participation of marginalized learners [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S], and belonging-focused interventions of this kind improve outcomes for students at risk of marginalization [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The teacher's role shifts from deliverer of content to diagnostician of group interaction, intervening with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) on collaboration moves rather than supplying answers.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Rich, open-ended tasks with multiple entry points and multiple solution paths ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Pre-assigned rotating roles and explicit group norms ("no one is done until everyone is done")
- Teacher training in status interventions — treating talking points, asking questions, and organizing ideas as intellectual competencies worth naming publicly
- Debriefing structures where groups report and the class connects solutions to core concepts ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Partial implementation fails: tasks without status work reproduce existing hierarchies, and status work without rich tasks has nothing legitimate to assign competence for [-M]
- Requires substantial sustained teacher professional development; one-off training rarely produces faithful enactment [-M]
- Status interventions can backfire if praise is perceived as inauthentic or as teacher favoritism [~W]
- Large classes and short class periods make observation-and-intervention cycles difficult to sustain [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Roles can rotate daily or weekly; some implementations add a fifth role or merge recorder-reporter and facilitator
- Status interventions range from brief public naming ("the Alexis Method") to structured multiple-ability treatments that pre-teach the full range of competencies the task demands
- CI is most developed in secondary mathematics (e.g., San Francisco Unified's adoption) but has been applied in science, language arts, and teacher education

### Target Learners
- Heterogeneous, tracked-against classrooms — CI was designed specifically for mixed-ability settings where status differences suppress participation [+M]
- English language learners and students from historically marginalized groups, who benefit from multiple entry points and public recognition of competence [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less suited to learners who already hold high classroom status without challenge, unless tasks genuinely require their interdependence [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through social construction of solutions rather than transmission
- Collaborative and communicative competencies: explaining, questioning, justifying
- Equitable participation and mathematical identity — learners coming to see themselves and their peers as capable
- Status awareness: recognizing diverse intellectual contributions (not only speed and correctness) as smartness

### Instructions
1. Design or select a rich task with low floor and high ceiling — multiple entry points and solution paths ([Application](../elements/application.md))
2. Form heterogeneous groups of four and assign roles ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)); teach norms explicitly before the first task
3. Launch the task whole-class so all learners access the problem before group work begins
4. Circulate and observe; intervene on group process through [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) ("Ask the resource manager what you need") rather than content answers
5. Assign status publicly when you observe a valuable contribution — name the specific intellectual move and its value to the group's work
6. Debrief with reporter roles sharing group reasoning, then connect multiple solution paths to the target concept ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

## Examples
- **San Francisco Unified School District** adopted CI as the core of its secondary mathematics sequence; teachers use role cards, participation quizzes, and status interventions district-wide (see [sfusdmath.org](https://www.sfusdmath.org))
- **"The Alexis Method"** — a teacher observing a learner's novel number-organization strategy publicly named it after her; the named strategy spread across groups and shifted peers' perceptions of her competence
- **Cohen & Lotan's Finding-Out/Descubrimiento programs** — bilingual elementary science implementations demonstrating CI with English language learners
