Calming Combinations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 theoretical), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Calming combinations pair two or more sensory-regulation strategies — for example, a tactile bin with white-noise headphones, a weighted blanket with visual sensory bottles, chewing gum during a fine-motor task, or rhythmic rocking paired with slow breathing — so that multiple calming channels operate simultaneously. The rationale is that regulation demands vary by learner and moment, and a single strategy rarely matches the profile of sensory input a learner needs to down-regulate. Learners (or their teachers and caregivers) identify effective pairings through structured trial and observation rather than a fixed protocol.

Design Implications¶

Combining strategies is a form of personalization: it treats self-regulation as an individualized matching problem between a learner's sensory profile and available regulatory inputs, rather than a one-size-fits-all routine. Evidence for sensory-based calming interventions is generally weak and inconsistent, so combinations should be treated as hypotheses to test with observation data, not validated prescriptions Systematic reviews find limited and low-quality evidence for sensory integration interventions. [+W]. Teaching learners to monitor and evaluate their own state during each trial builds the self-regulation skill itself, not just the calming outcome Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A menu of individually trialed calming strategies (Practice with each one alone before combining)
  • A simple observation or self-report record of arousal/behavior before, during, and after each combination (Check-Ins)
  • Adult Coaching for young children or learners who cannot yet self-monitor
  • Time and materials for repeated trials; combinations must be tested across contexts, not once

Constraints¶

  • Evidence base for sensory calming strategies is weak and largely anecdotal; combining strategies does not compound weak evidence into strong evidence [-W]
  • Over-stacking inputs can over-stimulate rather than calm learners with sensory sensitivities — more input is not always regulating [~W]
  • Combinations effective in one setting often fail in another; regulation is context-dependent, so a combination learned in a quiet resource room may not transfer to a noisy classroom [-W]
  • Requires additional materials, preparation, and adult observation time compared with single strategies [-W]
  • Risk of dependency: learners may come to need the specific combination rather than developing internal regulation strategies [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Adult-selected combinations for young children or learners with significant disabilities, versus learner-selected combinations for older students building self-advocacy
  • Static combinations (a fixed "calm-down kit") versus dynamic menus where learners assemble combinations on demand
  • Embedded in daily routines (e.g., gum plus fine-motor work during seatwork) versus reserved for a designated calm-down space

Target Learners¶

  • Children with sensory sensitivities, autism spectrum profiles, or ADHD who are easily overwhelmed by sensory input Systematic reviews find limited and low-quality evidence for sensory integration interventions. [~W]
  • Learners who have already trialed individual strategies and found each partially effective — combinations suit partial responders
  • Less appropriate as a first-line approach for learners whose dysregulation stems from instructional mismatch (task too hard, unclear expectations) rather than sensory load

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: recognizing arousal states and selecting effective responses Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Emotional regulation and relaxation during or after overwhelming episodes
  • Metacognitive self-knowledge: building a personal map of which sensory inputs regulate, alert, or over-stimulate

Instructions¶

  1. Trial individual calming strategies one at a time, with Practice in a low-demand setting, recording their effect.
  2. Use Check-Ins before and after each trial so the learner connects the strategy to a change in state.
  3. Pair two strategies that act on different sensory channels (e.g., proprioceptive + auditory) rather than two that compete.
  4. Coaching the learner through the combination the first few times, then fade adult support as the learner initiates it independently.
  5. Re-evaluate periodically: combinations lose effectiveness as learners grow, contexts change, or the learner develops internal strategies.

Related Strategies¶

  • Sensory breaks — the single-strategy routines that combinations extend and personalize
  • Calm-down corners — the environmental context where combinations are most often deployed
  • Breathing exercises — a frequent component strategy, often paired with movement or deep pressure

Examples¶

  • A student wears noise-reducing headphones and manipulates a tactile bin during the ten minutes after recess, when classroom noise and transition demands peak.
  • A learner lies under a weighted blanket while watching slow-moving sensory bottles, then transitions to seated work with gum as a maintenance input.
  • An occupational therapist and teacher co-design a menu card: the student selects one "body" input (rocking, wall push), one "sound" input (white noise, quiet music), and one "hands" input (putty, velcro strip) for each calm-down break.
