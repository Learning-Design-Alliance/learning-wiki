# Civic Online Reasoning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Civic online reasoning is the set of skills needed to judge the credibility of information encountered online — evaluating sources, evidence, and claims about social and political issues. The strategy, developed by the Stanford History Education Group (SHEG, now the Digital Inquiry Group), replaces checklist-based evaluation (e.g., CRAAP tests) with the strategies professional fact-checkers actually use: leaving the page to investigate the source elsewhere ("lateral reading"), checking prior claims, and triangulating across sources [Explicit instruction in lateral reading improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S].

## Design Implications

The core insight is empirical: professional fact-checkers evaluate far faster and more accurately than historians or undergraduate students because they read *laterally* — opening new tabs to see what other sources say about the original one — rather than reading vertically within the page [Explicit instruction in lateral reading improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. Conventional classroom checklists train students to scrutinize surface features (design, About pages, URLs), which manipulable sites easily fake; lateral reading redirects attention to what independent sources know about the source. Instruction works best when strategies are modeled through expert [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration and then practiced on authentic, messy web content rather than sanitized examples.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic online tasks: real search results, real websites, real social media posts — not simulated or pre-vetted materials
- Explicit modeling of expert moves ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of lateral reading, source checking, and claim verification)
- Repeated [Practice](../elements/practice.md) across varied content types (news, advocacy sites, images, videos, statistics)
- Low-stakes formative assessment with feedback on evaluation decisions

#### Constraints
- Checklist-style evaluation (currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, purpose) does not transfer to real web evaluation and can *increase* susceptibility to professional-looking misinformation [Checklist-based source evaluation is ineffective compared to lateral reading.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-S]
- One-off lessons produce weak effects; benefits require distributed practice across multiple sessions [Explicit instruction in lateral reading improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~M]
- Effectiveness drops when tasks use artificial or simplified sources, because the difficulty of real evaluation lies in ambiguity and deception
- Learners with weak background knowledge of civic institutions may misjudge source credibility even with good strategies

#### Implementation Variability
- Short-form: 6–15 minute classroom activities (SHEG's original format) targeting one skill per task
- Full curriculum: multi-week units (e.g., Digital Inquiry Group's *Digital Literacy* curriculum) integrating lateral reading, evidence evaluation, and data interpretation
- Domain-specific adaptations: health misinformation, election information, scientific claims — each requires domain-typical source landscapes for practice

### Target Learners
- Middle school through college students, the populations in which effects have been directly tested [Explicit instruction in lateral reading improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Adults in professional contexts that require information vetting (journalists, librarians, health consumers)
- Less effective without prior basic search fluency; students who cannot navigate tabs and search results fluidly need that prerequisite first

### Target Learning Goals
- Source evaluation: judging who is behind information and why it was created
- Evidence evaluation: judging whether evidence supports a claim
- Claim verification: triangulating contested factual claims across independent sources
- Transfer to civic participation: informed voting, media consumption, and public discourse

### Instructions
1. Diagnose current strategies: give students an authentic evaluation task and surface their default (usually vertical) reading habits.
2. Model expert strategy: demonstrate lateral reading via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) — open new tabs, search the organization's name, check Wikipedia and news coverage, and verbalize the decision rule ("What do *other* sites say about this one?").
3. Contrast cases: compare a fact-checker's approach with a historian's or student's approach on the same source, so learners see why lateral beats vertical [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S].
4. Practice with fading: sequence tasks from guided (hints, worked searches) to independent evaluation of unfamiliar sources, moving from simple to deceptive cases.
5. Assess with authentic tasks: score evaluation decisions on realistic web content, not recall of checklist criteria.

## Examples
- **Digital Inquiry Group (formerly SHEG) curriculum** ([https://www.digcit.org](https://www.digcit.org)) — free classroom activities and full units built around lateral reading, evaluated in large-scale field experiments.
- **Breakstone et al. (2021) national study** — assessed ~3,400 U.S. high school students on live web tasks; most struggled to evaluate sources, motivating strategy-based instruction over checklists.
- **NewsGuard and professional fact-checking organizations** (e.g., Snopes, PolitiFact) — real-world instances of lateral reading as professional practice, usable as case material.
