# Cold Call

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · no claims cited

## Description
Cold calling is the practice of directing a question to a specific, named student rather than soliciting volunteers. It is carried out by posing a question, allowing wait time, then selecting a respondent — ideally with norms established in advance so the call is experienced as expected rather than punitive. Variants range from fully random selection to warm calling (signaling the question before naming the student) or follow-up calls that build on a prior answer.

## Design Implications

Cold calling dramatically broadens participation beyond volunteers, distributing retrieval practice across the whole class instead of concentrating it in a few confident students [Active participation structures improve engagement and achievement relative to volunteer-only discussion.](../principles/active-learning.md) [+M]. Because being called on requires learners to retrieve and formulate an answer, it functions as low-stakes [retrieval practice](../principles/active-learning.md), which strengthens memory more than re-exposure to the material [+S]. Its affective impact depends on classroom norms: in supportive climates students report increased confidence and preparation; in unsupportive ones it produces anxiety that interferes with thinking [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Established norms communicated early: cold calling is expected, answers may be imperfect, and "I'd like to come back to you" is acceptable
- Questions calibrated to the responder's current level so most calls are answerable
- Adequate wait time (typically 3–5 seconds) between question and call, and after the answer
- A response to every answer — affirming, probing, or redirecting — so the exchange is formative rather than evaluative
- Questions that are genuinely answerable — tied to assigned reading, prior work, or recently taught content, not trivia or gotchas
- Adequate wait time after the question and after the student's response ([Wait Time](wait-time.md))
- A response climate where wrong or partial answers are treated as material for discussion, not failure
- Consistency: applying the technique predictably and equitably across the class, not targeting individuals
- Questions pitched within reach of the called student (differentiate by knowing who is asked what)
- Sufficient wait time (3+ seconds) after the question and after the student begins answering
- A response climate where "I'm still working on it" or a partial answer is acceptable, with follow-up prompts rather than immediate redirection
- Even, predictable distribution so students cannot opt out by anonymity or opt in by dominance

#### Constraints
- Without supportive norms, cold calling raises anxiety and reduces voluntary participation afterward [-M]
- Calling on unprepared students for high-stakes or complex questions produces guessing and embarrassment rather than learning [~M]
- Instructors who use cold call punitively (to catch inattention) undermine trust and the strategy's benefits [-M]
- Less effective in very large lectures without structures (think-pair-share, named cards) that give every student a plausible chance of being called
- Raises anxiety for some students, particularly those with social anxiety or who are new to a discussion culture; unmitigated, this reduces rather than increases engagement [-M]
- Perceived unfairness (calling on unprepared students to embarrass them) damages trust and belonging [~M]
- In large anonymous lecture settings without prior relationship-building, it can feel coercive and produce compliance rather than thinking
- If questions are recall-only, cold calling enforces attention without producing deeper processing [-W]
- Without established psychological safety, cold calling raises anxiety and reduces voluntary participation over time [Belonging interventions improve outcomes partly by reducing threat in evaluative settings.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]
- Instructors who use cold calling inconsistently or as a "gotcha" see students disengage and prep only when called on [~M]
- Less effective for high-stakes or emotionally loaded content, where public performance threat crowds out cognitive processing [-M]
- Requires small-to-moderate class sizes or name knowledge; in very large classes, name-level cold calling is impractical without tools

#### Implementation Variability
- **Warm call**: announce the question to the class first, then name the student — reduces threat while preserving accountability
- **Randomized selection** (name cards, apps like Wheel of Names): signals fairness and prevents bias toward volunteers
- **Pre-briefed call**: tell a student in advance they will be asked, useful for anxious learners
- **Follow-up chain**: call on a second student to extend or evaluate the first answer, deepening [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- **Warm calling**: warn students in advance (e.g., "I'll come to you in a moment") or share the question before naming the student
- **Pre-briefed cold calling**: distribute questions before class or assign roles in [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) so all students prepare an answer
- **Randomized selection** (name cards, apps): signals fairness and prevents perceived targeting
- **Group cold calling**: direct the question to a team that confers briefly before one member responds, lowering individual stakes
- **Warm calling**: signal the question to the class, give think/pair time, then call a name — reduces threat while keeping accountability
- **Randomized selection** (name cards, wheel, LMS picker): increases perceived fairness and prevents instructor bias toward confident students
- **Pre-briefed calling**: tell students in advance which question they will answer, trading spontaneity for preparation quality
- **Follow-up chains**: call a second student to build on or challenge the first answer, shifting from recall to [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)

### Target Learners
- Students who rarely volunteer but are prepared; cold call surfaces their thinking and builds confidence over time [+M]
- Anxious or novice learners benefit from warm-call variants and pre-briefed calls [~M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for learners with high communication anxiety or those responding in a second language
- Undergraduate and graduate students in discussion-heavy courses (business, law, medicine, seminar formats), where evidence is strongest [~M]
- Reluctant participants who have relevant preparation but do not volunteer; cold calling draws them in without requiring self-initiation [~M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for students experiencing acute anxiety or for classes early in the term before norms are established
- Reluctant or quiet students who would otherwise never volunteer — the largest participation gains accrue to them [~M]
- All students when norms are established: perceived engagement and in-class participation rise across the distribution [~S]
- Students with high communication anxiety need scaffolding (pair time, warning, partial-credit norms) or the strategy backfires [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retrieval and consolidation of previously taught material [+S]
- Formative assessment: exposing misconceptions across the class, not just among volunteers
- Discussion skills: articulating reasoning aloud, responding to peers
- Verbal reasoning and articulation: students must formulate and defend answers aloud
- Preparation accountability: ensuring assigned reading or pre-work has been processed
- Class-wide engagement: keeping all students cognitively active during discussion, not only speakers [Active participation improves exam performance relative to passive listening.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Retrieval and consolidation: being called on forces retrieval, which strengthens memory more than re-exposure [+S]
- Formative assessment: hearing from a representative sample of students, not just volunteers, gives the instructor accurate evidence of class understanding
- Discussion skills and academic discourse: sustained use increases students' willingness to speak and the quality of contributions

### Instructions
1. Establish the norm explicitly on day one: explain that everyone will be called on, that partial answers are welcome, and that the goal is thinking, not performance.
2. Pose the question to the whole class and provide wait time so all students rehearse an answer ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) prompts can model what a good answer sounds like).
3. Select a responder — randomly or deliberately — matched to question difficulty.
4. Respond to the answer with [feedback](../elements/assessment.md) at the process level: affirm correct reasoning, probe incomplete claims, or redirect to a peer.
5. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or peer discussion so the whole class, not just the respondent, retrieves and applies the content.

## Description

It is carried out by posing a genuine, prepared question, naming the student, and treating the response as a contribution to be built upon rather than a test to be passed. It functions as a participation-distribution and accountability technique, most common in discussion-based and case-based classrooms.
