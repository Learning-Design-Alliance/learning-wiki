# Self-Talk Brainstorm

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Provide learners with scenarios of challenging situations and have them brainstorm possible neutral or positive self-talk responses. Examples include: being excluded from a lunch table, a teacher not having time for your question, being blamed for something you didn't do, and failing a test. For each scenario, present examples of negative self-talk and prompt learners to generate more positive or neutral alternatives. The strategy operationalizes cognitive restructuring in a low-stakes, rehearsal format: learners practice noticing automatic negative interpretations and substituting deliberate, constructive ones before they need the skill in real situations.

## Design Implications

Self-talk brainstorming is a form of guided rehearsal for metacognitive and emotional regulation. Verbal self-statements can direct attention, effort, and emotional response, and instructional programs that train constructive self-statements (e.g., Meichenbaum's stress-inoculation and self-instructional training) show reliable effects on coping and task performance [+M]. The brainstorming format works because generating *multiple* candidate responses for a scenario forces elaboration and discrimination — learners must evaluate which phrasing is believable and useful, not just parrot a supplied affirmation. Affirmations learners do not believe can backfire [~M]; effective alternatives are typically neutral and realistic ("I can ask again at lunch," "One test doesn't define me") rather than grandiose ("I'm amazing at everything").

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of challenging scenarios that are authentic and recognizable to the learner population
- Worked examples of the negative-to-neutral transformation, so learners see the move modeled before generating their own ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or instructor modeling)
- A structure for generating many responses quickly — pairs, small groups, or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) with rapid round-robin
- A norm that responses must be *believable* to the speaker, not merely positive

#### Constraints
- Learners under acute stress or with low self-efficacy may reject positive alternatives as implausible, producing reactance rather than relief [~M] — neutral, task-focused phrasing mitigates this
- Brainstorming in the abstract does not transfer; without rehearsal in the actual situation or role-play, generated phrases are rarely deployed when needed [-W]
- Group settings can pressure learners to perform optimism, suppressing genuine negative reactions that deserve discussion
- Younger children's private speech is developmentally adaptive; treating all self-talk as something to "fix" misreads the literature on private speech [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Scenario cards**: small groups rotate through scenario stations, adding responses to a shared chart
- **Negative-talk sort**: learners sort pre-written self-statements into helpful / unhelpful / believable / unbelievable, then rewrite the unhelpful ones
- **Role-play extension**: pairs act out the scenario and deliver the self-talk aloud, adding behavioral rehearsal ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md))
- **Personal scenario swap**: learners contribute their own recurring challenging situations, which become the brainstorm prompts
- **Written variant**: journaling prompt — "Write the harsh version, then the fair version, of what you tell yourself after a mistake"

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults, who have the metacognitive capacity to notice and evaluate their own inner speech [+M]
- Learners experiencing performance anxiety (tests, public speaking, athletics), where instructional self-talk training has the strongest evidence base [+M]
- Whole-class use is feasible, but learners with internalizing tendencies may need private or written formats rather than public sharing

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional regulation: building a repertoire of coping statements for setbacks and social stress
- Metacognitive awareness: recognizing automatic thoughts as interpretations rather than facts
- Resilience and persistence: reframing failure feedback to support continued effort

### Instructions
1. **Model the move.** Present one scenario and its typical negative self-talk; think aloud while generating two neutral alternatives, explaining why each is believable ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. **Brainstorm in pairs or groups.** Give each group a scenario; they generate as many neutral/positive responses as possible in 3–5 minutes, aiming for variety over polish.
3. **Share and evaluate.** Collect responses via [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md); as a class, test each against two criteria — *Is it believable? Would it actually help?* — discarding affirmations that fail either test.
4. **Rehearse.** Learners select one response per scenario and rehearse it aloud, in role-play, or in writing.
5. **Personalize.** Learners identify one real situation from their own life and draft self-talk for it, keeping it private or sharing voluntarily.

## Examples
- **Second Step (SEL curriculum, Committee for Children)** — middle-school lessons present social scenarios (exclusion, peer conflict) and guide students to generate constructive self-talk as a coping strategy. [https://www.secondstep.org](https://www.secondstep.org)
- **Sport psychology self-talk training** — athletes brainstorm and rehearse instructional ("elbow up") and motivational ("I've trained for this") cues; meta-analytic evidence supports self-talk for sport performance [+M] (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011).
- **Test-prep classrooms** — before a high-stakes exam, students brainstorm responses to "I'm going to fail this" and post selected phrases as classroom anchors.
