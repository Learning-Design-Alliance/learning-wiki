Design interventions at teacher and test levels to reduce content bias in math tests¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 2 against) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 associational), q2–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Because excluding money, food, and social-interaction content from tests is "neither desirable nor feasible", and monetary understanding is a crucial learning goal, the authors propose "two possible levels for interventions: a) teacher level (e.g., giving teacherstools to provide additional guidance to students during test taking) and b) test level (e.g., removal of speciﬁcc o ntent that may be particularly triggering, or adding speci ﬁc instructions when using content that can produce bias)". These interventions aim to make math tests fairer and enhance social equality in education.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Developing and applying bias-reducing interventions; the authors note simply excluding triggering content is neither desirable nor feasible given monitoring-study practice and the educational value of money content.

Constraints¶

  • The article states the interventions are a proposed promising avenue; their effectiveness has not been tested in this study.

Target Learners¶

  • low-SES elementary and middle-school students taking standardized math tests

Target Learning Goals¶

  • accurate assessment of mathematics ability regardless of socioeconomic background

Claims¶

  • Items with low-SES ecologically relevant content show Differential Item Functioning to the disadvantage of low-SES students more often than neutral items [+W]
  • The expected performance advantage of ecologically relevant content for low-SES students, predicted by the hidden talents approach, was not found; the opposite pattern emerged [-W]
  • Low-SES students have a lower chance of correctly answering math items with money, food, or social-interaction content than expected given their average math performance [+W]
  • The disadvantage from ecologically relevant content is driven by money and social-interaction items in both grades, but food items show the effect only in grade 4 [+W]
  • At item level, money and social-interaction content are significantly related to lower odds of correct responding by low-SES students, but food content is not when compared with neutral word problems [+W]
  • Contrary to the hidden-talents hypothesis, low-SES students scored 18% lower on mathematics items of real-world importance [-W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Use design-based implementation research with educator co-design and iterative pre-post testing cycles to develop a usable, feasible classroom intervention.
  • Leverage cognitive strengths of low-SES children via group work, real-world application, and oral/narrative learning and assessment
