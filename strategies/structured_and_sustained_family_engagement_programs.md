# Structured and Sustained Family Engagement Programs

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structured and sustained family engagement programs are school-wide, institutionalized approaches to partnering with families — moving beyond one-off events (open houses, conferences) toward ongoing activities tied directly to student learning. They are carried out through a framework of engagement types (parenting, communicating, volunteering, learning at home, decision-making, collaborating with community), led by committed school leadership, and continuously evaluated so that effective offerings are retained and ineffective ones dropped.

## Design Implications

Engagement that is *structured* (planned, role-assigned, scheduled) and *sustained* (multi-year, embedded in school routines) outperforms episodic contact, because the benefits of family involvement on achievement accumulate through consistent learning-linked activities rather than attendance at isolated events [~M]. The critical design distinction is between engagement that is merely *present* and engagement that is *connected to learning*: programs that link family activities to specific academic content produce measurable achievement gains, while generic involvement does not reliably do so [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A principal who values family partnership, shares decision-making power, and holds high expectations for all students
- A written program plan assigning responsibility, timelines, and evaluation points (e.g., an action team using Epstein's Six Types of Involvement framework)
- Activities tied to the curriculum — not just attendance or fundraising — such as structured home-learning interactions with materials provided by the school
- Two-way communication channels in families' home languages
- Evaluation metrics: participation rates disaggregated by subgroup, learning-activity completion, and student outcome data

#### Constraints
- Requires sustained investment of staff time and budget; programs collapse when grant funding ends or a champion leader departs [-M]
- One-size-fits-all events systematically under-serve working-class, shift-working, and non-English-speaking families, reproducing rather than closing engagement gaps [-M]
- Engagement without power-sharing (families consulted but never deciding) produces compliance, not partnership, and erodes trust over time [-W]
- Programs emphasizing attendance at school events rather than home learning interactions show weak or null effects on achievement [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **School-wide partnership model** (Epstein's framework): action teams plan six types of involvement across the year
- **Home-learning focus**: interactive homework ("TIPS" — Teachers Involve Parents in Schoolwork) that requires students to discuss schoolwork with a family partner
- **Community-school model**: full-service community schools integrate family engagement with health and social services on site
- **Digital-first engagement**: two-way messaging platforms (e.g., ParentSquare, TalkingPoints) lower barriers for families who cannot attend in person

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grades students, whose achievement responds most strongly to home-based learning involvement; effects attenuate in high school as families shift toward academic planning and postsecondary support [~M]
- Students from low-income families benefit disproportionately when programs are designed around their constraints rather than traditional school-event formats [~M]
- Multilingual families, when communication and materials are provided in home languages

### Target Learning Goals
- Improved student achievement, attendance, and behavior over multiple years
- Family efficacy: families' knowledge of and confidence in supporting academic work at home
- School climate and family–school trust as enabling conditions for learning

### Instructions
1. Form a school–family action team including teachers, families, and the principal; [state objectives](../elements/state-objectives.md) for each engagement activity in terms of student learning, not attendance.
2. Audit current engagement: which families participate, which do not, and why. Redesign offerings around identified barriers (timing, language, transportation, childcare).
3. Design learning-linked activities — interactive homework, family literacy or math nights with take-home materials, goal-setting conferences where students lead — and connect each to classroom [application](../elements/application.md) of academic content.
4. Establish two-way communication routines (check-in messages, translated newsletters, response-capable messaging platforms) rather than one-way announcements.
5. Include families in decision-making structures (advisory councils with real authority over program design and budget).
6. [Assess performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) annually: participation by subgroup, completion of learning activities, student outcome trends; [provide feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) to families and staff and revise the plan.

## Related Strategies
- Family literacy and home-learning programs — the learning-linked activity type with the strongest achievement evidence
- Community-school partnerships — extend engagement to wraparound services
- Student-led conferences — shift engagement from parent-recipient to student-led partnership

## Examples
- **[National Network of Partnership Schools](https://nnps.jhucsos.com)** (Johns Hopkins University) — Epstein's framework in practice; member schools form action teams, write annual partnership plans, and evaluate six types of involvement.
- **TIPS (Teachers Involve Parents in Schoolwork)** — interactive homework assignments requiring structured family–student conversation; field studies show higher family involvement and positive homework attitudes.
- **[Communities In Schools](https://www.communitiesinschools.org)** — site-coordinated model embedding family and community supports within schools.
- **TalkingPoints** — two-way translated family messaging used to sustain engagement between families and teachers.
