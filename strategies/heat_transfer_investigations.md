# Heat Transfer Investigations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Heat transfer investigations are structured hands-on experiments in which learners compare how different materials, colors, or environments conduct, insulate, or radiate heat — for example, testing which cup keeps water warmest or which surface melts ice fastest. Learners generate predictions, collect temperature data, and reconcile results with their initial conceptions of conduction, convection, and insulation. The strategy pairs conceptual learning about thermal energy with authentic practice in scientific investigation: controlling variables, measuring, and reasoning from evidence.

## Design Implications

Inquiry-based science teaching produces moderate positive effects on conceptual understanding when it is *guided* rather than open-ended [Furtak et al.'s meta-analysis found guided inquiry outperformed unguided conditions.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654312457206) [+M]. Unguided discovery alone is consistently less effective than explicit guidance [Alfieri et al. found enhanced-guidance conditions outperformed unassisted discovery.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017) [+S]. Heat transfer is well suited to investigation because the phenomena are observable, measurable with simple tools, and produce results that directly contradict common misconceptions (e.g., that metal is "colder" than wood at room temperature) — creating the cognitive conflict needed for conceptual change.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Simple, reliable measurement tools (thermometers, timers) so data quality does not swamp conceptual reasoning
- A comparison structure — at least two contrasting conditions per investigation, since [multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying principle](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Prediction and discussion phases before and after data collection, where [self-explanation improves conceptual understanding](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Teacher guidance that structures the question, variables, and interpretation rather than leaving learners to search aimlessly

#### Constraints
- Unguided "explore and see what happens" versions produce minimal conceptual gain [Alfieri et al. found unassisted discovery underperformed guided conditions.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017) [-S]
- Procedural demands of measurement can consume working memory and crowd out conceptual learning; chunking the procedure or providing pre-built data tables mitigates this [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Misconceptions are durable: a single investigation rarely displaces them without explicit discussion of why the intuitive view fails
- Long equilibration times (heat flow is slow) can break the link between action and result; choose fast-responding setups (ice on metal vs. plastic, warm water in different cups)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Prediction-first (POE format):** learners predict, observe, explain — strongest for targeting misconceptions
- **Fair-test investigations:** learners design the comparison themselves, adding experimental-design practice at the cost of conceptual focus
- **Productive failure variant:** learners attempt to explain results before instruction, which [improves later conceptual learning from direct teaching](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]
- **Everyday-materials versions:** insulation tests with cups, foil, and fabric make the phenomenon accessible without lab equipment

### Target Learners
- Elementary through secondary science learners; the phenomenon is concrete enough for young learners but supports quantitative reasoning at higher grades
- Learners holding common thermal misconceptions benefit most, provided results are explicitly contrasted with predictions
- Novices need structured data tables and guided questions; open investigation design suits learners with stronger prior knowledge

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of conduction, insulation, and thermal energy flow
- Scientific practices: controlling variables, measurement, evidence-based reasoning
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect models of heat and cold

### Instructions
1. **Activate prior conceptions.** Pose a familiar question ("Why does a metal bench feel colder than a wooden one?") and collect predictions, surfacing misconceptions for later contrast.
2. **Structure the comparison.** Present or co-design a fair test with two or more contrasting conditions; contrasting cases support abstraction of the conduction principle [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
3. **Predict and commit.** Learners record predictions before data collection to maximize the impact of disconfirming evidence.
4. **Collect data.** Use simple tools and pre-structured tables to keep measurement from overloading working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
5. **Explain and reconcile.** Learners explain results in their own words and compare against predictions; self-explanation at this step drives conceptual change [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
6. **Apply.** Pose a transfer problem (design a better lunchbox insulator) requiring application of the principle to a new context.

## Examples
- **FOSS Science (Full Option Science System)** — the *Energy* module includes structured investigations of heat transfer between materials with guided data collection ([https://www.fossweb.com](https://www.fossweb.com))
- **"Which cup keeps water hottest?"** — a classic classroom investigation comparing ceramic, paper, metal, and insulated cups, with learners graphing temperature over time
- **Ice-melt race** — ice blocks on metal, wood, and plastic surfaces; the counterintuitive result (metal melts ice fastest despite feeling "cold") directly confronts the temperature-vs-conductivity misconception
