# Shape Search

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Shape Search is an activity-based strategy in which learners hunt for two- and three-dimensional shapes in their physical environment — classroom objects, buildings, playground equipment, packaging — and name, classify, or sketch what they find. It converts abstract geometric vocabulary into perceptually grounded referents by anchoring each term to multiple real instances. Challenge is typically scaled by moving from prototypical shapes (circles, squares, cubes) to harder or less familiar ones (hexagons, prisms, pyramids in non-standard orientations).

## Design Implications

Shape Search leverages the link between spatial perception and geometric reasoning: young children's geometric understanding develops from visual-holistic recognition of whole shapes toward property-based reasoning, and rich, varied exposure to non-prototypical examples drives that progression [~M]. Searching for the *same* category across many different-looking instances supports abstraction of the defining features rather than surface features [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying concept.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The activity also functions as authentic [Application](../elements/application.md) of newly taught vocabulary, which strengthens retention relative to worksheet-only practice.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An environment rich enough to contain target shapes (classroom, schoolyard, home, or curated image sets)
- Explicit prior teaching of shape names and defining properties, so learners have vocabulary to apply ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or a brief [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) phase)
- A recording method: checklist, tally, sketch, or photo annotation
- A debrief in which learners justify classifications ("How do you know that's a cylinder?") — self-explanatory justification deepens conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends on availability of shapes in the environment; sparse or repetitive settings produce shallow, repetitive searches
- Prototypical examples can entrench misconceptions — children may define triangles by orientation ("point up") or exclude non-regular quadrilaterals; searches dominated by textbook-like instances slow movement past these visual prototypes [~M]
- May become repetitive if played too long without variation; rotate target sets, add constraints, or shift to [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) ("find something that is *almost* a rectangle but isn't")
- Naming shapes without discussing properties reduces the activity to vocabulary drill; require attribute talk, not just labels

#### Implementation Variability
- **Checklist hunt**: fixed list of shapes; learners tally occurrences
- **Photo scavenger hunt**: learners capture and annotate images (digital or paper camera)
- **Property-first variant**: learners find objects matching a property ("all faces flat") rather than a named shape
- **3D-to-2D mapping**: learners identify which 2D shapes appear as faces of a 3D object they find
- **Progressive difficulty**: start with circles and squares, advance to hexagons, rhombi, prisms, and pyramids in unusual orientations
- **Scaffolded lists**: provide the target shape list as support for novices; remove it for learners ready to generate their own categories

### Target Learners
- Elementary students, especially pre-K through grade 3, who are consolidating shape vocabulary and moving from holistic to property-based recognition [~M]
- English language learners, because the activity binds new vocabulary to concrete referents [~W]
- Older learners can benefit when the search targets harder distinctions (e.g., prisms vs. cylinders, regular vs. irregular polygons) rather than basic naming

### Target Learning Goals
- Shape recognition and geometric vocabulary in context
- Classification by defining properties rather than appearance
- Connecting formal geometric concepts to real-world objects and settings
- Early spatial reasoning that supports later mathematics achievement [~W]

### Instructions
1. Briefly review target shapes and their defining properties, using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and real objects to anchor the vocabulary.
2. Model one search example yourself, naming the shape and justifying the classification aloud.
3. Send learners to search the environment with a checklist, sketchbook, or camera ([Application](../elements/application.md) of the vocabulary in an authentic setting).
4. Debrief as a group: learners present finds and justify classifications; the teacher introduces [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) and borderline cases to sharpen the definitions.
5. Repeat over time with harder shapes and fewer scaffolds; distributing searches across days improves retention relative to a single session [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].

## Tools
- Paper checklists, clipboards, and sketchbooks (no-tech version)

- [PBS Kids / Sesame Street shape games](https://pbskids.org) for digital follow-up practice

## Examples
- **Kindergarten classroom hunt**: after a lesson on 3D shapes, children find a cylinder (glue stick), a sphere (ball), and a cube (block), then explain which faces are flat during circle time.
- **Schoolyard architecture search**: grade 4 students photograph quadrilaterals and triangles in the building's structure and sort them by side properties in a shared slideshow.
- **Home–school connection**: families complete a weekend "shape hunt" at the grocery store, returning with annotated drawings that feed into a class tally chart.
