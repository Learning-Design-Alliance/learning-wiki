# Incorporate SILS into surgical training via designated simulator time, simulation technology development, and longer resident operating room times

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 mixed) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
The authors recommend that surgical training programs take the lead in safely teaching new techniques, and that imparting SILS skills may require several educational resources. They write that imparting SILS skills "may require designated simulator time, development of simulation technology to reinforce such skills, and devotion of slightly longer operating room times to resident education". The conclusion states that successful incorporation into training programs depends on developing innovative simulation strategies.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Novel teaching methods suited to an era of work-hour restriction, patient safety concerns, and surgical department economics
#### Constraints
- The recommendation is based on a small single-surgeon early experience rather than a controlled evaluation of simulation methods

### Target Learners
- general surgery residents in training programs

### Target Learning Goals
- safe and effective acquisition of SILS technique
