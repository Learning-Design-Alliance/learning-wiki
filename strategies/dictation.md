# Dictation

## Description
Dictation asks learners to write down text spoken aloud by an instructor or audio system, moving from single words to sentences to connected passages. Because learners must segment speech into phonemes and map each onto a grapheme or spelling pattern, dictation forces active retrieval of orthographic knowledge rather than passive copying. It is typically followed by immediate comparison against the correct text and targeted correction.

## Design Implications

Dictation is a form of retrieval practice for orthographic knowledge: producing a spelling from memory strengthens phoneme-grapheme connections far more than re-reading or copying [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization to new words.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. Explicit spelling and encoding instruction of this kind measurably improves spelling performance, and spelling accuracy in turn supports reading development by consolidating word representations [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. The immediate feedback loop — writing, then checking against the source — is what makes the activity instructional rather than merely evaluative.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Text at the learner's instructional level, containing the spelling patterns or structures currently being taught
- Clear, well-paced audio delivery (live or recorded), with repetition permitted
- Immediate comparison with the correct text and a correction routine ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)), not just a score
- Sufficient writing fluency or keyboarding skill so transcription mechanics do not consume working memory

#### Constraints
- Dictation of untaught or irregular patterns produces guessing and error reinforcement rather than learning [-M]
- For learners with slow transcription or weak phonemic awareness, the dual demands of segmenting and writing can overload working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M] — start with word-level or chunk-level dictation before sentences
- As a comprehension exercise, verbatim transcription encourages word-by-word processing and can undermine meaning-focused reading [-W]; dictation should target encoding, not comprehension
- Overuse as a high-stakes spelling test shifts attention from learning to performance and penalizes learners with processing-speed differences

#### Implementation Variability
- **Word dictation** — isolated words targeting a specific pattern (e.g., the /ai/ spelling family)
- **Sentence dictation** — adds punctuation, capitalization, and syntax; hold sentence length short for novices
- **Running dictation** — collaborative variant where pairs alternate reading a posted text and writing it from memory, adding movement and [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Dictogloss** — learners take notes on a short spoken text, then reconstruct it in a grammatically accurate version in small groups; shifts the goal from verbatim accuracy to grammar and discourse
- **Audio self-paced dictation** — recorded audio with pause controls (e.g., in language-learning apps) lets learners control pacing and repetition

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and spellers learning phoneme-grapheme correspondences [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization to new words.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]
- Second-language learners building sound-to-orthography mapping in the new language [+M]
- Learners with dyslexia benefit when dictation is limited to explicitly taught patterns and paired with multisensory routines (as in Orton-Gillingham–based programs) [+W]
- Less appropriate for fluent spellers, for whom dictation adds little beyond independent writing [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Encoding and spelling accuracy: retrieving spellings from memory rather than recognizing them
- Phonemic awareness and phoneme-grapheme mapping
- Automatic word recognition, which supports comprehension downstream [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Grammar and punctuation conventions (sentence-level variants)

### Instructions
1. Pre-teach the target spelling pattern or structure explicitly before dictating words containing it.
2. Dictate at the word or short-chunk level first, keeping each unit small enough to hold in memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].
3. Have learners write the word or sentence from memory — this retrieval step, not the listening, is where learning occurs.
4. Reveal the correct text immediately and have learners self-correct, marking the specific grapheme where they erred ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)).
5. Re-dictate missed items after a delay, distributing review across sessions rather than massing it [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
6. Progress to sentence and passage dictation only once word-level accuracy is secure.

## Examples
- **Orton-Gillingham–based programs** (e.g., [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com)) — every lesson includes dictation of sounds, words, and sentences with immediate correction, targeting explicitly taught patterns.
- **[Dictogloss](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/article/dictogloss)** (British Council) — a grammar-focused dictation variant for second-language classrooms in which learners reconstruct a spoken text collaboratively.
- **[Duolingo](https://www.duolingo.com)** — "type what you hear" exercises are audio dictation items with instant feedback, self-paced by the learner.
