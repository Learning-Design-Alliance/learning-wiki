# Pictures & Visuals

## Description
This strategy uses images, diagrams, videos, animations, and graphic organizers to represent content alongside or instead of verbal explanation. Its theoretical basis is [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md): information encoded both verbally and visually leaves two retrieval paths, improving recall [Pairing words with relevant images improves recall compared with words alone.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]. The benefit is conditional, not automatic — visuals help when they are relevant, explanatory, and integrated with text, and can harm learning when they are decorative or overloaded [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning and can reduce it by diverting attention.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-S].

## Design Implications

Visuals work by directing attention to essential content, making abstract or invisible structures concrete, and providing a second memory code. The multimedia principle — presenting words and pictures together rather than words alone — reliably improves transfer when the picture is explanatory [Mayer's multimedia principle research shows illustrations foster deeper understanding than text alone.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.81.4.615) [+S]. But every visual competes for limited working memory; irrelevant or redundant graphics impose extraneous load [Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory demands exceed capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]. The design question is never "should I add a picture?" but "does this picture explain something the words alone cannot?"

### Context
#### Requirements
- Visuals that are *relevant to the learning objective* — explanatory (diagrams, process illustrations, concept sketches), not merely attractive
- Integration of visual and verbal channels: captions, labels, and placement near the corresponding text ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of complex visuals into sequential displays)
- Alignment with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): strip decorative clutter, highlight essential features, and avoid presenting identical text verbatim on screen and in narration

#### Constraints
- Decorative stock images, clip art, and "engaging" photos do not improve learning and can reduce it by splitting attention [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning and can reduce it by diverting attention.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]
- Overly complex or dense visuals (busy diagrams, animations with excessive motion) overload the visual channel [Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory demands exceed capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Redundancy harms learning: full on-screen text duplicating narration consumes the same channel and reduces comprehension [~S]
- Learners with low prior knowledge benefit most from well-designed visuals; experts may find them redundant [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Static vs. animated:** animation helps only for content involving continuous change (e.g., circulatory flow); static diagrams with stepwise presentation often outperform animation for complex systems [~M]
- **Learner-generated visuals:** having students draw or build their own representations (sketchnoting, concept sketches) can outperform viewing, because generation forces deeper processing [+M]
- **Graphic organizers:** advance organizers and structured diagrams frame incoming information; see [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)
- **Annotated visuals:** learner or instructor markup on diagrams directs attention; see [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)

### Target Learners
- Novices, who lack the prior knowledge to generate their own mental imagery from verbal description alone [+M]
- Young learners and second-language learners, for whom visuals reduce the verbal comprehension burden
- Less beneficial for experts, who already hold adequate mental models and may find explanatory visuals redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Recall and retention of factual and conceptual content [Pairing words with relevant images improves recall compared with words alone.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]
- Comprehension of systems, processes, and spatial relationships (anatomy, circuits, maps, workflows)
- Vocabulary development through image-mediated meaning
- Transfer of principles to new situations, when visuals illustrate underlying structure rather than surface detail [+M]

### Instructions
1. Identify the core concept the visual must explain; reject any image that does not map onto the learning objective.
2. Select or design an *explanatory* visual — diagram, illustration, or animation — that makes structure or process visible.
3. Integrate words and pictures closely: label parts, place captions adjacent to the relevant visual region, and avoid redundant verbatim text.
4. Sequence complex visuals in [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) steps rather than presenting everything at once.
5. Pair viewing with active processing — labeling, predicting, drawing, or explaining — via [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md).
6. Assess understanding by having learners interpret, complete, or produce visuals, not just recognize them.

## Tools
- **InferCabulary** (https://www.infercabulary.com) — teaches vocabulary by presenting multiple images from which learners infer meaning
- **Canva / Adobe Express** — for designing clean, low-clutter instructional graphics
- **PhET Interactive Simulations** (https://phet.colorado.edu) — interactive visual simulations of science and math concepts
- **BioDigital / Visible Body** — 3D anatomical visuals replacing dense verbal description

## Examples
- A biology teacher replaces a text-only explanation of photosynthesis with a labeled, stepwise diagram that students annotate and then redraw from memory, exploiting dual coding for retention [+S].
- A statistics instructor uses PhET simulations to let learners manipulate sample size and watch the sampling distribution change, making an abstract concept manipulable.
- A vocabulary program like InferCabulary presents several related images per word, prompting learners to infer the shared meaning rather than memorize a definition.
- A negative case: a slide deck decorated with unrelated stock photos shows no learning benefit and slightly reduces recall compared with text-only slides [-S].
