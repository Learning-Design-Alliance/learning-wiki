Formative Assessment Loops¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

A formative assessment loop is a repeating cycle: elicit evidence of learning (a quick check, task, or question), interpret that evidence against the intended learning goal, and act on it — the teacher adjusts instruction while learners adjust their strategies. The loop closes only when the action step occurs; collecting evidence without responsive follow-up is measurement, not formative assessment Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

Design Implications¶

Formative loops work because they surface misconceptions while they are still correctable and because feedback arrives when learners can still use it Feedback is most effective when it answers where am I going, how am I doing, and where to next. [+S]. The design burden falls on the act step: each loop must specify in advance what evidence will be collected, what decision rule triggers a change, and what the change will be. Loops should be short and frequent rather than long and summative in disguise.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clearly stated learning goals against which evidence is interpreted (Clear Structure)
  • Low-stakes elicitation tasks that reveal thinking, not just right answers (Check-In, Assessment)
  • A pre-planned menu of instructional responses so the teacher can act immediately
  • Feedback that names the gap and the next move, not just a score (Action-Oriented Feedback)

Constraints¶

  • Grading loop artifacts converts them into summative events; students then hide confusion and the evidence dries up [-S]
  • Feedback without learner opportunity to act on it produces negligible gains Feedback is most effective when it answers where am I going, how am I doing, and where to next. [-S]
  • Loops fail when the elicitation task is answerable without understanding (recall-only checks), producing false confidence in progress
  • Over-frequent checking without instructional response consumes time and erodes trust in the process [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class loops: exit tickets or hinge questions with branching next-day plans
  • Individual loops: Adaptive Learning systems that route learners to remediation based on item responses
  • Peer loops: structured peer review where students apply criteria to each other's work [~M]
  • Self-loops: students judge their own work against criteria, building evaluative judgment

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling learners benefit most, since early detection prevents compounding gaps Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]
  • Novices, who cannot yet self-diagnose misconceptions and depend on external evidence
  • Less automatic benefit for advanced learners, for whom frequent low-level checks can feel like busywork [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: exposing and repairing misconceptions before they consolidate
  • Procedural fluency: catching error patterns during early Practice
  • Self-regulation: learners internalize the loop itself — monitoring, judging, adjusting

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning goal and the evidence that would demonstrate it (Clear Structure).
  2. Design a short elicitation task — hinge question, exit ticket, or one-item probe — targeting the most likely misconception (Assessment).
  3. Collect and rapidly interpret the evidence against a decision rule (e.g., "fewer than 70% correct → reteach via a different representation").
  4. Act: adjust instruction, regroup, or assign targeted follow-up (Coaching, Class Discussion).
  5. Give learners feedback that specifies the gap and the next action, and time to act on it (Action-Oriented Feedback).
  6. Re-elicited evidence in the next loop verifies whether the adjustment worked; repeat until the goal is met.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the feedback step of the loop must specify next moves to close the gap
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the first loop often serves as a diagnostic for what learners already hold, including misconceptions

Examples¶

  • Dylan Wiliam's "hinge questions" — a single multiple-choice question mid-lesson, with each distractor mapped to a specific misconception and a pre-planned reteaching route; the whole class's response set determines the next five minutes.
  • Khan Academy — mastery exercises continuously assess and route learners to prerequisite content or hints, enacting the loop algorithmically.
  • Retrieval-practice routines (e.g., two-question openers in Peer Instruction / Eric Mazur's ConcepTests) — students answer individually, discuss with peers, re-answer, then the instructor addresses the residual confusion.
