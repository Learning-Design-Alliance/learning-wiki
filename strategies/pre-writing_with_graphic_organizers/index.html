Pre-Writing with Graphic Organizers¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 review, 2 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Students use a visual planning tool — outline, concept map, story map, T-chart, or argument frame — to generate, connect, and sequence ideas before drafting. The organizer externalizes the planning phase of writing so that idea generation and rhetorical structuring are not competing with sentence production for working memory.

Design Implications¶

Pre-writing organizers reduce the load of juggling content, structure, and transcription simultaneously Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M], and planning strategies in general are among the better-supported writing interventions Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction. [+S]. The critical design choice is who builds the organizer: learners who construct their own maps outperform those who merely study author-provided ones Learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided organizers. [+S]. The organizer is scaffolding, not the product — it must give way to drafting.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An organizer whose structure matches the rhetorical task (compare/contrast grid, argument frame, narrative story map) — a generic template invites empty boxes
  • Brief modeling of how to use the organizer (Think-Aloud or a completed example)
  • A clear path from organizer to draft: each box should map onto a paragraph or section
  • Time limits on planning so most instructional time goes to drafting and revising

Constraints¶

  • Filling in a teacher-provided organizer is low-yield; learners learn from generating the structure and links, not from transcribing into boxes Learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided organizers. [-S]
  • Over-scaffolding can produce formulaic writing that follows the template rather than the ideas; fade the organizer as writers develop Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]
  • Organizers consume time that competes with drafting and revision; for fluent writers the planning aid may add little Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction. [~M]
  • A mismatch between organizer structure and genre (e.g., a five-paragraph frame for an open narrative) constrains thinking rather than supporting it

Implementation Variability¶

  • Learner-generated concept maps for exploratory or expository tasks, where the writer discovers the structure Nesbit & Adesope's concept-map meta-analysis. [+M]
  • Genre-specific frames (story maps, argument/counterargument charts) for novice writers who need the rhetorical skeleton supplied
  • Oral pre-writing (talking ideas into a partner or recorder before mapping) for learners whose ideas outrun their transcription speed
  • Digital organizers (Kidspiration, Popplet, Google Jamboard) that allow easy rearrangement — a key advantage over paper for revising plans

Target Learners¶

  • Novice and struggling writers, who benefit most from externalizing planning Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction. [+S]
  • Learners with limited working memory capacity or writing-related learning disabilities, for whom simultaneous planning and transcription is the bottleneck Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • English learners, who can draft ideas in the organizer before tackling sentence-level language
  • Less necessary for fluent writers with well-developed internal planning routines Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Planning and organization: selecting, sequencing, and connecting ideas for a purpose and audience
  • Genre knowledge: internalizing the structural conventions of narrative, argument, and expository forms
  • Self-regulated writing: making planning an explicit, deliberate phase rather than skipping straight to drafting

Instructions¶

  1. Set the task and activate knowledge. Briefly activate prior knowledge about the topic and the genre's purpose.
  2. Model the organizer. Demonstrate filling in a partially completed organizer for a different topic, thinking aloud about why ideas go where they do.
  3. Students generate their own. Learners brainstorm and map ideas, drawing connections themselves rather than copying prompts Learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided organizers. [+S]. Support with Advance Organizers for the genre structure.
  4. Talk through the plan. Partners or groups explain their maps, prompting Self-Explanation and exposing gaps before drafting.
  5. Draft from the organizer. Each section of the map becomes a paragraph or section; the organizer stays visible as a checklist, not a script.
  6. Fade. Across successive writing tasks, shift from full frames to blank paper with a prompt to "plan first," building independent planning routines Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — supplies the raw material the organizer structures
  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the demonstration step that makes organizer use learnable
  • Peer Feedback — a natural follow-on once drafts exist
  • Support English learners with sentence frames, graphic organizers, and word walls—but supplement frames with level-appropriate strategies because frames can limit the range of student responses

Related Elements¶

  • Advance Organizers — the genre-level frame presented before the task
  • Argument Construction — argument frames (claim–evidence–counterclaim) are the organizer form for persuasive writing
  • Summarization and Synthesis — synthesizing mapped ideas into prose is the bridge from plan to draft
  • Cognitive Load Management — the organizer's core function: separating planning from transcription

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Direct Instruction — modeled planning precedes guided and independent writing
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the modeled organizer makes expert planning visible
  • 4CID Four-Component Instructional Design — planning support as scaffolding that fades across task classes

Examples¶

  • Writing Next (Graham & Perin, Carnegie Corporation) — identifies strategy instruction including planning as one of the highest-effect writing interventions for adolescents.
  • The Writing Revolution — uses embedded outlines and single-paragraph organizers (T-charts, because-but-so frames) before every drafting task.
  • Kidspiration / Inspiration Software — digital concept-mapping tools with an integrated outline view, letting learners toggle between map and linear plan.
  • Read Write Think (NCTE/IRA) — free interactive organizers (essay map, story map, compare/contrast) that students complete online and print as drafting plans.
