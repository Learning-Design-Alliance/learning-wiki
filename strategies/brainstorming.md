# Brainstorming

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Brainstorming asks learners to generate as many ideas as possible around a problem or question within a set time, following rules that defer judgment, welcome wild ideas, and build on others' contributions. It is typically used at the start of a project, design task, or inquiry cycle to broaden the solution space before [evaluation and selection](../elements/assessment.md) begin.

## Design Implications

Brainstorming operationalizes the principle of separating idea generation from idea evaluation: premature criticism suppresses production, while deferred judgment increases both the quantity and, with good facilitation, the quality of ideas [~M]. However, the classic group format carries well-documented productivity losses — production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and free riding mean nominal groups (individuals pooling ideas) typically outperform interacting groups [~S]. Well-designed implementations mitigate these losses through silent individual generation, round-robin sharing, or written formats such as brainwriting.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clearly framed, open-ended prompt with a defined problem or question
- Explicit ground rules: no criticism during generation, quantity over quality, building on ideas permitted
- A visible capture mechanism (board, shared document, sticky notes) so ideas persist for later convergence
- A distinct, later phase for clustering, evaluating, and selecting ideas

#### Constraints
- Interactive group brainstorming often produces fewer and less diverse ideas than the same number of individuals working alone, due to production blocking — only one person can speak at a time [~S]
- Evaluation apprehension suppresses idea sharing when learners fear judgment, especially in low-trust or high-stakes classroom climates [~M]
- Without a follow-up convergence phase, brainstorming yields lists rather than decisions and can consume time without advancing the task [~M]
- Highly structured or well-defined problems benefit little; brainstorming is weakest when there is a single correct answer that prior instruction already covers

#### Implementation Variability
- **Brainwriting**: learners write ideas silently and pass sheets for others to build on, eliminating production blocking [~M]
- **Brainstorm-then-vote (nominal group technique)**: silent generation followed by round-robin sharing and independent ranking
- **Electronic brainstorming**: parallel anonymous idea entry in shared documents or tools (e.g., Padlet, Miro), which reduces evaluation apprehension and blocking
- **Individual-then-group sequencing**: solo generation before group sharing increases total idea diversity compared with group-first formats

### Target Learners
- Learners at the start of open-ended projects (design, writing, inquiry) who need to expand their solution space before committing
- Mixed-ability groups benefit from silent or written formats, which prevent dominant voices from capturing the floor [~M]
- Less useful for novices who lack domain knowledge to generate from — idea generation depends on retrievable prior knowledge; [activating prior knowledge](../principles/activation.md) first improves output [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Divergent thinking and idea fluency
- Problem framing and scoping before project work
- Collaborative knowledge building and perspective exchange

### Instructions
1. Frame the prompt and constraints clearly; post the ground rules (defer judgment, go for quantity, build on ideas).
2. Begin with silent individual generation (2–5 minutes) to counter production blocking [~S].
3. Collect ideas round-robin or via a shared board, building without evaluating.
4. Cluster and name idea groups, then move to explicit evaluation criteria and selection — keep generation and judgment in separate phases.
5. Record the final selection and revisit discarded ideas later if the chosen direction stalls.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Design thinking curricula** (e.g., the Stanford d.school K-12 Lab resources, https://dschool.stanford.edu) use brainstorming as the ideation stage, with explicit rules and silent "stacking" of sticky notes before clustering.
- **Project-Based Learning units** (e.g., PBLWorks, https://www.pblworks.org) typically open with a "need to know" brainstorm that generates student questions driving subsequent inquiry.
- **Writing workshops** use prewriting brainstorms — listing, mapping, freewriting — to generate content before drafting and outlining.
