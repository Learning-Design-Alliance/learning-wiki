# Use Pre Questions

## Description
Pre questions are questions posed to learners *before* they study the target material. Learners typically answer them incorrectly — the point is not assessment but preparation: the act of attempting retrieval, even unsuccessfully, primes related knowledge and makes the subsequent instruction more effective. Pre questions can be delivered as a short quiz, embedded questions in a text or video, or instructor prompts at the start of a lesson.

## Design Implications

Pre questions exploit the pretesting effect: unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance later learning of the tested material more than spending the same time studying it [Kornell, Hays, & Bjork, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729) [+S]. The mechanism appears to be a combination of [activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge and heightened attention to pre-questioned content during instruction — learners process related material more deeply because the failed attempt creates a knowledge gap they are motivated to fill [Richland, Kornell, & Kao, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016533) [+M]. Because the effect is content-specific, pre questions should target the concepts that matter most in the upcoming lesson, not serve as generic warm-ups.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions that target the *same* concepts (or close analogs) covered in the subsequent instruction — the pretesting effect is strongest for pre-questioned content
- Feedback or immediate instruction following the attempt, so errors do not persist [Pan & Rickard, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000121) [+M]
- Low or no stakes — learners must feel safe guessing, since wrong answers are expected and productive
- Multiple-choice or short-answer formats both work; multiple-choice with plausible distractors can add diagnostic value

#### Constraints
- Pre questions divert attention: learners remember pre-questioned content better but *unquestioned* content worse [Richland, Kornell, & Kao, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016533) [-S] — overusing them creates attentional tunneling at the expense of the rest of the lesson
- Guessing without subsequent instruction or feedback can entrench misconceptions [Pan & Rickard, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000121) [~M]
- Less effective when learners lack enough prior knowledge to generate even a plausible guess; the benefit depends on some retrievable related knowledge [activation improves learning](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- In video and e-learning, pre questions add time and can interrupt flow if overlong [Clark & Mayer-style coherence concerns: irrelevant or excessive material hurts learning](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-lecture quizzes** — 3–5 questions before a lecture or reading, ungraded
- **Embedded video questions** — questions inserted before the segment that answers them (as in many MOOC platforms)
- **Pre-instruction problems** — asking learners to attempt a problem they cannot yet solve, a variant used in productive-failure designs
- **Analogical pre questions** — asking about a related but different case, which supports transfer more broadly than verbatim pre questions [comparing and contrasting cases improves learning](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who can generate meaningful (if wrong) guesses [activation improves learning](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Novices benefit less than intermediate learners; complete beginners may lack the knowledge base for productive guessing
- Adult and self-directed learners, who tolerate the discomfort of being wrong when its purpose is explained

### Target Learning Goals
- Factual and conceptual knowledge: the effect is strongest for retention of pre-questioned facts and concepts [Kornell, Hays, & Bjork, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729) [+S]
- Attention management: directing limited attention to high-value content in a dense lesson
- Preparing for later [retrieance practice](../elements/assessment.md)-based study: pre questions act as an initial retrieval event in a spaced schedule

### Instructions
1. Identify the 3–5 most important concepts of the upcoming lesson; write one question per concept at a level learners can plausibly attempt.
2. Present the questions before any instruction, framed explicitly as "you are not expected to know these — guessing is the point."
3. Keep the activity short (under 5 minutes) and ungraded to avoid anxiety and to preserve the [coherence of the lesson](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md).
4. Proceed immediately to instruction that answers the questions, or provide brief feedback on the guesses.
5. Revisit the same questions after instruction as [retrieval practice](../elements/assessment.md), closing the loop between the failed attempt and the learned answer.
6. Optionally use analogical variants — a question about a parallel case — when transfer rather than retention is the goal.
