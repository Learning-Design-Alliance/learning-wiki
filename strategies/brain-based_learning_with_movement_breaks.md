# Brain-Based Learning with Movement Breaks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy intersperses short, structured movement breaks and socially interactive activities between segments of instruction, so that content is processed in smaller chunks with physical activity in between. Rather than treating movement as a reward or diversion, it positions brief physical activity as a mechanism for restoring attention, consolidating learning, and re-engaging students before the next instructional segment.

## Design Implications

Movement breaks work primarily by counteracting the vigilance decrement — sustained sitting degrades attention over time, and brief physical activity restores it [Classroom physical activity improves attention and on-task behavior.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. The learning benefit comes from *when* breaks occur: chunking instruction into shorter segments with breaks between them manages working memory load and prevents overload [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Movement alone does not teach content; it must be paired with substantive instruction, peer discussion, or practice to affect achievement rather than only affect and engagement [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructional content pre-chunked into segments of roughly 10–20 minutes, with a planned break or interactive activity between segments
- Space and routines that allow movement without extended transition costs (clear start/stop signals, defined movement boundaries)
- A follow-on activity that connects the break back to content — peer teaching, discussion, or [practice](../elements/practice.md) — so the reset is capitalized on rather than lost

#### Constraints
- Movement breaks consume instructional time; if content segments are already short and well-paced, breaks add little and can fragment learning [~M]
- Unstructured or purely recreational breaks can raise arousal without improving attention, and transitions afterward can cost more time than the break provided [-M]
- Benefits attenuate for learners who are already highly engaged; breaks imposed on a working flow state can disrupt it [~W]
- Some students experience social anxiety or physical limitations in group movement activities; participation must be optional or adaptable [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content-linked movement**: gestures, acting out processes ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)), or walking discussions that encode content during movement
- **Restorative breaks**: 2–5 minutes of general physical activity (stretching, GoNoodle videos, active transitions) between content segments
- **Peer-teaching formats**: pairs alternate explaining content to each other after a segment, combining movement, social interaction, and retrieval
- **Distributed scheduling**: spacing review and practice across sessions rather than massing it, leveraging the same chunk-and-space logic

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, especially younger learners whose sustained attention spans are shorter [Classroom physical activity improves attention and on-task behavior.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]
- Students with ADHD or high activity needs, for whom movement breaks can reduce off-task behavior [~M]
- Adult learners in long sessions (workshops, training days), where vigilance declines are equally well documented [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention and engagement maintenance across longer instructional blocks
- Retention of declarative and procedural content when breaks are paired with distributed review
- Social-emotional goals: turn-taking, collaboration, and peer explanation skills

### Instructions
1. Chunk the lesson into 10–20 minute segments aligned to a single idea or task ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
2. Deliver each segment with [clear structure](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) and a check for understanding ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
3. Insert a 2–5 minute movement break — content-linked (acting out a concept) or restorative (stretching, structured game).
4. Follow the break with social processing: [peer discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) or students explaining the segment to each other in pairs.
5. Reconvene with whole-class sharing or a quick [practice](../elements/practice.md) task that applies the segment's content.
6. Distribute review of earlier segments across subsequent lessons rather than massing it at the end.

## Examples
- **[GoNoodle](https://www.gonoodle.com)** — short guided movement videos designed for classroom transitions between instructional segments.
- **Elementary math block** — 15-minute mini-lesson on a strategy, 3-minute movement break, then pairs solve problems while alternating explaining their reasoning aloud.
- **Professional development workshops** — table groups stand and do a 2-minute "teach-back walk" after each module, summarizing the content to a partner while moving around the room.
