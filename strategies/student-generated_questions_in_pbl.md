# Student-Generated Questions in PBL

## Description
Student-generated questions in project-based learning (PBL) positions learners as the originators of the inquiry that drives a project: rather than receiving a teacher-posed driving question, students identify what they genuinely want to find out and refine those curiosities into investigable questions. This is carried out through structured question-generation routines (brainstorming, question-storming, question formulation protocols), followed by criteria-based refinement — sorting open vs. closed, prioritizing, and converting vague wonderings into researchable questions that anchor the project.

## Design Implications

Question generation activates prior knowledge and creates an information gap that learners are motivated to close, converting project work from task completion into genuine inquiry [Autonomy over learning goals supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]. The quality of student questions is not fixed: explicit instruction in question-asking strategies substantially improves the depth and cognitive level of questions students produce [Teaching question-asking strategies improves the quality of student-generated questions and subsequent learning.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312031002338) [+S]. Generated questions also function as self-explanation prompts — articulating what one wants to know surfaces gaps in understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A topic space broad enough that authentic student questions are possible; if the answer is predetermined, generated questions become decoration
- Explicit scaffolds for question formulation (e.g., the [Right Question Institute's Question Formulation Technique](https://rightquestion.org)), since untaught question-asking tends toward low-cognitive-level questions [Teaching question-asking strategies improves the quality of student-generated questions and subsequent learning.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312031002338) [+S]
- Criteria and routines for refining questions: open vs. closed, investigable vs. speculative, prioritization against project constraints
- Ongoing [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) to revisit and revise questions as inquiry progresses

#### Constraints
- Novices generate questions that are often superficial or off-target; without strategy instruction and refinement protocols, question generation consumes time without improving learning [Teaching question-asking strategies improves the quality of student-generated questions and subsequent learning.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312031002338) [-S]
- Full autonomy over question selection can overload working memory and misdirect inquiry when learners lack domain knowledge to judge what is central [Productive failure improves conceptual learning only when followed by structured instruction.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~M] — exploration first works when consolidated later
- Question generation adds time and curriculum flexibility; in tightly content-constrained courses it competes with required coverage
- Students accustomed to teacher-posed tasks may experience open question-asking as anxiety-producing rather than motivating until norms are established

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full ownership:** students generate, select, and drive all project questions (authentic PBL, community-connection projects)
- **Constrained generation:** teacher sets the problem context; students generate the sub-questions within it — a middle path that preserves autonomy while bounding scope
- **Question refinement cycles:** initial questions are treated as drafts, revised after first inquiry rounds, mirroring how real researchers iterate
- **Individual vs. collaborative generation:** group question-storming produces more diverse questions; individual generation ensures all students practice the skill

### Target Learners
- Students disengaged by traditional instruction; autonomy over the inquiry question increases ownership and persistence [Autonomy over learning goals supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Intermediate and advanced learners with enough prior knowledge to ask productive questions; complete novices need more framing [Productive failure improves conceptual learning only when followed by structured instruction.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~M]
- Students developing self-regulated learning capacity, since question-setting is a core self-regulation move (goal setting and strategic planning) [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Inquiry and research skills: formulating, refining, and pursuing investigable questions
- Conceptual understanding: questions that surface prior conceptions and gaps support deeper processing [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Motivation and ownership: connecting project work to personally meaningful questions [Autonomy over learning goals supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Metacognition: evaluating what one knows and what one needs to find out

### Instructions
1. **Launch with an entry event or phenomenon** — a provocative scenario, artifact, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) opening that creates authentic curiosity before any question generation.
2. **Run a question-storming round** — students produce as many questions as possible without evaluation, individually or in groups, using a protocol such as the Question Formulation Technique.
3. **Teach question criteria explicitly** — distinguish open vs. closed, investigable vs. speculative, and model upgrading vague questions into researchable ones ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. **Prioritize and select** — groups converge on driving and sub-questions, checking them against project constraints and learning goals.
5. **Plan and conduct inquiry** — students pursue their questions through research, data collection, or [Application](../elements/application.md) in authentic contexts.
6. **Revisit and revise questions** — build in structured checkpoints ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)) where questions are refined as understanding grows.
7. **Close the loop** — students answer their original questions in a public product and reflect on how their questions changed.

## Related Strategies
- **Driving-question design (teacher-posed)** — the complementary approach; a hybrid gives the teacher the frame and students the sub-questions
- **Case-based learning** — cases supply the rich context from which student questions emerge ([Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md))
- **Collaborative inquiry** — group question generation and investigation extend this strategy ([Collaborative Inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md))

## Patterns That Use This Strategy

- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — question generation plays the role of articulation and exploration phases

## Examples
- **[Right Question Institute — Question Formulation Technique](https://rightquestion.org)** — a structured protocol (produce, improve, prioritize) used in K-12 and higher education to make student question generation a routine instructional event.
- **[PBLWorks (Buck Institute)](https://www.pblworks.org)** — gold-standard PBL project designs in which students develop sub-questions under a project frame, with question revision built into project milestones.
- **Science inquiry classrooms** — students observe a phenomenon (e.g., a discrepant event), generate "I wonder" questions, then select one to investigate experimentally, with the teacher coaching investigability rather than supplying the question.
