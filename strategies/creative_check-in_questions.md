# Creative Check-In Questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A creative check-in question is a brief, low-stakes prompt posed at the start of a session — e.g., "What's a sound you love?" or "If your week were a weather forecast, what's the forecast?" — that learners answer in one or two sentences aloud, in chat, or on paper. Unlike routine attendance-taking or "any questions from last time?", the question is deliberately novel and personal-but-not-invasive, serving to surface affect, build relational trust, and mark a transition into learning. It is a specific, playful variant of [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md).

## Design Implications

Creative check-ins leverage the fact that emotion and cognition are inseparable in learning: affect shapes attention, memory, and engagement, so briefly attending to learners' emotional state before instruction supports readiness [Emotions are integral to learning and decision-making, not separable from cognition.](https://example.org) [+M]. Novel, non-evaluative questions also lower social risk, which supports participation and instructor–student relationship quality — a moderate predictor of engagement and achievement across meta-analytic work on teacher–student relationships [+S]. Because the activity is brief and voluntary-feeling, it can support a sense of autonomy and belonging that feeds intrinsic motivation [Autonomy-supportive environments strengthen intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely open question with no correct answer and no evaluation attached
- A predictable, time-boxed routine (2–5 minutes) so it signals transition rather than consuming the session
- A response channel that suits the group: round-robin aloud, chat waterfall, sticky notes, or private written response
- Instructor participation — answering the question first models vulnerability and equalizes the exchange

#### Constraints
- Repeated novelty questions can feel forced or performative; if learners sense the question is a compliance ritual, benefits collapse [~M]
- Questions that probe private emotional territory can expose reluctant sharers in front of peers; without an opt-out channel (private writing, "pass"), the practice can raise social threat rather than reduce it [-M]
- Overly long or elaborate check-ins displace instructional time and can read as filler, eroding [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) [-W]
- In very large or fully asynchronous settings, spoken round-robin formats become impractical; the strategy must be re-mediated (polls, discussion-board threads) or its relational benefits largely disappear [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Rotating student question-writers** — learners take turns supplying the question, deepening ownership and autonomy support
- **Themed check-ins** — questions tied to course content ("Which historical figure would you invite to dinner?") bridge into the lesson's activation of prior knowledge
- **Silent/written variants** — index cards or digital polls preserve the reflective function for introverted or anxious learners
- **Emotion-gradient scales** — "one word for how you're arriving today" trades creativity for speed and usable affect data for the instructor

### Target Learners
- K–12 and adult learners alike; the format scales across ages when question register is adjusted [+M]
- Learners in new cohorts or online courses where relational trust is thin — the routine builds familiarity quickly [+W]
- Anxious or socially cautious learners, *only if* a low-exposure response channel is offered; forced public sharing can backfire for this group [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social–emotional goals: emotional awareness, self-regulation, and community building
- Metacognitive goals: brief self-reflection that primes learners to monitor their own state [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Instructional readiness: surfacing affect that would otherwise compete for attention during content delivery

### Instructions
1. Choose a question that is novel, answerable in under 30 seconds, and free of evaluation — avoid anything requiring disclosure of private circumstances.
2. Answer it yourself first, briefly and honestly, to model the expected depth and risk level.
3. Collect responses through an appropriate channel: round-robin, chat, poll, or private writing with a "pass" option.
4. Acknowledge responses without grading or correcting — a brief appreciative summary is enough.
5. Transition explicitly into the lesson, optionally linking the question to the day's topic via [Activation](../principles/activation.md).
6. Vary question type across the term (whimsical, reflective, content-linked) to prevent ritualization.

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom (Center for Responsive Schools)** — the "Morning Meeting" greeting and sharing rounds institutionalize a daily creative check-in in elementary classrooms; teachers rotate question formats to keep novelty. [https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)
- **Zoom/chat waterfalls in online higher-ed courses** — the instructor posts "Type your answer, don't hit enter until I say go," and all responses appear at once, combining novelty with low individual exposure.
- **Advisory programs in middle schools** — weekly advisory periods open with prompts like "What's something small that went right this week?" to build advisor–advisee trust before academic goal-setting.
