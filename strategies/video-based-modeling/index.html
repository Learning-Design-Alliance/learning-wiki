Video Based Modeling¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 7 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q3–q4 · 0 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Video based modeling presents learners with recorded demonstrations of expert performance — of a psychomotor skill, a procedure, a problem-solving sequence, or a social interaction — so they can observe, replay, and study the target behavior before practicing it. Unlike live demonstration, video allows pausing, slow motion, repeated viewing, and multiple camera angles, and can be delivered at scale. Effectiveness depends on making the reasoning behind the actions visible, not just the actions themselves.

Design Implications¶

Video modeling draws on observational learning research: learners extract a symbolic representation of the modeled behavior and later reproduce it Bandura, A. (1977) [+M]. It works best when the model is competent, the narration makes expert decisions explicit, and viewing is followed by immediate practice Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S]. Video's replayability is its distinctive advantage over live demonstration, particularly for complex motor skills where a single observation is insufficient.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A high-quality recording of accurate expert performance, filmed from a learner-relevant viewpoint
  • Narration or annotation that verbalizes decision-making (Think-Aloud), not just visible actions
  • Segmenting into short, focused clips aligned to skill components (Chunking)
  • A structured follow-on activity requiring learners to attempt the skill (Practice)

Constraints¶

  • Extraneous video content — irrelevant visuals, background music, decorative motion — impairs learning Irrelevant material hurts learning. [-S]
  • Uninterrupted long videos overload working memory; learners who passively binge-watch without practice overestimate their competence Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [-S]
  • Less effective for open-ended tasks with no single correct performance to model
  • Learners with strong prior knowledge gain little and may find detailed modeling redundant Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Expert model vs. learning model: novice models who struggle and self-correct can outperform expert models for building learner self-efficacy and error detection [~M]
  • Point-model viewing: learners watch themselves on video performing the skill, used extensively in teacher education and clinical skills training
  • Interactive video: embedded questions, branching, or on-demand hints convert passive viewing into active processing
  • Static vs. dynamic display: for concepts better shown as a single diagram, animation or video can be inferior to a still image learners can study at their own pace [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Novices acquiring a psychomotor or procedural skill (surgery, sports, lab technique, classroom management) Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Learners who benefit from repeated, self-paced observation — replaying a clip is impossible with a live model
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners, who should move to independent performance Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural and psychomotor skill acquisition
  • Behavioral and interpersonal skills (e.g., video modeling in special education for social skills)
  • Professional vision: learning to notice and interpret expert-relevant features of practice

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the target skill and decompose it into observable components (Chunking).
  2. Record or select an expert demonstration with clear sightlines to critical actions.
  3. Add narration that verbalizes decisions and self-monitoring (Think-Aloud); strip out all decorative content Irrelevant material hurts learning. [-S].
  4. Segment the video into short clips; prompt learners to predict or summarize before each segment plays.
  5. Follow viewing immediately with Practice and feedback; fade modeling as competence grows (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the narration method that makes video modeling more than silent observation
  • Use Worked Examples — video modeling is the dynamic, often psychomotor counterpart of the worked example
  • Video Based Reflection — uses video of the learner's own performance for analysis rather than an expert model

Examples¶

  • JoVE — peer-reviewed video journal demonstrating laboratory methods in science; each protocol video models technique step by step before students attempt it at the bench.
  • TeachLivE / Mursion — teacher education programs pair video models of effective classroom management with simulated practice.
  • Video modeling in autism intervention — a well-established evidence-based practice in which learners with autism watch recordings of target social or self-help behaviors, then imitate them.
  • Khan Academy — narrated problem-solving videos functioning as video modeling for procedural skills, followed by practice exercises.
