# Build simulation airway curricula around environmental fidelity and deliberate practice, with formal teaching of nontechnical crisis management skills

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 against) · 1 study (1 theoretical), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article prescribes a curriculum-design recipe: conform to clinical practice with "typicality, standardization, and universal guidance following the difficult airway management algorithm", use a six-step curriculum design approach, and prioritize the two factors shown to matter most for crisis management simulation training. It stresses that nontechnical skills are not inherent and "must be formally taught even though they are increasingly rare in modern day medicine", and that environmental fidelity, incorporating all structural and mechanized elements present in the clinical realm, is particularly important for learning nontechnical skills such as communication and role identification.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Curriculum content and scenarios designed collaboratively by simulation instructors and airway management specialists
- Time set aside for time-consuming scenario programming
- Incorporation of structural and mechanized elements present in real clinical scenarios to raise environmental fidelity
#### Constraints
- High-fidelity simulation training is expensive
- Curricula for nontechnical skills are more difficult to teach and assess and probably require more development time than technical-skill curricula

### Target Learners
- medical students
- residents and attending physicians in anesthesiology, emergency, or intensive care

### Target Learning Goals
- management of difficult airway emergencies and CICV crisis situations
- nontechnical crisis resource management and communication skills
- technical airway management skills
