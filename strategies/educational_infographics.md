# Educational Infographics

## Description
Educational infographics are visual aids that simplify complex information into easily digestible formats using a combination of charts, icons, and text. They summarize topics, visualize statistics, explain processes, and present timelines in a single coherent artifact. Effective infographics use color and layout strategically — not decoratively — to highlight relationships and patterns within the data.

## Design Implications

Infographics exploit the capacity of the visual channel to carry information in parallel with verbal processing, consistent with dual coding of verbal and pictorial information [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. Their value depends on disciplined design: text and graphics must be integrated and mutually explanatory, not merely adjacent, and extraneous decoration should be stripped because it competes for limited working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Color and emphasis direct attention, so highlighting should mark what is actually important rather than what looks appealing [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear communicative goal: one infographic should answer one question, not catalog everything known about a topic
- Careful selection of visuals and concise, plain-language text ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Visual hierarchy that guides the eye through the intended reading order ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
- Integration into a lesson with accompanying activity — an infographic studied in isolation rarely produces durable learning

#### Constraints
- Overly dense infographics overload working memory and depress comprehension; adding more visual elements often reduces learning rather than enhancing it [~S]
- Decorative but irrelevant graphics (seductive details) divert attention from core content and harm retention [-S]
- Effectiveness drops for learners with high prior knowledge, for whom heavily pre-structured visuals can be redundant [~M]
- Data visualizations can mislead when axes are truncated or proportions distorted; learners may absorb the distortion as fact [-M]
- High-quality production requires time and design skill; poor execution undermines credibility of the content itself

#### Implementation Variability
- Static posters or handouts for reference and revision
- Annotated infographics where learners add labels or commentary ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Learner-authored infographics as an assessment or synthesis task, forcing selection and organization of content
- Interactive or animated infographics in digital environments, which can reveal process steps sequentially

### Target Learners
- Novices encountering an unfamiliar topic, who benefit from pre-structured visual overviews [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Learners with limited working memory capacity or reading difficulty, for whom visual chunking reduces load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for experts, who may find the simplification imprecise or unnecessary [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual overview: building an initial mental model of a topic's structure
- Data literacy: interpreting statistics, trends, and comparisons
- Retention of key facts and relationships through dual coding
- Synthesis and communication (when learners design their own infographics)

### Instructions
1. Define the single message or question the infographic must answer; discard everything else.
2. Draft the core visual (chart, timeline, process diagram) before writing any text.
3. Add minimal, integrated labels and captions; place text next to the graphic it explains ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md)).
4. Use color and emphasis only to mark relationships and key findings [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M].
5. Pair the infographic with an activity — prediction, [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), or learner annotation — rather than passive viewing [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].
6. Optionally, have learners create their own infographic to demonstrate synthesis ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) in reverse: learners produce the model).

## Examples
- **[Information is Beautiful](https://informationisbeautiful.net)** — David McCandless's data-journalism infographics; widely used in media literacy and statistics teaching to discuss both insight and visual distortion.
- **[Venngage](https://venngage.com)** and **[Canva](https://www.canva.com)** — template-based design platforms commonly used for learner-authored infographics in classroom and corporate training settings.
- **[Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org)** — research-backed interactive charts and infographics on global development, used in secondary and university curricula.
- Science classrooms using infographics of the water cycle or photosynthesis as pre-reading organizers, followed by [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) prompts.
