# Problem triples: qualitative, symbolic, and computational versions of one situation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (4 for) · 2 studies (2 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
A problem triple presents one situation in three linked versions: a qualitative problem tied to symbolic quantities to build the blend and call on fundamental principles, a symbolic problem showing how equations support qualitative reasoning, and a computational problem calculating forces from given numbers. The article notes the computational version "is typically the only one students are given in traditional classes", which assumes students can build the story without guidance.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- The qualitative version must be tied to symbolic quantities so students apply laws such as Newton's 1st and 2nd laws to conceptually blended variables and tell the story of each object.
#### Constraints
- The article presents this as a technique the author has found helpful in his own teaching, not as a tested intervention with outcome data.

### Target Learners
- introductory physics students (undergraduate)

### Target Learning Goals
- building a mathematized story tied to blended symbols
- distinguishing measurable forces from forces inferred via Newton's laws

## Claims

- [Introductory physics students score significantly higher on numeric versions of physics problems than on analogous symbolic versions](../claims/numeric-problems-score-higher-than-symbolic.md) [+W]
- [Numeric exam versions scored 85-95% on average while symbolic versions scored 45-60% in an earlier large-population study](../claims/earlier-exam-numeric-85-95-symbolic-45-60.md) [+W]
- [Students who solved a symbolic problem incorrectly using conflicting meanings of the same symbol t solved the numeric version correctly, with plugging in numbers acting as a cue to specify symbol meaning](../claims/plugging-in-numbers-cues-symbol-meaning.md) [+W]
- [In speak-aloud interviews, introductory physics students who could not solve symbolic problems often confused known and unknown symbols in the symbolic versions but not the numeric versions](../claims/interviews-known-unknown-confusion-symbolic.md) [+W]
