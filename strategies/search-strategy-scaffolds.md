# Search Strategy Scaffolds

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Search strategy scaffolds are explicit supports — query templates, source-evaluation checklists, staged search plans, or modeled searches — that structure how learners locate information. Rather than assuming students already know how to find and judge information, the strategy makes the search process itself an instructional target: learners are taught to decompose an information need, generate and refine queries, scan results strategically, and evaluate sources before using them.

## Design Implications

Unscaffolded search is dominated by keyword matching and surface cues; learners click the first plausible result and rarely revise [~M]. Scaffolds work because they externalize the expert's information-problem-solving cycle — defining the problem, planning, evaluating, monitoring — which novices otherwise skip [Brand-Gruwel et al. found students rarely regulate their search process spontaneously.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2004.10.003) [+M]. Effective scaffolds pair process structure with evaluation criteria, since locating information is worthless if learners cannot judge its credibility; explicit instruction in evaluating sources measurably improves the quality of information students select [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine information need or inquiry question that motivates the search ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md) provides such contexts)
- Explicit evaluation criteria (authority, evidence, currency, corroboration) taught before or during searching
- A model of expert searching — a [Think-Aloud](../strategies/think-aloud-modeling.md) demonstration of query formulation and result triage
- Opportunities to revise queries and compare sources, not a single one-shot search

#### Constraints
- Scaffolds that prescribe a rigid sequence can prevent learners from developing adaptive search behavior; overly prescriptive checklists produce compliance without transfer [-M]
- Scaffolds lose effectiveness when learners already possess strong domain and search expertise — the guidance becomes redundant and slows expert searchers [~M]
- Query templates tied to one database or interface transfer poorly to new tools unless the underlying decision rules are made explicit [-W]
- Evaluation checklists alone (e.g., "check the author's credentials") are less effective than teaching lateral reading — leaving a page to check what others say about it — because hostile or deceptive sources can satisfy checklist criteria [X for checklist-only approaches with deceptive sources]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Query scaffolds**: sentence frames ("How does ___ affect ___?"), question decomposition charts, keyword brainstorming grids
- **Evaluation scaffolds**: lateral-reading routines, source-comparison tables, credibility criteria cards
- **Process scaffolds**: staged search plans (explore → focus → verify), search logs with reflection prompts
- **Embedded scaffolds**: software prompts and hints within inquiry environments that adapt to the learner's current search phase

### Target Learners
- Novices with little prior knowledge of the topic, who cannot judge relevance or credibility from content alone and default to surface cues [~M]
- K-12 and early undergraduate students, who show large gains from explicit online-reasoning instruction [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Less beneficial for domain experts, who already possess the schemas needed to evaluate results efficiently [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: locating, evaluating, and selecting sources
- Inquiry skills: decomposing a question into searchable sub-questions
- Critical evaluation: judging source credibility and corroborating claims across sources
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own search process

### Instructions
1. Present an authentic information need or inquiry question, ideally embedded in a problem context ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md)).
2. Model an expert search with a think-aloud: formulating the need, generating keywords, scanning results, rejecting weak sources aloud ([Think-Aloud Modeling](../strategies/think-aloud-modeling.md)).
3. Provide a query scaffold — question decomposition or sentence frames — and have learners draft their own search plan.
4. Teach evaluation criteria explicitly, emphasizing lateral reading and corroboration across independent sources ([Civic Online Reasoning](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md)).
5. Have learners execute searches, log decisions, and compare selected sources with peers ([Comparing Cases](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md)).
6. Fade the scaffolds: move from full search plans to prompts to independent searching as learners show competent self-regulation.

- **Search logs in inquiry units**: students record query versions, rejected results, and rejection reasons, making the search process an object of reflection and assessment
- **Question decomposition charts in library instruction**: librarians scaffold a research question into sub-questions and keyword sets before students touch a database
