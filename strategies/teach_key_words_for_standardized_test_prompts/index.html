Teach Key Words for Standardized Test Prompts¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 15 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 5 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 5 of 15 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy involves identifying the small set of high-frequency terms that recur in standardized test prompts — verbs like analyze, compare, justify, trace, and evaluate, plus directional words like except, most likely, and primarily — and teaching them explicitly before test practice. Instruction pairs each term with a definition, a worked prompt example, and student-generated responses, so learners can rapidly classify what a prompt demands instead of losing time or misreading intent during the test itself.

Design Implications¶

Explicit vocabulary instruction has one of the most consistent evidence bases in education: direct teaching of targeted words improves comprehension of texts containing them Stahl & Fairbanks' meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction. [+S]. For test prompts specifically, the payoff is in reducing misread questions — a substantial share of errors on constructed-response items stem from answering a different question than the one asked. Teaching command terms functions as a form of Cognitive Load Management: under timed conditions, learners who must simultaneously decode unfamiliar directive language and compose an answer face extraneous load that fluent term recognition removes Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. The strategy works best when terms are taught through contrasting prompt examples rather than definitions alone, so learners discriminate compare from contrast or describe from explain Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A corpus of released or representative prompts from the target test, mined for recurring terms
  • A concise, learner-friendly definition and cue for each term (ideally 10 or fewer terms per cycle)
  • Worked examples showing a strong response to a prompt using each term, followed by Practice on new prompts
  • Spaced review so terms stay retrievable at test time Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Teaching test vocabulary without teaching the underlying content produces gains on prompt comprehension only — it does not raise domain knowledge and can create an illusion of readiness [-M]
  • Effects are test-specific: terms from one assessment framework do not transfer automatically to differently worded tests [~M]
  • Overdrilling terms as isolated definitions leads to fragile knowledge; learners may recite a definition yet still misclassify a novel prompt [-W]
  • For learners already fluent with academic command language, the instruction is redundant and consumes instructional time [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Word walls or anchor charts that persist across the unit, with terms added as they appear in practice prompts
  • Term-of-the-week routines with student-written practice prompts
  • Sorting activities in which students classify prompts by demanded response type before answering
  • Student-generated glossaries with self-explanation of why a given term fits a given prompt Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • English language learners and students with limited exposure to academic register, for whom command verbs are a genuine barrier [+M]
  • Middle and secondary students encountering extended-response and document-based prompts for the first time
  • Less valuable for students already fluent in academic language, who benefit more from content review

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Test-wiseness: accurate parsing of task demands under timed conditions
  • Academic vocabulary: durable understanding of high-utility directive terms
  • Metacognitive planning: mapping a prompt to a response structure before writing

Instructions¶

  1. Collect released prompts from the target assessment and tally recurring terms; prioritize the highest-frequency 8–12.
  2. Introduce each term through Direct Instruction: definition, a student-friendly paraphrase, and a worked prompt with a model response.
  3. Present contrasting prompts side by side (e.g., describe vs. explain) and have students classify the demanded response before answering Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M].
  4. Have students underline or annotate the key term in each practice prompt and state aloud what the response must do — an Advance Organizer for their answer.
  5. Follow with timed Practice on full prompts, then Assessment via practice tests, giving feedback on whether the response matched the prompt's demand Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  6. Space review of terms across weeks rather than massing them before the test Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Accessible Syntax — the broader language-support approach of which prompt-term teaching is a targeted instance
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — connecting new command terms to familiar everyday verbs (e.g., trace → "show the path")

Examples¶

  • Kechia Williams' "10 Words" routine — a middle-school teacher teaches ten high-frequency prompt terms (e.g., analyze, support, compare) with student-friendly definitions and practice prompts, reporting improved constructed-response scores on state tests.
  • SAT/ACT prep courses (e.g., Khan Academy's official SAT practice, https://www.khanacademy.org/sat) — highlight directive language in each practice question and explain what the question asks before students answer.
  • IB and AP rubric language — both programs publish official command-term glossaries (e.g., the IB's "Command Terms" list, https://www.ibo.org); teachers who drill these terms align instruction directly with how responses are scored.
