Communicating High Expectations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Communicating high expectations means explicitly telling learners that they are capable of succeeding at challenging work, holding them accountable to ambitious standards, and framing difficulty as normal and surmountable. It is carried out through direct verbal messages ("this is hard, and you can do it"), through assignment and feedback practices that treat all learners as capable of rigorous work, and through the instructor's own behavior — persistence, warmth, and refusal to lower standards. Crucially, high expectations are only effective when paired with high support; expectations without scaffolding read as indifference or threat.

Design Implications¶

Expectation communication works as a "wise intervention": it changes how learners interpret difficulty and belonging, which in turn changes persistence and achievement Social-psychological interventions produce lasting achievement gains when they target learners' interpretations of the environment. [+M]. Teacher expectancy effects are real but modest; the mechanism is largely behavioral — teachers who expect more give more opportunities to respond, more feedback, and more challenging tasks [~M]. The message must be credible: learners discount high expectations that are not accompanied by concrete support such as Scaffolding and actionable feedback.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Genuinely challenging tasks — the message must be true (Cognitive Disequilibrium requires work at the edge of competence)
  • Visible support structures (Scaffolding, Feedback, Practice) that make success plausible
  • Framing of difficulty as a normal part of learning, not a signal of low ability
  • Consistency across channels: syllabus, assignment language, feedback tone, and grading standards must all signal the same belief

Constraints¶

  • Unearned or unsupported praise reduces motivation and signals low expectations [~S] — learners with low prior achievement interpret inflated praise as evidence the teacher doubts them
  • Expectancy effects are weaker for older learners and in settings where the teacher has little instructional contact time [~M]
  • If expectations are high but support is absent, learners attribute failure to fixed ability and disengage [-M]
  • Messages that single out groups ("students like you usually struggle") can activate stereotype threat rather than confidence [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Assignment framing: "wise feedback" — "I'm giving you these comments because I have high standards and I know you can meet them" — increases revision rates, especially among students of color [~M]
  • Normative framing: presenting struggle as universal ("everyone finds this step hard at first") rather than individual
  • Structural communication: rigorous grading rubrics and refusal to accept minimal work communicate expectations without any motivational speech

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with low prior achievement or low self-efficacy, who are most sensitive to signals about whether effort will pay off [~M]
  • Students at risk of questioning their belonging in a field (first-generation, underrepresented students); belonging-focused messages improve outcomes for these groups Social-psychological interventions produce lasting achievement gains when they target learners' interpretations of the environment. [+M]
  • Less effective when applied indiscriminately to already high-achieving learners, who receive the message as redundant

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Persistence and engagement on challenging tasks
  • Self-efficacy and academic identity development
  • Dispositional goals: willingness to attempt difficult work, revise, and seek help

Instructions¶

  1. Set a genuinely ambitious standard for the task and verify it is reachable with support (Scaffolding).
  2. State the expectation explicitly and pair it with an expression of confidence in the learner's capacity — the "high standards + high belief" pairing.
  3. Normalize difficulty: tell learners where the work will get hard and that this is expected.
  4. Provide the support that makes the expectation credible: Feedback that is actionable, Demonstration of what quality looks like, and opportunities to revise.
  5. Hold the line in assessment — do not quietly lower standards; use Assessment for Learning so learners can see progress toward the standard.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — feedback that specifies how to improve makes high expectations actionable rather than merely stated
  • Belonging Interventions — expectation messages work through the same interpretive channel as belonging messages

Examples¶

  • Wise feedback (Cohen et al., 1999; Yeager et al., 2014): teachers attach a note to graded work — "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know you can reach them" — reliably increasing revision and improvement among low-performing students.
  • EL Education (https://eleducation.org) — school network whose design principles include "high expectations for all students," operationalized through public presentation of rigorous work and revision cycles ("authentic work of exceptional quality").
  • AVID (Advancement Via Individual Determination, https://www.avid.org) — college-readiness program that places underperforming students in rigorous coursework with explicit messaging that they are "college material," plus tutorials and organizational support.
