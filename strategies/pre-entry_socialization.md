# Pre-Entry Socialization

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Pre-entry socialization involves engaging students in brief, positive interactions before they enter the classroom — typically at the door — to set a productive tone. Behavior management expert Rob Plevin recommends "nonconfrontational statements" and "informal chitchat" to socialize students into productive behaviors before instruction begins. The practice is proactive rather than reactive: it addresses potential disruptions before they occur by establishing rapport and signaling teacher presence and expectations.

## Design Implications

Positive, personal teacher–student interactions at transitions improve engagement and reduce disruptive behavior; structured positive greetings at the door raised academic engaged time and reduced disruptions in middle school classrooms [Positive greetings at the door increased engaged time and reduced disruptions.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The mechanism is partly relational — students feel seen and known — and partly behavioral, since a calm, personal exchange interrupts the momentum of whatever happened in the hallway. Consistency matters: the practice works as a routine, not an occasional gesture [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teacher physically present at the doorway for every entry, with a consistent greeting routine
- Knowledge of individual students (names, interests, recent events) to make chitchat genuine rather than scripted
- Nonconfrontational framing: corrections of prior behavior are deferred or delivered privately, not at the threshold
- A short time budget — interactions must take seconds, not minutes, or the transition stalls

#### Constraints
- Depends on teacher–student rapport; with no existing relationship, doorway chitchat can feel forced or intrusive [-W]
- Less effective when delivered inconsistently or only after incidents, which students read as punitive surveillance [~M]
- Large class sizes and tight schedules compress the interaction to a perfunctory greeting, weakening the effect [-W]
- Students in active conflict with the teacher may experience the doorway exchange as a confrontation trigger; those cases need a different, quieter approach

#### Implementation Variability
- **Positive greeting + name**: brief personalized greeting with eye contact and use of the student's name (the version tested in controlled research)
- **Interest-based chitchat**: short exchange referencing the student's interests, weekend, or prior work
- **Pre-correction**: a calm, private reminder of an expectation delivered before entry ("Remember — straight to your seat today")
- **Nonverbal variant**: a handshake, fist bump, or nod for students who prefer minimal talk

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students, for whom hallway-to-classroom transitions carry the highest disruption risk [Positive greetings at the door increased engaged time and reduced disruptions.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students with weak sense of belonging, for whom being personally greeted signals membership in the class community
- Students with a history of conflict or late arrival, who benefit most from a fresh, nonconfrontational start to each lesson

### Target Learning Goals
- Classroom climate: establishing a positive, predictable atmosphere
- Behavior management: reducing disruption frequency and severity proactively
- Relationship-building: developing the teacher–student rapport that supports later correction and coaching

### Instructions
1. Position yourself at the door before students arrive; greet each student by name with a brief positive statement or [check-in](../elements/check-in.md).
2. Keep exchanges nonconfrontational — defer any correction of prior behavior to a private moment later in the lesson ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) conversation rather than public reprimand).
3. Vary content using what you know of the student (interests, recent work, events) so the interaction reads as genuine attention, not a script.
4. Hold the routine for every entry, every class; consistency converts the greeting from an event into a predictable social contract.
5. Monitor disruption frequency and engaged time at the start of lessons to evaluate whether the routine is paying off.
