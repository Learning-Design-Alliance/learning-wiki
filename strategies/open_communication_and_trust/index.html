Open Communication and Trust¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Open Communication and Trust is a strategy in which the teacher proactively establishes friendly, accessible channels of communication with students and their families, grounded in mutual respect rather than contact only around problems. When helping students succeed becomes a group effort among teacher, learner, and home, both academic performance and attitudes toward school improve.

Design Implications¶

Relational trust between teachers, students, and families is a strong correlate of school improvement and student engagement, and it functions as a precondition for other instructional strategies to take hold Cornelius-White's meta-analysis found learner-centered relational variables (empathy, warmth, non-directivity) associated with increased participation, motivation, and achievement. [+M]. The mechanism is partly motivational: students who feel known and safe are more willing to take intellectual risks, ask questions, and persist Self-efficacy beliefs predict academic persistence. [+M]. Communication must be bidirectional — outreach that invites response, not one-way broadcasting — and framed around growth rather than deficit.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Easy and convenient means to communicate with students and families (messaging apps, email, phone, translated materials where needed)
  • A positive first contact early in the term, before any problem arises
  • Consistent, brief, strengths-based updates rather than crisis-only contact
  • Follow-through: trust erodes quickly if commitments made in conversation are not honored

Constraints¶

  • One-way teacher-to-home messaging produces weak effects; families must be positioned as respondents and partners, and low response rates can stall the strategy entirely [~M]
  • Communication framed only around misbehavior or failure conditions families to avoid contact and undermines trust [-M]
  • In large classes or under-resourced settings, individualized outreach is costly to sustain; effects attenuate when frequency drops [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Positive phone/text campaigns: brief individualized messages home about progress (e.g., the Kraft & Dougherty texting studies)
  • Student–teacher check-ins: brief structured one-to-one conversations using Check-Ins
  • Family conferences and home visits: deeper relational investment, common in programs such as the Flamboyan Foundation's family engagement model
  • Digital channels: LMS announcements, ClassDojo, Remind, or TalkingPoints (which supports translation) for scalable two-way messaging

Target Learners¶

  • Students at risk of disengagement, for whom a trusted adult relationship is often the pivot point for persistence Self-efficacy beliefs predict academic persistence. [+M]
  • Adolescents, who are especially sensitive to whether teachers treat them with respect and offer autonomy rather than control Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • Families unfamiliar with school norms, for whom accessible, translated, jargon-free communication lowers participation barriers

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and relational goals: belonging, trust, willingness to seek help
  • Behavioral goals: engagement, attendance, homework completion
  • Metacognitive goals: students learning to articulate their own progress and needs through regular dialogue

Instructions¶

  1. Open the term with a friendly introductory message to each family stating goals, communication channels, and an invitation to respond.
  2. Establish routine Check-Ins with students — brief, predictable, low-stakes conversations about progress and obstacles.
  3. Deliver Feedback framed at the task and process level, not the person, so communication builds competence rather than defensiveness Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  4. Send periodic positive updates home, weighted toward strengths and specific evidence of growth.
  5. When concerns arise, contact families early and frame the conversation as a shared problem-solving effort, then follow through on agreed actions.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-Ins — the student-facing counterpart; structured brief conversations that operationalize trust day to day
  • Building Empathy — the teacher disposition that makes communication feel genuine rather than procedural
  • Community of Inquiry — extends trust into the social presence needed for productive online and classroom discourse
  • Offering Multiple Options for Family Engagement
  • Align teachers, families, and staff around a shared vision to build trust and momentum

Examples¶

  • Positive texting campaigns — In randomized studies, teachers sending brief individualized texts to families about student progress improved homework completion and engagement (Kraft & Dougherty, 2013).
  • ClassDojo — Two-way messaging, class stories, and translated communication connecting teachers and families.
  • TalkingPoints — Translation-enabled family messaging designed for multilingual school communities.
  • Flamboyan Foundation family engagement — Teacher home visits and relationship-building conferences implemented across Washington, DC schools.
