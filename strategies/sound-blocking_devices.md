# Sound-blocking Devices

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Sound-blocking devices — noise-reducing headphones, earmuffs, or earplugs — reduce the intensity of ambient auditory input, helping learners with auditory sensitivities regulate sensory load in noisy environments. They are used across settings (classrooms, cafeterias, assemblies, public transit) to lower decibel exposure and free attentional resources for the task at hand. They function as an environmental [accommodation](../elements/accommodations.md) rather than an instructional method: they change the learning context, not the pedagogy.

## Design Implications

Background noise measurably degrades children's speech comprehension, attention, and academic performance, with the largest effects on younger children and those with special educational needs [Classroom noise impairs children's speech perception and comprehension.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Reducing auditory load is therefore a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): it removes competing input so working memory can be allocated to instruction. However, devices are a support, not a substitute for instruction in coping skills — they should be paired with explicit teaching about when and how to use them, and with gradual exposure where appropriate.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Appropriately fitted noise-reducing headphones, earmuffs, or earplugs; passive earmuffs (e.g., NRR 22–31 dB) differ from active noise-cancelling headphones, which work best on low-frequency hum and less well on speech
- A pre-taught routine for requesting and using the device, so it does not require adult mediation each time ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines can support this)
- Agreement about *when* device use is appropriate — full removal of instruction audio during direct teaching defeats the purpose; use during independent work, transitions, or high-noise settings (cafeteria, assemblies) is typical

#### Constraints
- Devices block the very signal learners need during teacher-led instruction and peer discussion [-M] — overuse during interactive segments can reduce participation and social learning
- Some children find headphones uncomfortable, over-stimulating in themselves, or socially stigmatizing, and will resist wearing them [~W]
- Evidence for sensory-based equipment as an intervention is weak; most support comes from studies of noise's harmful effects rather than trials of the devices themselves [~W] — expected benefits should be monitored rather than assumed
- Complete auditory blocking can increase startle responses when the device is removed or when unexpected touch/vibration occurs [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Passive earmuffs vs. active noise cancellation:** passive attenuation is more predictable and cheaper; ANC suits low-frequency environments (buses, HVAC hum)
- **Partial attenuation:** some learners need noise *reduced*, not eliminated — lower-NRR devices or one-ear use preserve awareness of instructions
- **Fading:** planned gradual reduction of device use, paired with self-regulation instruction, prevents long-term dependence
- **Learner control:** allowing the learner to decide when to use the device builds self-advocacy and self-regulation rather than compliance

### Target Learners
- Children with autism spectrum conditions and documented auditory sensitivities [~W]
- Learners with auditory processing difficulties, for whom noise disproportionately degrades speech comprehension [+M]
- Learners with ADHD or anxiety who are distractible in noisy settings [~M]
- Generally *not* needed for learners without sensitivity; unnecessary attenuation can isolate them from instructional audio [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention and focus during independent work in noisy environments
- Self-regulation: recognizing overstimulation and selecting a coping tool
- Participation: enabling presence and endurance in settings (cafeteria, assemblies) the learner would otherwise avoid

### Instructions
1. **Identify the trigger contexts.** Observe or interview the learner to determine which environments cause overstimulation; do not apply the device globally.
2. **Select and fit the device.** Trial passive vs. active options with the learner; comfort and acceptable attenuation level matter more than maximum noise reduction.
3. **Pre-teach the routine.** Explicitly teach when to use, request, and remove the device; rehearse it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)) before deploying it in the target setting.
4. **Pair with self-regulation instruction.** Teach the learner to notice early signs of overstimulation and choose the device as one option among several coping strategies.
5. **Monitor and adjust.** Track focus, participation, and distress across settings; fade or expand use based on observed behavior, and check that instructional audio is not being blocked during teacher-led segments.

## Examples
- A student with autism wears passive earmuffs (NRR ~27 dB) during cafeteria lunch and school assemblies, with a pre-taught routine to remove them when an adult signals instruction is starting.
- A learner with ADHD uses low-cost ANC headphones during independent reading block in an open-plan classroom, choosing to use them via a [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md) of regulation options.
- Speech-language therapy sessions for children with auditory processing disorder conducted in quiet, acoustically treated rooms — the environmental equivalent of the device.
