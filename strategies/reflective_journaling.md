# Reflective Journaling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reflective journaling is a writing technique in which students record their understanding, reactions, and analysis of an event, experience, or concept — encompassing thoughts and emotions, not just content. It encourages reflective learning by making students' perceptions visible, increases attention during class, and supports transformative learning by prompting critical reflection on beliefs and deliberate plans to revise them [Mezirow's transformative learning framework](https://doi.org/10.1002/ace.7401) [+W].

## Design Implications

Reflective journaling converts passive exposure into elaborative processing: writing about what was learned and why it matters forces retrieval, connection to prior knowledge, and metacognitive monitoring. Its effectiveness depends on prompt quality and feedback — unstructured "write about today" journals tend to produce summary rather than reflection [Dyment & O'Connell's review of journaling in higher education](https://doi.org/10.1080/0309877X.2010.540319) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A journal (physical or digital) and dedicated, recurring time for writing
- Focused prompts that push beyond summary toward analysis, self-questioning, and belief revision
- Instructor feedback or structured response, so students learn what reflection (vs. reporting) looks like
- A supportive environment where tentative or critical entries are safe to write

#### Constraints
- Journals that become summaries of course material or mere learning logs lose their reflective benefit [-W] — the focus must be reactions to and reasoning about material, not restatement
- Without feedback or dialogue, entries often plateau at description; reflection quality rarely improves unaided [~M]
- Grading journals for "correct" content suppresses the honest self-assessment the practice depends on [-W]
- Time-intensive for both students and instructors; feedback load grows with class size

#### Implementation Variability
- **Prompted vs. open journals** — structured prompts (e.g., "What did you previously believe that you now think is wrong?") produce deeper reflection than free writing, especially for novices
- **Dialogue journals** — instructor or peers respond to entries, creating a written conversation
- **Double-entry journals** — left column for source material or observations, right column for personal response and analysis
- **Digital platforms** — blogs or LMS journals (e.g., a university LMS journal tool, WordPress-based class blogs) enable peer visibility and instructor commenting at scale

### Target Learners
- Higher education students and adult learners in professional development, where experience is rich enough to reflect on [+W]
- Pre-service professionals (teaching, nursing, clinical practice) developing reflective-practitioner habits [~M]
- Less effective for learners with little relevant experience to draw on, who tend to produce summary rather than reflection

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition and self-regulated learning: monitoring one's own understanding and study strategies, consistent with [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+W]
- Transformative learning: critically examining and revising beliefs and assumptions
- Integration of theory and practice: connecting course concepts to lived or professional experience
- Critical thinking and perspective-taking

### Instructions
1. Set the purpose and norms — explain that entries should analyze and question, not summarize; establish that journals are low-stakes or private.
2. Provide a focused prompt tied to the session's content or experience (e.g., "What surprised you, and what prior belief did it challenge?").
3. Have students write for a fixed period (5–15 minutes), optionally using [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) on readings beforehand to seed the entry.
4. Ask students to articulate their reasoning explicitly — why they reacted as they did — building the habit of [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md).
5. Respond with brief feedback or pair entries with [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) so reflection feeds back into collective sense-making.
6. Periodically ask students to reread earlier entries and trace how their thinking changed, closing the reflective cycle.

## Examples
- **Prompt set for a course session:** What was the most interesting thing I read for this lesson? What were three main things I learned? What did I previously think was true but now know to be wrong? What did we not cover that I expected we should?
- **Teacher education programs** commonly require practicum journals in which student teachers analyze classroom events against theory, then discuss entries with a supervising instructor.
- **Clinical and nursing education** uses reflective journals after patient encounters to develop diagnostic reasoning and professional identity.
