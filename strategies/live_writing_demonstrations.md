# Live Writing Demonstrations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Instructors demonstrate the writing process in real-time, composing text in front of learners while verbalizing their thought process, revisions, and decisions. Using a projector or shared document, the instructor drafts, revises, and sometimes deliberately struggles — making the normally invisible cognitive work of writing observable. Learners observe, critique, and contribute input, turning writing from a private product into a transparent, collaborative process.

## Design Implications

Live writing demonstrations are a form of modeling with expert [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md): their value comes not from the text produced but from the visible reasoning, monitoring, and revision [~M]. Like worked examples, they reduce the search burden on novices by showing a complete path through a task before learners attempt it themselves [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load for novices.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. The demonstration must be followed by immediate writing practice; observation alone produces shallow encoding and illusions of competence [~S]. Inviting learners to critique and steer the instructor's draft converts passive watching into [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and prompts self-explanation of writing decisions [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A display tool (projector, shared document) and a pre-planned writing task matched to learners' current needs
- Instructor willingness and preparation to verbalize decisions, including uncertainty and revision ("I'm cutting this because…")
- A classroom norm that supports learner critique and input on the instructor's draft
- A follow-on activity in which learners immediately apply the demonstrated moves to their own writing

#### Constraints
- Learners who watch without an assigned role become passive observers; effectiveness drops sharply without structured participation or immediate practice [-M]
- Demonstrating a single approach can anchor learners to one formulaic structure; showing contrasting versions or inviting alternative openings reduces this risk [~M]
- Less beneficial for experienced writers, for whom explicit process modeling is redundant [Expertise-reversal-effect.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Requires real-time adaptability; instructors uncomfortable composing publicly may revert to presenting pre-written text, which loses the revision modeling that drives the effect [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interactive co-construction**: learners supply sentences, evidence, or revisions while the instructor edits live — highest engagement, highest preparation demand
- **Think-aloud drafting**: instructor composes solo while verbalizing decisions; learners observe then debrief
- **Deliberate error and repair**: instructor writes a flawed draft and revises it, normalizing struggle and modeling self-monitoring
- **Partial demonstration**: instructor models only the hardest move (e.g., a thesis revision) and fades support as learners take over subsequent sections

### Target Learners
- Novice writers encountering a new genre, structure, or revision strategy for the first time [~M]
- Secondary and higher-education students learning discipline-specific writing conventions
- Less beneficial for proficient writers, who gain more from independent practice with feedback [Expertise-reversal-effect.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural knowledge of the writing process: drafting, revising, prioritizing concision
- Metacognitive modeling: what expert monitoring, self-questioning, and repair look like during composition
- Demystifying writing: reducing defensive reactions to critique by showing the instructor's own struggles and revisions

### Instructions
1. Select a writing task matched to learners' current assignment and identify the specific moves to make visible.
2. Open with brief [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) on the target strategy, then begin composing live while verbalizing decisions ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
3. Pause at decision points and invite learner critique or input; revise in response so learners see feedback acted on [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
4. Deliberately model revision — reread, cut, restructure — rather than producing clean first-draft text.
5. Debrief the moves demonstrated, then have learners immediately apply them to their own drafts with the demonstration available as a reference.

## Examples
- A history teacher composes an introductory paragraph in front of the class, verbalizing choices about evidence and framing while students nominate a worthy historical question for the teacher to tackle and critique each revision.
- **[Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)** curricula embed sentence-level live modeling in which teachers compose and revise sentences with whole-class input before students write independently.
- A first-year composition instructor drafts and revises a thesis statement live, deliberately writing a vague version and cutting it, then asks students to diagnose the problem before revising together.
