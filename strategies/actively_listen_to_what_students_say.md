# Actively Listen To What Students Say

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Active listening in the classroom means giving a student's contribution full attention — attending to words, tone, and hesitation — and demonstrating that the contribution has been received before moving on. It is carried out through paraphrasing, probing follow-up questions, wait time, and visibly building instruction on what the student actually said rather than on the anticipated answer.

## Design Implications

Active listening converts student talk from performance into formative evidence: what students say reveals their current mental models, which is the raw material for responsive teaching [Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and process rather than the self.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Listening attentively also signals that student reasoning is valued, which supports participation and autonomy [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]. The practice is closely tied to [Responsive Teaching](../principles/responsive-teaching.md) and to contingent support — the teacher's next move should depend on what was heard [Scaffolding is most effective when contingent on learner understanding.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine wait time (3+ seconds) after asking a question, so students produce substantive talk worth listening to
- Paraphrasing or restating moves ("So you're saying the graph flattens because…") that verify interpretation
- A disposition to treat unexpected or wrong answers as data about student thinking, not noise to correct quickly
- Classroom norms that make student talk safe — see [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md)

#### Constraints
- Loses effectiveness when the teacher has already decided the next instructional step; "listening" that is actually waiting to confirm the expected answer teaches students that only correct answers matter [-M]
- Whole-class settings with many voices limit depth; sustained listening works better in [One-on-One Conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md) or small groups [~M]
- Can slow coverage of content; teachers under pacing pressure tend to truncate student contributions [-W]
- Cultural norms around turn-taking and eye contact vary; a single "attentive listening" script can misread students from different backgrounds [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Revoicing** (Michaels & O'Connor): the teacher repeats, attributes, and asks the student to confirm — "So, Maya, you're claiming the experiment failed because of temperature?"
- **Written listening**: [5-Minute Writing Conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md) apply the same stance to student writing
- **Peer listening**: structured protocols (e.g., restatement before rebuttal in [Debate](../patterns/debate.md)) train students in the same skill

### Target Learners
- Younger students and reluctant speakers, for whom visible attention from the teacher builds the confidence to contribute [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Students whose partial or intuitive conceptions need surfacing before they can be revised — listening is the diagnostic step
- Less applicable when students lack any prior knowledge to articulate; pair with [Activating Prior Knowledge](../principles/activation.md) first

### Target Learning Goals
- Formative assessment: diagnosing misconceptions and partial understanding
- Disciplinary discourse: learning to articulate and defend reasoning
- Social-emotional: feeling heard, which underpins engagement and belonging

### Instructions
1. Ask an open question and hold genuine wait time; resist filling silence.
2. Listen for the reasoning, not just the answer — attend to tone and hesitation as affective data.
3. Paraphrase or revoice the contribution and check the interpretation with the student.
4. Probe with a follow-up ("What makes you say that?") rather than evaluating immediately.
5. Make an instructional decision based on what was heard — redirect, connect to another student's idea, or adjust the next activity ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
6. Close the loop publicly: reference the student's idea later so the class sees that contributions shape instruction.

## Examples
- **Talk Science / Accountable Talk** (Michaels & O'Connor, [SERP Institute](https://www.serpinstitute.org/talkscience)) — trains teachers in revoicing and probing moves that operationalize listening to student reasoning in math and science discussion.
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction** ([CGI for Math](../patterns/cgi-for-math.md)) — teachers listen to children's invented strategies for arithmetic problems and build subsequent instruction on the strategies heard.
- **Responsive Classroom** ([Center for Responsive Schools](https://www.responsiveschools.org)) — teacher language practices, including paraphrasing and validating student voice, embedded in daily [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md).
