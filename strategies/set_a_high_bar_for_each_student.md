# Set a High Bar for Each Student

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Setting a high bar means communicating — through words, feedback, and task design — that every student is capable of meeting rigorous standards, and pairing that message with the support needed to reach them. It is carried out through social-psychological interventions: brief, well-timed messages and practices that change students' beliefs about their ability and belonging, such as growth-mindset framing, wise feedback, and normalized struggle.

## Design Implications

High expectations only raise achievement when students believe the expectations apply to them and that effort will pay off. Brief social-psychological interventions can produce meaningful achievement gains, especially for students at risk of underperformance [Social-psychological interventions can produce lasting achievement gains.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The mechanism is not cheerleading but credibility: students must see that the teacher's confidence is backed by actionable support and honest, high-quality feedback. Feedback framed as a belief in the student's capacity to reach high standards ("wise feedback") increases trust and revision behavior [+M]. Autonomy-supportive delivery — offering choice and rationale rather than pressure — sustains the motivation these messages are meant to build [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Rigorous tasks that are genuinely attainable with effort and support — the bar must be real, not rhetorical
- Feedback that is specific, actionable, and explicitly tied to high standards ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md), [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
- Consistent messaging across the classroom environment that struggle is normal and ability is malleable
- Teacher training on expectation communication, since students detect low expectations quickly

#### Constraints
- Interventions lose effect when the surrounding environment contradicts the message — a growth-mindset poster paired with ability-grouped, low-challenge work undermines credibility [-M]
- Students with deeply ingrained negative self-beliefs require repeated, consistent messaging over time; single-shot interventions show weaker and more heterogeneous effects at scale [~M]
- Empty praise ("you're so smart") or unearned praise signals low expectations and reduces persistence [-M]
- High standards without adequate support produce disengagement rather than resilience [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Wise feedback**: attach "I'm giving you these comments because I have high standards and I know you can meet them" to graded work
- **Growth-mindset framing**: teach that the brain grows with effortful practice; integrate into subject content rather than delivering as a standalone lesson
- **Normalization of struggle**: share examples of experts revising and failing; require revision cycles
- **Belonging framing**: message that early difficulty is common and temporary, not a signal of not belonging

### Target Learners
- Students at risk of internalizing low expectations — first-generation, low-income, and stereotyped-group students show the largest gains from belonging and expectation interventions [Social-psychological interventions can produce lasting achievement gains.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students transitioning into demanding environments (middle school, first-year college) where self-doubt spikes
- Less effective for students who already hold strong self-efficacy and are performing well [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Dispositional: growth mindset, self-efficacy, academic belonging
- Motivational: persistence through difficulty, willingness to attempt challenging tasks
- Achievement: improved grades and completion, particularly for underperforming students

### Instructions
1. Set tasks at a genuinely rigorous level and communicate the standard explicitly before work begins ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
2. Deliver feedback that names the gap *and* expresses confidence in the student's ability to close it ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
3. Build revision into assessment so the high bar is reachable through iteration ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
4. Check in with students who show signs of doubt, normalizing struggle and pointing to concrete next steps ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
5. Foster a classroom norm that all students belong and are expected to succeed ([Belonging](../elements/belonging.md))

## Examples
- **Yeager et al. (2019) national study**: a short online growth-mindset module embedded in core curriculum raised GPA among lower-achieving high school students across the U.S.
- **Cohen et al.'s wise-feedback experiments**: adding a single sentence expressing high standards and confidence to essay feedback increased revision rates among Black and Latino students.
- **EL Education (https://eleducation.org)**: school network built around "high expectations with support for all," using crew structures, revision culture, and public exhibition of rigorous work.
