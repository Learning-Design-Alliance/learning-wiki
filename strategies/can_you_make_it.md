# Can You Make It?

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners draw a dot card showing a small quantity arranged in a pattern (e.g., 3 dots in a triangle), then recreate that arrangement on a large, blank dot card using physical counters. The task requires learners to perceive the quantity, hold its structure in mind, and map it onto a spatial arrangement — exercising both subitizing (rapid recognition of small quantities without counting) and one-to-one counting. Beginners start with quantities of 1–3; the range expands as recognition becomes automatic.

## Design Implications

Recreating dot arrangements connects perceptual pattern recognition to physical action, which supports early number sense by binding quantity to spatial structure [~M]. Manipulatives benefit early mathematics learning most when they are used to represent abstract structure rather than as ends in themselves [~S]; the dot-card-to-counter mapping does this by making the *arrangement* — not just the count — the target of attention. Keeping quantities small (1–3 initially) exploits young children's subitizing range and limits working-memory demands [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A deck of dot cards with quantities arranged in recognizable patterns (standard and non-standard configurations)
- A large blank dot card or work mat and a supply of counters
- An adult or peer who can check the recreation and prompt comparison ("How did you know it was 3?")

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops when learners count one-by-one without ever perceiving the pattern; facilitation should prompt "How many did you see?" rather than only verifying the final count [-W]
- Physical materials must be reliably available; the activity does not function as a paper-only task [-W]
- Learners who already subitize fluently to 5 gain little from quantities in that range — repetition without increasing range or pattern complexity produces disengagement rather than consolidation [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Range progression:** start at 1–3, extend to 4–5, then introduce two arrangements of the same number (e.g., 5 as a line vs. a dice pattern) to loosen attachment to a single configuration
- **Memory variant:** flash the dot card briefly (2–3 seconds), remove it, and have learners recreate from memory — this shifts the task toward visualization and strengthens the perceptual representation
- **Matching variant:** learners recreate the arrangement, then find other dot cards showing the same quantity in different arrangements
- **Partner variant:** one learner builds an arrangement; the partner makes a matching card, then they compare

### Target Learners
- Pre-K to Grade 1 learners who are beginning to subitize and count reliably
- Learners who can count but do not yet recognize quantities arranged in patterns (e.g., cannot identify 4 dots on a die face without counting)
- Less useful for learners already fluent in subitizing to 10; for them, the same structure works better with larger quantities or two-card combinations

### Target Learning Goals
- Subitizing: instant recognition of small quantities and their spatial structure
- Number sense: understanding that quantity is invariant across different arrangements
- Counting fluency: accurate one-to-one correspondence when recreating arrangements
- Early part-whole thinking: seeing 5 as "3 and 2" within an arrangement

### Instructions
1. Give the learner a deck of dot cards (quantities within their current range) and a large blank card with counters.
2. Learner draws a dot card, names or estimates the quantity ("How many do you see?"), then recreates the arrangement with counters on the blank card.
3. Compare the recreation to the original card side by side; ask the learner to check their own work before the facilitator confirms ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Prompt reflection on structure: "Did you make yours the same way? Could 3 look different?" ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
5. Increase range or introduce alternate arrangements of the same quantity once recreation is quick and accurate.

## Related Strategies
- Quick-image routines (flash a dot card briefly and discuss what learners saw) — the memory variant above is a direct adaptation
- Counting collections — extends quantity recognition to larger, countable sets

## Examples
- **Clements & Sarama's [Building Blocks](https://www.buildingblocksmath.org) curriculum** — early mathematics program built on learning trajectories that include dot-pattern recognition and recreation activities of exactly this type
- **Everyday Mathematics "Quick Looks" routine** — teachers flash dot cards; children describe the quantity and how they saw it, then build equivalent arrangements with counters
- A Pre-K small-group station: children rotate through the dot-card recreation task for 5–7 minutes, with the teacher recording which quantities each child recognizes instantly versus counts
