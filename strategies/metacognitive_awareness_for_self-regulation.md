# Metacognitive Awareness for Self-Regulation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structured journaling (or audio/video recording) prompts learners to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own thinking and study behavior. By externalizing these processes in writing or speech, learners build awareness of their strategies and outcomes — the foundation of self-regulated learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. The strategy operationalizes the three phases of Zimmerman's self-regulation model — forethought, performance, and self-reflection — as recurring, prompted reflection tied to real learning tasks.

## Design Implications

Metacognitive awareness — knowledge of one's own thinking — is trainable, and structured reflection is among the most practical ways to train it [~M]. Journals work when prompts target specific regulatory phases (planning, monitoring, evaluating) rather than general "how did you feel" reflection [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Because self-assessment is often miscalibrated, reflection should be checked against actual performance data, not treated as accurate on its own.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent journaling routine with scheduled prompts tied to real learning tasks
- Prompts addressing planning ("What is my goal?"), monitoring ("Where am I stuck?"), and evaluation ("What would I do differently?") — process-level focus outperforms outcome-level focus for novices [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Instructor or peer feedback on the reflection itself, at the task and process level [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- A low-stakes format so honest self-assessment is not penalized
- Periodic comparison of self-predictions against actual performance (e.g., predicted vs. actual exam scores) to calibrate judgment

#### Constraints
- Unstructured diary-style reflection drifts into emotional venting with little regulatory value [-W]
- Journaling adds time; if perceived as busywork, compliance and quality collapse [~M]
- Self-monitoring judgments are often inaccurate without calibration training or external checks against performance [~M] — learners frequently report ineffective strategies (e.g., rereading) as their most-used techniques [Dunlosky et al. found rereading and highlighting among the least effective techniques.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]
- Learners with weak writing skills may spend capacity on transcription rather than reflection, reducing the benefit [-W]
- Reflection without a follow-through mechanism (plans never revisited) produces awareness without behavior change [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Audio or video recordings for younger learners or students with writing difficulties
- Exam wrappers — short post-assessment reflection forms tied to a specific test
- Structured learning logs with templates vs. free-form journals
- Digital portfolios with periodic self-review across a term
- Peer reflection pairs, where partners compare strategies and challenge each other's plans

### Target Learners
- K-12 and post-secondary students developing independent study habits [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Students with learning disabilities or executive-function challenges, who benefit from externalized structure
- Low-achieving students who lack strategy knowledge — provided prompts name concrete alternative strategies rather than only asking what went wrong
- Less effective for learners who already regulate well without prompting [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive knowledge: awareness of one's strategies, strengths, and gaps
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and evaluating learning behavior
- Strategy transfer across courses and tasks [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Calibration: aligning confidence and self-predictions with actual performance

### Instructions
1. Introduce the purpose by modeling a reflection on a recent task yourself ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
2. Provide structured prompts covering planning, monitoring, and evaluation ([Cognitive Strategies](../elements/cognitive-strategies.md))
3. Have learners reflect immediately after a learning episode or assessment ([Act](../elements/act.md))
4. Offer feedback on the reflection itself at the process level ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
5. Periodically ask learners to review past entries and identify patterns in their study behavior
6. Close the loop: require learners to translate each evaluation into a concrete plan for the next task ([Action Planning](action_planning.md))

## Examples
- **Exam wrappers** (Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center, https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html) — post-exam forms asking what students predicted, how they studied, and what they will change next time
- **Learning portfolios** (e.g., Digication, https://www.digication.com) — students maintain term-long reflective portfolios, reviewing entries to identify strategy patterns
- Audio journals in which students record a two-minute reflection after each study session, useful for learners with writing difficulties
