Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

PBIS is a proactive, school-wide framework for improving behavior and climate by teaching behavioral expectations the way academic content is taught, then consistently recognizing and reinforcing them. It is organized in three tiers: universal expectations for all students (Tier 1), targeted small-group supports (Tier 2), and intensive individualized interventions (Tier 3). The core premise is that students can only meet expectations they know, have practiced, and see reinforced — so the emphasis is on prevention and explicit instruction rather than punishment.

Design Implications¶

PBIS applies behavioral learning principles at the systems level: expectations are defined operationally, taught explicitly, and followed by consistent reinforcement Behaviorism. Randomized trials show school-wide PBIS reduces office discipline referrals and suspensions and improves school climate School-wide PBIS reduces disciplinary exclusions and improves climate. [+S], with effects strongest when implementation fidelity is high and sustained over multiple years.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A small set (3–5) of positively stated, school-wide expectations defined in observable terms
  • Explicit lessons teaching what each expectation looks like in every setting (classroom, hallway, bus, cafeteria)
  • A consistent acknowledgment/reinforcement system and a data system (e.g., office referral data) for monitoring and decision-making
  • Administrative commitment and a team that meets regularly to review data and adjust supports
  • A leadership team with administrator support and a defined set of 3–5 positively stated expectations
  • Explicit lesson plans that teach expectations in each setting, like any Direct Instruction sequence
  • A reinforcement/acknowledgment system (tokens, praise, privileges) delivered consistently by all staff
  • Behavioral data collection (referrals, attendance) reviewed regularly for tier placement decisions
  • Family and staff buy-in; the framework fails when only some classrooms participate

Constraints¶

  • Implementation without fidelity — expectations posted but never taught or reinforced — produces little or no benefit Effects depend on implementation fidelity and duration. [~S]
  • Punitive or inconsistent staff responses undermine the reinforcement system; partial adoption (posters without teaching) is a common failure mode
  • Tier 1 alone is insufficient for students with intensive behavioral needs; without Tier 2/3 supports, effects for high-need students wash out [~S]
  • Rewards perceived as controlling can crowd out intrinsic motivation for some students Autonomy-supportive framing matters for intrinsic motivation. [~M] — acknowledgment systems should emphasize progress and competence rather than compliance-for-prizes
  • Implementation without fidelity — expectations posted but never taught or reinforced — produces no measurable benefit [-S]; effects depend on sustained (3+ years) high-fidelity implementation
  • Punitive or inconsistent staff responses undermine the reinforcement system; mixed messaging reduces effectiveness [~M]
  • Tier 1 alone is insufficient for students with intensive needs; withholding Tier 2/3 supports while claiming "PBIS doesn't work" is a common misattribution [-M]
  • Critiques note that acknowledgment systems can feel controlling or inequitable if rewards favor already-compliant students; culturally responsive adaptation is required [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Tier 1 (universal): school-wide expectations, acknowledgment systems, ~80% of students
  • Tier 2 (targeted): check-in/check-out, small-group social skills instruction for ~10–15% of students
  • Tier 3 (individualized): function-based behavior support plans, wraparound services for ~5% of students
  • Classroom-level adaptations: Classroom PBIS integrates expectations into routines, Check-In routines, and precorrection of problem transitions
  • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) — a Tier 2 strategy pairing students with a mentor for daily goal-setting and feedback; widely replicated
  • Classroom-level PBIS — the same teach-and-reinforce cycle applied to routines (transitions, group work) within a single classroom
  • Integrating with SEL — combining PBIS with social-emotional curricula to address skill deficits alongside behavior

Target Learners¶

  • All students in a school benefit from universal expectations and predictable routines School-wide PBIS reduces disciplinary exclusions and improves climate. [+S]
  • Students with IEPs, 504 plans, or histories of disciplinary exclusion benefit most from the tiered escalation of support Tiered supports reduce exclusionary discipline for high-risk students. [+M]
  • Empathic, relationship-based discipline messaging is especially effective for students at risk of suspension Brief intervention empathic discipline cuts suspensions. [+M]
  • All students benefit from predictable, positively framed expectations [+S]
  • Students with externalizing behavior or a history of discipline referrals benefit most from Tier 2/3 supports [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral self-regulation: knowing and demonstrating expectations across settings
  • School climate and belonging: predictable, safe environments Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • Social-emotional skills: cooperation, responsibility, and conflict resolution taught as explicit curricula
  • Procedural fluency in behavioral routines (transitions, help-seeking, collaboration)
  • Self-regulation and social skill acquisition
  • School climate and engagement as preconditions for academic learning

Instructions¶

  1. Define 3–5 positively stated school-wide expectations (e.g., "Be respectful, be responsible, be safe") with behavioral examples for each setting — State Objectives
  2. Teach expectations explicitly in every setting, using Modeling and Act-It-Out practice rather than posting rules alone
  3. Establish a consistent acknowledgment system that reinforces expected behavior promptly — Provide Feedback
  4. Use a continuum of consequences for problem behavior that is instructional, not exclusionary, wherever possible
  5. Monitor office discipline referrals, attendance, and climate data on a regular cycle and adjust tier supports — Assessment
  6. Escalate support through tiers: Coaching and check-in/check-out for at-risk students, function-based individual plans for intensive need

Examples¶

  • Center on PBIS — the national technical assistance center providing implementation guides, fidelity measures (e.g., TFI, SAS), and state network resources.
  • Maryland Safe and Supportive Schools / Maryland PBIS initiative — the large randomized trial of school-wide PBIS across 37 elementary schools that documented reductions in suspensions and referrals School-wide PBIS reduces disciplinary exclusions and improves climate. [+S]
  • A typical Tier 1 implementation: students learn "Be Safe" on the bus through a taught lesson and role-play in the first week, bus drivers hand out acknowledgment tickets, and a team reviews referral data monthly.
  • Maryland Safe and Supportive Schools / Maryland PBIS Partnership — statewide implementation studied in a large randomized trial (Bradshaw et al., 2010), showing reduced suspensions and improved organizational health.
  • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) — a Tier 2 intervention in which a mentor greets the student each morning, reviews point goals, and gives end-of-day feedback; one of the most replicated PBIS Tier 2 practices.
  • Illinois PBIS Network (now Midwest PBIS, pbis.org) — the national technical assistance center providing implementation guides, fidelity measures (e.g., TFI, BoQ), and state-level scaling.
