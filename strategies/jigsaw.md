# Jigsaw

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
In the Jigsaw method (Aronson, 1978), material is divided into interdependent segments. Learners first join an "expert group" responsible for mastering one segment, then regroup into "jigsaw groups" composed of one expert from each segment, where each member teaches their portion to peers. The interdependence is structural: no learner can complete the full task without the contributions of the others, so peer teaching is a necessity rather than an optional activity.

## Design Implications

Jigsaw operationalizes positive interdependence and individual accountability — the two conditions most consistently associated with achievement gains in cooperative learning research [Johnson & Johnson's social interdependence framework.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X09339057) [+S]. The teaching phase is itself a learning event: preparing to explain and explaining aloud produce deeper processing than passive reception [Learning by teaching improves retention relative to studying for a test.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.07.001) [+M]. Because each learner must reconstruct and articulate their segment, jigsaw naturally elicits [self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M] and builds the communication skills the method targets.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content that can be partitioned into coherent, roughly equal segments — none trivially dispensable
- Carefully prepared segment materials at appropriate reading levels, since each segment is a single point of failure for the group
- Sufficient time for two full phases (expert-group mastery, then jigsaw-group teaching and synthesis)
- Individual accountability mechanisms (quizzes, written synthesis) so learners cannot free-ride on experts

#### Constraints
- Time cost is substantially higher than direct instruction for the same content coverage [-M] — jigsaw trades coverage for depth and engagement
- Learners with low confidence or social anxiety may teach inaccurately or avoid teaching, degrading the whole group's model of that segment [-M]; the method amplifies the cost of any individual's failure to master their segment
- Poorly structured expert-group time produces shallow mastery that propagates errors through jigsaw groups [~M] — monitoring during the expert phase is essential
- Less effective for tightly sequenced, hierarchical content where segments cannot be understood independently [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jigsaw II** (Slavin): all learners first read common base material, then expert groups specialize in a topic within it — reduces the risk of a weak segment
- **Reverse jigsaw**: experts teach in their original groups rather than regrouping
- Independent pre-review of materials before expert groups supports learners with varying processing speeds and raises the baseline quality of expert teaching
- Segment formats can vary (text, data sets, cases, videos) as long as each segment is teachable in the time allotted

### Target Learners
- K–12 through adult learners; the structure scales if segment difficulty is calibrated [~M]
- Particularly valuable for learners who benefit from structured talk and low-stakes rehearsal before whole-class performance; peer teaching builds confidence that supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Weaker fit for novices with no prior knowledge of *any* segment, since they must both learn and teach under time pressure [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and retention of distributed content, with synthesis across segments
- Communication and explanation skills — articulating understanding to peers [Peer teaching requires articulation that deepens the explainer's own understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Collaboration and interdependent problem-solving

### Instructions
1. Partition the content into interdependent segments and prepare segment materials at the right difficulty ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — each segment should fit working memory limits [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]).
2. Form home ("jigsaw") groups of one-member-per-segment size, then temporarily regroup members into expert groups by segment ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md)).
3. Support expert groups to reach genuine mastery — provide source materials, guiding questions, and instructor check-ins ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)); consider independent pre-reading first.
4. Return experts to home groups; each member teaches their segment while peers question and take notes ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Require an individual synthesis task that spans all segments, ensuring accountability for learning beyond one's own segment ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).

## Examples
- **The Jigsaw Classroom** (https://www.jigsaw.org) — Aronson's original implementation for desegregated classrooms, with free implementation guides across subjects.
- **Mathematics jigsaw for math talk** — students tackle new material in expert groups (e.g., each group masters one solution strategy or one data set), then teach their approach in home groups, strengthening mathematical communication and flexibility; independent pre-review before small-group work supports varying processing speeds.
- **Jigsaw II in reading** (Slavin's Student Team Learning) — all students read a common base text, expert groups specialize in subtopics, and home groups teach and are quizzed individually, with team scores based on individual quiz improvements.
