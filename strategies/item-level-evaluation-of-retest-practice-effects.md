# Evaluate high-stakes admission-test practice effects at the item level and reconsider selection procedures where re-sitting may disadvantage first-time candidates

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 associational), `q3` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article recommends that because practice effects are large enough to matter for selection, admission tests such as the UMAT should be analysed with individual item-level data, including guessing-adjusted scaling, to determine whether re-sitting improves competence or only performance. It further suggests selection bodies reconsider current approaches where re-sitting "may be introducing inequity in student selection for medical or dental schools in Australia or NZ." The authors also call for item-level analysis to delineate construct-irrelevant versus construct-relevant contributions.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to item-level response data and appropriate psychometric scaling that adjusts for guessing on multiple-choice items
#### Constraints
- The article's own analyses used only total percentile scores, so it could not tell whether improvement was in easy or difficult items, and the identifier-based matching may have missed candidates who changed name

### Target Learners
- Medical and dental school applicants in Australia and New Zealand

### Target Learning Goals
- Fair and valid selection of candidates for health professional courses
