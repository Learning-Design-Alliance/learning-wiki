# Chunk Directions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Chunk Directions means delivering instructions in small, sequenced units — one step or two at a time — rather than as a single long string of directions. Each chunk is presented, acted on, and checked before the next is introduced, so learners hold only a manageable amount of procedural information in working memory at once.

## Design Implications

Working memory can hold only a few novel elements at a time, and long instruction strings routinely exceed that capacity, causing steps to be dropped or abandoned mid-task [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Presenting directions in chunks aligns with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) by offloading the "what do I do next?" burden onto the environment rather than memory. Chunking also creates natural checkpoints where learners can ask questions or receive [feedback](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) before errors compound.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Directions pre-analyzed into a logical step sequence, with each chunk independently actionable
- A consistent signal (verbal cue, slide, gesture) marking when a new chunk begins
- A way for learners to indicate readiness or confusion between chunks ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), thumbs-up signals, or a designated question pause)

#### Constraints
- Over-chunking fragments a coherent procedure and can prevent learners from building an integrated mental model of the whole task [~M] — the [expertise-reversal effect](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) applies: fluent learners often prefer and benefit from complete directions presented at once
- Increases pacing demands on the instructor; learners who finish a chunk quickly must have a productive next action or they disengage
- Written multi-step directions that learners can revisit may serve better than spoken chunks for tasks with many steps, since speech evaporates [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Sequential release:** give one chunk at a time orally or on screen, revealing the next only after the current step is underway
- **Chunked reference card:** provide all steps, but visually segmented and numbered, so learners self-pace through chunks
- **Progressive disclosure in digital tools:** multi-step forms and tutorials (e.g., TurboTax, Duolingo lessons) that reveal one instruction per screen
- **Format variety:** pair verbal chunks with a written or pictorial anchor so learners can re-check without asking

### Target Learners
- Learners who experience anxiety, sensory overload, or difficulty with transitions between activities [~M]
- Novices on unfamiliar multi-step procedures, for whom all steps compete for working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Young learners and learners with attention or executive-function challenges, who lose track in long instruction strings
- Less necessary for experts or learners familiar with the task type, for whom chunked delivery adds friction [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency: correctly executing multi-step processes (lab protocols, math algorithms, craft techniques)
- Sustained attention and task persistence across longer activities
- Executive-function support: internalizing how tasks are segmented, which learners can eventually apply to their own planning

### Instructions
1. Analyze the task and write out all steps; group them into chunks of one to three actions that form a natural unit.
2. Deliver the first chunk, modeling it if the procedure is new, and connect it to prior knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Pause for learners to act on the chunk; circulate and [provide guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md) where needed.
4. Check understanding briefly before releasing the next chunk ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
5. Keep a visible, segmented record of all chunks (numbered list, anchor chart) so learners can see task structure and self-monitor progress.
6. As learners gain fluency, deliver larger chunks or hand over the full segmented list — fading the chunking itself.

## Related Strategies
- Chunking content more broadly — the same working-memory logic applied to material to be learned, not just directions
- Modeling multi-step tasks before independent work — demonstration pairs naturally with chunked delivery

## Examples
- **Elementary transitions:** A teacher gives directions in three chunks ("First, put your crayons in the basket… show me when you're ready. Next, move to the carpet…"), waiting for a readiness signal between each.
- **Science labs:** Lab manuals that present each procedure stage on its own page with a checkpoint question, rather than a single paragraph of ten steps.
- **Duolingo (https://www.duolingo.com)** — presents one instruction or prompt per screen, releasing the next only after the learner responds.
- **Recipe and DIY apps (e.g., Tasty's step mode)** — one step at a time with a timer, replacing the full ingredient-plus-method wall of text.
