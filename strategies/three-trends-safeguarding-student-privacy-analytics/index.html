Safeguard student privacy in learning analytics by involving students in tool creation, building contextualized explainable configurable analytics, and empowering student agency¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Drawing on Ochoa and Wise's practitioner response, the article identifies three general trends in learning analytics that may help safeguard privacy. These are "involving students in tool creation," developing "analytics that are contextualized, explainable and configurable," and "empowering student agency with analytics as part of a 'larger process of learning.'"

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Practitioners implementing learning analytics must treat analytics as part of a larger process of learning rather than an isolated monitoring function.

Constraints¶

Target Learners¶

  • students in institutions using learning analytics systems

Target Learning Goals¶

  • student agency and privacy protection in learning analytics

Claims¶

  • Students' willingness to accept learning analytics systems is moderately predicted by their attitudes toward data control and sharing [+M]
  • Students consider some types of data much more important to keep private than others (e.g., medical data vs. email addresses) [+M]
  • Most consumers accept terms and conditions of apps and websites without reading them, so student data are used in ways students may not knowingly consent to [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Involve teachers as co-designers of AI-based intelligent systems, with agency to train, correct, interpret, and override system recommendations
  • Apply an affective data governance framework: minimization, purpose limitation, consent, human oversight, and separation from evaluation
  • Establish AI-specific evaluation and accountability systems using independent audits, role-based dashboards, and privacy and equity safeguards
