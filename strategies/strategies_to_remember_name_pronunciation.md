# Strategies to Remember Name Pronunciation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy has the instructor deliberately learn each learner's name and its correct pronunciation using memory techniques: associating the name with a familiar word or image, writing syllables in English phonetics, rhyming, rehearsing aloud, and consulting pronunciation resources (e.g., NameShouts, HearNames) for names from unfamiliar language backgrounds. Correct pronunciation is then used consistently in discussion, feedback, and check-ins.

## Design Implications

Learning names is a low-cost relational practice with outsized effects on classroom climate: students who report their names are mispronounced describe feeling diminished and less willing to participate, while correct pronunciation signals respect and belonging [Kohli & Solórzano's work on name-based microaggressions.](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811211400107) [+M]. Belonging is a meaningful predictor of persistence and achievement, particularly for students from marginalized groups [Brief belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The memory techniques themselves draw on established encoding principles — elaborative association and phonological rehearsal — though keyword-style mnemonics show mixed durability for retention [Learning technique effectiveness varies; keyword mnemonics show mixed evidence.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A roster with enough lead time before the first class meeting to research pronunciations
- Willingness to ask learners directly how to pronounce their names, and to be corrected without defensiveness
- A private system for recording phonetic spellings (roster notes, seating chart annotations)
- Repeated, spaced use of names in class — a single correct attempt does not consolidate memory

#### Constraints
- Impractical to do thoroughly in very large classes; partial approaches (learning a subset, using name tents, group introductions) trade off the personal signal [-W]
- Word-association mnemonics can produce silly or even offensive associations; associations must be chosen with care and kept private [-W]
- Asking a student repeatedly "how do you say your name?" across many sessions shifts the burden of remembering onto the student, which itself signals low care [-W]
- Mnemonic encoding without spaced retrieval decays quickly; names learned for week 1 are often lost by week 5 [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Phonetic respelling**: write each name syllable-by-syllable in English orthography on the roster (e.g., "Ngozi" → "n-GO-zee")
- **Association**: link the name to a vivid image or a person the instructor already knows with the same name
- **Rhyme/chunking**: pair the name with a rhyming phrase or break it into memorable chunks
- **Technology**: use pronunciation websites or audio tools (NameShouts, HearNames, NameCoach in LMS rosters) to hear the name before meeting the learner
- **Learner-mediated**: have students record their own name audio (NameCoach, Flip introductions) — this removes guesswork entirely and scales better than instructor-only mnemonics [+W]
- **In-class rituals**: name tents, first-week introductions with pronunciation repeated by the whole class, or a check-in round where names are used

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit, but the effect is strongest for students whose names are uncommon in the instructor's language or culture, who disproportionately experience name-based microaggressions [Students whose names are mispronounced report feeling marginalized and disengaged.](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811211400107) [+M]
- Students from minoritized groups, for whom name respect is entangled with broader experiences of belonging [Brief belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners who may otherwise anglicize or shorten their own names to avoid correction

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective/classroom climate: establishing instructor care and psychological safety
- Participation: students are more willing to speak when they expect to be addressed correctly
- Instructor metacognition: building the habit of treating names as content worth learning, not overhead

### Instructions
1. Before the term, review the roster and flag names you are unsure how to pronounce; consult a pronunciation resource or colleague rather than guessing.
2. Record a phonetic respelling and, where useful, a private word association for each name on your roster or seating chart.
3. In the first session, ask each learner to say their own name (and any preferred name) and repeat it aloud immediately — self-correction from the source beats any mnemonic.
4. Use spaced retrieval: call on learners by name during [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) across the first several weeks rather than cramming name-learning into week one.
5. If you mispronounce a name, correct it briefly, apologize once, and move on — repeated visible anxiety about a name can make the student manage *your* discomfort.
6. In large classes, adopt a scaled variant: name tents, student-recorded audio names, or learning names in small-group rotations.

## Examples
- **NameCoach** (https://www.name-coach.com) — an LMS-integrated tool where students record their own name pronunciation; instructors hear the recording directly from the roster. Scales to large classes where individual mnemonics fail.
- **First-week phonetic roster**: an instructor teaching a course with names from many language backgrounds writes English phonetic respellings on the printed roster and rehearses before each of the first three class meetings, retiring the crutch as retrieval becomes automatic.
- **K–12 practice**: teachers asking students "Can you say it for me once so I get it right?" on day one, then correcting themselves publicly when they err — modeling that getting it right matters more than appearing competent.
