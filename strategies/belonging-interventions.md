# Belonging Interventions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), `q3` · 0 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Belonging interventions address students' uncertainty about whether they fit in academically and socially — doubt that is especially acute during transitions (first year, first-generation status, underrepresented minority status). They typically combine (a) information that belonging difficulties are common and temporary ("at first almost everyone worries they don't belong; this fades with time") and (b) structured opportunities for students to articulate their own belonging experiences and connect with peers and instructors. The canonical format is a brief social-belonging session early in a course or program, sometimes followed by reinforcement activities.

## Design Implications

Belonging interventions work by reframing adversity: students who attribute early struggle to a normal, surmountable transition rather than to evidence they don't belong persist longer and perform better [Brief belonging interventions improve GPA and persistence, especially for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+S]. Effects are strongest when the intervention is delivered at a transition point and when institutional messages reinforce it afterward — a one-off activity contradicted by a competitive, exclusionary climate loses its effect [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Credible messengers and plausible framing — the "difficulties are common and temporary" message must come from data, stories, or older peers, not feel like empty reassurance
- A transition point (course start, program entry) when belonging uncertainty is high
- Follow-through: instructor practices such as welcoming [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), inclusive feedback framing, and visible peer community that confirm the message over time
- Credible, non-patronizing delivery: findings presented as research results, not reassurance or cheerleading
- Student-generated content (essays, peer testimonials) so the message is self-persuasive rather than lectured
- Timing at a transition point (first weeks of a course, first year, entry into a selective program)
- An institutional climate where belonging is at least plausibly attainable

#### Constraints
- Effects attenuate when the surrounding climate contradicts the message — students who continue to experience exclusion or stereotype threat discount the intervention [-M]
- One-shot delivery without reinforcement shows weaker long-term effects than interventions with follow-up modules [~M]
- Poorly framed versions ("you should feel you belong") can backfire by invalidating students' actual experiences of marginalization [-W]
- Benefits are concentrated among at-risk groups; effects on already well-served students are near zero, so these interventions are not a substitute for structural climate improvements [~S]
- One-shot delivery in a genuinely hostile or unsupportive environment produces little or no lasting benefit [Yeager & Walton, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-2409.2011.01008.x) [-M] — the message is contradicted by daily experience
- Effects fade when the intervention is framed as remedial help for "at-risk" students, which itself signals lower expectations [-M]
- Repeating the identical activity verbatim can feel inauthentic; effectiveness depends on students generating their own meaning, not receiving a script [-W]
- Benefits concentrate among students experiencing belonging uncertainty; universal delivery to secure, well-supported students shows negligible effects [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Written reading-and-reflection modules (Walton & Cohen's social-belonging intervention)
- Peer-mentoring or near-peer testimony formats where senior students share their own early struggles
- Instructor-led norm-setting: explicit statements that struggle is expected and help-seeking is normal
- Combined packages pairing belonging content with [Growth Mindset](../strategies/growth-mindset-interventions.md) framing about ability
- **Written reflection**: students read survey findings, then write about their own experience for a future student — the most validated format
- **Peer testimonials**: videos or quotes from senior students describing doubts that faded; works well at scale in orientation programs
- **Embedded messaging**: instructors weave "belonging is uncertain for everyone early on" into feedback and course norms rather than a standalone session
- **Combined packages**: belonging content delivered alongside [growth mindset](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) or value-affirmation activities in multi-component onboarding

### Target Learners
- First-generation and underrepresented minority students, who show the largest GPA and persistence gains [Brief belonging interventions improve GPA and persistence, especially for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+S]
- Students in transition (first-year, transfer, first course in a discipline)
- Students entering competitive or gatekeeping environments (STEM majors, pre-professional programs)
- Students entering a setting where they are numerically minoritized or stereotyped (first-generation students, women in male-dominated STEM, students of color at predominantly white institutions) [Walton & Cohen, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198364) [+S]
- Students in transition periods — first year, transfer, entry into selective majors — where belonging uncertainty is naturally elevated [~M]
- Students already confident of their place gain little; targeting by need rather than blanket delivery is more efficient [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Persistence and retention: staying enrolled through difficult transitions
- Academic self-efficacy and help-seeking: interpreting struggle as normal rather than diagnostic of non-belonging
- Engagement and participation in course community
- Persistence and retention: staying enrolled and engaged through early difficulty [+S]
- Academic help-seeking: students who feel they belong are more likely to use office hours, tutoring, and peer support [~M]
- Non-cognitive outcomes: reduced vigilance and rumination that otherwise consume working memory during learning [~W]

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the transition point and the specific belonging concerns of your population (survey or focus group).
2. Deliver a brief session presenting evidence that belonging worries are common and temporary, using peer testimony or data rather than reassurance.
3. Have students reflect in writing on their own experience and how it might change — self-persuasion is the active ingredient, not being told what to believe.
4. Reinforce through course practices: regular [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), collaborative structures from [Cooperative Learning](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md), and feedback framed as belief in the student's capacity to improve.
5. Monitor belonging indicators (participation, help-seeking, retention) and adjust; treat the intervention as a message the institution must keep confirming.

## Related Strategies

- [Wise Feedback](wise-feedback.md) — critical feedback framed with assurance of high expectations and capacity, reinforcing belonging at the moment of evaluation
- [Value Affirmation](value-affirmation.md) — a complementary brief intervention targeting self-integrity rather than belonging; often combined
- [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — ongoing relational practices that maintain the belonging message after the initial intervention

## Examples
- **University of Texas at Austin** — Walton & Cohen's social-belonging module delivered to incoming students; the original randomized study showed narrowed racial achievement gaps that persisted through three years of college.
- **College Transition Collaborative** — partnerships embedding brief belonging and mindset activities in first-year gateway courses across multiple university systems ([collegetransitioncollaborative.org](https://collegetransitioncollaborative.org)).
- **PERTS** — offers validated, scalable belonging survey and intervention toolkits for courses and programs ([perts.net](https://www.perts.net)).
- **Walton & Cohen's college intervention (2011)** — a one-hour reading-and-writing activity in the first year of college raised GPA among African American students and halved the racial achievement gap over three years [doi:10.1126/science.1198364](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198364)
- **University of Texas "Texas Interdisciplinary Plan"** — belonging and mindset messaging embedded in first-year scholar programs for students admitted by exception
- **PERTS (Project for Education Research That Scales)** — offers an online, classroom-ready belonging intervention used in secondary and postsecondary settings ([perts.net](https://www.perts.net))

## Description
Belonging interventions are short, structured activities — typically delivered early in a course or transition — that present evidence that worries about belonging ("Do I fit here?") are common, temporary, and not a signal of inability. Students typically read summary findings from surveys of older students, write a reflective essay connecting the message to their own experience, and sometimes hear testimonials from successful peers. The approach draws on social-psychological theory: uncertainty about belonging, when left unaddressed, triggers vigilance and disengagement that compounds into achievement gaps [Walton & Cohen, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198364) [+S].

## Design Implications

Belonging interventions work not by boosting self-esteem but by changing the *interpretation* of adversity: students who attribute early setbacks to a universal, transient adjustment process rather than to a fixed lack of fit persist through difficulty [Walton & Cohen, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1198364) [+S]. Effects are strongest for students entering stigmatized or majority-dominated settings, and strongest when the message is framed as informational rather than remedial. Because the intervention is brief, its durability depends on the surrounding environment actually being hospitable — a one-shot message cannot compensate for a chronically exclusionary climate [Yeager & Walton, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-2409.2011.01008.x) [~M].

### Instructions
1. **Present the evidence.** Share survey data showing that most students worry about belonging early on and that these worries fade with time — framed as research findings, not comfort.
2. **Elicit personal connection.** Ask students to write about how the message relates to their own experience, ideally addressed to a future student ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)); self-generated arguments are more persuasive than received ones.
3. **Normalize help-seeking as belonging behavior.** Pair the message with explicit norms that asking questions and using support services are what successful students here do ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines sustain this).
4. **Reinforce through feedback.** Deliver critical feedback with high expectations plus affirmation of the student's capacity to meet them, so setbacks don't read as evidence of non-belonging [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
5. **Follow the environment.** Audit whether course structures — group work norms, inclusive examples, accessible office hours — actually support the message ([Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md)).
