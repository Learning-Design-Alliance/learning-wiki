# Portfolio Assessment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Portfolio assessment asks learners to assemble a purposeful, criterion-referenced collection of work samples produced over time — drafts, final products, reflections, and self-assessments — as the primary evidence of learning. Unlike single-point testing, it documents process and growth, and typically requires learners to select artifacts and justify their selections against explicit rubrics or standards.

## Design Implications

Portfolios shift assessment from a summative snapshot toward [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): the acts of selecting, comparing across time, and justifying artifacts are themselves learning activities that develop evaluative judgment [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The assessment value depends on structured reflection — an unreflective folder of work is an archive, not a portfolio. Clear criteria and exemplars are essential; without them, learners cannot judge what counts as evidence of growth.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit criteria or rubrics learners can apply to their own work ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) criteria made visible)
- Multiple work samples spanning enough time to show change
- Structured reflection prompts (e.g., "Why did you choose this artifact? What does it show about your progress?")
- Periodic instructor or peer feedback loops, not just end-of-term review
- A defined audience and purpose (certification, exhibition, employment, progression)

#### Constraints
- Reliability is weak when scoring is holistic: inter-rater agreement on portfolios is consistently lower than on constrained-response assessments [~M], requiring analytic rubrics and moderated scoring
- Grading portfolios summatively can corrupt the process — when artifacts are graded, learners curate for polish rather than honest evidence of growth [-M]
- Time-intensive for both learners and assessors; scaling to large enrollments without digital tooling is impractical [-W]
- Self-assessment quality depends on prior experience with criteria; novices given portfolios without rubric training show poor selection judgment [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Process portfolios** emphasize drafts and revision traces; **showcase portfolios** emphasize best work — the former supports growth goals, the latter performance demonstration
- **Working portfolios** (everything retained) vs. **presentation portfolios** (curated subset with rationale)
- Digital platforms (e.g., Mahara, Seesaw, Portfolium) enable multimedia artifacts, instructor commenting, and longitudinal tracking across courses
- Program-level portfolios can aggregate evidence across courses for [Competency-Based Assessment](../principles/competency-based-assessment.md) decisions

### Target Learners
- Learners developing complex, ill-structured skills (writing, design, teaching, clinical practice) where single tests under-sample competence
- Learners building self-regulation: selection and reflection cycles train monitoring and evaluation [Self-regulated learning strategies improve achievement.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-strategies-improve-achievement.md) [+S]
- Less suitable as a *graded* measure for novices who cannot yet judge quality; early use should be low-stakes with heavy rubric scaffolding

### Target Learning Goals
- Longitudinal skill growth and revision as a habit of practice
- Metacognition and evaluative judgment: learning to assess one's own work against standards
- Complex, integrated performance that cannot be captured by objective tests
- Professional identity formation and evidence-of-competence documentation

### Instructions
1. Define the purpose and audience, and publish the criteria or rubric learners will self-apply against ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Have learners maintain a working portfolio of drafts and artifacts over the term, with brief dated annotations.
3. At checkpoints, require structured reflection: select artifacts, justify choices against criteria, and identify change over time ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) conferences work well here).
4. Provide instructor or peer feedback on the portfolio-as-a-whole, not just individual artifacts, so learners practice evaluative judgment ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
5. Close with a curated presentation portfolio and a reflective cover narrative that synthesizes growth.

## Examples
- **National Board Certification (USA)** — teachers submit portfolio entries with student work samples, video, and written analysis scored against professional standards ([https://www.nbpts.org](https://www.nbpts.org)).
- **Mahara** — open-source ePortfolio platform used by universities for program-level portfolio assessment with templated reflection pages ([https://mahara.org](https://mahara.org)).
- **Seesaw** — K–12 digital portfolio where students upload work, record audio explanations, and receive family and teacher feedback ([https://seesaw.com](https://seesaw.com)).
- **Writing program assessment** — many first-year composition programs (e.g., the Council of Writing Program Administrators outcomes model) use end-of-term portfolios with reflective cover letters as the primary passing evidence ([https://wpacouncil.org](https://wpacouncil.org)).
