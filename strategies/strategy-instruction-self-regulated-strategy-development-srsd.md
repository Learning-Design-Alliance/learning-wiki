# Strategy Instruction (Self-Regulated Strategy Development - SRSD)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Strategy instruction explicitly teaches learners cognitive and metacognitive strategies — planning, drafting, revising, self-monitoring — rather than leaving them to infer how skilled writers work. Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD), developed by Graham and Harris, is the most validated form: instructors model a strategy, guide its use, and gradually release responsibility while learners develop self-regulation supports such as goal setting, self-monitoring, self-instruction, and self-reinforcement. Strategies replace negative self-talk with positive self-statements, helping learners overcome frustration and past failure. In adult education, strategy instruction has been introduced through the National Institute for Literacy's professional development programs *Bridges to Practice* and *Learning to Achieve*, designed for students with learning disabilities.

## Design Implications

SRSD is among the best-supported interventions in writing research: meta-analyses show large effects on writing quality, with effects maintained over time and generalized to new tasks [Strategy instruction produces large, durable gains in writing quality.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-writing-quality.md) [+S]. Its power comes from combining explicit strategy teaching with self-regulation training — the self-regulation components, not just the strategy steps, drive transfer and maintenance. Instruction must be sustained and reinforced across lessons; one-off strategy workshops do not produce durable change.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Professional development for instructors; SRSD is a staged instructional approach, not a single lesson
- Consistent reinforcement over multiple sessions until learners use the strategy independently
- A positive learning environment where self-instruction can replace negative self-talk
- Developmental instruction in the knowledge and skills the strategy presupposes (e.g., genre knowledge, vocabulary)

#### Constraints
- Requires significant instructor training and time; poorly implemented SRSD loses most of its effect [~M]
- Ineffective when learners lack motivation or engagement — self-regulation components presuppose learners who want to improve
- Less effective for learners who already use comparable strategies spontaneously; instruction becomes redundant [~W]
- Gains depend on continued practice; strategies fade if the classroom does not reinforce them after instruction ends [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Strategy content varies by genre: POW+TREE for opinion essays, PLANS for narrative writing, STOP+LIST+DARE for persuasive writing
- Can be delivered to whole classes, small groups, or individuals; small-group and one-to-one formats show the strongest effects [~M]
- Self-regulation components can be emphasized or trimmed; full SRSD (all six stages) outperforms strategy-only versions [~M]
- Adaptable across ages from elementary through adult education, with simpler mnemonics for younger learners

### Target Learners
- Low-achieving writers and students with learning disabilities, who typically lack both strategies and self-regulation [+S]
- Elementary and secondary students across ability levels — effects are positive but largest for weaker writers [~M]
- Adult learners returning to writing tasks after negative school experiences, where self-instruction addresses learned helplessness
- Less beneficial for already-proficient writers, who may find explicit strategy steps constraining [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: planning, content generation, organization, and revision
- Self-regulation: goal setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation, and persistence through difficulty
- Metacognitive awareness: knowing when and why to deploy a strategy, supporting transfer to new tasks
- Motivational outcomes: reduced avoidance, increased writing self-efficacy

### Instructions
1. **Develop background knowledge** — build prerequisite skills (genre features, topic vocabulary) the strategy depends on.
2. **Discuss the strategy** — explain what it is, why it helps, and when to use it; connect to learners' current difficulties ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. **Model the strategy** — instructor [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) while using the strategy, verbalizing self-instructions ("First I'll brainstorm because…") and positive self-statements.
4. **Memorize it** — learners commit the strategy steps and mnemonic to memory so working memory is free for composing ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
5. **Support it** — collaborative and guided [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with instructor [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md), gradually [Fading](../elements/fading.md) prompts as learners take over [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
6. **Independent performance** — learners use the strategy alone, set goals, self-monitor, and self-reinforce; instructor provides [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) and reinforces maintenance.

## Examples
- **POW+TREE (Graham & Harris)** — elementary students learn Pick my idea, Organize notes, Write and say more, then Topic sentence, Reasons, Examine, Ending — with self-instructions like "I can do this; take my time." Repeatedly validated in randomized studies.
- **SRSD in adolescent persuasive writing** — Graham et al.'s meta-analytic review documents effect sizes above 0.70 on writing quality for middle and high school implementations.
- **Learning to Achieve (National Institute for Literacy)** — professional development modules bringing strategy instruction to adult educators teaching learners with learning disabilities.
