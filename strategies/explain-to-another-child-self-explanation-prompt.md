# Prompt young children's self-explanations by having them explain the solution to another child, guided by open-ended step questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 5 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 5 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
To elicit self-explanations from young children in a procedural domain, the article adapted Brown and Kane's methods: "Students were instructed to pretend they were explaining the solution to another child who did not know how to solve the problem." Three open-ended questions guided the explanations (what is the first step, the second step, and if necessary the next step), and explanations were tape recorded and transcribed. The article reports this procedure was nonthreatening yet productive, though Experiment 3 found no significant performance benefit from the prompts.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A study-time equating procedure for no-explanation comparison groups (Experiment 3 used the average self-explanation time of about 84 seconds per example)
#### Constraints
- The article concludes generating self-explanations may not always enhance learning from worked examples, particularly for procedural tasks

### Target Learners
- young elementary school children (Year 3 and Year 4)

### Target Learning Goals
- understanding and explaining worked solutions to two-step compare word problems
