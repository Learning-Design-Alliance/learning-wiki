# Shared Writing

## Description
Shared writing involves a teacher and students collaboratively composing a text, with the teacher acting as a scribe. Students contribute ideas, wording, and decisions while the teacher records them, models conventions in the moment, and guides the group to revise and refine the text. Because the teacher holds the physical and mechanical burden of transcription, students can devote their full attention to composing — planning, generating, and evaluating ideas at the discourse level.

## Design Implications

Shared writing is a form of collaborative modeling: the teacher makes expert composing decisions visible while students supply the content, sitting between [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) (teacher does all the work) and independent writing (students do all the work). This positioning manages [cognitive load](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) by removing transcription and mechanics from students' working memory during the hardest phase — learning what it means to compose [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Collaborative composition also improves text quality relative to writing alone, because students encounter peers' reasoning and negotiate word choice and structure in real time [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible shared surface (chart paper, whiteboard, shared document) and a scribe — usually the teacher
- A meaningful, authentic reason to write ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)) so the group is composing, not just producing sentences to order
- Teacher facilitation that elicits contributions, negotiates decisions aloud, and revises visibly (different pen colors help make revision an object of study)
- A follow-up move toward guided or independent writing so the modeled decisions transfer

#### Constraints
- Dominant voices can skew the composition; without structured turn-taking the text reflects a few students, not the group [-W]
- Weak facilitation degrades into teacher dictation, which removes the negotiation that drives learning [~M]
- Time-intensive: a shared text typically takes a full session or more, and the payoff depends on students later applying the modeled moves [-W]
- Less effective when the text type is entirely unfamiliar and students have nothing to contribute; some prior exposure or [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of relevant knowledge is needed first

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interactive writing** (early literacy): students share the pen and write known letters and words themselves
- **Modeled-to-shared fade**: teacher begins composing, then hands over increasing control as the group shows competence
- **Shared writing in content areas**: composing a group explanation, argument, or lab conclusion to make disciplinary language visible [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- **Digital shared writing**: a live collaborative document replaces chart paper, allowing individual typing contributions and preserved revision history

### Target Learners
- Emergent and developing writers (K–8) who cannot yet manage transcription and composition simultaneously
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from hearing academic phrasing negotiated aloud before producing it independently [~M]
- Whole classes or small groups; in small groups, individual accountability mechanisms matter more [Cooperative learning group rewards and individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Composition skills: planning, generating, and revising text at the discourse level
- Genre knowledge: internalizing the structure and language of a text type through joint construction
- Concept internalization: articulating content-area understanding in precise written language
- Oral language development: negotiating sentences aloud builds expressive language [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Establish purpose.** Identify an authentic reason for the text (a class book, a letter, a summary for another class) [Authentic audiences improve student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]
2. **Activate prior knowledge.** Brainstorm or discuss the topic; build a word bank students can draw on [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
3. **Compose together.** Students propose ideas and wording; the teacher scribes, thinking aloud about decisions ("I'm starting with what we observed because…") — a [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) modeling move
4. **Revise publicly.** Reread the draft, mark improvements in a different color, and discuss why each change makes the writing better — making revision itself an object of [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)
5. **Read and confirm.** Reread the finished text chorally so students connect spoken and written language
6. **Release responsibility.** Move to guided or independent writing on a similar task, fading the shared support [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

## Examples
- A third-grade teacher used shared writing to consolidate a geometry unit: students generated a word bank of 3-D shape attributes, composed sentences describing the shapes, and revised them publicly for precision and word choice.
- In early-years classrooms, [Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)-informed shared writing is used to jointly construct a topic sentence before students write their own.
- Teachers using the [Talk for Writing](https://www.talk4writing.com) approach (Pie Corbett) jointly innovate on a "model text," rewriting a familiar story as a class before children write their own versions.
