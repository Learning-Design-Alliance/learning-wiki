# Domain Check

## Description
Domain Check asks learners to inspect a source's URL — specifically its top-level domain (.edu, .gov, .org, .com, .net) — as a rapid first-pass filter for credibility. The strategy treats domain type as a weak proxy for authority: educational and government sites are presumed more reliable than commercial ones. It is typically taught as an entry-level move in source evaluation, before deeper techniques such as reading laterally, checking authorship, or tracing claims to primary sources.

## Design Implications

Domain Check gives novices a concrete, low-effort procedure when they have no schema for evaluating web sources, functioning as a [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) step that can later be faded [~M]. Its instructional value lies in starting the evaluation habit, not in the accuracy of the signal itself — research on fact-checkers shows that experts rely on domain cues far less than novices do, and that novices who over-rely on surface features (including URLs) are systematically fooled by low-quality sites [~M]. Effective instruction pairs the check with explicit teaching of its limits, then moves students toward strategies like lateral reading, in which they leave the page to see what other sources say about the site.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task or source set that includes a mix of domain types so the check has something to discriminate
- Explicit discussion of what each domain type does and does not guarantee (a .org can be a lobbying front; a .com can be a rigorous newsroom)
- A follow-on evaluation step — authorship, evidence, corroboration — so the domain check does not become the *only* criterion students apply

#### Constraints
- Domain type is a weak and increasingly unreliable credibility signal [-M]: predatory journals, advocacy groups, and misinformation sites routinely use .org and .edu-adjacent domains, while high-quality journalism lives on .com
- Students taught domain checking alone show no improvement in actual source-evaluation performance and may gain false confidence [-W] — the heuristic creates an illusion of having "checked" the source
- The heuristic fails fastest on contested or politicized topics, where professionally designed advocacy sites exploit trusted domain conventions [-M]
- As a fixed rule it resists transfer; it must be faded toward expert strategies (lateral reading, source triangulation) or it becomes a crutch [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **First-pass filter**: taught as a triage step in a multi-criteria rubric (currency, authority, purpose, evidence)
- **Contrast set**: students compare a .gov page, a .org advocacy page, and a .com news article on the same topic to see where the heuristic breaks
- **Faded heuristic**: early lessons require domain checks; later lessons replace them with lateral reading and claim tracing
- **Checklist integration**: one line in a broader evaluation protocol such as SIFT (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims)

### Target Learners
- Novice researchers (upper elementary through early undergraduate) with no prior schema for evaluating web sources [~M]
- Students who over-trust polished web design and need *some* concrete entry point into evaluation — provided the limits are taught alongside the rule
- Less useful for experienced researchers, who already attend to authorship, venue, and corroboration and may find the check trivial or misleading [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: initial screening of source credibility
- Metacognition: building the habit of asking "who published this?" before reading
- Critical reading: recognizing that provenance, not appearance, signals reliability

### Instructions
1. Present a source set with mixed domain types and ask students to sort by top-level domain before reading.
2. Have students state what each domain type implies — and then test those implications against the actual content, surfacing counterexamples (a biased .org, an excellent .com).
3. Introduce a stronger strategy to replace the check: leave the page and search for what other sources say about the site or author (lateral reading), or trace the claim to its original source.
4. Fade the domain check: in subsequent research tasks, require the stronger strategies and treat domain as incidental context rather than a criterion.

## Related Strategies
- Lateral reading — the expert strategy that domain checking should develop into; leaving the page to verify the source outperforms staying on it
- SIFT protocol — a four-step evaluation routine in which source investigation is one move among several
- CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) — a multi-criteria rubric that embeds domain as one weak signal among stronger ones

## Examples
- **Stanford History Education Group (SHEG) "Civic Online Reasoning" curriculum** ([https://cor.stanford.edu](https://cor.stanford.edu)) — teaches source evaluation with exercises that explicitly contrast surface cues like domains with lateral-reading strategies; its studies found students frequently misjudged .org sites as credible.
- **First-year composition library instruction** — many university libraries (e.g., the UNC Libraries evaluation guides) teach domain checking as a first-pass filter within a broader source-evaluation checklist, then require corroboration with a second independent source.
