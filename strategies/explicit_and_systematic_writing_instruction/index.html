Explicit and Systematic Writing Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Explicit and systematic writing instruction directly teaches the strategies, skills, and knowledge proficient writers use, rather than expecting them to be absorbed through exposure. The instructor models writing strategies — planning, drafting, revising, self-regulation — making decisions visible, then guides learners through supported practice toward independent application across purposes (recording, communicating, persuading, self-expression, reflection). Foundational skills such as spelling, handwriting, and keyboarding are taught to automaticity so working memory is freed for composing Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+M] — the same logic applies to transcription fluency in writing. Skilled writing is heavily a planning-and-revising activity: accomplished writers spend roughly half their composing time planning and revising text, so instruction must treat these as teachable strategies, not afterthoughts.

Design Implications¶

Explicit strategy instruction works because it converts tacit expert processes into learnable procedures and then scaffolds learners' regulation of those procedures Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]. Its effects are among the largest in writing research: meta-analyses of explicit strategy instruction show substantial gains in text quality for adolescents, and comparable effects for students with learning difficulties Graham & Perin meta-analytic evidence. [+S]. Instruction must be systematic as well as explicit — sequenced from simple to complex, with each strategy taught, modeled, practiced, and revisited — because isolated mini-lessons without coordinated practice do not accumulate into writing competence.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A structured, sequenced curriculum that specifies which strategies and skills are taught when
  • Modeling of strategies with instructor think-alouds that make planning and revision decisions visible
  • Extended time for authentic writing practice across purposes and audiences
  • Explicit feedback on strategy use and text quality, not just surface errors
  • Instruction to automaticity of foundational transcription skills (spelling, handwriting, keyboarding)

Constraints¶

  • Requires significant instructional time; effects shrink when strategy lessons replace rather than accompany extended writing Process-writing approaches without explicit strategy support yield weaker effects. [~M]
  • Less effective when learners lack motivation or a reason to write; engagement and authentic purpose are prerequisites, not extras
  • Overly formulaic strategy teaching (e.g., rigid essay templates) can produce formulaic writing and transfer poorly to new genres [~M]
  • Foundational-skill drill that dominates instructional time crowds out composing practice, which is where strategy use consolidates

Implementation Variability¶

  • Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD): pairs strategy instruction with explicit teaching of self-regulation — goal setting, self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement — and is the most heavily validated variant, especially for struggling writers [+S]
  • Sentence-level instruction: explicit teaching of sentence combining and construction, effective as a complement to whole-composition strategy instruction [+M]
  • Reading–writing integration: teaching text structures through reading and then having students write in those structures capitalizes on the relationship between the two modalities [+M]
  • Prewriting and organizing: explicit instruction in planning procedures (e.g., outlining, graphic organizers, goal setting) before drafting [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling writers and students with learning difficulties benefit most; explicit strategy instruction produces some of the largest effects for these learners [+S]
  • Adolescents and older students who have not internalized planning and revision strategies [+S]
  • Beginning writers building transcription automaticity, for whom cognitive load during composing is otherwise prohibitive Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+M]
  • Capable writers still benefit from strategy instruction, though effects are smaller and depend on matching strategies to their current repertoire [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: producing clearer, better-organized, more persuasive text
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, evaluating, and revising one's own composing process
  • Procedural fluency: automating transcription so attention shifts to ideas and audience
  • Genre knowledge: understanding how purpose and audience shape text structure

Instructions¶

  1. Teach the strategy explicitly. Name the strategy, explain when and why to use it, and connect it to what proficient writers do (Direct Instruction).
  2. Model it. Demonstrate the strategy through think-aloud while composing, making planning and revision decisions visible (Cognitive Apprenticeship).
  3. Guide practice. Support learners as they apply the strategy with prompts, checklists, and collaborative composing, fading support as competence grows (Coaching).
  4. Teach self-regulation. Train goal setting, self-monitoring, and self-evaluation so learners can deploy and adjust strategies independently across tasks.
  5. Extend to independent writing. Provide sustained writing practice across purposes and genres, with feedback on both strategy use and text quality.

Examples¶

  • Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD) — Graham and Harris's extensively validated framework (e.g., the POW + TREE strategy for persuasive writing), in which students learn a planning strategy alongside self-regulation procedures; see SRSD Online.
  • The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler) — a systematic, sentence-level method that sequences instruction from sentence construction to composition, explicitly taught across content areas; see The Writing Revolution.
  • Graham & Perin's Writing Next recommendations — the meta-analysis identified explicit strategy instruction, summarization, collaborative writing, and sentence combining as the highest-effect practices for adolescent writers.
