# Critique Protocol

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 15 studies (7 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 5 of 15 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
A critique protocol is a fixed structure — often with prescribed sentence stems, turn-taking rules, and norms — that governs how learners present work and how peers respond to it. Well-known variants include Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" norms and the "I like / I wonder / I suggest" ladder. The protocol's purpose is to convert the social risk of public feedback into a reliable routine: critique targets the *work*, not the *maker*, and produces concrete revision steps rather than general praise or vague reactions.

## Design Implications

Structured critique improves the quality of both feedback and revision because it constrains attention to specific features of the work and forces evaluative comparison against criteria [Assessment criteria used actively by learners improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Unstructured peer feedback tends toward uninformative praise ("nice job") or personal judgment; protocols suppress both by prescribing what kind of statements are allowed. Critique also benefits the critic: evaluating others' work against criteria sharpens the evaluator's own understanding of quality [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M], since each peer's work functions as a contrasting case.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible model of quality — an exemplar or rubric — so critique has a shared reference point ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) or annotated exemplars serve this role)
- Norms explicitly taught and rehearsed before the first live critique; the protocol itself needs [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) and practice
- Time and expectation for **revision** — critique without a subsequent revision cycle signals that feedback is performative, and learners disengage
- A facilitator who enforces turn-taking and redirects personal or vague comments
- A draft or incomplete work product — critique targets improvable work, not finished pieces
- Explicit criteria or a rubric so comments anchor to qualities of the work, not personal taste
- A protocol script: norms, sentence stems, time limits, and turn-taking structure
- A visible model of quality work (exemplar or [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)) so reviewers share a standard
- Clear criteria or a rubric that the critique references
- Work-in-progress rather than finished work, so feedback can still change the outcome
- A taught protocol with sentence stems and time limits, rehearsed before it carries stakes

#### Constraints
- Without a revision requirement, critique sessions degrade into ritual and produce no measurable learning gain [-M]
- Groups with low trust or status hierarchies (e.g., mixed-ability groups where one member dominates) suppress honest critique; protocols alone do not overcome social threat [-M]
- Novices can give inaccurate feedback; uncritically accepted peer errors can reinforce misconceptions [~M] — pair peer critique with instructor review for high-stakes work
- Overly rigid protocols applied to creative work can flatten risk-taking and stylistic divergence [~W]
- Unstructured peer feedback ("I liked it") produces negligible learning gains; without criteria and stems, critique degrades into social pleasantries [Checklist-style evaluation without criteria is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Critique before learners have enough domain knowledge to judge quality yields confident but inaccurate feedback [~M] — early-stage novices need models of quality first
- Public critique can threaten belonging for anxious or minoritized learners unless norms are actively enforced and drafts are explicitly framed as unfinished
- If the same few students always present, the protocol becomes performance rather than feedback
- Low-trust classrooms suppress honest critique; learners hedge to protect relationships, and feedback quality drops [-M]
- Novice reviewers can transmit misconceptions; instructor moderation or spot-checking is needed in early iterations [-W]
- Critique of highly subjective or idiosyncratic work (personal narrative, artistic voice) can flatten expression if criteria over-specify style [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gallery critique**: work is displayed publicly and discussed by the whole group (Berger's whole-class model)
- **Critical friends / tuning protocols**: small groups follow timed rounds — presentation, clarifying questions, warm feedback, cool feedback
- **Studio critique**: art and design education's format, in which the maker listens silently while peers discuss the work, then responds
- **Written structured critique**: sentence stems on sticky notes or in LMS peer-review tools (e.g., [Peergrade](https://www.peergrade.io), [Eli Review](https://elireview.com)) for asynchronous settings
- **Gallery critique**: work is posted; peers leave written comments on sticky notes (low social risk, asynchronous)
- **Author-driven rounds**: the presenter asks the group a specific question ("Does my evidence support my claim?"), focusing attention
- **Warm/cool feedback**: timed rounds of affirmations then suggestions, common in design studio practice
- **Teacher-modeled critique**: the instructor critiques a sample (or their own) work first, demonstrating the moves before students try ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) style)
- **Gallery critique**: whole-group examination of one work, modeled by the instructor (Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" routine)
- **Critical friends / tuning protocols**: small groups follow timed steps — presenter describes, group questions, group discusses while presenter listens, presenter responds
- **Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise–Question–Suggest**: lightweight stems for younger or newer critics
- **Studio walk / design crit**: rotating stations with written sticky-note feedback, common in art and design education
- **Asynchronous critique**: structured comment threads in platforms like Peergrade or Perusall, using the same stems

### Target Learners
- Learners producing extended, revisable work (writing, design, code, projects) rather than single-attempt answers
- Adolescents and adults; younger learners need heavily simplified stems and facilitator mediation
- Learners developing evaluative judgment — critiquing peers builds the criteria knowledge they then apply to their own work [Assessment criteria used actively by learners improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Intermediate learners who have seen models of quality but not yet internalized criteria [~M]
- Learners in iterative production contexts: writing, design, code, studio arts, research
- Less suitable for absolute novices, who lack the evaluative schema to say anything useful; pair with [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or worked models first
- Learners old enough to articulate observations against criteria (upper elementary through adult) [Peer assessment works across grade levels but needs more scaffolding for younger students.](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]
- Learners producing iterative artifacts (writing, design, code, proofs) where revision is expected
- Reviewers benefit even when their own work is not critiqued — judging others' work builds evaluative judgment [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality improvement of complex products through iterative revision
- Development of evaluative judgment and internalized quality criteria
- Discourse and collaboration skills: giving specific, actionable, respectful feedback
- Revision as a habit: normalizing iteration and treating drafts as improvable
- Communication: articulating specific, evidence-based observations [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Quality criteria internalization: understanding what makes work good, not just producing work
- Revision and iteration: treating drafts as improvable [Authentic audiences and iterative critique improve the quality of student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]
- Evaluative judgment and self-assessment: the capacity to critique one's own work independently

### Instructions
1. Establish norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and model the protocol on an anonymous sample work before any live critique.
2. Present the work and the criteria or exemplar it should be judged against.
3. Run structured rounds — e.g., clarifying questions first, then warm feedback, then cool feedback — using prescribed sentence stems.
4. The maker responds only after all feedback is given, stating what they will revise.
5. Schedule time for revision and, ideally, a second critique round so learners see feedback translate into improved work.

Well-known formulations include Ron Berger's norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and the [Critical Friends](https://nsrfharmony.org/) protocol suite from the National School Reform Faculty. The protocol, not the teacher's discretion, carries the structure: it specifies what is looked at, in what order, and what kind of comments are permitted.
