# Critique Protocol

## Description
A critique protocol is a fixed structure — often with prescribed sentence stems, turn-taking rules, and norms — that governs how learners present work and how peers respond to it. Well-known variants include Ron Berger's "kind, specific, helpful" norms and the "I like / I wonder / I suggest" ladder. The protocol's purpose is to convert the social risk of public feedback into a reliable routine: critique targets the *work*, not the *maker*, and produces concrete revision steps rather than general praise or vague reactions.

## Design Implications

Structured critique improves the quality of both feedback and revision because it constrains attention to specific features of the work and forces evaluative comparison against criteria [Assessment criteria used actively by learners improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Unstructured peer feedback tends toward uninformative praise ("nice job") or personal judgment; protocols suppress both by prescribing what kind of statements are allowed. Critique also benefits the critic: evaluating others' work against criteria sharpens the evaluator's own understanding of quality [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M], since each peer's work functions as a contrasting case.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible model of quality — an exemplar or rubric — so critique has a shared reference point ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) or annotated exemplars serve this role)
- Norms explicitly taught and rehearsed before the first live critique; the protocol itself needs [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) and practice
- Time and expectation for **revision** — critique without a subsequent revision cycle signals that feedback is performative, and learners disengage
- A facilitator who enforces turn-taking and redirects personal or vague comments

#### Constraints
- Without a revision requirement, critique sessions degrade into ritual and produce no measurable learning gain [-M]
- Groups with low trust or status hierarchies (e.g., mixed-ability groups where one member dominates) suppress honest critique; protocols alone do not overcome social threat [-M]
- Novices can give inaccurate feedback; uncritically accepted peer errors can reinforce misconceptions [~M] — pair peer critique with instructor review for high-stakes work
- Overly rigid protocols applied to creative work can flatten risk-taking and stylistic divergence [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gallery critique**: work is displayed publicly and discussed by the whole group (Berger's whole-class model)
- **Critical friends / tuning protocols**: small groups follow timed rounds — presentation, clarifying questions, warm feedback, cool feedback
- **Studio critique**: art and design education's format, in which the maker listens silently while peers discuss the work, then responds
- **Written structured critique**: sentence stems on sticky notes or in LMS peer-review tools (e.g., [Peergrade](https://www.peergrade.io), [Eli Review](https://elireview.com)) for asynchronous settings

### Target Learners
- Learners producing extended, revisable work (writing, design, code, projects) rather than single-attempt answers
- Adolescents and adults; younger learners need heavily simplified stems and facilitator mediation
- Learners developing evaluative judgment — critiquing peers builds the criteria knowledge they then apply to their own work [Assessment criteria used actively by learners improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality improvement of complex products through iterative revision
- Development of evaluative judgment and internalized quality criteria
- Discourse and collaboration skills: giving specific, actionable, respectful feedback

### Instructions
1. Establish norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and model the protocol on an anonymous sample work before any live critique.
2. Present the work and the criteria or exemplar it should be judged against.
3. Run structured rounds — e.g., clarifying questions first, then warm feedback, then cool feedback — using prescribed sentence stems.
4. The maker responds only after all feedback is given, stating what they will revise.
5. Schedule time for revision and, ideally, a second critique round so learners see feedback translate into improved work.

## Examples
**[Expeditionary Learning / EL Education](https://eleducation.org)** — Ron Berger's critique norms ("kind, specific, helpful") and multiple-draft revision cycles are embedded across EL Education schools' project-based curriculum.

**[High Tech High](https://www.hightechhigh.org)** — project-based school network using gallery critique and protocol-driven peer feedback on exhibition-ready student work.

**[Eli Review](https://elireview.com)** — Michigan State-developed peer feedback platform that structures written peer critique with rubric-anchored comments and revision plans.
