# Role-Reversal 2.0 (Flipping the Teacher)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students create videos in which they explain concepts, demonstrate procedures, or teach material to an audience of peers — filming themselves, their group work, or screencasts. The act of producing an explanation forces learners to organize, articulate, and monitor their own understanding, and the resulting videos form a reusable repository for future classes. It is a technology-mediated form of learning-by-teaching in which the "tutee" is an imagined or actual peer audience.

## Design Implications

Role reversal converts passive reception into generative work: preparing to teach requires retrieving, organizing, and verbalizing knowledge, which produces deeper processing than restudying [Learning-by-teaching improves retention and understanding relative to restudying.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Critically, the benefit comes from the *teaching behavior itself*, not merely the expectation of teaching — students who expect to teach but do not actually explain show little gain [Fiorella & Mayer (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.07.001) [+S]. Video production therefore must include genuine explanation, not just a polished artifact. Tutors also benefit from detecting and repairing tutee confusion, so peer viewing and feedback rounds amplify the effect [Roscoe & Chi (2007)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543073001075) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Video creation tools (screencasting or camera) with a low technical barrier to entry
- Clear guidelines on video length, audience, and required content, plus assessment criteria shared in advance
- A feedback or viewing structure so videos have a real audience ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) for learning, not just of it)
- Instructor review to catch and remediate misconceptions before videos are archived for reuse

#### Constraints
- Students who script and read verbatim from notes may engage in shallow copying rather than generative explanation [~M] — require unscripted or minimally scripted explanation to force retrieval
- High production demands (editing, effects) consume working memory and time on media craft rather than content [~M]; keep the format deliberately simple
- Student-made videos can propagate errors if archived without instructor vetting [-M] — misconception-laden videos reused by future classes entrench errors
- Students with low self-efficacy or strong camera anxiety may experience the task as threatening rather than generative [~W]; offer alternatives such as audio or screencast-only formats

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual explainer videos vs. group-produced videos (groups distribute cognitive load and add discussion)
- Screencast walkthroughs of problem solutions vs. talking-head conceptual explanations
- Peer feedback rounds before final submission, converting the video into a draft for revision
- Teachable-agent variants (e.g., [Betty's Brain](https://teachableagents.org)) where students teach a computer agent instead of a camera

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary learners who already have basic familiarity with the material and can retrieve and reorganize it [Fiorella & Mayer (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.07.001) [+M]
- Learners who benefit from articulating reasoning aloud; weaker for complete novices, who lack enough structure to explain anything coherent [~M]
- Students building communication and metacognitive monitoring skills alongside content knowledge

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and integration — explaining forces learners to connect ideas rather than restate them [Learning-by-teaching improves retention and understanding relative to restudying.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive monitoring — gaps in understanding surface immediately when one must teach
- Communication and domain discourse skills — practicing the genre of expert explanation

### Instructions
1. Assign a focused topic and audience ("explain X to a student one level below you"); share assessment criteria up front ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Have students prepare by studying source material and drafting a brief outline — not a full script — to prompt retrieval rather than reading.
3. Students record short (3–7 minute) videos, applying and articulating the concept ([Application](../elements/application.md)); require them to explain *why*, not just *what*.
4. Run structured peer viewing with feedback against the criteria, so students detect gaps in their own and others' explanations.
5. Instructor reviews videos, flags misconceptions, and gives task- and process-level feedback [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
6. Curate vetted videos into a class repository for future cohorts, with instructor annotation of any known limitations.

## Examples
- Engineering courses where students create short videos explaining design principles and trade-offs in their prototypes; the best videos are archived as orientation material for the next cohort.
- A statistics course using screencast walkthroughs: students narrate their solution to a novel problem, exposing their decision points for instructor diagnosis.
- Language courses where students record themselves teaching a grammar point to an imagined beginner, then receive peer feedback on accuracy and clarity.
