# Have Explicit Conversations About Emotions and Social Issues, Including Issues of Social Justice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy treats emotions and contested social realities — race, class, gender, disability, power, exclusion — as legitimate, planned subjects of classroom talk, taught with the same deliberateness as any other content. Explicit means named and scheduled: emotion vocabulary is taught rather than assumed, and social issues are raised as planned curriculum rather than only when an incident forces them. The alternative is not neutrality. A classroom that never names these things still teaches something about them — that they are unspeakable here — and the learners for whom they are daily experience are the ones who learn it most clearly.

## Design Implications

The emotions half rests on a fairly direct finding: emotional competence is teachable, and programmes that teach it explicitly and sequentially produce gains in social-emotional skills, behaviour, and academic achievement [SEL programs improve social emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S] [SEL Programs Improve Behavior And Achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [+S], with effects detectable at follow-up [Social-emotional learning benefits persist at follow-up](../claims/sel-benefits-persist-follow-up.md) [+M]. Vocabulary is the load-bearing part — a learner with words for what they feel can report it, and a learner without them acts it out.

The social-issues half rests on a different and less settled base. Jagers and colleagues' argument for transformative SEL is that competence-focused programmes can teach learners to regulate their responses to unjust conditions without ever examining the conditions, and that self-management taught this way functions as compliance training. Adding explicit discussion of power and identity is proposed as the corrective. That argument is well-developed conceptually and only partially tested empirically — treat it as a well-reasoned design commitment rather than an established effect [~W]. What is better supported is that structured, high-quality discussion improves reasoning and comprehension [Discussion Quality Drives Comprehension](../claims/discussion-quality-drives-comprehension.md) [+M], and that guided perspective-taking shifts intergroup attitudes [Building Empathy Improves Intergroup Attitudes](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Established norms for disagreement, set before any contested topic is raised ([Norm Setting](norm_setting.md), [Class Discussion Norms](class-discussion-norms.md))
- Explicit emotion vocabulary taught as content, not assumed as background ([Feelings Vocabulary](../elements/feelings-vocabulary.md))
- Relational groundwork: learners will not speak candidly with a teacher they do not trust [Teacher Student Relationships Improve Engagement](../claims/teacher-student-relationships-improve-engagement.md) [+M]
- Planned curricular slots, so these conversations are not only reactive to incidents
- A teacher prepared to sit with discomfort rather than close the topic down when it becomes tense
- Genuine option not to disclose — participation must be possible without self-exposure

#### Constraints
- Learners from affected groups are routinely conscripted as spokespeople or living examples; this is the most common and most damaging failure of the practice [-S]
- Discussion without norms and structure can license harm, leaving those targeted worse off than if the topic had never been raised [-M]
- Unfacilitated disagreement tends to entrench positions rather than shift them; the structure has to require engaging with the opposing case [Structured Discussion Approaches Improve Comprehension](../claims/structured-discussion-approaches-improve-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Emotion talk without vocabulary and structure becomes therapeutic in register and instructional in nothing — and teachers are not therapists [-M]
- Content can be genuinely activating for learners with trauma histories; advance notice and an exit route are requirements, not courtesies [-M]
- The evidence base is uneven: the SEL-competence half is well supported, the social-justice half considerably less so, and citing the former to justify the latter overstates what is known [~W]
- Community and institutional context constrains what can be taught, and a teacher who ignores that may lose the ability to teach any of it [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Emotion vocabulary curriculum** — structured teaching of feeling words embedded in academic content, as in RULER's Feeling Words approach ([Emotion Identification and Labeling](emotion_identification_and_labeling.md))
- **Circles** — a structured turn-taking format where every voice is heard and no one is required to speak ([Community-Building Circles](community-building-circles.md), [Restorative Circles](restorative-circles.md))
- **Literature as the entry point** — texts carry the social issue, so learners analyse a character's situation before their own ([Read-Alouds with Deliberate Topics](read-alouds_with_deliberate_topics.md))
- **Structured controversy** — assigned positions and required side-swapping on genuinely contested questions ([Structured Academic Controversy](structured-academic-controversy.md))
- **Explicit perspective work** — planned examination of how the same situation is experienced from different positions ([Explicit Discussion of Perspectives](explicit_discussion_of_perspectives.md))
- **Seminar formats** — text-anchored discussion with defined roles and turn-taking ([Socratic Seminar](socratic-seminar.md))

### Target Learners
- All learners, but with the caution that costs and benefits fall unevenly: the learners for whom the topic is lived experience carry more risk in the conversation
- Younger learners, for whom the emotions half is developmentally central and the vocabulary genuinely new
- Adolescents, who are already conducting these conversations without adults and generally without structure
- Learners in diverse classrooms, where the issues are present in the room whether or not they are named
- Learners in homogeneous classrooms, where the absence of the conversation is easiest to mistake for the absence of the issue
- Requires particular care with learners who have trauma histories connected to the topic

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotion vocabulary and the ability to identify and report internal states
- Self-management and social awareness as taught competencies [SEL programs improve social emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S]
- Perspective-taking and reduced intergroup bias [Building Empathy Improves Intergroup Attitudes](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Belonging — the sense that one's experience is speakable in this room [Belonging Interventions Improve Outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Reasoning about contested questions, including the ability to state an opposing case fairly [Argumentation Improves Reasoning](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Reduced exclusionary discipline, where relational and empathic approaches replace punitive ones [Brief Intervention Empathic Discipline Cuts Suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Build norms and relationships first.** Do not open a contested topic in a group that has not agreed how disagreement works ([Norm Setting](norm_setting.md)).
2. **Teach the vocabulary before requiring the talk.** Learners need words with useful precision — frustrated, excluded, dismissed — before they can name experience accurately ([Feelings Vocabulary](../elements/feelings-vocabulary.md)).
3. **Plan the topics into the curriculum.** Schedule them alongside the content they connect to, so the conversation is not always a response to something going wrong.
4. **Anchor in a text or case.** Start from a character, a source, or a scenario rather than from learners' own lives, so participation does not require disclosure ([Read-Alouds with Deliberate Topics](read-alouds_with_deliberate_topics.md)).
5. **Give notice and an exit.** Say what is coming and make it explicit that stepping out or staying silent is a legitimate option.
6. **Structure the turn-taking.** Use a circle, a protocol, or assigned positions so airtime is distributed rather than claimed ([Community-Building Circles](community-building-circles.md), [Structured Academic Controversy](structured-academic-controversy.md)).
7. **Never assign a learner to represent a group.** Speak from the text and from research; if a learner chooses to speak from experience, that is their decision to make unprompted.
8. **Close deliberately.** Summarize what was said, name what remains unresolved, and say what happens next — a conversation left open at the bell does its damage in the corridor ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)).

## Examples

**Feeling-words curriculum embedded in literature:** Emotion vocabulary is taught through the characters in the class text — naming what a character feels and the evidence for it — so the vocabulary is practised at one remove before it is applied to the self.

**Weekly circle in a primary classroom:** A fixed, predictable slot where each learner may speak or pass on a prompt; the predictability is what makes the format usable when something difficult actually arises.

**Structured controversy on a curricular question:** Learners are assigned positions on a contested historical or civic question, argue them, swap sides, and then attempt a joint statement — the swap being what prevents entrenchment.

**Planned response protocol for current events:** An agreed sequence for when something significant happens outside school — acknowledge it, offer a structured space, provide an exit — prepared in advance rather than improvised on the morning.
