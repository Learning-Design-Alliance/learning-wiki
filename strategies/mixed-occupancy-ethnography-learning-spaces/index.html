Combine quantitative occupancy monitoring with ethnographic observation and field interviews to target qualitative data collection on learning-space transitions¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The study's method pairs automated occupancy data (collected 24 h per day, 7 days per week) with naturalistic non-participant observation, sociograms and brief 5–10 min field interviews. The occupancy data guided "a more resource-intensive targeting of ethnographic observations and field interviews which allowed us to interpret the meaning and nuance of those transitions". Thematic analysis of qualitative data in NVivo, with reflexivity to minimise bias, completed the triangulation, which the authors say supported a more complete understanding and more resourceful collection of authentic data.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Requires occupancy monitoring technology generating anonymous data for the spaces of interest
  • Requires ethical approval; this study obtained approval for brief verbal consent instead of informed consent for 5–10-min field interviews

Constraints¶

  • Anonymity of occupancy data meant student identities could only be inferred and required observation and interviews to confirm demographic details

Target Learners¶

  • university students in timetabled and non-timetabled learning spaces

Target Learning Goals¶

  • understanding student engagement with spatial, pedagogic and agentic transitions

Affordances¶

  • Spatial Pedagogic Agentic Transitions Tensions Taxonomy

Claims¶

  • Students perceive the breakout space as more democratic than the teacher-owned lecture theatre, feeling freer to direct questions and take learning risks [+W]
  • The type 2 configuration creates a 'bridge' hour of self-directed pedagogic potential between formal teaching in which students discuss difficult lecture content [+W]
  • The fixed power dynamic of the lecture theatre produces 'failed', hidden and postponed pedagogic interactions among confused students [+W]
  • Feelings of ownership of the breakout space shift across the academic year among user groups, constraining how different cohorts can use it [+W]
  • On-ramp engagement is higher in the type 2 timetable: 52% of 1st year students engaged versus 20% of 2nd year students in type 1 [+W]
  • In an observational comparison of timetable configurations, 43% of students meaningfully engaged in off-ramp transitions during the type 2 'bridge' configuration versus 19% in type 1 [+W]
