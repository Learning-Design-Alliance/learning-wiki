# Gallery Walk

## Description
Stations, posters, or artifacts are set up around the classroom, on walls, or on tables. Small groups of learners travel from station to station together, performing a task or responding to a prompt at each stop, with written or spoken conversation as the intended outcome. Variations include groups creating posters and acting as tour guides, commenting on peers' work with sticky notes, or rotating through learner-created video tutorials.

## Design Implications

Gallery Walk is a structured form of [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md): it converts passive whole-class presentation time into simultaneous small-group engagement, which is the mechanism behind the exam-performance benefits of active learning [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Its learning value depends on the quality of the task at each station — prompts that require explanation, evaluation, or comparison produce deeper processing than prompts that ask only for recall, because articulating ideas to peers drives [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)-like elaboration [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Reading and building on prior groups' annotations also exposes learners to multiple perspectives on the same problem, supporting abstraction [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Physical space to arrange 4–8 stations with clear sightlines and circulation paths
- Well-designed station tasks: a prompt, artifact, or problem at each stop, with an explicit response format (sticky note, recording sheet, verbal report)
- Clear rotation rules — time per station, movement signal, and role assignments within groups
- A synthesis phase afterward in which the instructor surfaces patterns across stations; without this, insights remain scattered across the room

#### Constraints
- Crowded or small rooms undermine circulation and can turn the activity into social time [-M]
- Tasks requiring sustained individual concentration (extended writing, complex computation) fit poorly with the 3–7 minute station format [~M]
- Groups that arrive later see earlier groups' responses, which can anchor thinking and reduce independent generation [-W]
- Without a timekeeper or visible timer, uneven pacing leaves some stations under-visited and conversations truncated [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Commentary walk:** student work is posted; peers leave structured feedback (e.g., "glow and grow" notes), enacting peer assessment
- **Poster carousel:** groups build a poster at their home station, then rotate to annotate others' — a jigsaw-like variant
- **Digital gallery walk:** stations become slides or Padlet/Jamboard boards; useful for hybrid or large-enrollment settings
- **Question generation:** each station poses a different sub-question of one larger problem; the synthesis phase assembles the full picture

### Target Learners
- Works across ages and subject areas; particularly valuable for learners who benefit from movement and from processing ideas verbally before writing
- Effective for mixed-ability groups when station tasks are tiered or open-ended, since every group can contribute at its own level
- Less suitable for learners who need extended quiet time to process; offering a brief individual preview of station prompts mitigates this

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through comparison and discussion of multiple cases and perspectives
- Peer feedback and evaluation skills — critiquing work against criteria
- Collaborative knowledge building and communication of ideas in writing
- Review and consolidation of previously taught content

### Instructions
1. Identify 4–8 prompts, problems, or artifacts aligned to the learning goal; chunk complex content so each station is digestible in a short visit [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
2. Post materials around the room and prepare a recording sheet or sticky-note protocol for responses
3. Assign small groups (3–4 learners) and, where useful, [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) such as recorder, timekeeper, and reporter to ensure all members participate
4. Model the expected response quality at one station before rotating — a brief [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of a strong written comment raises the level of all subsequent contributions
5. Run timed rotations; monitor circulation and use [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and targeted [Questioning](questioning.md) at stations where discussion stalls
6. Close with whole-class synthesis: surface patterns, resolve contradictions between stations, and connect to the day's objective, using [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
7. Collect written artifacts as [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) evidence of individual and group understanding

## Related Strategies
- Think-Pair-Share — the same discussion-first logic compressed into a single location rather than distributed across stations
- Jigsaw — complementary group-rotation structure; gallery walks can serve as the expert-group reporting phase
- Peer Review — commentary variants of gallery walks are essentially structured peer review
- Station Rotation (blended learning) — the technology-integrated cousin, where stations include digital activities

## Related Elements
- [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) — the synthesis phase that consolidates station learning
- [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) — the core interaction structure within each rotating group
- [Application](../elements/application.md) — station tasks should require applying, not just recalling, content
- [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) — writing and voicing reasoning at each station is what produces the learning benefit
- [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) — written artifacts double as formative evidence

## Patterns That Use This Strategy
- [Collaborative Learning](../patterns/collaborative-learning.md) — gallery walk is a canonical structured collaborative format
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — stations can each present a different case or aspect of a case for comparative analysis
- [Gagné's 9 Events of Instruction](../patterns/gagnés-9-events-of-instruction.md) — supports "eliciting performance" and "providing feedback" events through peer interaction

## Examples
- **Science poster sessions (secondary):** groups design experiment posters, then rotate to critique peers' methods and conclusions with structured sticky-note feedback before revision
- **[Peer Instruction](https://blog.peerinstruction.net/)**-style courses (Harvard, Eric Mazur): ConcepTest results posted at stations; groups rotate to argue through disagreements before whole-class resolution
- **History document analysis:** 5 primary-source documents posted around the room; groups annotate each with sourcing questions, then synthesize across sources in the closing discussion
- **Digital variant:** large lecture courses use shared slides or Padlet boards (https://padlet.com) as virtual stations, with groups rotating on a timer and the instructor projecting emerging themes
