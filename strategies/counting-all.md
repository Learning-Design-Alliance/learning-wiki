# Counting All

## Description
Counting All is the earliest arithmetic strategy children use to solve additive word problems: given 4 + 3, the learner counts out a set of 4 objects, counts out 3 more, then counts the entire combined set from 1 to 7. It is typically enacted with physical objects, fingers, or drawings, and is developmentally prior to [counting on](counting-on.md) and derived-fact strategies. In instruction, it is treated not as an error to eliminate but as a legitimate, meaningful first strategy that grounds the concept of addition as combining sets.

## Design Implications

Counting All embodies the developmental progression documented in [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md): children's solution strategies move from direct modeling (counting all) through counting strategies to derived facts and recall [Carpenter et al. showed that teaching aligned with children's strategy progression improves achievement.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because it requires holding a count in working memory while tracking objects, it is working-memory intensive, and instruction should reduce extraneous load by using organized arrangements of objects [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. The strategy's value is conceptual: it makes the part–whole structure of addition physically visible before symbolic notation is introduced.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Countable objects, fingers, or drawings that can be physically grouped and combined
- Word problems or number stories whose semantics match the counting action (e.g., join and separate problems)
- Teacher familiarity with the developmental sequence from direct modeling to abstract strategies, as in [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cgi-for-math.md)

#### Constraints
- Counting All becomes inefficient and error-prone as numbers grow; learners who remain dependent on it past the point of efficiency fall behind peers who transition to counting on and derived facts [-M] — persistent use signals a need for intervention, not more of the same practice
- Counting errors compound: miscounting either subset corrupts the total, and learners rarely detect the error [-W]
- If instruction treats it as the *goal* rather than a waypoint, learners may plateau; the strategy must be deliberately superseded [Automaticity of basic facts frees working memory for higher-level reasoning.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **With objects (direct modeling):** physical counters moved and counted — the canonical form
- **With fingers:** counts represented on fingers when objects are unavailable
- **With drawings/tallies:** a step toward abstract representation
- **Counting All from a hidden set:** one addend is visible, the other must be remembered, creating pressure toward [counting on](counting-on.md)

### Target Learners
- Preschool through Grade 1 learners encountering addition for the first time
- Learners with mathematical learning difficulties, for whom concrete direct modeling is the accessible entry point [~M]
- Older struggling learners as a diagnostic: observing whether a child counts all, counts on, or derives facts reveals their current strategy level

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual foundation for addition and subtraction as combining and separating
- One-to-one correspondence and cardinality
- Part–whole reasoning that later supports fact families and missing-addend problems

### Instructions
1. Pose a join or separate word problem with small numbers (sums under 10) grounded in a familiar context.
2. Have learners model each quantity with objects, acting out the story ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)).
3. Combine the sets and count the total from 1, emphasizing that the combined count answers "how many in all?"
4. Record the count with drawings or equations to connect the action to symbolic notation.
5. As learners become fluent, increase numbers or hide one set to create pressure toward more efficient strategies, following the progression in [Develop Understanding](../patterns/develop-understanding.md).

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction classrooms** — Teachers diagnose each child's strategy (counting all vs. counting on) from problem-solving interviews and sequence word problems to prompt strategy advancement (see [CGI for Math](../patterns/cgi-for-math.md)).
- **Number Talks (Parrish, 2010)** — Early-years number talks ask children to share *how* they found a total, making counting-all strategies public and comparable to more efficient peer strategies.
- **DreamBox Learning** — Adaptive early-numeracy lessons use virtual counters and ten-frames, first accepting counting-all solutions then nudging toward counting on as efficiency grows.
