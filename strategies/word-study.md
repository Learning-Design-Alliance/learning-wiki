# Word Study

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
Word study replaces rote spelling memorization with structured investigation of how words work. Students examine sets of words with shared features — sounds, spelling patterns, meanings, or morphological elements — and compare and contrast them to discover spelling generalizations themselves. Instruction is differentiated by developmental spelling stage, typically assessed with a spelling inventory, so each group works on patterns at the edge of what they already control.

## Design Implications

Word study operationalizes [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) for orthography: learners induce the rule from sorted exemplars rather than receiving it as a stated fact to memorize. Its core activity — sorting words into categories and contrasting minimal pairs (e.g., *hop/hope*, *hard c/soft c*) — is a direct application of case comparison, which reliably improves discrimination and transfer [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Because pattern knowledge generalizes to untaught words, word study builds the automatic word recognition that frees working memory for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S].

#### Constraints
- Pure discovery is not sufficient for all learners: some students, especially those with weak phonological awareness or dyslexia, need the pattern named and explained explicitly rather than induced [~M] — word study works best as guided discovery, not unguided exploration
- Requires a substantial shift from traditional Friday-test drill; teachers accustomed to memorization-based spelling may implement sorts as new forms of drill, losing the comparison and generalization work [-W]
- Differentiated grouping multiplies the teacher's preparation and facilitation load; poorly managed groups default to unstructured play with word cards [-W]
- Assessment must change accordingly: testing only the studied list rewards memorization and masks whether pattern knowledge transferred to new words [-M]
- Pure discovery of patterns without guidance is inefficient for students with weak phonological awareness; some learners need explicit teacher framing of the feature before sorting can be productive [-M]
- Developmental grouping can leave struggling older students working on patterns far below grade level, which risks disengagement and reduced access to grade-level text [~M]
- Requires sustained teacher expertise in the developmental progression; poorly sequenced sorts (too many features at once) overload attention and produce guessing rather than analysis [-M]
- Transfer to writing depends on follow-through: pattern knowledge assessed only through sorts may not appear in students' independent writing without [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in authentic composition contexts [~M]

- **Speed sorts, blind sorts, and writing sorts** — successive variations that shift from sound-based to visual to memory-based sorting as the pattern consolidates
- **Word hunts** — students search authentic reading and writing for words fitting the pattern, connecting word study to real text
- **Digital adaptations** — drag-and-drop sorting tools and interactive word walls replicate the physical sort in online settings
- **Closed sorts** (teacher-defined categories) for introducing a feature; **open sorts** (student-defined) for extending analysis; **speed sorts** and **blind sorts** for building automaticity
- **Word hunts** extend sorting into connected text, linking the pattern to authentic reading

### Instructions
1. **Assess and group.** Administer a spelling inventory; group students by developmental stage rather than grade level.
2. **Select and sequence words.** Choose 15–25 words that exemplify the target pattern and contrast it with a previously mastered pattern, keeping set size within working memory limits [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].
3. **Model the sort.** Demonstrate categorizing a few words aloud, naming the features being noticed — a brief [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)-style model before students sort independently.
4. **Sort and discuss.** Students sort word cards, then explain their categories to a partner; require verbalization of the discovered generalization, not just correct placement.
5. **Provide feedback and reflect.** Confirm or correct sorts, prompt students to state the rule in their own words, and record it in the word study notebook.
6. **Extend and transfer.** Assign a word hunt in authentic text and a writing sort with new, untaught words to assess whether the pattern transferred.

## Description
Word study is an approach to spelling and word knowledge instruction in which students actively investigate regularities in words — sound, pattern, and meaning relationships — rather than memorizing arbitrary word lists. Students examine words with shared features, compare and contrast them through sorting activities, and discover spelling generalizations they can apply to unfamiliar words. Instruction is differentiated: teachers assess each student's developmental spelling stage (typically via a spelling inventory) and group accordingly, following the developmental sequence from emergent to derivational relations described in the Words Their Way framework (Bear et al., 2020).

Word study aligns with research showing that instruction connecting spelling to phonics and meaning supports generalization better than whole-word memorization [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. ### Instructions
1. **Assess and group.** Administer a spelling inventory; place students in developmental groups and select a feature for each group.
2. **Introduce the pattern.** Model a sort with a few exemplar words, thinking aloud about the feature ([Provide guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md)); contrast exemplars with oddballs so the rule boundary is visible.
3. **Sort and discuss.** Students sort the full word set individually or in pairs, then explain their categories ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)); the teacher circulates and prompts justification ([Provide feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
4. **Extend across the week.** Rotate activities — speed sorts, blind sorts, word hunts in connected text, and notebook entries — distributing practice across days ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)); spaced revisiting of earlier features supports retention [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
5. **Reflect and assess.** Students write generalizations in their own words ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)); assess with a new-word sort and a writing sample to check transfer, not memorized-list recall.
