# Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)

## Description
Academic Choice empowers learners by providing them with options in their learning and involves a three-phase process: planning, working, and reflecting. Teachers decide on the goal of the lesson or activity, then give learners a list of options for what to learn and/or how to go about their learning in order to reach the defined goal.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teachers need to pre-plan the learning activities, the different choices offered, and the methods of planning and reflecting for the learners.
#### Constraints
- It may be difficult for teachers to fit Academic Choice into an already full schedule.  If not implemented correctly, learners may struggle to make effective choices.

### Target Learners

- Elementary school students, but can be adapted for older learners

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Increase engagement, promote self-directed learning, foster deeper thinking, enhance productivity, reduce behavior problems

### Affordances
- Supports intrinsic motivation by meeting the needs for competence, belonging, and autonomy. Allows learners to work from their areas of strength and interest. Mirrors the natural learning cycle by allowing learners to generate goals, interact with materials, and make sense of their experiences.

### Personalization
- Having choices allows learners to work from their areas of strength and personal interest. They're then more likely to feel invested in their work and to draw personal meaning from it.

## Assessment Evidence
- Observe learner engagement and motivation levels. Assess the quality of learner work. Collect learner feedback on their experience.

## Impact
- When learners have choices in their learning, they become highly engaged and productive. They’re excited about learning and sharing their knowledge. They’re likely to think more deeply and creatively, work with more persistence, and use a range of academic skills and strategies. In addition, research has generally found that children have fewer behavior problems when they have regular opportunities to make choices in their learning, a finding supported by anecdotal evidence from teachers.

## Examples
- A third grade class is studying insects, and the teacher wants the children to get some basic information about these animals. The teacher gives children choices in how to get such information, including by reading a book about insects, listening to a recording about insects, interviewing someone who studies insects, or observing and recording insects’ appearance and behavior.
