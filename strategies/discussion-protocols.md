# Discussion Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Discussion protocols are explicit, repeatable structures — turn-taking rules, sentence stems, role assignments, and timing constraints — that organize classroom conversation. Rather than asking learners to "discuss," the instructor specifies who speaks, in what order, for how long, and with what obligation to respond to prior contributions. Well-known examples include Think-Pair-Share, Socratic Seminar, and the Harkness method.

## Design Implications

Protocols work because they convert discussion from a social performance into a structured reasoning task: every learner is expected to produce and evaluate ideas, not merely listen [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Structure also raises the quality of peer talk — protocols that require learners to agree, disagree, or build on a prior turn push talk toward explanation and justification rather than unconnected opinions [Structured peer discussion improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/peer-discussion-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task or question genuinely worth discussing — protocols cannot rescue low-level recall prompts
- Explicit norms and, ideally, sentence stems ("I agree with ___ because…", "An alternative is…") that model academic discourse
- Individual think time before talk, so contributions are reasoned rather than reactive ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), wait time)
- A visible record — board notes, shared document — so the discussion accumulates rather than evaporates ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- A task or question genuinely worth discussing — one with defensible multiple positions or genuine ambiguity ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Explicit teaching of the protocol itself before first use, including sentence stems ("I agree with X because…", "What evidence supports that?")
- Norms for respectful disagreement, established and revisited ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Protocols add overhead; for simple factual checks, the structure costs more than it returns [~M]
- Over-scripted protocols can suppress authentic disagreement and reduce discussion to turn-taking compliance [-W]
- Dominant speakers can still capture loosely structured rounds; without role rotation or timed turns, participation stays unequal [-M]
- Protocols depend on a shared task; in heterogeneous groups with divergent preparation, discussion degrades into information asymmetry [~M]
- Over-scripted protocols can produce mechanical turn-taking where students perform the format without engaging ideas [-M]
- Protocols require rehearsal; the first several uses are slower and rougher, and instructors who abandon them at this stage never realize the benefit [-W]
- Groups without established norms can amplify status differences — high-status students' ideas get taken up regardless of quality [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Think-Pair-Share** — individual writing, pair rehearsal, whole-class share; lowest overhead, scales to any class size
- **Socratic Seminar / Harkness** — text-based, student-led questioning in a circle; high preparation demands, strong for argumentation
- **Structured controversy / assigned positions** — learners argue assigned stances before synthesizing ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md))
- **Protocol stacks** — combining a check-in ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)), a timed round, and a synthesis step for longer discussions
- **Think-Pair-Share** — individual writing, then pair talk, then whole-class share; lowest-risk entry point
- **Socratic seminar** — text-anchored, student-led questioning in inner/outer circle formation
- **Jigsaw** — each member learns one piece in an expert group, then teaches it in a home group
- **Structured academic controversy** — pairs argue one side, switch sides, then drop roles and seek consensus
- **Protocol stacks** — combining stems, roles, and timing (e.g., "each claim must cite the text; each response must begin with a stem")

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults with sufficient language proficiency to justify and critique ideas verbally
- Reluctant participants, who benefit from guaranteed, structured turns [~M]
- Multilingual learners, when paired with sentence stems and [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+W]
- Less suitable for novices lacking enough prior knowledge to contribute meaningfully — activate it first ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from sentence stems that model academic syntax
- Less necessary for small seminars of highly self-regulated students, who can sustain productive unstructured conversation [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through explaining and resolving ideas with peers [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Argumentation and reasoning: constructing, critiquing, and revising claims ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Academic language and discourse norms
- Perspective-taking and collaborative sensemaking
- Reasoning and justification: articulating and defending claims [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Perspective-taking: encountering and evaluating interpretations different from one's own
- Conceptual understanding: surfacing and resolving misconceptions through [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md)

### Instructions
1. Pose a discussion-worthy question or task tied to the learning goal; ensure learners have a common text, case, or artifact ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)).
2. Give individual think time — silent writing or [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) notes — so every learner arrives with a position.
3. Run the protocol: specify turn order, timing, and response obligations (agree/disagree/build-on), rotating roles if used.
4. Monitor and collect evidence of reasoning on a visible record rather than evaluating individual contributions mid-round.
5. Debrief both the content and the process — what claims held up, and what made the talk productive — so norms improve over time.

## Examples
- **Think-Pair-Share** (Lyman, 1981) — the most widely used protocol; individual think time, pair rehearsal, whole-class share, used across K-12 and higher education.
- **Socratic Seminar** — text-based student-led questioning in inner/outer circles; common in humanities curricula such as the College Board's Pre-AP program.
- **Peer Instruction** ([https://peerinstruction.cs.harvard.edu](https://peerinstruction.cs.harvard.edu)) — Eric Mazur's protocol: individual clicker vote, structured peer discussion, revote; documented large conceptual gains in physics.
- **Harkness Method** (Phillips Exeter Academy) — oval-table, student-led discussion with an observer role, used across Exeter's curriculum.
- **Socratic seminars in ELA classrooms** — students discuss a shared text in an inner circle while outer-circle peers track evidence use, then swap; widely used in International Baccalaureate and Expeditionary Learning (EL Education) schools ([EL Education protocols](https://eleducation.org))
- **Structured Academic Controversy** — developed by David and Roger Johnson at the University of Minnesota's Cooperative Learning Center; used in teacher professional development and social studies classrooms ([Cooperative Learning Center](https://www.co-operation.org))
- **Accountable Talk** — the Institute for Learning (University of Pittsburgh) protocol family with three talk moves (accountability to the learning community, knowledge, and rigorous thinking) ([IFL](https://ifl.pitt.edu))
