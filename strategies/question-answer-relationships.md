# Question Answer Relationships

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), `q2`–`q3` · 1 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Question Answer Relationships (QAR) is a questioning strategy in which learners are explicitly taught a taxonomy of question types based on the source of the answer: **Right There** (answer stated in one place in the text), **Think and Search** (answer assembled from multiple places in the text), **Author and Me** (answer requires combining text information with prior knowledge), and **On My Own** (answer comes entirely from the learner's own knowledge and experience). Instruction proceeds by labeling questions with the category, having learners sort questions into categories, and finally having learners generate their own questions of each type. The goal is metacognitive: learners stop treating all questions the same way and instead ask "where does this answer live?" before searching.

## Design Implications

QAR improves comprehension by making the question–text relationship explicit, which reduces unproductive search and helps learners recognize when an answer cannot be found by scanning alone [Making text structure and task demands explicit improves comprehension outcomes.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. The National Reading Panel identified question generation and comprehension strategy instruction — of which QAR is a canonical example — as having solid experimental support [+S]. Because the four categories are few, concrete, and mutually distinguishable, the taxonomy itself is easy to learn and apply, consistent with principles of [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) and [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts rich enough to support all four question types (narrative and informational)
- Explicit modeling of how to classify a question and justify the classification ([Think-Aloud](../strategies/think-aloud-modeling.md) works well here)
- Gradual release: teacher-labeled questions → student sorting → student question generation
- Discussion of *why* a question belongs to a category, not just the label
- A set of authentic questions spanning all four QAR types, drawn from texts learners are actually reading
- Explicit teacher modeling of how to classify a question and justify the classification ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) style narration where available)
- Gradual release: modeled → guided sorting → independent application, with learners eventually writing their own questions
- Texts rich enough to support both literal and inferential questions

#### Constraints
- Over-reliance on the labels can reduce questioning to a sorting exercise; learners must still construct the answers, not just categorize them [-W]
- Less effective when texts are trivially short or when all questions are surface-level "Right There" items — the taxonomy has nothing to discriminate
- Transfer beyond the trained genre is limited if instruction uses only one text type [~M]
- If teachers use QAR labels to justify asking mostly low-level questions, the strategy reinforces shallow reading rather than correcting it [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when QAR is taught as a labeling drill on isolated questions rather than applied to connected text [Strategy instruction transfers poorly when taught decontextualized from authentic reading.](https://doi.org/10.2307/747623) [-M]
- Weaker for learners whose decoding is not yet automatic — the strategy presumes comprehension-level processing [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Over-reliance on the four labels can lead to mechanical sorting without genuine evaluation of where an answer lives; teachers must keep justification of classifications central
- Less useful for tasks where the answer location is genuinely ambiguous or where questions are poorly constructed

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-category version**: some implementations collapse to "In My Head" vs. "In the Book" for younger learners
- **Question generation**: learners write their own questions of each type, shifting from answering to interrogating the text
- **Cross-curricular adaptation**: applied to primary sources in history or data interpretation in science, where "Author and Me" becomes "Source and Me"
- **Digital adaptation**: question-type badges in reading platforms prompt learners to identify the QAR type before answering
- Two-category simplified version (In the Book / In My Head) for younger or struggling readers
- Extended to writing: students generate questions of each type for peers, deepening understanding of question structure
- Adapted for content areas (science, social studies) where "Author and Me" questions require disciplinary prior knowledge
- Digital adaptations: question-type tagging in reading platforms and adaptive quizzing systems

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grade readers still treating comprehension as literal retrieval [+S]
- Struggling readers who over-apply a "find the word" strategy to inferential questions [~M]
- English learners, who benefit from the explicit, named categories [~M]
- Less necessary for proficient readers who already flexibly allocate search effort [~W]
- Upper elementary through secondary readers who can decode but answer comprehension questions inconsistently [+M]
- Struggling readers who treat all questions as literal look-up tasks and miss inferential demands
- English learners, when paired with [accessible vocabulary and syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) instruction, because the categories make question demands explicit
- Less beneficial for advanced readers who already flexibly allocate search effort [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension, especially distinguishing literal from inferential understanding
- Metacognitive monitoring: knowing when an answer requires text search versus reasoning
- Question generation as a comprehension and discussion skill
- Reading comprehension, particularly inferential and integrative questions
- Metacognitive awareness of task demands — knowing *what kind of thinking* a question requires
- Test-taking strategy and self-regulated reading
- Question generation as a higher-order comprehension activity

### Instructions
1. Introduce the four QAR categories with one worked example of each, modeling the classification decision aloud.
2. Present a short text with pre-written questions; classify each together, asking "where does this answer live?"
3. Have students sort a mixed question set independently, then compare and justify classifications in pairs.
4. Move to answering: for "Think and Search" and "Author and Me" questions, require learners to cite the text parts they combined.
5. Have students generate their own questions of each type and exchange them — question generation is itself a comprehension activity [+S].
6. Fade the labels, prompting only "What kind of question is this, and how do you know?"

## Description
Question Answer Relationships (QAR) is a metacognitive reading strategy in which learners are explicitly taught to categorize questions into types — "Right There" (answer stated in one place in the text), "Think and Search" (answer assembled across parts of the text), "Author and Me" (text clues combined with prior knowledge), and "On My Own" (answer drawn entirely from the learner's own knowledge) — and to select a search strategy matched to each type. Instruction typically moves from teacher-modeled classification, to guided sorting of questions, to learners generating and answering their own questions.

## Design Implications

QAR works because it makes the otherwise invisible logic of comprehension questions explicit, turning test-taking into a transferable reading strategy rather than a guessing game [Raphael's original studies showed improved comprehension and question-answering accuracy when students were taught QAR categories.](https://doi.org/10.2307/747623) [+M]. It functions as a form of strategy instruction: the learner learns *when and why* to apply a search process, not just the process itself. Because the categories force learners to distinguish text-based from knowledge-based reasoning, QAR also builds awareness of when inference is required — a key discriminator between skilled and unskilled readers.

### Instructions
1. Select a short text and a set of questions representing all four QAR types.
2. Model classification aloud: read a question, verbalize the decision ("the answer is stated right here, so it's Right There"), and answer it.
3. Guide learners to sort additional questions by type, requiring them to justify each classification with evidence from the text.
4. Have learners answer questions using the strategy matched to the type — text-locating for Right There/Think and Search, text-plus-knowledge integration for Author and Me, prior knowledge for On My Own.
5. Fade support: learners classify and answer independently, then generate their own questions of each type for [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or peer exchange.
6. Apply across texts and content areas until classification becomes automatic.
