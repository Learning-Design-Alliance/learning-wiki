# Have students test the three-segment jump model against real jumps by measuring body geometry and calibrating forces from knee-dominant and glute-dominant jumps

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article sketches a measurement activity in which students parameterize a real jumper into a three-segment model: the upper body first rotates to vertical through distance d1 = ℓcom1 (1−cos(90−α+β−γ)) under force F1, then β opens to 180 degrees with the feet fixed, giving d2 from the combined center-of-mass displacement. "F2 can be determined by measuring the jump height when ℓ1 starts at (and stays) vertical, essentially using only the knees (quad-dominant) for the jump", after which F1 follows from a glute-dominant jump, and the model predicts height for any initial (α, β, γ).

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Measurements of segment lengths ℓ1, ℓ2, ℓ3 and initial angles α, β, γ as indicated in the article's Fig. 6
- Two calibration jumps: one quad-dominant (upright torso) to determine F2, and one glute-dominant (very small α) to determine F1
#### Constraints
- The article warns that failure may reflect "diﬃculties in experimental measurement, dubious as sump-tions about the human body, or even incorrect simpliﬁcations of the physics", for example that muscle force may depend on angle (F1 = F1(α), F2 = F2(β)) or on the rate of change of angle

### Target Learners
- Introductory mechanics students
- Life and health sciences students studying biomechanics

### Target Learning Goals
- Model real human motion with physics
- Practice measuring and curve fitting
- Reason about what a model lacks by testing it against the real world

### Affordances
- Two Segment Jump Model
