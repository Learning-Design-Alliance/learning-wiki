# Connecting Languages

## Description
Connecting Languages is a strategy in which teachers explicitly prompt multilingual learners to compare their home language with English — identifying cognates, shared structures, and points of divergence. The teacher asks how the home language is the same as and different from English, hunts for words that sound alike and mean the same (or sound alike but mean something different), and treats the home language as an instructional resource rather than an obstacle. The goal is metalinguistic awareness: understanding that languages are systems that can be analyzed, compared, and leveraged.

## Design Implications

Cross-linguistic transfer is a well-documented resource for second-language learners: literacy skills and metalinguistic insight built in a first language support literacy development in a second [~S]. Explicitly drawing attention to cognates and structural contrasts activates prior knowledge that would otherwise remain inert [Activating prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The strategy works because comparison forces analysis of language form — the same analytic stance that supports vocabulary depth and word-reading skill.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A teacher who is knowledgeable about language structures (at minimum, willing to investigate the student's home language alongside the student)
- A classroom climate that visibly values multilingualism, so students see their home language as an asset worth discussing
- Accurate information about students' home languages, gathered from students, families, or reference resources
- Opportunities for structured talk where comparisons can surface ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Monolingual teachers may struggle to verify cognates or structural claims, risking false cognates or superficial comparisons [-W]
- False cognates and typologically distant language pairs (e.g., languages without alphabetic writing) limit direct transfer; comparisons must be checked rather than assumed [~M]
- If framed as correction ("that's not how we say it in English"), the strategy can stigmatize the home language and undermine the belonging it is meant to build [-M]
- Little benefit if comparisons are one-off teacher statements rather than student-generated analysis [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Cognate awareness routines: word walls pairing English terms with home-language cognates, especially in content-area vocabulary
- Contrastive analysis discussions: students explain how plurals, verb tense, or sentence order work in their home language versus English
- Student-as-expert formats: multilingual students teach classmates words or structures, positioning them as knowledgeable contributors
- Translation and code-meshing writing tasks where students deliberately draw on both languages

### Target Learners
- English Language Learners, particularly those with developing literacy in their home language, where transfer effects are strongest [~S]
- Students in bilingual or dual-language programs, where both codes are instructionally active
- Younger learners benefit from oral comparison routines; adolescents benefit from explicit morphology and cognate instruction in content areas

### Target Learning Goals
- Metalinguistic awareness: understanding how languages are structured and organized
- Vocabulary growth in both English and the home language
- Transfer of reading comprehension strategies across languages

### Instructions
1. [Activate prior knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) by asking students what they already know about how their home language works — sounds, words, word-building, sentence order.
2. Select a target English word, structure, or text feature and ask students to find its counterpart or nearest equivalent in their home language ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)).
3. Facilitate a structured comparison through [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md): How is it the same? How is it different? Are there words that sound alike but mean different things?
4. Record comparisons on a visible artifact (bilingual word wall, comparison chart) so connections accumulate over time.
5. Have students apply the connection — use the cognate in writing, or apply the structural insight to a new word ([Application](../elements/application.md)).

## Examples
- A third-grade class builds a cognate wall during a science unit on animals: *elefante/elephant*, *océano/ocean* — Spanish-speaking students verify the pairs and flag false cognates they discover.
- A middle-school English teacher asks a student to explain how past tense works in their home language during a grammar lesson, then compares it with English irregular verbs.
- Dual-language programs such as two-way immersion models routinely use contrastive analysis ("the bridge") to make cross-language connections explicit during literacy blocks.
