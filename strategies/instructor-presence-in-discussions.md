# Instructor Presence In Discussions

## Description
Instructor presence in discussions means the teacher participates visibly in learner-to-learner discourse: framing prompts, probing responses, synthesizing threads, correcting misconceptions, and modeling disciplinary reasoning. It is a core component of [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md), where "teaching presence" — the design and facilitation that moves a conversation toward learning outcomes — predicts perceived learning and satisfaction in online and blended settings [+M].

## Design Implications

Discussion without instructor facilitation tends to stall at opinion exchange; instructor moves that push learners toward evidence, synthesis, and mutual critique raise the cognitive level of posts [+M]. Presence is not volume: frequent but low-substantive instructor posts can suppress peer-to-peer interaction, while well-timed facilitation sustains it [~M]. The goal is to make the instructor's disciplinary thinking visible — a form of [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) enacted through discourse.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Prompts that invite reasoning and position-taking, not fact recall ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- A facilitation plan: when to enter, what to probe, when to summarize and redirect
- Instructor replies that model the discourse moves expected of learners (asking for evidence, connecting posts, offering counterexamples)
- Timely participation — presence decays in value when it arrives days after the thread peaks

#### Constraints
- Excessive or immediate instructor intervention can shut down peer interaction and create dependence on the instructor as sole authority [~M]
- Instructor posts that merely praise ("great point!") add presence without cognitive value and do not raise discussion quality [-M]
- In very large enrollments, individual instructor presence is unsustainable; substitute structured peer roles or teaching assistants, or presence becomes performative rather than substantive
- Learners may defer to the instructor's view once posted, ending productive disagreement [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Facilitator role**: probe and question without evaluating, deferring judgment to keep peer discourse alive
- **Synthesizer role**: enter mid-week to weave threads together and name emerging disagreements, then step back
- **Participant role**: post as a co-learner with a disciplinary perspective, explicitly framed as one view among several
- **Scheduled presence**: predictable instructor checkpoints (e.g., a mid-discussion synthesis post) rather than continuous monitoring

### Target Learners
- Novices who cannot yet evaluate the quality of peer arguments and benefit from instructor modeling of disciplinary standards [+M]
- Online and blended learners, where absence of instructor presence is a primary driver of dissatisfaction and attrition [+M]
- Advanced learners may need less facilitative presence and more autonomy; heavy instructor steering can reduce their ownership of inquiry [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking and argumentation quality in discourse
- Conceptual understanding through [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — instructor probes that surface contradictions between posts
- Norms of disciplinary conversation and [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md) membership

### Instructions
1. Design a discussion prompt that requires a defensible position or analysis, not a summary ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
2. Open the discussion with a framing post that states the intellectual stakes and the discourse norms expected.
3. Monitor without intervening for the first phase; let peer exchange develop.
4. Enter with facilitation moves — probing questions, requests for evidence, connections between posts — rather than verdicts ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Post a mid-discussion synthesis that names disagreements and redirects toward unresolved questions.
6. Close with a summary post that models expert synthesis, and require learners to respond to it or revise their earlier position ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of discussion quality).

## Examples
- **Online graduate seminars (e.g., Penn State World Campus courses)** — instructors post a required "synthesis" message mid-week that weaves student threads together, a practice associated with higher teaching-presence ratings in Community of Inquiry research.
- **Piazza-based CS courses** — instructor answers are marked as such and endorsed answers surface instructor-validated reasoning, balancing presence with peer contribution.
- **Harvard Business School case discussion** — the instructor's cold-calling and probing is the canonical face-to-face analogue: presence that raises the cognitive level of student talk without supplying the answer (see [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md)).
