# Quick Look Cards

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Quick Look Cards are compact reference aids — a single card or small set of cards containing key steps, rules, definitions, or decision prompts — that learners can consult *during* task performance. Unlike full instructional materials, they are designed for seconds-long glances, not study: the learner is working, hits a point of uncertainty, and retrieves exactly the support needed before continuing.

## Design Implications

Quick Look Cards function as performance support: they offload routine or easily-forgotten information from working memory so the learner can devote capacity to the actual task [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Their effectiveness depends on ruthless brevity and selective content — a card that requires reading rather than glancing becomes a distraction and adds extraneous load [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]. Cards work best when they support a task the learner can mostly perform, filling specific gaps rather than substituting for instruction.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Task analysis identifying the specific points where learners stall or err — cards target those points, not everything
- Extreme concision: one idea per card, scannable layout, minimal prose ([Chunking](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) applied to the card itself)
- Consistent visual conventions (icons, color, numbering) so learners can locate information in under a few seconds
- A placement or access plan: cards must be available at the moment of need (desk reference, laminated station card, digital overlay)

#### Constraints
- Cards cannot teach a skill the learner does not already partially have; for novices with no prior instruction they are insufficient on their own and should follow, not replace, [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) or explicit teaching
- Overly dense or verbose cards increase extraneous load and get abandoned [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Over-reliance can prevent automatization if learners never internalize the supported steps; cards should be faded or made less available as fluency develops
- Poorly organized cards impose search costs that exceed the benefit of the information they contain

#### Implementation Variability
- **Procedural cards**: numbered steps for a recurring process (lab protocol, software workflow)
- **Decision cards**: if/then prompts or decision trees supporting judgment during a task
- **Reference cards**: key facts, formulas, or vocabulary for quick retrieval
- **Digital variants**: tooltips, in-app hint panels, and on-screen overlays serve the same function; the design constraints (brevity, moment-of-need access) are identical

### Target Learners
- Novices who have received initial instruction but have not yet automatized procedures — cards bridge the gap while fluency develops [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Learners with working memory constraints or processing-speed challenges, for whom offloading routine information is especially valuable
- Less useful for learners with strong prior knowledge, who rarely need the support and may find it clutter [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency: sustaining accurate performance while steps are still being learned
- Error reduction: preventing common mistakes during early practice
- Self-regulation: prompting learners to check their own work or apply decision rules independently

### Instructions
1. Analyze the task and identify the two or three points where learners most often hesitate or err; do not attempt to document the whole task.
2. Draft cards with one idea per card, using numbered steps, if/then prompts, or a single worked micro-example ([Worked Examples](../principles/worked-examples.md) in miniature).
3. Apply [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and consistent visual structure so each card is scannable in seconds.
4. Pilot the cards during actual task performance and revise: any card learners read for more than a few seconds is too dense.
5. Fade availability as learners gain fluency — move cards from the desk to a drawer, or from always-visible to on-demand — so steps become automatized rather than permanently externally supported.

## Related Strategies
- [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — both provide structured support before/during learning, but organizers prepare for instruction while Quick Look Cards support performance at the moment of need
- [Hint Systems](../elements/hints.md) — digital, on-demand variant of the same just-in-time support function

## Examples
- **Writing process cards in elementary classrooms** — laminated cards at each desk listing the steps of the writing cycle (plan → draft → revise → edit) with one revision prompt per card, letting students self-check without teacher intervention.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) hint panels** — progressive on-demand hints during practice problems act as digital Quick Look Cards, revealing one step at a time only when the learner requests support.
- **Clinical and lab settings** — WHO Surgical Safety Checklist-style cards and bench-top protocol cards are canonical job aids: brief, sequential, and consulted during performance rather than memorized in advance.
