# Cooperative Learning

## Description
Cooperative learning organizes instruction around small groups working toward a shared goal, structured so that each member's success depends on the group's success (positive interdependence) while each member is individually accountable for their contribution. It is distinct from mere group work: the defining features are deliberate task structure, assigned roles, and reward or assessment structures that link individual and group outcomes.

## Design Implications

Cooperative learning is among the most consistently supported instructional strategies, but its effects depend on structure: groups with group rewards and individual accountability outperform unstructured or purely competitive arrangements [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Unstructured "group work" without these conditions frequently produces social loafing and unequal participation, eroding the benefit [-S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task genuinely requiring multiple perspectives or interdependent contributions — not a task one member could complete alone
- Explicit positive interdependence: shared goals, shared materials, or complementary roles
- Individual accountability: individual quizzes, role deliverables, or randomized reporting so no member can hide
- Group processing time — brief structured reflection on how the team worked together
- Sufficient group size discipline (typically 2–4 members; larger groups invite loafing)

#### Constraints
- Without individual accountability, higher-ability members dominate and lower-ability members disengage (social loafing) [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Group interaction itself imposes coordination costs; for simple, well-structured content, collaboration can add extraneous load and slow learning relative to individual study [~M]
- Heterogeneous grouping benefits lower achievers most; high achievers sometimes gain less or not at all on routine tasks [~M]
- Poorly managed group dynamics (status hierarchies, free-riding) can harm belonging and participation, particularly for marginalized learners

#### Implementation Variability
- **Think-Pair-Share / peer instruction** — brief, low-structure cooperation embedded in whole-class instruction
- **Jigsaw** — each member masters a segment and teaches it to others; interdependence is built into the information structure
- **Team-based learning** — individual readiness assurance tests followed by team tests, making accountability structural
- **Complex instruction** — role assignment and status interventions to equalize participation in heterogeneous classrooms

### Target Learners
- Benefits span achievement levels, with the largest gains for lower-achieving and previously low-motivated learners [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]
- Learners who benefit from verbalizing reasoning — explaining to peers consolidates understanding [~M]
- Less suitable as a default for novices on high-element-interactivity material, where initial [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) may be better served by explicit guidance before collaboration

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and problem solving requiring discussion and negotiation of meaning
- Collaborative and communication skills as explicit outcomes
- Retention through elaborative explanation (retrieval plus generation)
- Intergroup relations and belonging in diverse classrooms

### Instructions
1. Specify the shared goal and success criteria so interdependence is explicit, not assumed.
2. Form heterogeneous groups of 2–4; assign or rotate roles (recorder, checker, skeptic, facilitator).
3. Design the task so it cannot be completed by one person alone (information gaps, complementary subtasks).
4. Build in individual accountability — individual exit tickets, randomized spokespersons, or individual-then-team assessment sequences.
5. Monitor groups, intervening on process (participation, status) rather than supplying content answers.
6. Close with group processing: a brief structured reflection on what the team did well and what to change.

## Examples
- **Jigsaw classroom** (Aronson) — students master one segment in "expert groups," then teach it in heterogeneous home groups; originally developed to reduce intergroup tension in desegregated schools.
- **Team-Based Learning** (Michaelsen) — individual readiness tests followed by the same test taken as a team, then team application exercises; used widely in medical and business education ([teambasedlearning.org](https://www.teambasedlearning.org)).
- **Complex Instruction** (Cohen & Lotan) — role structures and status treatments to equalize participation in heterogeneous math and science classrooms.
- **PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies)** — structured reciprocal peer tutoring in reading and mathematics with assigned roles and point systems.
