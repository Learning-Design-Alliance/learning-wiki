Modeling Skills and Interaction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 14 studies (6 causal, 6 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 14 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Modeling means the instructor (or a peer, or a recorded expert) performs the target skill — an analysis, a discussion move, a problem-solving procedure, an academic interaction — while learners observe. Effective modeling goes beyond showing correct output: the modeler narrates decisions, monitors their own understanding, and revises course, making expert reasoning visible rather than leaving learners to infer it from finished products. Modeling applies both to cognitive skills (how to analyze a text, evaluate a claim) and to interactional skills (how to disagree productively, question a peer, build on others' ideas).

Design Implications¶

Modeling draws on social learning theory: learners acquire behaviors and standards by observing a competent model [Bandura, 1977]. Its effectiveness depends on making reasoning explicit — silent demonstration of the final product produces far weaker learning than narrated modeling of the process — and on pairing observation with guided practice, since watching alone yields illusory competence Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone. [+S]. Modeling also manages cognitive load for novices by replacing unguided search with a studyable reference model Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A model whose performance is visible and accurate, ideally with the modeler's reasoning verbalized (a think-aloud protocol)
  • Decomposition of complex skills into observable segments, so learners can attend to one component at a time Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]
  • An immediate opportunity for learners to attempt the skill with support (Coaching, Application)
  • For interactional modeling: authentic discussion or collaborative tasks where the modeled moves are actually needed

Constraints¶

  • Observation without subsequent practice creates an illusion of mastery and transfers poorly [-S] — learners consistently overestimate what they learned from watching
  • Overly polished expert modeling can hide the struggle and error-recovery that learners most need to see; showing initial flawed attempts and revisions is often more instructive [~M]
  • Modeling a single canonical approach can anchor learners and reduce flexibility on open-ended tasks; contrasting multiple models mitigates this Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  • For learners with substantial prior knowledge, explicit modeling becomes redundant and can depress performance Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Cognitive modeling: instructor think-alouds while analyzing a text, solving a problem, or evaluating evidence
  • Interactional modeling: instructor deliberately enacts discussion norms — rephrasing a peer's point, asking for evidence, respectfully disagreeing — before Class Discussion or Argumentation tasks
  • Peer modeling: a near-peer performs the skill, which can raise self-efficacy by making the model's competence feel attainable Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Faded modeling: full demonstration → partial demonstration with learner-completed steps → independent performance Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+S]

Target Learners¶

  • Novices encountering a skill or discourse practice for the first time, who would otherwise search unproductively for a strategy [+M]
  • Learners with low self-efficacy for the task, for whom a credible model (especially a peer model) builds confidence [+M]
  • Less beneficial for experts or near-experts, for whom modeling is redundant and can interfere [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural and strategic skill acquisition (how to carry out an analysis or procedure)
  • Norms and practices of academic interaction (discussion, critique, collaboration)
  • Metacognitive and self-regulatory habits — what monitoring, checking, and revising look like in expert performance

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the target skill or interaction norm precisely, and decide what expert reasoning must be made visible.
  2. Plan the modeling episode: perform the skill while verbalizing decisions, uncertainty, and self-checks (Articulation).
  3. Where useful, model a flawed attempt and its repair, or contrast two approaches, so learners discriminate good from weak performance.
  4. Follow immediately with structured learner practice — Coaching during Application — while fading support as competence grows.
  5. Debrief: name the moves that were modeled so learners can label and transfer them.

Related Strategies¶

  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the core narration technique for making expert reasoning visible
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — prepares learners to extract relevant features from the model
  • Action-Oriented Feedback — complements modeling by correcting learners' own attempts after observation

Examples¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown) — the teacher models predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing while reading, then transfers each role to student groups; a canonical faded-modeling design.
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — positions modeling as the first phase, followed by coaching and articulation.
  • Writing conferences and mentor texts — instructors model revising a paragraph live, verbalizing evaluation of word choice and structure, before students revise their own drafts.
  • Discussion norm modeling — before a seminar, the instructor and a colleague (or the instructor role-playing both sides) model evidence-based disagreement, then students rehearse the moves in Case-Based Learning.
