# Prosocial Modeling

## Description
Bandura's observational-learning research showed that model consequences shape imitation in both directions: children who watched a model punished for aggressive behavior decreased their own aggression, while children who watched the same behavior praised or simply ignored (not punished) imitated it, including specific words and actions (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961) — see [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md). Bandura concluded this dynamic has both **prosocial** (positive) and **antisocial** effects: children observe and learn from models' behavior, including parents' own conduct, and tend to copy what a model actually *does* rather than what the model merely *says* to do. Research on abuse cycles illustrates the antisocial side: children who grow up witnessing parents manage anger through violence often learn to behave the same way, and roughly 30% of abused children go on to become abusive parents themselves (Murrell, Christoff, & Henning, 2007; U.S. Department of Health & Human Services, 2013).

**Prosocial modeling** deliberately uses this same mechanism for socially desirable ends: parents, teachers, and public figures (Martin Luther King Jr. and Mahatma Gandhi are commonly cited examples) can function as models who inspire imitation of reading habits, healthy behavior, kindness, courtesy, and honesty — but only if the model's actual behavior, not just their stated expectations, consistently demonstrates the target behavior.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Consistency between what the model says and what the model actually does, since observers copy the latter
- Live, verbal, or symbolic models (see [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md)) that are visible and credible to the observer
#### Constraints
- Inconsistent modeling ("do as I say, not as I do") undermines the strategy, since the behavior actually observed — not the verbal instruction — is what tends to be copied
- The same mechanism transmits antisocial as well as prosocial behavior, so an adult modeling aggression, dishonesty, or disengagement risks that behavior being imitated just as readily

### Target Learners
- Children and adolescents forming behavioral norms from observed adult and peer behavior
- Learners at risk of imitating antisocial behavior modeled at home, where deliberately introducing consistent prosocial models (teachers, mentors, public figures) may help counteract it

### Target Learning Goals
- Adoption of socially desirable behaviors and values (reading habits, health behaviors, kindness, honesty) through consistent observed example rather than instruction alone

### Instructions
1. Identify the specific prosocial behavior to be modeled (e.g., reading, exercise, honesty).
2. Ensure the model (teacher, parent, or featured public figure) is consistently seen actually performing the behavior, not only describing or requesting it.
3. Make the model's engagement visible and discussable (e.g., talking about favorite books while visibly reading them) rather than private or assumed.
4. Where possible, pair modeling with the natural or social consequences the model experiences, since observers are more motivated to imitate a model they see reinforced rather than punished.

## Related Strategies
- [Conditioning Natural Reinforcers](conditioning-natural-reinforcers.md) — both rely on the target behavior eventually carrying its own reinforcing consequence, whether observed vicariously (modeling) or experienced directly (natural reinforcement)

## Examples
- A parent who wants a child to read keeps books visibly present at home, is seen reading regularly, and talks about favorite books, rather than only telling the child to read.
