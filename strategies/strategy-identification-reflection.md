# Strategy Identification & Reflection

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Strategy identification & reflection asks learners to explicitly name the cognitive and metacognitive strategies they use when working through a challenge — "I reread the problem," "I drew a diagram," "I broke it into parts" — and then reflect on which strategies helped, which did not, and when each applies. The instructor collects, labels, and shares these strategies so the class builds a common vocabulary of learning processes that can be reused across tasks. It is typically carried out through brief debrief prompts after problem-solving, strategy walls or anchor charts, and structured reflection journals.

## Design Implications

Naming strategies converts tacit, in-the-moment decisions into explicit, transferable knowledge, which is the core move of metacognitive instruction [Schraw, 1998] [+M]. Reflection after task completion is most effective when it targets the *process* rather than the outcome, consistent with evidence that process-level feedback drives the largest gains [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because strategy reflection adds a second layer of processing on top of the primary task, it must be brief and well-timed to avoid overloading working memory [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A challenging but solvable task that actually requires strategy selection — reflection on a trivial task yields nothing to name
- A visible, growing record of named strategies (anchor chart, shared document, strategy wall) so the vocabulary accumulates rather than evaporating
- Structured prompts that ask *what* was done, *why*, and *when else it would work* ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- Instructor modeling of strategy naming during the task itself ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))

#### Constraints
- Reflection prompts added on top of a high-load task can disrupt the primary learning itself; debrief after completion, not during [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [-M]
- Generic prompts ("reflect on your learning") produce generic, low-value responses; without specific strategy vocabulary to draw on, learners default to effort attributions ("I tried hard") [~M]
- Less effective when learners lack the prior knowledge to recognize *that* they used a strategy at all — novices often cannot identify their own procedures without instructor modeling first [-W]
- If the same strategy is always the right answer for a task type, reflection becomes ritual rather than decision-making [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Post-task debrief**: 2–3 minute whole-class naming session after problem solving ("What did you try first? What did you do when you got stuck?")
- **Strategy wall / anchor chart**: named strategies accumulate visibly and are referenced by name in later lessons
- **Reflection journals**: individual written entries using a fixed prompt frame (strategy → why → when else)
- **Peer interviews**: learners interview a partner about their approach, practicing the vocabulary receptively before producing it ([Learning by teaching improves tutor learning.](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M])
- **Contingent prompting**: instructor names strategies for novices and gradually hands the naming responsibility to learners [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novices and struggling learners, who often apply effective strategies implicitly but cannot retrieve or transfer them without naming [Mnemonic instruction improves recall for students with disabilities.](../claims/mnemonic-instruction-improves-recall-for-students-with-disabilities.md) [+M]
- Middle-grade and older learners who can describe their own thinking; younger learners need instructor naming plus simplified vocabulary
- High-achievers with already-automated strategies may find structured reflection redundant [Expertise reversal: guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive knowledge: knowing *what* strategies exist and *when* to deploy them [Schraw, 1998] [+M]
- Transfer: building a reusable strategy repertoire that applies across task types
- Self-regulated learning: the monitoring-and-evaluation phase of self-regulated study [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Academic language: a precise shared vocabulary for discussing learning processes

### Instructions
1. **Choose a strategy-rich task** — a problem or challenge with multiple viable approaches, so naming strategies is meaningful ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) or open problems work well).
2. **Model the naming** — during the task, the instructor verbalizes their own strategies aloud ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)), giving learners the vocabulary before they are asked to produce it.
3. **Debrief immediately after the task** — ask learners to name one strategy they used and why, using prompts like "What did you do when you got stuck?" ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. **Record and label** — add named strategies to a shared strategy wall or chart, refining vague descriptions into precise, reusable labels ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can later organize the collection).
5. **Reference by name in future tasks** — prompt learners to *choose* from the accumulated repertoire before and during new tasks, closing the loop from identification to deliberate deployment ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
6. **Revisit and evaluate** — periodically ask which strategies earned their place and which apply only in narrow situations, deepening conditional knowledge.

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) math classrooms** — after children solve word problems, teachers publicly name and chart the strategies children used (direct modeling, counting on, derived facts), building a shared repertoire the class references in later problems ([CGI for Math](../patterns/cgi-for-math.md)).
- **Reading strategy anchor charts in workshop-model classrooms** — teachers at [TCRWP](https://readingandwritingproject.org)-affiliated schools co-construct charts naming comprehension strategies ("back up and reread," "stop and jot") that students are then prompted to deploy by name during independent reading.
- **Programming courses using post-solution debriefs** — after debugging exercises, students write a one-line "what I tried" entry to a shared log, giving the class a named toolkit of debugging strategies (binary search for the bug, rubber-duck explanation, minimal reproduction).
