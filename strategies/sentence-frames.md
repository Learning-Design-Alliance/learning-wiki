# Sentence Frames

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q3` · 2 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Sentence frames are partially completed sentence templates ("I agree with ______ because ______.") that learners complete with their own content when speaking or writing. They scaffold the *language* of a task so learners can devote working memory to the *ideas*, and they model the discourse conventions of a discipline or community — including how to agree, disagree, and ask for elaboration respectfully.

## Design Implications

Sentence frames reduce the linguistic and cognitive burden of response production, letting learners focus on reasoning rather than formulating syntax from scratch [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. They function as a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): by externalizing the sentence structure, frames prevent learners from abandoning substantive participation because they lack the words. Frames are most effective when they model authentic disciplinary discourse (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning in science) rather than generic fill-in-the-blank forms, and when they are faded as learners internalize the structures [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Frames matched to the actual discourse function of the task (agreeing, disagreeing, citing evidence, hedging)
- A language model worth imitating — frames should reflect how experts in the discipline actually talk and write
- Opportunities for [Peer Discussion](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) where frames are used in authentic exchanges, not just written worksheets
- A plan for fading: moving from full frames to word banks to independent production
- Frames matched to the actual discourse moves of the task (agree, counter, elaborate, cite evidence), not generic sentence starters
- Teacher modeling of frame use before independent or peer use ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or live demonstration)
- A structured exchange context where frames are genuinely needed — e.g., [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or structured academic controversy
- A plan for fading: shifting from full frames to word banks to independent production as fluency grows

#### Constraints
- Frames can feel artificial or stilted, especially for learners with stronger language proficiency, and may suppress authentic voice if mandated indefinitely [-M]
- Over-reliance without fading can produce formulaic responses that mimic structure without understanding [~M]
- Effectiveness depends on community norms: in discussion settings, frames only reduce conflict if peers actually use and reinforce them; without moderation or modeling, learners abandon them [-W]
- Frames that supply too much content (not just structure) do the intellectual work for the learner and reduce the task to cloze completion [-M]
- Overuse without fading produces formulaic, mechanical responses and can suppress authentic voice [-M] — learners may fill frames without genuine engagement when the frame no longer matches their thinking
- Frames that exceed learners' current syntactic competence shift the load back to language production and defeat the purpose [~M]
- In online or asynchronous settings, frames can feel artificial and invite perfunctory compliance unless community norms and modeling establish their purpose
- Less useful for learners already fluent in the target register, for whom frames add redundancy rather than support [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Oral discussion frames** — posted sentence starters for accountable talk in seminars and debates ("Can you say more about ______?")
- **Writing frames** — paragraph or argument templates with discourse connectives ("One counterargument is…; however,…")
- **Digital citizenship frames** — templates for respectful online disagreement ("I see it differently because ______. Have you considered ______?")
- **Differentiated frames** — multiple frame tiers offered simultaneously so learners self-select support level
- **Speaking frames** for structured discussion protocols (e.g., Socratic seminars, debate prep) versus **writing frames** for constructed responses
- **Word banks + frames** for multilingual learners at earlier proficiency levels; open frames ("One thing that complicates this is…") for advanced learners
- **Digital adaptations**: discussion-board templates, chat starters in collaborative platforms, or comment stems embedded in peer-review tools
- Frames can be co-constructed with students from an anchor text, which builds awareness of the discourse genre itself

### Target Learners
- Multilingual learners and students developing academic language, who benefit most from explicit models of disciplinary syntax [~S]
- Novices to a discussion format (e.g., first-time debaters, online forum participants) who lack scripts for respectful disagreement
- Less beneficial — and potentially irritating — for proficient speakers, for whom frames can be redundant or constraining [~M]
- Multilingual learners and students acquiring academic language, where the gap between ideas and expression is largest [+M]
- Novices to a discipline's discourse conventions (e.g., students learning to argue from evidence in science) [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Reluctant participants in discussion, for whom the frame lowers the social risk of entering conversation
- Less beneficial for learners already fluent in the register, who may find frames constraining [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic language development: producing discipline-appropriate syntax and discourse moves
- Argumentation quality: structuring claims, evidence, and rebuttals
- Social-emotional goals: respectful dialogue, perspective-taking, and productive disagreement in online and face-to-face settings
- Academic language development: producing discipline-appropriate syntax and vocabulary [Accessible vocabulary and syntax supports comprehension.](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+M]
- Dialogic skill: disagreeing respectfully, building on others' ideas, citing evidence
- Argumentation quality: structuring claims with reasons and evidence [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Participation equity: distributing talk beyond confident volunteers

### Instructions
1. Identify the discourse function of the task (agree, disagree, elaborate, cite evidence) and draft 2–4 frames per function using authentic expert language.
2. Model the frames in use before requiring them — think aloud while completing a frame with real content.
3. Post frames visibly (slide, poster, chat template) during the [Peer Discussion](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) activity.
4. Require initial use, then invite learners to adapt or abandon frames as fluency grows — fade deliberately.
5. Assess both the content of responses and the quality of interaction (clarity, respectfulness), using observation and participant feedback.

## Examples
- **Science argumentation**: "The evidence shows ______, which supports my claim that ______" used in claim–evidence–reasoning (CER) writing frames common in NGSS-aligned curricula.
- **Online discussion boards**: An instructor pins a "respectful disagreement" template ("I understand your point about ______, but I see it differently because ______") at the top of a course forum thread.
- **Structured academic controversy**: In debate formats, each side is given frames for presenting positions ("Our position is ______ because ______") and responding ("We hear your claim that ______; however, ______").
- **Academic conversations (Zwiers & Crawford, 2011)** — content-area teachers use prompt, respond, and follow-up frames ("What do you mean by ___?" "I agree/disagree with ___ because ___") to build sustained peer dialogue across subjects.
- **Structured academic controversy in online forums** — digital citizenship lessons supply disagreement frames ("I see it differently because ___," "Can you say more about ___?") so that online debate stays evidence-based and respectful rather than adversarial.
- **Science explanation frames** — claim-evidence-reasoning stems ("The data show ___, which supports my claim that ___ because ___") scaffold scientific writing in curricula such as those from the [Claim-Evidence-Reasoning framework](https://k12science.org/).

## Description
Sentence frames are partially completed sentence templates ("I disagree with ___ because ___") that learners use to structure their spoken or written responses. They make the *discourse moves* of a discipline — agreeing, disagreeing with evidence, building on a peer's idea, hedging, citing — explicit and available to learners who have ideas but not yet the language to express them. Frames are typically introduced, modeled, practiced in low-stakes exchanges, and faded as learners internalize the structures.

## Design Implications

Sentence frames are a language scaffold in the Vygotskian sense: they lower the linguistic demands of a task so cognitive effort can go to the content of reasoning rather than to formulating syntax under pressure. This is most valuable when the target discourse genre is unfamiliar — academic argument, scientific explanation, respectful disagreement — because learners cannot be expected to infer its language patterns from exposure alone [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Frames work best when paired with modeling of their use and with opportunities for structured peer exchange, not as standalone handouts [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].
