# Replace Negative Influences with Positive Ones

## Description
This strategy asks learners to audit their social and informational environment — relationships, peer groups, media consumption, and physical study settings — and to reduce exposure to influences that undermine motivation, self-efficacy, or wellbeing. In place of these, learners deliberately seek out supportive peers, credible role models, and constructive content. It is carried out through a cycle of identification, substitution, and periodic re-evaluation, often with the help of a mentor, coach, or peer group.

## Design Implications

The strategy rests on social learning theory: learners acquire attitudes, expectations, and behavioral patterns by observing the people around them, so the composition of one's environment functions as an implicit curriculum [Social models shape observer behavior, self-efficacy, and expectations.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+S]. Because perceived self-efficacy strongly predicts persistence in the face of difficulty, environments that model success and provide encouragement measurably support learner persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- An honest inventory of current influences: who learners spend time with, what media they consume, and which settings precede discouragement or avoidance
- Concrete, available substitutes — a study group, mentor, community, or content source — identified *before* removing the negative influence, so the learner is not left with a vacuum
- A mechanism for periodic re-evaluation ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), journaling, or coaching conversations), since environments drift back toward old patterns
- Where possible, facilitator support: instructors can structure peer grouping and curate exemplar communities rather than leaving substitution entirely to individual willpower

#### Constraints
- Negative influences are often structurally unavoidable (family, required workplaces, mandatory schooling); framing the strategy as pure individual choice can produce guilt rather than change [-W]
- Substitution fails when the "positive" replacement is superficial — passive consumption of uplifting content without interaction or modeling produces little durable change [~W]
- Learners in acute distress (e.g., bullying, clinical anxiety or depression) need professional support; environmental substitution alone is insufficient [-W]
- Overly homogeneous positive environments can create echo chambers that reduce exposure to productive challenge and [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Social substitution:** joining or forming study groups, [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md), or peer mentoring programs
- **Informational substitution:** curating feeds, subscriptions, and reading lists toward credible, growth-oriented sources
- **Physical/environmental substitution:** changing study locations to spaces with visible productive norms (libraries, maker spaces)
- **Facilitated substitution:** instructor-assigned collaborative structures such as [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) that guarantee exposure to constructive models

### Target Learners
- Learners whose motivation has eroded through repeated discouragement, unsupportive peers, or demoralizing comparison environments [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and adults in transition (new institutions, career changes) who are actively re-forming their reference groups and can choose affiliations
- Less applicable to young children, whose environments are largely adult-controlled; for them the equivalent work belongs to caregivers and teachers designing the environment directly

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and motivational goals: sustaining persistence, rebuilding self-efficacy, and maintaining engagement over long learning projects
- Self-regulation: learning to monitor and manage one's own environmental conditions for learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Identity formation: internalizing the norms and aspirations of a chosen learning community

### Instructions
1. **Audit.** Learners list the people, media, and settings that most affect their mood and motivation, marking each as energizing or draining. A structured [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) or reflective journal works well for this step.
2. **Prioritize.** Identify the one or two influences with the largest negative effect that are realistically modifiable; attempting wholesale change at once typically fails.
3. **Substitute before removing.** Secure a concrete replacement — a study partner, a mentor, a curated feed, a community — so the withdrawal of the negative influence leaves a functioning support in place.
4. **Increase contact with positive models.** Deliberately schedule interaction with people who model the desired behavior and mindset; observation of successful peers is the mechanism by which the substitution works [Social models shape observer behavior, self-efficacy, and expectations.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+S].
5. **Re-evaluate on a cycle.** Revisit the audit every few weeks; environments drift, and substitutions lose novelty. Adjust rather than treating the plan as one-time.

## Examples
- **First-year seminar programs** (e.g., the University of South Carolina's University 101 model) deliberately place new students into cohort communities with peer mentors, substituting structured supportive networks for the anonymous, sometimes alienating default environment. See [https://sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/university_101/](https://sc.edu/about/offices_and_divisions/university_101/)
- **Study-group formation in MOOCs:** platforms such as Coursera organize learners into cohort forums and local meetups, replacing isolated viewing — a known driver of attrition — with peer accountability.
- **Digital wellbeing curricula:** Common Sense Education's digital citizenship lessons have students audit their media diets and consciously curate feeds toward constructive content. See [https://www.commonsense.org/education](https://www.commonsense.org/education)
