# Activating Background Knowledge

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Activating background knowledge involves prompting learners to recall and connect prior experiences, information, and understandings before engaging with new content. Prompts take the form of questions, brief discussions, analogies, or short activities that surface what learners already know and make it available for integration with new material. The strategy works because new information is encoded more durably when it can be assimilated into existing mental schemas rather than stored as isolated facts.

## Design Implications

Activation improves comprehension by reducing the working-memory burden of processing unfamiliar content and by providing retrieval hooks for new information [Dochy et al.'s review found prior knowledge the strongest single predictor of learning outcomes.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [~M]. The effect depends on the *relevance* of what is activated: prompts that surface knowledge actually needed for the task direct attention productively, while prompts that surface interesting-but-irrelevant knowledge can divert it [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. Activation is most powerful when followed by an opportunity to use the recalled knowledge, not merely state it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Prompts tightly aligned to the knowledge the new content actually presupposes, not just topically adjacent
- Enough wait time and low-stakes framing that learners genuinely retrieve rather than wait for the teacher's answer
- A mechanism for the instructor to diagnose gaps or misconceptions revealed by the activation and adjust instruction ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md), quick polls, or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners with genuinely limited prior knowledge on a topic cannot activate what they lack; activation prompts then waste time or produce guessing [Recht & Leslie's baseball study showed comprehension depended on prior knowledge, not reading skill.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [-M] — for these learners, build knowledge first rather than activating it
- Activation can surface misconceptions; if these are not explicitly addressed, they may be strengthened rather than corrected
- Prompts that activate vivid but irrelevant knowledge can pull attention away from the learning goal [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M]
- Repeated personal-experience sharing can consume instructional time disproportionate to its comprehension payoff

#### Implementation Variability
- **Question-based**: brief oral or written prompts before reading or a new unit
- **Analogy-based**: mapping a familiar domain onto the new one ([Analogies](analogies.md), [Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md))
- **Structural**: [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that present the schema into which new content will fit
- **Written**: brief freewrites or KWL charts that make prior knowledge visible to both learner and instructor

### Target Learners
- Learners who possess relevant knowledge but do not spontaneously retrieve it — the most common case, since access problems (not absence of knowledge) drive many comprehension failures [~M]
- Learners with diverse backgrounds benefit when prompts invite multiple valid entry points rather than one expected experience
- Ineffective for learners with no relevant prior knowledge; these learners need direct knowledge building instead [Recht & Leslie's baseball study showed comprehension depended on prior knowledge, not reading skill.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of texts, lectures, or new domains that presuppose familiar schemas
- Retention: knowledge connected to existing structures is recalled more reliably [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Transfer: activating analogous prior experience supports analogical mapping to new problems

### Instructions
1. Identify the specific prior knowledge the new content presupposes; design a prompt that targets it, not the topic in general.
2. Open with the activation prompt — a question, quick write, or [Analogies](analogies.md)-based comparison — before presenting new content ([Activation](activation.md)).
3. Surface and record responses publicly so the class sees the range of relevant knowledge ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. Diagnose gaps and misconceptions; if knowledge is missing, teach it briefly rather than proceeding.
5. Explicitly bridge: name the connection between what learners recalled and the new material as it is presented ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
6. Require learners to *use* the activated knowledge in the first task, not merely recall it ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)).

## Examples
- Before reading a narrative text, a teacher asks, "Have you ever been afraid to try something new?" and collects responses on the board, then maps them onto the protagonist's situation as the class reads.
- [KWL charts](https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/kwl_chart) — learners list what they *Know*, *Want* to know, and later what they *Learned*; the K and W columns are the activation phase.
- A physics instructor opens a unit on current with the analogy of water flowing through pipes, prompting learners to articulate what they already know about flow and pressure before mapping it to circuits.
- [Annenberg Learner](https://www.learner.org) video-based lessons frequently open with a prediction question that requires learners to commit prior intuitions before the explanation revises them.
