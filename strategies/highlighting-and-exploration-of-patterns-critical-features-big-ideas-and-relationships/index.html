Highlighting and Exploration of Patterns, Critical Features, Big Ideas, and Relationships¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

This strategy directs learner attention to what matters most in a task, text, or domain — critical features, recurring patterns, underlying big ideas, and the relationships among them — rather than leaving learners to discover salience on their own. It is carried out through two complementary moves: highlighting (signaling, underlining, advance organizers, contrastive framing) and exploration (guided comparison of cases, pattern-finding prompts, self-explanation questions that push learners to articulate the structure themselves).

Design Implications¶

Novices cannot reliably distinguish deep structure from surface features, so unguided attention is often spent on salient-but-irrelevant details. Explicit signaling reduces extraneous processing by cueing what to attend to Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of the emphasized content. [+M], and pre-organizing structures help learners map new information onto a coherent schema. Exploration matters as much as highlighting: comparing multiple contrasting cases before instruction helps learners abstract the invariant features that define a concept Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep structure. [+M], and prompting learners to explain patterns to themselves consolidates conceptual understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear analysis of which features, patterns, and relationships are actually critical to the learning goal — highlighting the wrong things is worse than highlighting nothing
  • Visual or verbal signaling devices (headings, color, underlining, advance organizers) applied sparingly and consistently
  • Comparison tasks or prompts that require learners to examine how cases differ and what stays invariant
  • Follow-up questions or Self-Explanation prompts that make the pattern explicit in the learner's own words

Constraints¶

  • Learner-generated highlighting is often ineffective: students highlight too much and indiscriminately, with weak evidence of benefit Experimenter-provided underlining is more effective than student self-underlining. [~M] — over-highlighting creates an illusion of mastery without encoding
  • Signaling loses its power when everything is emphasized; the effect depends on genuine selectivity
  • Highlighting surface features without connecting them to a big idea can encourage rote pattern-matching rather than transfer
  • For learners with strong prior knowledge, heavy signaling can be redundant and may interfere with self-regulated processing [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-provided signaling: advance organizers, bolded critical features, annotated worked examples
  • Contrastive exploration: presenting non-examples and near-misses so learners must discriminate the critical feature
  • Learner-guided exploration: pattern-hunting tasks, sorting/classifying activities, "what changed, what stayed the same" comparisons
  • Relational mapping: concept maps, analogies, and explicit "this is like X because…" prompts

Target Learners¶

  • Novices, who lack the knowledge to detect deep structure unaided and benefit most from external signaling Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of the emphasized content. [+M]
  • Learners in complex, feature-rich domains (science, mathematics, medicine) where surface features mislead
  • Less beneficial for experts, who already extract structure efficiently and may find signaling redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: identifying defining features and distinguishing them from surface features
  • Schema formation and abstraction: seeing the big idea across varied instances
  • Transfer: recognizing when a familiar pattern applies in a new context

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the critical features, patterns, and big ideas of the target content through task analysis before designing anything.
  2. Open with an Advance Organizer that frames the big idea and the relationships learners should watch for.
  3. Present 2–3 contrasting cases side by side and ask learners what varies and what stays constant Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep structure. [+M]; include Non-Examples to sharpen discrimination.
  4. Signal critical features in materials — selective underlining, color, headings — rather than asking learners to highlight on their own Experimenter-provided underlining is more effective than student self-underlining. [~M].
  5. Prompt Self-Explanation: "Why does this work here? Where would it fail?"
  6. Connect the pattern back to the big idea explicitly, using Analogies where a familiar domain shares the same structure.

Related Strategies¶

  • Headings and Highlight — the concrete text-marking implementation of the signaling component
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — gives learners a framework onto which highlighted patterns can attach
  • Chunking — grouping related features so the highlighted structure fits in working memory

Examples¶

  • Contrasting Cases (ThinkerTools) — Schwartz & Bransford's "A Time for Telling" studies had students compare paired motion-graph cases before instruction, letting them discover the critical feature (slope) that the subsequent lecture then named.
  • Khan Academy — worked-example videos use color and annotation to single out the critical step in each problem, then follow with practice on varied surface features of the same underlying pattern.
  • Cognitive Tutor / Carnegie Learning MATHia — the tutor highlights the relevant quantities in each problem context, teaching students to attend to the structural features that drive the solution rather than the story details.
