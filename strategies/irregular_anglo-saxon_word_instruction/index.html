Irregular Anglo-Saxon Word Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 11 studies (4 review, 3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Irregular Anglo-Saxon words (e.g., said, was, one, two, who, come) contain letter–sound correspondences that do not follow the regular patterns taught in systematic phonics, so they cannot be fully decoded or spelled by rule. Effective instruction teaches these words by (a) grouping words with memorable similarities, (b) explicitly marking the "odd part" that must be learned by heart while mapping the regular parts to sounds, (c) using multisensory practice routines (tracing, writing, saying), (d) mnemonics, and (e) close visual attention tasks such as visualizing the letter sequence and recalling it backwards as well as forwards. The goal is secure orthographic representations — instant recognition in reading and accurate recall in spelling.

Design Implications¶

Irregular-word instruction works best when it leverages, rather than bypasses, alphabetic knowledge: most letters in an "irregular" word map regularly onto sounds, and only the truly irregular part needs rote attention [~S]. This approach — sometimes called "spotlighting" the tricky part — produces stronger word learning than whole-word memorization, which treats the word as an arbitrary visual unit Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word methods for generalization. [+S]. Repeated, spaced encounters with the word build the orthographic memory that supports automatic recognition Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S], and automatic word recognition in turn frees working memory for comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A curated list of high-frequency irregular words, sequenced by frequency and by shared irregular features (e.g., said, again, friend)
  • Explicit marking of the irregular grapheme (e.g., underlining the ai in said) so learners map regular parts phonetically and attend deliberately to the exception
  • Multisensory practice materials: letter tiles, tracing surfaces, writing tools for repeated encoding
  • Mnemonics or meaning-based hooks (e.g., "be cause the ause is odd") tied to each word's odd part
  • Frequent cumulative review in both reading and spelling contexts (Practice)

Constraints¶

  • Pure whole-word flashcard memorization without sound-mapping yields fragile, context-bound word knowledge and does not transfer to novel words Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word methods for generalization. [-S]
  • Overloading a lesson with many new irregular words exceeds working memory limits; small sets with cumulative review are necessary Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]
  • Teaching irregular words before learners have basic phonemic awareness and letter–sound knowledge undermines the mapping process; premature sight-word drilling can encourage guessing habits [-M]
  • Mnemonics add memory load of their own; if the mnemonic is more complex than the word, it interferes rather than supports [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Orton-Gillingham–style multisensory routines (simultaneous oral spelling, tracing with finger while saying sounds) suit one-on-one or small-group remediation
  • Whole-class routines can use "heart word" formats (e.g., UFLI Foundations, https://ufli.education.ufl.edu) that mark the part to know "by heart"
  • Spelling-focused variants emphasize writing the word from dictation immediately after study; reading-focused variants emphasize rapid recognition in text

Target Learners¶

  • Students in grades 1–3 acquiring high-frequency vocabulary during early reading instruction
  • Struggling readers and spellers who need explicit, structured support to form orthographic representations Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word methods for generalization. [+M]
  • Learners with dyslexia, for whom multisensory, structured routines are a standard component of remedial programs [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Sight-word recognition: instant, effortless reading of high-frequency irregular words Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S]
  • Spelling accuracy of exception words
  • Development of self-teaching memory strategies (visualization, self-testing) that generalize to later vocabulary

Instructions¶

  1. Select a small set (3–5) of irregular words that share a feature or occur together in text; sequence them by frequency.
  2. Read the word aloud and have students segment its sounds (Practice with phonemic awareness).
  3. Map the regular letter–sound parts explicitly, then spotlight the odd part and explain why it breaks the pattern (Analogies to already-known words sharpen the contrast).
  4. Attach a mnemonic or meaning hook to the odd part.
  5. Run a multisensory encoding cycle: say it, tap the sounds, build it with tiles, write it while naming letters, then write it from memory and check.
  6. Have students visualize the word, recall it backwards and forwards, and self-test (Practice).
  7. Provide immediate corrective feedback on attempts (Assess Performance), then distribute review across days and weeks Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — connecting new irregular words to known regular patterns strengthens mapping
  • Multisensory Encoding — the tracing/saying/writing routines used in step 5

Examples¶

  • UFLI Foundations "Heart Words" (University of Florida Literacy Institute, https://ufli.education.ufl.edu) — lessons that map regular parts phonetically and mark the "heart part" to learn by heart.
  • Orton-Gillingham simultaneous oral spelling — student says the word, writes it while naming each letter, and reads it back; a staple of structured literacy remediation.
  • Read Write Inc. Phonics "Red Words" (Oxford University Press, https://global.oup.com/education) — high-frequency irregular words taught with attention to the tricky grapheme within a systematic phonics program.
