# Emotion Identification and Labeling

## Description
Learners identify, label, and match facial expressions and feelings, building a vocabulary for internal states. Activities include categorizing feeling words by type, analyzing characters' feelings in literature, and connecting actions with emotions. The strategy treats emotional literacy as a learnable skill: accurate labeling of one's own and others' emotions is the foundation for regulation and empathy.

## Design Implications

Emotion labeling converts diffuse affective experience into discrete, manipulable categories, which supports regulation — affect labeling reduces amygdala reactivity and improves self-reported regulation in adults [~M], and emotion knowledge in young children predicts later social competence and school readiness [+M]. Because emotion recognition is initially perceptual, instruction should pair [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)-style contrasts (e.g., angry vs. frightened faces) with situational cues, since facial expressions alone are ambiguous without context [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Visual aids (emotion face cards, photographs), books, and drawing materials
- Facilitator guidance prompting learners to connect feelings with events and behaviors ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- A safe emotional climate; labeling exercises should be voluntary and low-stakes

#### Constraints
- Facial-expression-only training produces recognition of posed expressions that transfers weakly to real, mixed, and culturally variable emotional displays [-M] — emotion categories are constructed from context, body posture, and culture, not read from faces alone
- Can oversimplify complex or ambivalent emotions if the label set is too small
- Learners with alexithymia, autism, or communication difficulties may need augmented supports (visual choice boards, simplified scales) rather than open-ended verbal labeling [~M]
- Forcing public emotional disclosure can backfire; labeling should focus on characters, scenarios, or optional self-report

#### Implementation Variability
- Literature-based: identify and discuss characters' feelings during read-alouds
- Embodied: emotion charades or [Acting-Role-Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md) where learners enact and guess feelings
- Check-in routines: brief daily mood labeling using a feelings chart ('what's my emotion?' game check-in)
- Drawing: learners illustrate a time they felt an emotion, then label it

### Target Learners
- Early childhood to elementary learners (K–5), when emotion vocabulary is developing rapidly [+M]
- Learners with social-emotional skill deficits, including many autistic learners, with adapted materials [~M]
- Older learners benefit from more nuanced labeling (distinguishing frustration from disappointment) rather than basic face matching [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional vocabulary and emotion recognition
- Self-awareness: connecting internal states to triggers and behaviors
- Foundation for empathy and self-regulation — labeling others' emotions precedes perspective-taking

### Instructions
1. Introduce a small set of emotion words with [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md), pairing each label with facial, postural, and situational cues.
2. Provide [Practice](../elements/practice.md) through matching games, charades, and character analysis in read-alouds.
3. [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on label accuracy and on connecting emotions to causes ("You said the character was frustrated — what made her feel that way?").
4. Gradually increase label granularity and embed labeling in real situations via [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and daily check-ins.

## Examples
- **[PATHS Curriculum](https://www.pathseducation.com)** (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies) — a published SEL curriculum whose "Feelings" units teach emotion identification through face cards, stories, and the "Turtle Technique"; evaluated in randomized trials including the Head Start REDI study.
- **Emotion face cards** — matching activities connecting expressions to situation descriptions (Center of Excellence for Infant and Early Childhood Mental Health Consultation).
- **Read-aloud character analysis** — pausing during stories to ask "How does she feel? How do you know?" links facial, situational, and behavioral cues.
