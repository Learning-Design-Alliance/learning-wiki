Portfolios¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 causal, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

A portfolio is a purposeful, curated collection of a learner's work assembled over time, accompanied by the learner's own reflection on what each artifact shows about their learning. Unlike a folder of everything, a portfolio requires selection against criteria — the learner must justify why each piece is included and what it evidences. Portfolios serve both assessment (documenting competence) and learning (driving self-evaluation and goal-setting).

Design Implications¶

Portfolios shift assessment authority toward learners, requiring them to evaluate their own work against criteria — a form of Assessment for Learning that improves achievement when learners act on evidence of their own performance Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]. Their learning value comes chiefly from the reflective commentary, not the artifacts themselves; selection and justification force retrieval and evaluation of one's own development. Portfolios also connect work to real audiences and purposes, which raises the quality of student work Authentic audiences improve student work. [+M], and the choice inherent in curation supports intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear criteria or a rubric defining what the portfolio must evidence, shared before selection begins
  • Structured reflection prompts for each artifact (what it shows, how it was produced, what changed since)
  • Multiple work samples spanning time, so growth is visible rather than a single snapshot
  • Scheduled checkpoints for review and feedback, not a single end-of-term submission

Constraints¶

  • Without explicit criteria and training in self-evaluation, reflections become descriptive ("here is my essay") rather than evaluative, adding workload without learning benefit [-M]
  • Grading portfolios holistically shows poor inter-rater reliability compared with analytic rubrics or tests [-M] — reliability depends heavily on well-specified scoring guides
  • Heavy production demands (formatting, media, documentation) can consume time that would better go to learning itself [~M]
  • Reflection quality degrades when portfolios are compulsory and routine; the reflective stance is hard to mandate [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Process portfolios document development (drafts, revisions) — suited to writing and design disciplines
  • Showcase portfolios present best work against standards — suited to summative evaluation and credentialing
  • ePortfolios (e.g., Mahara, Digication, Seesaw) add multimedia artifacts and easier sharing with external audiences
  • Assessment portfolios in professional education (medicine, teaching) document competence against practice standards

Target Learners¶

  • Learners developing complex, ill-structured skills (writing, design, clinical practice) where competence cannot be captured by a single test
  • Adolescents and adults with enough metacognitive development to evaluate their own work; younger learners need heavily scaffolded reflection prompts
  • Learners in professional preparation programs where portfolios are an authentic genre of the field

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulated learning: monitoring, evaluating, and setting goals for one's own development
  • Demonstrating integrated competence across multiple performances over time
  • Metacognition and identity formation: articulating "what kind of learner/professional am I becoming"

Instructions¶

  1. Define the purpose and audience (learning, assessment, or credentialing) and select the portfolio type accordingly.
  2. Publish the criteria or rubric learners will select against; model an annotated exemplar portfolio.
  3. Have learners collect artifacts over the term, pairing each with a structured reflection (claim about learning, evidence in the artifact, next goal).
  4. Schedule midpoint conferences or peer review so learners act on feedback before final submission.
  5. Close with a synthesis reflection and, where possible, a presentation to an authentic audience Authentic audiences improve student work. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Rubric-Based Self-Assessment — supplies the criteria that make portfolio selection and reflection evaluative rather than descriptive
  • Learning Journals — the reflective engine that generates portfolio commentary over time
  • Exhibition of Learning — a culminating authentic audience for the finished portfolio
  • Use student-owned portfolios with digital and non-digital documentation to assess maker growth over time
  • Prepare for rising demand for assessment tools and embedded assessment models in maker learning
  • Demonstrate teaching competencies through performance-based artifacts

Examples¶

  • New Tech Network schools — students defend digital portfolios of project work before panels of teachers and community members at graduation (https://newtechnetwork.org).
  • Medical education (e.g., at Maastricht University and UK foundation programmes) — trainees maintain portfolios documenting clinical competencies, with mentor review sessions tied to workplace assessment.
  • IB Middle Years and Diploma Programmes — require documented process work and reflective statements in arts and theory of knowledge, functioning as curriculum-embedded portfolios.
