Selecting Tiered Vocabulary Words¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 0 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy categorizes candidate vocabulary words into three tiers to prioritize instruction: Tier 1 (everyday words like run or happy that rarely require instruction), Tier 2 (high-frequency academic words like analyze, reluctant, or significant that appear across contexts and are crucial for mature language), and Tier 3 (low-frequency, content-specific words like photosynthesis or tariff tied to a topic or text). Instruction concentrates on Tier 2 words for broadest academic benefit, supplemented by Tier 3 words needed to access current content. Learners may rate their own word knowledge and propose candidate words, giving the instructor formative data for selection.

Design Implications¶

Because instructional time and working memory are limited, word selection is the highest-leverage decision in vocabulary instruction: a meta-analysis found vocabulary instruction improves comprehension mainly when it targets words that actually appear in the texts learners must read Stahl & Fairbanks, 1986 [+M]. Tiering converts an unbounded word list into a prioritized, teachable set, and learner self-ratings add a metacognitive layer that surfaces gaps the instructor would otherwise miss [~W].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Familiarity with the three-tier framework and criteria (frequency, utility across contexts, conceptual demand)
  • Pre-reading analysis of the target text or unit to generate a candidate word list
  • A quick self-rating protocol (e.g., "know it / sort of know it / don't know it") learners can apply honestly
  • Time to revisit selected words across multiple encounters — single-exposure instruction produces weak gains [~S]

Constraints¶

  • Tier boundaries are judgment calls; some words straddle tiers, and misclassifying a high-utility word as Tier 3 removes it from instruction [-W]
  • Selecting too many words dilutes instruction; depth of processing for a few words beats shallow exposure to many Stahl & Fairbanks, 1986 [-M]
  • Learner self-ratings can be inflated by partial familiarity (recognizing a word without knowing its meaning) [~W]
  • Over-reliance on Tier 3 word lists can reduce vocabulary work to memorizing definitions for a test, which transfers poorly [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-selected: teacher tiers the candidate list from the text before reading; fastest, works with young learners
  • Learner-nominated: students skim the text, flag unknown words, and the class tiers them together; builds word consciousness and metacognition
  • Hybrid: teacher pre-selects Tier 2 words; students nominate Tier 3 words they need for the task
  • Digital: tools like Flocabulary and Vocabulary.com tag words by frequency and difficulty, approximating tiering at scale

Target Learners¶

  • Middle and high school students encountering academic language across disciplines Nagy & Townsend, 2012 [+M]
  • English learners, who benefit disproportionately from explicit Tier 2 instruction because these words are rarely acquired incidentally [+M]
  • Adult learners in developmental or career-technical education building academic register
  • Less useful for beginning readers still consolidating Tier 1 decoding and word recognition Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Academic language development: acquiring words that recur across disciplines and texts
  • Reading comprehension: removing lexical obstacles before reading complex text
  • Metacognition: calibrating learners' awareness of what they do and don't know
  • Independent word-learning dispositions: noticing and selecting words on their own

Instructions¶

  1. Preview the text or unit and draft a candidate word list of 10–20 words (Advance Organizers can frame the preview).
  2. Tier the candidates: set aside Tier 1 words; flag Tier 3 words essential to the content; identify Tier 2 words with cross-context utility.
  3. Have learners rate their knowledge of the shortlist using a three-point self-rating before instruction (Assess Performance).
  4. Select 5–8 instructional words, weighting Tier 2 words and unknown Tier 3 words required for comprehension; drop words most students already rate as known.
  5. Teach selected words with robust instruction — student-friendly definitions, multiple contexts, and active processing — rather than dictionary-copying (Direct Instruction paired with Annotating).
  6. Revisit words across days in new contexts so knowledge consolidates (Distributed practice improves retention. [+S]).

Related Strategies¶

  • Frayer Model — a common format for deep processing of the selected Tier 2 words
  • Pre-teaching Vocabulary — the instructional act that tiering feeds
  • Word Walls — ongoing display and reuse of tiered words

Examples¶

  • Before reading Lord of the Flies, students skim chapter one and sort words into "know it," "sort of know it," or "don't know it at all"; the teacher tiers the resulting list and selects efflorescence (Tier 3, needed for the text) and reluctant, scrutinize (Tier 2, broadly useful).
  • Word Generation (SERP Institute) — a middle-school program built around weekly sets of high-utility Tier 2 words (circumstance, controversy) cycled through discussion, debate, and writing across subjects.
  • A biology teacher tiers osmosis and mitochondria as Tier 3 must-teach words but also adds derive and component as Tier 2 words students need for lab reports in every unit.
