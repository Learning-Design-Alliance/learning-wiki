# Leveraging Instructor Office Hours

## Description
Office hours are scheduled, recurring times when instructors make themselves available for individual or small-group consultation. Used deliberately as a teaching strategy rather than a passive availability, they provide personalized help with course content, feedback on work in progress, and academic and career advice that extends beyond a single course.

## Design Implications

One-on-one interaction allows instruction to be calibrated precisely to the individual learner's needs, functioning as [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) that is contingent on what the student actually understands [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Office hours also build instructor–student relationships and students' sense of belonging and self-efficacy, which supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Because help is dialogic, instructors can deliver feedback at the task and process levels — the levels most strongly associated with learning gains [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Regular, predictable weekly times, advertised in the syllabus and reinforced in class
- A welcoming, low-stakes format (e.g., "drop in with any question," sign-up slots, or group office hours) that lowers the social barrier to attending
- Instructor preparation to diagnose misconceptions on the spot and respond with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) rather than simply re-lecturing

#### Constraints
- Many students — especially first-generation and novice learners — do not attend unless attendance is normalized, incentivized, or required; unstructured "my door is open" availability alone reaches few students [-M]
- Students may perceive office hours as remedial or fear appearing incompetent in a private setting, suppressing help-seeking [-M]
- In large-enrollment courses, individual office hours cannot scale to demand; without group formats or triage, access becomes inequitable [-M]
- Office hours used for re-delivering lecture content waste the format's main advantage — individualized diagnosis and dialogue [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Group office hours**: several students at once; peers hear each other's questions, normalizing help-seeking and leveraging [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Scheduled sign-up slots**: guarantee individual time and reduce the awkwardness of "dropping in"
- **Required early-semester visits**: every student attends once early in the term, which dramatically increases later voluntary use and lets instructors match names to faces in large lectures
- **Virtual office hours**: video calls or open online meeting rooms extend access to commuter and online learners
- **Exam-review sessions**: structured group formats that channel high-demand periods

### Target Learners
- Undergraduates of all levels, but especially first-year and first-generation students who lack tacit knowledge about how to seek academic help [+W]
- Students in large lecture courses who otherwise have no individual contact with the instructor [+W]
- Struggling students benefit most from the diagnostic, contingent help — but are the least likely to attend voluntarily, so design must actively recruit them [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Remediation of specific misconceptions through individualized diagnosis and feedback [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Metacognitive and study-skills development: how to prepare for exams, read the discipline, plan a paper
- Academic socialization and mentoring: navigating the major, research opportunities, career pathways
- Motivation and belonging: instructor attention raises perceived task value [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Advertise and normalize.** State office hours in the syllabus, explain what they are *for* (questions, drafts, advice — not punishment), and describe a typical visit so novices know what to expect.
2. **Require or incentivize an early visit.** Assign a short first-visit task (e.g., bring one question about the syllabus or a draft) so every student crosses the threshold once.
3. **Diagnose before telling.** Open each consultation by asking the student to explain their current thinking or attempt, then respond contingently with hints and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) rather than full re-explanation.
4. **Give process-level feedback.** Focus on strategies and self-regulation ("how did you study for this?") in addition to task-level corrections [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. **Use group formats for scale.** In large courses, run group or exam-review office hours so peers hear each other's questions and demand is distributed.
6. **Follow up.** Note recurring confusions and address them in class; close the loop between office hours and whole-class instruction.

## Related Strategies
- [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — brief structured conversations that share office hours' relationship-building function at classroom scale
- Structured peer tutoring — extends the same one-on-one diagnostic help when instructor time is the bottleneck

## Examples
- **Required early-semester interviews** in large introductory STEM courses: each student signs up for a 5–10 minute individual meeting in the first three weeks; instructors report sharply increased voluntary attendance and better name recognition afterward.
- **Group exam-review office hours**: before midterms, instructors convert office hours to open sessions where students work problems collaboratively while the instructor circulates and diagnoses errors.
- **Virtual office hours in online courses**: instructors hold recurring open video-meeting blocks, the primary channel for individualized feedback in programs with no physical campus.
