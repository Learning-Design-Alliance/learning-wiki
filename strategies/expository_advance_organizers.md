# Expository Advance Organizers

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Expository advance organizers give students a broad idea of the lesson's purpose before it begins by clearly stating the goals of the lesson. Introduced by Ausubel, they present material at a higher level of abstraction and inclusiveness than the lesson itself, providing an anchoring structure into which new content can be assimilated. In practice this means stating objectives verbally or in writing, previewing the key concepts, and showing how the lesson connects to prior knowledge before instruction begins.

## Design Implications

Advance organizers work by giving learners a pre-existing schema to subsume new material, reducing the working-memory burden of organizing unfamiliar content on the fly [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on the organizer being genuinely more general and inclusive than the lesson content — a mere summary of what will be covered adds little [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [~M]. Stating goals also supports [Activation](../principles/activation.md) by prompting learners to retrieve relevant prior knowledge before new material arrives [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clearly defined learning goals and a means to communicate them (verbally, on the board, or in a handout)
- An organizer pitched *above* the lesson content in abstraction — a concept map, overarching principle, or framing question, not just an agenda
- Explicit connection to learners' prior knowledge ([Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md))

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when the organizer is too broad or not clearly connected to lesson activities — a generic "today we will learn about photosynthesis" adds little structure [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [-M]
- Effects are small and inconsistent for learners who already possess well-organized prior knowledge of the topic [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [~M]
- Benefits fade when the organizer is presented but never referenced during the lesson; it must be used as an anchor, not a preamble

#### Implementation Variability
- Verbal goal statements at lesson opening (common in [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md))
- Written objectives displayed throughout the lesson ("objectives board")
- Graphic organizers or concept maps presented before a unit
- Comparative organizers that link new material to familiar content ("this is like the circulatory system, but for delivering information")

### Target Learners
- Novices encountering an unfamiliar topic, who lack an existing structure to organize incoming information [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]
- Learners with limited prior knowledge, who benefit most from an external anchoring structure
- Less beneficial for advanced learners whose existing knowledge already provides organization [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: building an inclusive framework for a new topic or unit
- Knowledge integration: connecting new material to prior knowledge [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Orientation and expectations: guiding attention toward the lesson's central ideas

### Instructions
1. Identify the one or two overarching concepts the lesson or unit serves, and state them in learner-friendly language ([State Objectives](../elements/state-objectives.md))
2. Present the organizer *before* the content — a framing question, concept map, or analogy at a higher level of abstraction than the lesson itself ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
3. Activate relevant prior knowledge with a brief prompt or question ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
4. Refer back to the organizer at key points during instruction, showing how each activity or concept fits the structure
5. Close the lesson by returning to the organizer and having learners elaborate it with what they learned

## Examples
- A biology teacher opens a unit on ecosystems by presenting a concept map of energy flow, then positions each subsequent lesson (producers, consumers, decomposition) within it.
- **Explicit Direct Instruction lessons** typically open with a "learning intention and success criteria" board, a routine popularized in Australian and UK schools.
- A history instructor frames a unit with "revolutions occur when three conditions align" before examining the French and Russian revolutions case by case.
