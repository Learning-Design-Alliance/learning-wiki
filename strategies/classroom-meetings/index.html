Classroom Meetings¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

A classroom meeting is a recurring, structured gathering — often daily or weekly — where the whole class discusses community issues, plans activities, solves interpersonal problems, and makes shared decisions. The teacher facilitates rather than directs, using protocols (greeting, sharing, problem-solving agenda items) that give every student voice. The format originates in Dewey's democratic education and Glasser's class meetings, and is institutionalized in programs such as Responsive Classroom and the Tribes Learning Community.

Design Implications¶

Classroom meetings operationalize the classroom as a community of practice, building the relational trust and shared norms on which collaborative learning depends. Regular meetings strengthen students' sense of belonging, which predicts engagement and persistence Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M], and shared decision-making supports autonomy, a core driver of intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. Meetings also give teachers formative insight into classroom climate that individual check-ins miss.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A predictable structure and schedule (e.g., Responsive Classroom's greeting–sharing–activity–news sequence)
  • Agreed norms for speaking and listening, taught explicitly (Clear Structure)
  • A visible agenda or problem-solving protocol so discussion stays purposeful
  • Teacher facilitation skill: open-ended questioning, neutral mediation, and willingness to share real decision-making power

Constraints¶

  • Meetings without genuine decision authority become performative and erode trust [~M] — students detect token consultation quickly
  • Poorly facilitated meetings can be dominated by a few voices, marginalizing quieter students [~M]; structured turn-taking or talking-piece protocols mitigate this
  • Time cost is real: meetings displace instructional minutes, and benefits depend on the meeting actually improving climate and self-regulation rather than consuming time [~W]
  • In classrooms with weak norms or unresolved conflict, open discussion can escalate rather than resolve problems [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Morning meeting (Responsive Classroom): daily 15–25 minute opening ritual for K–8
  • Problem-solving class meeting (Glasser): convened ad hoc around a specific behavioral or academic issue
  • Democratic meeting (Sudbury/Summerhill tradition): full voting authority over class rules and budgets
  • Circle formats (restorative practices): talking-piece circles for conflict repair and community building

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and middle-school students, for whom community rituals strongly shape engagement Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]
  • Students with weak self-regulation or social skills, who benefit from modeled and rehearsed prosocial routines
  • Adolescents, when meetings carry authentic decision-making weight; token versions backfire with this age group Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Social-emotional competencies: empathy, perspective-taking, conflict resolution
  • Self-regulation and shared responsibility for classroom norms
  • Civic and discussion skills: turn-taking, argument, collective decision-making
  • Academic uses: goal-setting, reflection on Assessment results, planning Collaborative Learning work

Instructions¶

  1. Establish norms first: co-create speaking and listening rules and rehearse them (Clear Structure).
  2. Open with connection: a greeting round and brief sharing so every student speaks early (Check-In).
  3. Work the agenda: address one or two student- or teacher-submitted items using a problem-solving protocol (identify the problem, brainstorm, agree on an action).
  4. Close with commitment: restate decisions, assign follow-ups, and revisit them at the next meeting.
  5. Fade facilitation: shift agenda-setting and chairing roles to students as they gain competence (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-In — the individual-level counterpart; meetings aggregate check-ins into collective sense-making
  • Restorative Circles — a conflict-repair variant of the meeting format
  • Cooperative Learning — meetings build the norms that make group work function

Examples¶

  • Responsive Classroom Morning Meeting — daily greeting, sharing, group activity, and news-and-announcements; supported by a three-year longitudinal study linking implementation fidelity to reading and math gains (Rimm-Kaufman et al., 2007).
  • Glasser-style problem-solving meetings — a class convenes to address recurring disruption, brainstorming solutions and voting on a class agreement.
  • Restorative practice circles in secondary schools — weekly talking-piece circles used to build community and repair harm, as implemented in Denver Public Schools' restorative justice program.
