Visualizations and Mind Maps¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 2 mixed) · 15 studies (6 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 15 report an effect size

Description¶

Visualizations and mind maps present information graphically — as concept maps, diagrams, timelines, flowcharts, or infographics — so that relationships among ideas are visible rather than buried in prose. A mind map radiates from a central concept with branching associations; concept maps and diagrams additionally label the type of relationship between nodes. The strategy can be enacted by providing learners a ready-made graphic or by having learners construct one themselves.

Design Implications¶

Visual representations exploit the capacity of the visual channel to carry information in parallel with verbal processing, improving recall when words and pictures are integrated Dual coding of verbal and visual information improves recall. [+S]. Graphic organizers are most valuable for novices, who lack the schemas to impose structure on new material themselves Graphic organizers support comprehension for learners with low prior knowledge. [+M]. However, visuals must carry the structure of the content; decorative images that do not map to the to-be-learned relations add load without benefit Decorative illustrations do not improve learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A visual whose spatial arrangement genuinely encodes the conceptual structure (hierarchy, sequence, causality, part–whole), not decoration
  • Labels on nodes and relationships — unlabeled arrows leave learners guessing the connection
  • Integration of visual and verbal information on the same display rather than split across pages Chunking and integrating information reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • For learner-constructed maps: a modeling example, criteria for good links, and a revision cycle

Constraints¶

  • Provided organizers can encourage shallow copying; learners who construct their own graphic organizers often outperform those given one Learner-constructed graphic organizers outperform provided ones. [~M]
  • Benefits shrink or reverse for high-knowledge learners, for whom the organizer restates what they already know Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • Overly dense or ornate visuals (3D effects, gratuitous icons) impose extraneous load and depress learning Decorative illustrations do not improve learning. [-M]
  • Mind maps' free-form radial structure suits brainstorming and association but poorly represents causal chains, procedures, or quantitative relations — use flowcharts or diagrams instead

Implementation Variability¶

  • Advance organizer: present a skeletal visual before instruction to frame incoming content (Advance Organizers)
  • Fill-in-the-map: provide nodes, have learners supply or verify the links — an intermediate step between given and fully constructed maps
  • Progressive disclosure: build the map incrementally during instruction rather than showing the finished artifact at once
  • Learner-generated: students draw their own maps as a summarization or assessment activity (Annotating in visual form)

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and low-prior-knowledge learners, who benefit most from externally supplied structure Graphic organizers support comprehension for learners with low prior knowledge. [+M]
  • Learners struggling with dense expository text; the visual offloads relational structure from working memory Chunking and integrating information reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less valuable for experts, who already hold the schema and may find the map redundant Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: seeing how ideas relate, not just what they are
  • Knowledge organization: building well-structured schemas that support retrieval and transfer
  • Summarization and study skills: condensing material into a retrievable visual form

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the core structure of the content (hierarchy, cycle, causal chain, comparison) and choose a visual form that matches it — do not default to a mind map for everything.
  2. Decide whether to provide, co-construct, or have learners build the map; for novices, start provided and shift toward construction as knowledge grows Learner-constructed graphic organizers outperform provided ones. [~M].
  3. Label every node and every link with the relationship type ("causes," "is a kind of," "precedes").
  4. Integrate the visual with concise text on the same display; avoid split-attention between map and legend Chunking and integrating information reduces working memory load. [+M].
  5. Use the map actively: have learners explain it aloud, predict a missing branch, or revise it after new content (Activation, Application).
  6. Revisit and revise the map across the unit so it functions as a growing external model rather than a one-off artifact.

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — a visualization presented before instruction to scaffold incoming information
  • Analogies — a verbal counterpart that maps unfamiliar structure onto familiar; often paired with a visual
  • Annotating — learner-generated marks and notes that can take graphical form
  • Chunking — the working-memory principle that visual grouping enacts

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy — concept summaries and diagrams accompanying video lessons in science and economics.
  • MindMeister / Coggle — collaborative mind-mapping tools used for brainstorming and shared concept maps in classrooms.
  • Biointeractive (HHMI) — published diagrams and visual models (e.g., nutrient cycling, genetics) designed to encode scientific processes rather than decorate text.
  • History timelines in the Reading Like a Historian curriculum (Stanford History Education Group) — structured visuals that situate documents in causal and chronological relation.
