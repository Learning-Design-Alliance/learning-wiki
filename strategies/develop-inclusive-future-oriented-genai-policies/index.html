Higher education leaders should develop more inclusive and future-oriented GenAI policies integrating social equity, interdisciplinary experimentation, and sustainability considerations¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

The article recommends that higher education leadership move beyond widely embraced ethical and governance principles to address the components their sampled policies neglected. It "underscores the necessity for higher education leaders to develop more inclusive and future-oriented policies that integrate social equity, interdisciplinary experimentation, and sustainability considerations." This responds directly to the finding that inclusion, equity, and sustainability issues are often overlooked in current institutional GenAI policies.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Institutional leadership with authority to design and revise GenAI policies

Constraints¶

  • Grounded in policies of highly ranked universities in the top ten countries for AI preparedness; long-term policy impact on operations was not studied

Target Learners¶

  • higher education students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • safe and responsible integration of generative AI
  • promotion of academic integrity

Affordances¶

  • Unesco Eight Component Genai Framework

Related Strategies¶

  • Foster communities of practice for AI governance instead of rigid top-down policies
  • Plan frequent policy updates and adapt instructional methods for AI integration
  • Address responsible design, learner experience, equity, validity, and relational assessment practice when implementing AI-agent-supported assessment
  • Build ethical AI integration on four coordinated levels: faculty professional development, ethics curricula, clear institutional guidelines, and interdisciplinary updating
  • Prioritize real-world testing of AI tools in university settings, environmental impact measurement, and integrated ethical-sustainability policies
  • Institutions should respond to GenAI with clear governance frameworks and structured professional training programs for staff.
  • Foster interdisciplinary collaboration and actively include student perspectives in institutional GenAI responses.
  • Classify and design AI teaching tools by governance mechanism, using transferable design questions rather than generic tool categories
  • Transition from restrictive AI governance to frameworks that formally integrate operational and epistemic workflow competencies with subject-sensitive guidance
  • Address equity, transparency, and teacher professional judgment when deploying AI-mediated collaboration
  • Incorporate AI literacy into educational-leadership preparation curricula
  • Position AI detection tools as one component within a broader institutional decision-making process, with regularly updated benchmarks
  • Tripartite institutional response: AI literacy, ethical infrastructure, and competence-based educator training
  • Develop student AI literacy and institutional GenAI policies, guidelines and safeguards for responsible integration
  • Define institutional value from the bottom up, incorporating learning outcomes, community engagement, and career readiness beyond earnings thresholds
