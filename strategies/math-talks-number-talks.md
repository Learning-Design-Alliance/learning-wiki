# Math Talks / Number Talks

## Description
Math Talks, also known as Number Talks, are short (5–15 minute), frequent classroom routines in which students solve a computation or estimation problem mentally — no paper or pencil — and then share their answers and the strategies behind them. The instructor records and names each strategy, facilitates comparison across approaches, and uses probing questions to clarify and extend student thinking. The routine builds mental computation fluency, flexibility with numbers and operations, and mathematical communication, while positioning errors and divergent strategies as objects of productive discussion.

## Design Implications

Math Talks operationalize [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) in a compact, repeatable format. Their effectiveness rests on two mechanisms: students must articulate their own reasoning, which functions as prompted self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and exposure to multiple peer strategies creates contrasting cases that support abstraction of underlying number relationships [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The mental-math constraint also prevents over-reliance on taught procedures, forcing flexible decomposition and recomposition of quantities.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A problem displayed visibly (board, dot card, rekenrek) solvable by multiple strategies
- A classroom climate where wrong answers and partial strategies are treated as discussion material, not failures
- Instructor fluency with common mental strategies (e.g., compensation, decomposition, friendly numbers) so they can name, sequence, and — if needed — introduce strategies the class does not surface
- Prepared clarifying and extending questions ("How is this strategy like hers?", "Will that always work?")

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends heavily on facilitation skill; a talk that merely collects answers without comparing strategies degrades into answer-checking [~M]
- Students hesitant to share publicly, or unable to verbalize intuitive strategies, may participate minimally — wait time, partner rehearsal, and recording strategies for them reduce this risk
- Poorly chosen problems (too easy, or with only one viable strategy) eliminate the contrasting-case benefit [~M]
- As a short routine, it supplements but does not replace explicit instruction on new procedures; it is not a vehicle for first exposure to unfamiliar content [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dot cards and rekenreks** (early grades): subitizing and part-whole relationships
- **Estimation talks**: "Would you rather" or "Which is more?" prompts emphasizing reasonableness
- **Fraction/decimal talks** (upper grades): same routine applied to non-integer operations
- **Silent starts and partner talk**: lower-risk entry points before whole-class sharing
- **Student-led talks**: older students facilitate, deepening their own reasoning

### Target Learners
- All grade levels, from early elementary through upper grades; problems are scaled to student skill
- Particularly valuable for students with procedural fluency but weak number sense, who must build flexible mental models [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners benefit from the oral, strategy-naming structure, though they may need sentence frames to articulate strategies [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency grounded in conceptual understanding of number and operations
- Flexible strategy selection and mental computation
- Mathematical discourse: explaining, justifying, and critiquing reasoning
- Norms of mathematical community and productive error analysis

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — removing paper and pencil constrains working memory load to what can be held mentally, pushing students toward efficient decomposition rather than laborious written algorithms
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — every student solves before anyone shares; the routine structures generative engagement rather than passive listening
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — peer strategies become shared instructional content; students learn approaches from one another
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — the public exchange of strategies gives the instructor real-time formative evidence of reasoning, not just answers

### Instructions
1. **Prepare the problem.** Choose a computation with multiple solution paths and anticipate the strategies students will likely use.
2. **Present and solve mentally.** Display the problem; students solve silently without paper or pencil ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. **Collect answers first.** Record all offered answers without judging, so revision is safe.
4. **Elicit strategies.** Ask "How did you solve it?" and record student thinking verbatim ([Eliciting Student Thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md)).
5. **Facilitate comparison.** Have students discuss, name, and connect strategies ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)); ask which are efficient, which generalize, and whether a strategy always works.
6. **Name and extend.** Attach mathematical language to strategies and, if none surface, model one briefly; close by revising the answer list.

## Examples
- **Dot card quick images** (K–2): flash a card of dots for 3 seconds; students report how many and how they grouped them, building part-whole structure.
- **Decomposition talk** (grades 3–5): 24 × 8 solved as (20 × 8) + (4 × 8), compared against doubling (24 × 2 × 2 × 2) and compensation (25 × 8 − 8).
- **Greg Tang's *The Grapes of Math* (Scholastic, 2001)** — published riddle pages used as Math Talk prompts for finding efficient counting groupings.
- **[Number Talks by Sherry Parrish](https://www.mathsolutions.com/number-talks/)** — the widely used professional resource with grade-banded problem sequences and classroom video.
