# Math Talks / Number Talks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Math Talks, also known as Number Talks, are short (5–15 minute), frequent classroom routines in which students solve a computation or estimation problem mentally — no paper or pencil — and then share their answers and the strategies behind them. The instructor records and names each strategy, facilitates comparison across approaches, and uses probing questions to clarify and extend student thinking. The routine builds mental computation fluency, flexibility with numbers and operations, and mathematical communication, while positioning errors and divergent strategies as objects of productive discussion.

## Design Implications

Math Talks operationalize [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) in a compact, repeatable format. Their effectiveness rests on two mechanisms: students must articulate their own reasoning, which functions as prompted self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S], and exposure to multiple peer strategies creates contrasting cases that support abstraction of underlying number relationships [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The mental-math constraint also prevents over-reliance on taught procedures, forcing flexible decomposition and recomposition of quantities.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A problem displayed visibly (board, dot card, rekenrek) solvable by multiple strategies
- A classroom climate where wrong answers and partial strategies are treated as discussion material, not failures
- Instructor fluency with common mental strategies (e.g., compensation, decomposition, friendly numbers) so they can name, sequence, and — if needed — introduce strategies the class does not surface
- Prepared clarifying and extending questions ("How is this strategy like hers?", "Will that always work?")
- Problems deliberately selected to invite multiple efficient strategies (e.g., 16 × 25, 198 + 47)
- A classroom norm that errors are sites of learning and that all strategies are recorded before evaluation
- Teacher fluency in notation for recording strategies (open number lines, arrays, partial products) and in neutral facilitation questions ("Who can explain why that works?")
- Consistent routine structure: problem posed → silent think time → signal when ready → strategies shared and annotated
- Carefully sequenced problems that make target strategies likely to emerge (e.g., problems with friendly numbers)
- A classroom culture where errors are treated as sites of learning and students feel safe offering partial ideas
- Teacher facility with anticipating student strategies and representing them clearly (open number lines, arrays, partial products)
- A consistent protocol: silent think time, signal when ready, share, teacher records and names strategies

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends heavily on facilitation skill; a talk that merely collects answers without comparing strategies degrades into answer-checking [~M]
- Students hesitant to share publicly, or unable to verbalize intuitive strategies, may participate minimally — wait time, partner rehearsal, and recording strategies for them reduce this risk
- Poorly chosen problems (too easy, or with only one viable strategy) eliminate the contrasting-case benefit [~M]
- As a short routine, it supplements but does not replace explicit instruction on new procedures; it is not a vehicle for first exposure to unfamiliar content [-W]
- Fails when the teacher steers toward one "correct" method, converting the discourse into recitation [-M] — premature evaluation suppresses strategy generation
- Problems requiring single-step recall or with only one viable path produce no strategy comparison and collapse into answer-checking [-M]
- Dominant voices can crowd out reasoning from struggling students; without turn-and-talk or hand-signal wait structures, participation narrows [-W]
- Students with very weak fact fluency may spend all capacity on retrieval rather than strategy, gaining little until fluency is supported [~M]

### Instructions
1. **Prepare the problem.** Choose a computation with multiple solution paths and anticipate the strategies students will likely use.
2. **Present and solve mentally.** Display the problem; students solve silently without paper or pencil ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. **Collect answers first.** Record all offered answers without judging, so revision is safe.
4. **Elicit strategies.** Ask "How did you solve it?" and record student thinking verbatim ([Eliciting Student Thinking](../elements/eliciting-student-thinking.md)).
5. **Facilitate comparison.** Have students discuss, name, and connect strategies ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)); ask which are efficient, which generalize, and whether a strategy always works.
6. **Name and extend.** Attach mathematical language to strategies and, if none surface, model one briefly; close by revising the answer list.
