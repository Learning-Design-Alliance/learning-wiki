    Understanding By Design Unit Template¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    The Understanding by Design (UbD) Unit Template operationalizes backward design: planners first specify desired results (enduring understandings, essential questions, knowledge and skills), then determine acceptable evidence (performance tasks and other assessments aligned to those results), and only last design the learning plan (activities, instruction, and resources). The template enforces this three-stage sequence — Stage 1 → Stage 2 → Stage 3 — so that activities are justified by the evidence they should produce rather than selected for engagement alone.

    Design Implications¶

    Backward design counters the common "activity-oriented" and "coverage-oriented" planning habits in which engaging tasks or textbook pacing drive units, leaving assessment as an afterthought [~M]. Starting from the evidence requirement makes alignment the central design constraint: every Stage 3 activity must plausibly produce the performance demanded in Stage 2, which in turn must operationalize the understandings named in Stage 1. The template's insistence on transfer tasks — assessments in which learners apply learning to a novel, realistic situation — connects unit planning to research on transfer and Authentic Audiences & Purposes Authentic audiences improve student work. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Clear answers to "What should learners understand and be able to do?" — typically 1–3 enduring understandings and a small set of essential questions per unit, not a long list of objectives
    • A performance task (GRASPS-style: Goal, Role, Audience, Situation, Product, Standards) with an analytic rubric that defines what understanding looks like in performance
    • Alignment checks across stages: each Stage 1 goal has Stage 2 evidence, and each Stage 2 evidence item has Stage 3 learning events
    • Formative checkpoints embedded in Stage 3, not only a summative task (Assessment)

    Constraints¶

    • The template does not itself improve learning; units built in the template but with vague "understandings" or rubrics that reward compliance rather than understanding show no benefit [-W]
    • Backward design is effortful; teams under time pressure often fill the template front-to-back, defeating its purpose [-W]
    • Transfer-oriented performance tasks are harder to score reliably than selected-response tests; without rubric calibration, grading consistency suffers [~M]
    • Less suited to units targeting fluent procedural skill, where distributed practice and accuracy checks matter more than novel application [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Design-only vs. design-and-review: some districts use the template purely as a planning artifact; stronger implementations add peer review protocols (e.g., ASCD's UbD design standards) in which colleagues critique alignment
    • Granularity: the template scales from a single lesson to a year-long course map; most value comes at the unit level
    • Disciplinary adaptation: math and science teams often pair the template with problem-based performance tasks; humanities teams with document-based questions and seminars

    Target Learners¶

    • All K–16 learners benefit indirectly through better-aligned instruction; the template is a teacher-facing tool, not a learner-facing one
    • Transfer-oriented tasks and essential questions particularly serve learners ready to apply concepts in novel contexts [~M]
    • Explicit Stage 1 "knowledge and skills" listings help teachers plan Advance Organizers and prerequisite scaffolds for novices

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Conceptual understanding and transfer, not recall alone — Stage 1 explicitly separates "enduring understandings" from "worth being familiar with"
    • Disciplinary practices: performance tasks ask learners to do the subject (interpret data, argue from evidence, design)
    • Self-assessment and revision, when rubrics are shared with learners and used formatively Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]

    Instructions¶

    1. Stage 1 — Desired Results. Draft the established goals, then 1–3 enduring understandings ("Students will understand that…"), essential questions, and the knowledge/skills learners will acquire. Keep the list short; depth over coverage.
    2. Stage 2 — Evidence. Design a transfer performance task in GRASPS form plus "other evidence" (quizzes, prompts, observations). Write or select an analytic rubric describing levels of understanding, not just task completion (Assessment).
    3. Stage 3 — Learning Plan. Sequence learning events using a WHERETO check (Where/Why, Hook, Explore/Equip, Rethink/Revise, Evaluate, Tailor, Organize), embedding formative checkpoints and opportunities to Apply understanding before the summative task.
    4. Align and review. Audit the three stages against each other; have a colleague check that every Stage 1 understanding has observable Stage 2 evidence.

    Related Strategies¶

    • Backward Design — the underlying planning logic the template operationalizes
    • Essential Questions — Stage 1's engine for framing understandings as inquiry
    • Performance Task Design — the Stage 2 assessment genre at the template's core
    • Rubric Design — the scoring instrument that makes understanding visible

    Examples¶

    • ASCD UbD Exchange and Understanding by Design Professional Development Workbook — the official template with design standards used in thousands of school systems (ASCD)
    • AP course frameworks (College Board) — course and exam descriptions that specify learning objectives before instructional approaches, mirroring backward design at curriculum scale (College Board)
    • Next Generation Science Standards storyline units — district units (e.g., OpenSciEd, https://www.openscied.org) that begin with a transfer-oriented anchoring phenomenon and design assessments backward from it
