# Attention Check

## Description
An attention check is a brief, structured pause in which learners are prompted to notice the current state of their attention — present, wandering, or fatigued — and to deliberately redirect it toward the task. It functions as a micro-intervention for metacognitive monitoring, typically lasting under a minute, and can be delivered verbally, through a signal, or via a quick response prompt. Unlike comprehension checks, which assess understanding of content, attention checks target the learner's cognitive and affective readiness to engage with content.

## Design Implications

Attention checks operationalize self-monitoring: prompting learners to evaluate their own attentional state improves subsequent self-regulation of learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. They also interrupt mind-wandering, which competes directly with encoding during instruction; brief external cues to re-engage reduce the time spent in an off-task state [~M]. Because attention is a limited resource, checks work best when paired with [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and segmenting of content so that redirection lands at natural boundaries rather than mid-explanation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A predictable, low-stakes routine (a signal, a question, or a one-word response) so the check itself does not consume working memory
- Content segmented into short segments with natural pause points ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
- A non-punitive stance: the check must make mind-wandering safe to admit, or learners will report false attention and the practice loses its metacognitive value
- A redirection move ready at hand — a stretch, a re-read, a restate-to-a-neighbor — not just the noticing itself

#### Constraints
- Frequent or intrusive checks fragment the flow of demanding tasks and add extraneous load, particularly for learners already near working-memory capacity [~M]
- Checks framed as surveillance (identifying "who wasn't listening") suppress honest self-report and undermine the monitoring habit they are meant to build [-M]
- In long individual work sessions, externally imposed interruptions can be more disruptive than the mind-wandering they prevent; self-initiated checks are preferable once learners have the habit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class verbal check**: "Thumbs up / sideways / down — where is your attention right now?" at segment boundaries
- **Private self-check**: learners mark a tracking sheet or digital pulse-check (e.g., a one-click emoji poll in Zoom or Top Hat) so honesty is not socially costly
- **Physical reset**: a 30-second stand-and-breathe routine before re-engaging, useful after high-load segments
- **Learner-initiated version**: students are taught to run the same check independently during [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or study sessions, transferring the routine from teacher-managed to self-managed

### Target Learners
- Novices and younger learners, whose attention is more likely to drift during explicit instruction and who benefit from external monitoring cues until self-monitoring is established [+M]
- Learners in long lectures or extended reading, where mind-wandering increases with time on task [~M]
- Less useful for highly engaged, self-regulated learners working on intrinsically motivating tasks, for whom interruptions cost more than they recover [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: building the monitor-and-repair habit that underlies independent study
- Sustained attention during [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) segments and complex explanations
- Metacognitive awareness: distinguishing "I was present" from "I understood," a discrimination that supports later [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) about understanding

### Instructions
1. Segment the lesson into blocks of roughly 8–12 minutes, aligned with natural content boundaries ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
2. At each boundary, run the check: a brief signal, a private or public attention self-report, and 10–20 seconds of silence for noticing.
3. Offer an immediate redirection option — stretch, restate, or re-engage prompt — so the check ends in action, not just awareness.
4. Over the term, fade the external cue and prompt learners to run the check themselves during independent work, transferring responsibility as in [Scaffolding](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).
5. Pair attention checks with content-focused [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) so learners learn to distinguish attentional presence from actual understanding.

## Examples
- A middle-school teacher uses a silent hand signal at the end of each worked-example segment; students show 1–3 fingers for their attention level, then complete a two-minute [Practice](../elements/practice.md) problem before the next segment.
- An online course in Canvas embeds a one-question "Where was your attention just now?" pulse after each 10-minute video segment, giving learners private feedback on their drift patterns across the term.
- Mindfulness-in-the-classroom programs such as those studied by Schonert-Reichl and colleagues use brief "mindful attention" pauses at lesson transitions as a whole-class routine.
