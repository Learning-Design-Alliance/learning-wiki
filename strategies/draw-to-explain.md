# Draw To Explain

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Draw To Explain asks learners to produce their own drawing, sketch, or diagram that explains a concept, process, or causal system — not to view or copy one. The act of constructing the visual forces learners to select key ideas, organize spatial relationships, and integrate them with verbal knowledge, making it a generative activity rather than a receptive one. It is typically paired with a short verbal explanation or label requirement so the drawing must communicate, not merely decorate.

## Design Implications

Drawing is a generative learning activity: learners must construct external structure from their own understanding, which exposes gaps and supports integration of pictorial and verbal representations [Dual coding of verbal and visual information improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]. The benefit comes from the *construction* process, not the visual product — learner-generated drawings outperform viewing equivalent provided images [+M], and decorative or instructor-supplied pictures that learners do not process deeply add little [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Because drawing consumes working memory, it works best when the content is moderately complex and the drawing conventions are simple [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prompt that specifies what the drawing must explain (a mechanism, sequence, or relationship), not just what it should depict
- Simple drawing conventions (boxes, arrows, labels) so tool handling does not dominate working memory
- A feedback or self-explanation step — comparing the drawing to a reference or explaining it aloud — so misconceptions surface rather than being encoded [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Time: generative drawing is slower than reading or viewing; tasks must budget for it

#### Constraints
- Drawing can overload learners when the material is already high in element interactivity; for complex content, completing or annotating a partial diagram is more effective than drawing from scratch [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Learners with weak drawing conventions or low prior knowledge may spend effort on aesthetics rather than structure, producing no learning benefit [-M]
- For learners who already have well-formed mental models, drawing from scratch adds little and can be redundant [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Without feedback, inaccurate drawings can consolidate misconceptions [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Draw-from-scratch** — highest generative load; best for moderate-complexity content after initial instruction
- **Complete-the-diagram** — learners fill in missing nodes or arrows on a partial structure; lowers load for complex systems
- **Draw-to-a-peer** — the drawing must explain to a hypothetical audience, adding an audience constraint that improves selectivity
- **Sketchnoting / annotated drawing** — combines drawing with [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) text notes; useful for lecture and reading comprehension

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge — enough to select relevant content, not so much that drawing is redundant [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Students in science domains where spatial and causal structure matters (mechanisms, cycles, systems) [+M]
- Younger learners benefit when drawing conventions are explicitly taught first [-W if not]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of systems, processes, and causal mechanisms
- Integration of verbal and pictorial knowledge structures [Dual coding of verbal and visual information improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]
- Formative diagnosis: drawings make misconceptions visible to instructors in ways written answers may not

### Instructions
1. Teach the content briefly so learners have raw material to work with; drawing with zero prior exposure produces unguided search.
2. Give a focused prompt: "Draw how X causes Y," with required labels and arrows — constrain format to keep load manageable ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the content into one drawable unit per prompt).
3. Have learners draw, then explain their drawing aloud or in writing ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)); the explanation is where integration happens.
4. Provide a reference diagram or peer comparison for feedback; have learners revise their drawing to correct errors.
5. Fade support over time — from completing partial diagrams to drawing from scratch — as competence grows [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Related Strategies
- **Draw-to-remember** — same drawing act but oriented to recall rather than explanation; weaker for causal understanding
- **Peer explanation** — drawings become artifacts for peer critique, adding feedback loops

## Examples
- **[Picturing to Learn](https://picturingtosolve.org)** (Harvard/MIT) — undergraduate science students create drawings to explain concepts to non-experts; the audience constraint improves selectivity and reveals misconceptions.
- **Sketchnoting in biology courses** — students sketch processes like cellular respiration as arrow-and-box diagrams during lecture, then explain their sketch to a partner.
- **Model-based inquiry curricula** (e.g., [Ambitious Science Teaching](https://ambitiousscienceteaching.org)) — students draw initial models of a phenomenon, revise them across a unit as evidence accumulates, making conceptual change visible.
