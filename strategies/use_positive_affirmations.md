# Use Positive Affirmations

## Description
Positive affirmations are short, self-referential statements ("I am capable of solving hard problems") that learners repeat or reflect on to counter negative self-talk. The strategy draws on two distinct literatures: *self-affirmation theory*, in which reflecting on core personal values protects self-integrity under threat [Cohen & Sherman, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137) [+S], and *positive self-statements*, in which learners directly assert positive qualities — a practice with much weaker and more conditional support [+W].

## Design Implications

Affirmation works best when it is framed as **values reflection** rather than direct self-praise: having learners write about their most important values before a stressful academic task reliably reduces defensive responses and improves performance for threatened groups [Cohen & Sherman, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137) [+S]. Direct positive self-statements ("I am lovable") are far less reliable — for learners with low self-esteem they can backfire, producing more negative mood, likely because the statement conflicts with self-beliefs and triggers counterarguing [Wood, Perunovic, & Lee, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609337163) [~M]. Affirmations support persistence by strengthening [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M], but they are a complement to instruction, not a substitute for it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Affirmations or value reflections that are credible to the learner — grounded in real values, past successes, or specific evidence, not generic praise
- Consistent timing: before assessments, presentations, or other performance-relevant moments
- A private or low-stakes channel (journal, reflection prompt, check-in) so learners are not performing positivity publicly

#### Constraints
- Direct positive self-statements can *worsen* mood for learners with low self-esteem, who reject statements that contradict their self-view [Wood et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609337163) [-M]
- Affirmations that feel imposed or artificial by the instructor lose credibility and can read as dismissive of real difficulty [-W]
- Repeating affirmations without accompanying skill-building or feedback produces confidence without competence; it does not substitute for [practice](../elements/practice.md) or [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) [-W]
- Effects of values-affirmation interventions in field settings are sometimes small and fade without reinforcement [Cohen & Sherman, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Values affirmation**: learners rank and write about their most important values (the best-supported variant) [+S]
- **Coping self-talk**: process-focused statements ("I can improve with effort") tied to [growth framing](../elements/feedback.md) [~W]
- **Pre-performance routines**: brief affirmation paired with goal-setting before exams or presentations
- **Embedded prompts**: affirmation moments integrated into [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) rather than delivered as a standalone exercise

### Target Learners
- Learners experiencing stereotype threat or academic self-doubt — values affirmation shows its largest benefits here [Cohen & Sherman, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115137) [+S]
- Learners whose negative self-talk undermines persistence; affirmations tied to real strengths support [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners with low self-esteem need process-focused, evidence-based statements rather than absolute positive claims [Wood et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609337163) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and motivational goals: reducing anxiety, sustaining persistence on difficult tasks
- Metacognitive goals: recognizing and reframing unproductive self-talk as part of [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Not appropriate as a goal in itself for knowledge or skill acquisition

### Instructions
1. Have learners identify 2–3 core personal values or genuine past successes (private writing, 5–10 minutes).
2. Guide learners to convert negative self-statements into credible, process-focused alternatives ("I struggled with fractions before, and practice helped" rather than "I am a math genius").
3. Schedule brief affirmation or values-reflection moments before high-stakes activities — pair with [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md).
4. Connect affirmations to concrete action: each statement should point to a strategy or next step, not just a feeling.
5. Revisit and revise affirmations periodically so they stay tied to real progress and [autonomy](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) — learners should author or choose their own.

## Examples
- **Values-affirmation writing before exams**: middle-school students wrote about their most important values at the start of the term, narrowing the racial achievement gap in a series of field experiments (Cohen et al., 2006, *Science*).
- **Journaling apps with prompt-based reflection**: tools such as Day One or Penzu can host private affirmation and values prompts on a recurring schedule.
- **Pre-presentation routines**: a brief written affirmation of preparation and past successes before a class presentation, paired with a concrete rehearsal plan.
