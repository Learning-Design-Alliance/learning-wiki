# Listening to the Voices of Students in PBL

## Description
Listening to the voices of students means structuring project-based learning so that students have genuine authorship — they pose questions, tell their own stories, make consequential decisions about project direction, and see their input visibly shape the work. It is carried out through elicitation (structured sharing and discussion), responsiveness (instructor acts on what students say), and shared leadership (students hold roles with real decision rights). This contrasts with traditional transmission models in which students are positioned as recipients rather than co-designers of learning.

## Design Implications

Student voice works as a motivational lever: autonomy and perceived ownership support intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], and connecting projects to students' own experiences raises task value and engagement [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Voice alone is insufficient, however — it must be paired with responsiveness; students who perceive that their input is collected but ignored show disengagement and distrust [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A psychologically safe, inclusive space where students believe sharing will be respected rather than judged
- Authentic decision points in the project where student input genuinely changes outcomes ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))
- Structured elicitation routines — [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), and [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — rather than open-ended "any questions?"
- Instructor willingness to relinquish control of project direction and to model responsive revision of plans

#### Constraints
- Tokenism backfires: soliciting voice without acting on it reduces trust and future participation [-M]
- Students with low [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) may initially avoid voice opportunities; scaffolding and low-stakes sharing formats are needed before high-stakes authorship
- Unstructured voice can drift into unfocused discussion; moderation and clear project constraints keep autonomy productive [~M]
- Requires a shift in instructor role from content deliverer to facilitator, which can be difficult in accountability-driven settings

#### Implementation Variability
- Entry-level: structured sharing circles and story-telling prompts within teacher-defined projects
- Mid-level: student choice of driving questions, products, and team roles
- Full co-design: students negotiate project scope, assessment criteria, and public exhibition with the instructor
- Digital variants: asynchronous discussion boards and project journals that surface quieter students' voices

### Target Learners
- All students in PBL settings, but especially those whose perspectives are marginalized in traditional instruction; voice opportunities build belonging and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and older learners, who respond strongly to autonomy support [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Younger learners need more scaffolding of decision-making before full authorship is productive [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Agency and leadership: students learn to initiate, negotiate, and direct collaborative work
- Communication and self-advocacy: articulating one's perspective and story to real audiences
- Ownership of learning: metacognitive investment in project outcomes
- Equity outcomes: surfacing community knowledge and lived experience as legitimate project content

### Instructions
1. Open the project with structured elicitation — use [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and story-telling prompts to surface students' questions, experiences, and priorities related to the topic.
2. Establish visible decision points where student input determines project direction; record decisions and revisit them so students see their voice mattered.
3. Use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) at project milestones to keep voice continuous rather than one-time.
4. Assign rotating leadership roles so authorship is distributed, supported by [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) for students new to facilitation.
5. Close with [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) — students present their process and story to an authentic audience, then reflect on how their voice shaped the work.

## Examples
- **Expeditionary Learning (EL Education)** — crew structures and student-led conferences institutionalize student voice in project design and exhibition ([https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)).
- **High Tech High (San Diego)** — students co-define driving questions and present project narratives at public exhibitions; student voice is a stated design pillar ([https://www.hightechhigh.org](https://www.hightechhigh.org)).
- **PBLWorks (Buck Institute) Gold Standard PBL** — "student voice and choice" is one of the seven essential project design elements, with choice of products, questions, and roles ([https://www.pblworks.org](https://www.pblworks.org)).
