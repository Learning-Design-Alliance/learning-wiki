# Include Physical Activity

## Description
This strategy embeds brief bouts of physical activity — stretching, jumping jacks, active games, or movement breaks — into the learning environment, or schedules active play immediately before demanding cognitive tasks. It is carried out through classroom-based activity breaks (typically 4–20 minutes), pre-task exercise, or active seating and movement options, rather than relying solely on scheduled recess or PE.

## Design Implications

Single bouts of moderate physical activity acutely improve attention and executive function in children, with effects strongest for tasks requiring inhibitory control and sustained focus [Classroom physical activity improves attention.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. Timing matters: activity placed immediately before or between demanding segments of instruction yields measurable on-task gains, whereas activity unrelated to lesson structure tends to dissipate [Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes.](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~M]. The strategy works as a cognitive reset — restoring attentional resources depleted by sustained seatwork — rather than as direct instruction.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A predictable routine: brief (4–20 minute), moderate-intensity activity anchored to lesson transitions
- Space and simple equipment (open floor area, exercise balls, stretch cards) that do not require lengthy setup
- Clear behavioral norms so the break re-enters instruction quickly
- Optional: activity integrated with content (e.g., acting out vocabulary, movement-based math games) to combine the break with [Practice](../elements/practice.md)

#### Constraints
- Poorly managed breaks can increase off-task behavior and cost instructional time if re-entry routines are weak [-M]
- High-intensity or over-arousing activity immediately before tasks requiring calm focus can impair performance [Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes.](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~M]
- Learners with motor impairments, self-consciousness, or certain medical conditions may be excluded unless alternatives are offered [-M]
- Effects on *learning outcomes* (not just attention) are smaller and less consistent than effects on on-task behavior [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-task activity**: 15–20 minutes of active play before homework or a challenging lesson to prime attention
- **In-class breaks**: 4–5 minute movement breaks every 20–30 minutes during long instructional blocks
- **Integrated movement**: content-embedded activity (act out a story, gesture-based recall) that doubles as encoding support
- **Active seating/allowances**: exercise balls, standing desks, or permission to move for individual learners who need an outlet without whole-class breaks

### Target Learners
- Children who struggle with sustained attention, including learners with ADHD [Classroom physical activity improves attention.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]
- Whole classrooms during long blocks of seatwork, where attentional fatigue is universal
- Less necessary for short, varied lessons with frequent [active-learning](../principles/active-learning.md) segments already built in

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention and self-regulation: restoring focus for subsequent instruction
- Behavioral management: reducing restlessness and disruptive behavior
- Indirect support of academic objectives by protecting time-on-task

### Instructions
1. Identify the points in a lesson or homework session where attention typically declines (e.g., after 20 minutes of seatwork).
2. Schedule a brief, moderate-intensity activity break (4–10 minutes) at those points; keep intensity moderate rather than exhausting [Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes.](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [~M].
3. Establish a fast re-entry routine (a signal, a breathing count, or a [check-in](../elements/check-in.md)) so the break transitions cleanly back to instruction.
4. For learners with persistent attention difficulties, offer pre-task active play (e.g., 15 minutes outdoors before homework) or active seating as an individual [accommodation](../elements/accommodations.md).
5. Where possible, integrate movement with content — acting out processes, gesture-based recall — so the break also serves [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and encoding.

## Examples
- **[GoNoodle](https://www.gonoodle.com)** — Free classroom movement-break videos (2–5 minutes) used in thousands of elementary classrooms; teachers play a segment between instructional blocks.
- **TAKE 10!®** ([https://www.take10.net](https://www.take10.net)) — A published curriculum that integrates 10-minute physical activity breaks with academic content in language arts, math, and science.
- A teacher schedules a 5-minute stretching and jumping-jacks break between two segments of a writing lesson, using a consistent signal to return to desks.
- A parent allows 15 minutes of outdoor play before homework to increase concentration on the first, most demanding task.
