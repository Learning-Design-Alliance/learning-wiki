# Process Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Process writing structures composition instruction around the recursive stages expert writers use — planning, drafting, revising, and editing — rather than evaluating a single finished product. Teachers model each stage, provide feedback on drafts, and give students sustained time to revise. The approach originated in the Hayes & Flower (1980) cognitive model of writing, which decomposed composing into planning, translating, and reviewing processes coordinated under working-memory load.

## Design Implications

Process writing works because it externalizes and distributes the cognitive demands of composing: separating idea generation from transcription and from mechanical editing reduces the working-memory burden of doing all three at once [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows process writing instruction produces substantial gains in writing quality, among the largest effects of any writing intervention studied [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis found process approach effects on writing quality.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on explicit modeling of strategies at each stage — unstructured "just write and revise" versions without instruction show much weaker effects [Process writing with explicit strategy instruction outperforms exposure-only versions.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671003610897) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit teacher modeling of planning, drafting, and revising decisions ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of a live draft)
- Multiple drafts with feedback cycles between them ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) focused on ideas and organization before mechanics)
- Sustained writing time built into instruction, not assigned as homework alone
- Mini-lessons targeting specific skills (transitions, elaboration, sentence combining) tied to student drafts
- Explicit teaching of each stage (planning, drafting, revising, editing) — not just time to write
- Multiple drafting cycles with feedback between drafts, from teacher, peers, or both
- Mini-lessons targeting specific skills (e.g., topic sentences, evidence integration) delivered when learners need them
- Authentic audiences or purposes so revision has a reason beyond the grade ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))

#### Constraints
- Weak implementations — workshop time without explicit instruction or feedback — show near-zero effects [Process writing with explicit strategy instruction outperforms exposure-only versions.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671003610897) [-M]
- Endless revision cycles without deadlines can fragment texts and exhaust novice writers; revision must be focused on one or two dimensions per draft
- Overemphasis on process can shortchange sentence-level and transcription skills; handwriting and spelling fluency constrain composition quality for young writers and need direct attention
- Less efficient for short, low-stakes writing where full cycles add overhead without benefit
- Unstructured "just write and revise" versions without explicit strategy instruction show much weaker effects [Process writing instruction improves writing quality.](../claims/process-writing-improves-writing-quality.md) [~S] — the cycle alone is not the active ingredient
- Weak peer feedback can mislead; peer review requires training and protocols to be productive
- Over-emphasis on process can stall fluent writers in endless revision; editing must be separated from composing or transcription quality suffers
- Time-intensive: multiple full cycles per piece reduce the number of compositions per term

#### Implementation Variability
- **Writers' workshop** (Atwell, Calkins): student-directed cycles with peer conferencing and mini-lessons
- **Strategy-focused variants**: [Self-Regulated Strategy Development](../strategies/self-regulated-strategy-development.md) adds explicit self-regulation instruction (goal setting, self-monitoring) to the process cycle
- **Collaborative variants**: joint composing and structured peer review [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- **Digital variants**: version-tracking tools (Google Docs revision history) make revision visible and assessable
- **Strategy-focused** (e.g., Self-Regulated Strategy Development): teaches genre-specific planning and revising strategies with self-regulation supports — strongest evidence base
- **Workshop model** (e.g., Calkins' Units of Study): mini-lesson, sustained writing time, conferring, share
- **Digital tools**: version history and commenting (Google Docs, Perusall) make revision visible and feedback asynchronous

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and older learners composing extended texts [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis found process approach effects on writing quality.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445) [+S]
- Struggling writers, who benefit most from explicit stage-by-stage modeling and focused feedback
- Young writers still building transcription fluency need shorter cycles and more direct sentence-level instruction alongside process work
- English learners, when paired with [accessible vocabulary and syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and sentence-level scaffolds
- Proficient writers need less stage scaffolding; excessive structure can suppress their own composing strategies [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: organization, elaboration, audience awareness
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own composing [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive knowledge of writing as a craft rather than a one-shot performance
- Self-regulated composing: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own text
- Genre knowledge: understanding how purpose and audience shape text structure

### Instructions
1. **Model planning**: demonstrate generating and organizing ideas for a prompt, thinking aloud about audience and purpose ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. **Guide drafting**: students draft with permission to write imperfectly; teacher confers briefly during writing ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. **Teach revision explicitly**: use mini-lessons and [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) — students explain what they changed and why — to make revision a learnable strategy, not vague "make it better."
4. **Structure peer feedback**: train students to give specific, criterion-referenced comments on drafts ([Peer Review](../elements/peer-review.md)).
5. **Edit last**: address mechanics only in a final pass, once content and organization are settled, to avoid splitting attention during composing.
6. **Publish and assess the process**: evaluate drafts, revision evidence, and reflection alongside the final product ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).

5. **Revise for meaning, then edit for mechanics**: two distinct passes with different focus
6. **Publish and reflect**: share with a real audience and have writers articulate what they changed and why
