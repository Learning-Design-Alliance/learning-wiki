# Writing Conferences

## Description
Writing conferences are brief, individualized meetings between a teacher and a student focused on the student's writing. The teacher listens to the student read or discuss their draft, asks questions that prompt reflection, and offers one or two specific, actionable suggestions tied to a clear goal. Rooted in the process-writing tradition of Graves, Atwell, and Calkins, the conference treats the student as the primary decision-maker about their text, with the teacher acting as coach rather than editor.

## Design Implications

Conferences deliver feedback at the moment it can be used — while the student is still composing — which makes them far more actionable than delayed written comments [Feedback is most effective when it answers where the learner is going, how they are doing, and where to next.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Their power comes from dialogic, individualized interaction: the student articulates intentions, the teacher probes, and revision follows immediately. Because each conference is short (3–7 minutes), the design problem is managing the rest of the class and keeping the conference focused on one teaching point rather than correcting everything.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Dedicated class time with students working independently on writing while the teacher rotates through conferences
- A predictable conference structure: research (ask what the student is working on), decide on one teaching point, teach, and link to a goal
- A record-keeping system (conference notebook, sticky notes, or digital log) to track what each student has worked on and follow up over time
- Established routines so students know what to do while waiting and can self-manage during independent writing

#### Constraints
- Time-intensive: in a class of 30, a full rotation at 5 minutes per student takes multiple sessions, leaving some students unattended for days [-M]
- Effectiveness collapses if the teacher takes over the text and dictates revisions — students learn dependence rather than self-assessment [~M]
- Requires strong classroom management; off-task behavior during independent writing time erodes the conditions for conferencing [-W]
- Whole-class mini-lessons must still cover shared needs; conferences alone cannot deliver a coherent curriculum [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **[5-minute writing conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md)** — tightly timed check-ins focused on a single teaching point
- **Peer conferences** — students use the same research–suggest structure with partners, trading teacher time for peer feedback quality
- **Roving vs. scheduled conferences** — brief on-the-spot check-ins versus pre-scheduled longer meetings
- **On-demand conferences** — students request a conference when stuck, building self-regulation
- **Small-group conferences** — 3–4 students with a shared need meet together, tripling efficiency

### Target Learners
- K–12 writers at all stages, especially developing writers who benefit from immediate, dialogic feedback [Feedback is most effective when it answers where the learner is going, how they are doing, and where to next.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Struggling writers who need individualized scaffolding that whole-class instruction cannot provide
- Less effective for students who lack the independent work habits to sustain writing time while the teacher is occupied [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision skill: learning to reread and improve one's own drafts
- Self-assessment and goal setting: internalizing the questions a good writing coach asks
- Procedural knowledge of the writing process: planning, drafting, revising, editing [Process-writing approaches improve writing quality across grade levels.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Writer identity and motivation: seeing oneself as a writer with agency over the text

### Instructions
1. Establish independent writing routines and teach students what to do during conferences before beginning them.
2. Open with research: ask the student what they are working on and have them read aloud or point to their draft ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
3. Identify one teaching point — never more — based on what the student needs next, not everything the draft lacks ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
4. Teach briefly: model, give an example, or ask a probing question that lets the student name the fix themselves ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
5. Have the student restate the goal and try it immediately on their draft ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
6. Record the teaching point and follow up at the next conference.

## Examples
- **Teachers College Reading & Writing Project (Units of Study, [https://www.unitsofstudy.com](https://www.unitsofstudy.com))** — Calkins' workshop curriculum builds conferences into every writing session, with published conference protocols and record-keeping templates.
- A high school English teacher runs roving conferences during drafting week, logging each teaching point in a shared spreadsheet and grouping students with similar needs for small-group conferences the following week.
- A middle school teacher trains students in a peer-conference protocol ("listen, ask one question, suggest one thing") so every student gets feedback on each draft even though teacher conferences happen biweekly.
