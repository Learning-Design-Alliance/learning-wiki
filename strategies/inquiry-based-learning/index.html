Inquiry Based Learning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 9 claims (7 for, 2 mixed) · 23 studies (10 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 23 report an effect size

Description¶

Inquiry based learning positions learners as investigators: they pose or explore questions, collect and evaluate evidence, and construct explanations, with the instructor acting as guide rather than transmitter. It spans a continuum from structured inquiry (the question and procedure are given; learners generate the explanation) to open inquiry (learners generate questions and design investigations themselves) Banchi & Bell's four-level framework [~M].

Design Implications¶

Inquiry can produce strong conceptual learning, but only when it is scaffolded — pure discovery without guidance consistently underperforms guided instruction Alfieri et al. (2011) meta-analysis [-S]. The critical design work is in the scaffolds: question prompts, evidence-organization tools, and instructor feedback during the investigation Hmelo-Silver et al. (2007) [+M]. Structured and guided inquiry reliably outperforms unguided discovery, and unguided inquiry can actively harm learning for novices Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) [-S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A driving question or problem that is genuinely problematic for learners but tractable with available resources
  • Scaffolds for each phase: question formulation, data collection, evidence evaluation, and explanation construction (Coaching, Argumentation)
  • Instructor monitoring with feedback targeted at the reasoning process, not just the final answer Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Activation of relevant prior knowledge before investigation begins (Activation)

Constraints¶

  • Unguided or minimally guided inquiry overloads working memory for novices, who lack schemas to organize the search Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) [-S]
  • Inquiry benefits diminish and can reverse as expertise grows; experts learn better from direct information than from rediscovery The expertise reversal effect. [~M]
  • Open inquiry without structure can leave learners practicing and encoding errors Erroneous examples can build conceptual knowledge when errors are made explicit. [~M]
  • Assessment misalignment: inquiry develops reasoning and explanation skills that traditional recall tests do not capture, undermining perceived value

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured inquiry: question and method given, learners generate explanations — appropriate entry point for novices
  • Guided inquiry: learners design methods within instructor-set questions, with prompts and tools
  • Open inquiry: learners pose questions and design full investigations — best for learners with substantial domain knowledge
  • Productive failure variants: learners attempt problems before instruction, then receive canonical teaching Productive failure improves conceptual learning. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with moderate prior knowledge benefit most; complete novices need heavier scaffolding, and advanced learners may find guided inquiry redundant The expertise reversal effect. [~M]
  • Learners developing scientific practices — hypothesis generation, evidence evaluation, argumentation — rather than only content recall
  • Learners who benefit from Self-Explanation prompts during investigation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding and mental-model revision, especially where prior misconceptions must be confronted (Cognitive Disequilibrium)
  • Epistemic practices: formulating questions, evaluating evidence, constructing and defending arguments
  • Transferable inquiry skills and dispositions (curiosity, tolerance of ambiguity) rather than efficient acquisition of large bodies of factual content

Instructions¶

  1. Activate prior knowledge and surface initial ideas or predictions (Activation, Advance Organizers)
  2. Present a driving question or phenomenon that creates a gap between what learners know and what they need to explain
  3. Support investigation with structured tools — data tables, question prompts, evidence organizers — fading support as competence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+S]
  4. Coach during investigation, using process-level feedback and Check-Ins to monitor reasoning Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]
  5. Have learners construct and defend explanations, comparing cases to support abstraction Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  6. Consolidate with instructor-led synthesis that names the canonical concepts and corrects residual misconceptions — the phase most often omitted and most needed by novices

Related Strategies¶

  • Problem-Based Learning — inquiry organized around authentic professional problems
  • Case-Based Learning — inquiry anchored in a rich case narrative Case-based learning improves exam performance. [+M]
  • Productive Failure — inquiry-before-instruction sequencing
  • Direct Instruction — the complementary high-guidance approach; effective inquiry often interleaves with it
  • Differentiate by guiding higher-ability students to build models that validate their analysis, and support self-directed and collaborative learning goals

Examples¶

  • BSCS 5E Instructional Model — Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate; a widely adopted structured-inquiry sequence for science classrooms that pairs exploration with explicit explanation.
  • IQWST (Investigating and Questioning our World through Science and Technology) — Middle-school inquiry curriculum with heavily scaffolded investigations and explanation construction.
  • Problem-Based Learning at Maastricht University — Guided inquiry in small groups with trained tutors, illustrating the scaffolding infrastructure inquiry requires at scale.
