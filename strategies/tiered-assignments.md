# Tiered Assignments

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
Tiered assignments present the same essential learning goal to all learners but vary the task's complexity, scaffolding, or abstraction across tiers. Unlike differentiated *outcomes*, tiers differ only in the pathway: every student produces work evidencing the same standard, but the support structure, text complexity, number of steps, or degree of open-endedness is adjusted to readiness. Teachers typically build 2–4 tiers and assign them based on formative assessment data rather than fixed ability labels.

## Design Implications

Tiering operationalizes instruction within each learner's zone of proximal development: tasks too hard overload working memory [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S], while tasks too easy produce little learning. Because tiers are assigned from ongoing evidence rather than static grouping, the strategy depends on and reinforces formative assessment practice [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The critical design rule is that tiers must vary in *scaffolding and complexity*, not in intellectual demand or respect — a "low" tier that is merely simplified or remedial signals lowered expectations and depresses motivation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clearly specified, shared learning goal or standard that all tiers serve
- Formative assessment data (exit tickets, pre-assessments, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md)) to place students in tiers
- Tasks at each tier that are genuinely engaging and intellectually honest, not busywork for struggling learners
- Flexible re-assignment: students move between tiers as evidence changes
- A clearly defined essential understanding or skill that all tiers share
- Readiness evidence: a short diagnostic, pre-assessment, or prior performance data to inform (not dictate) placement
- At least two, at most three or four versions — more tiers rarely justify the design cost
- Comparable cognitive value across tiers: the "low" tier must still require thinking, not just simplified recall

#### Constraints
- Placement based on fixed ability labels rather than current evidence entrenches achievement gaps and undermines growth mindset messaging [-M]
- If tiers differ in outcome quality expectations rather than support, lower-tier students receive a diluted curriculum [-S]
- Publicly visible tier assignment can stigmatize; tiering works best when tiers are discreetly assigned or framed as [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) [~M]
- High preparation cost: designing 3–4 parallel quality tasks per lesson is unsustainable for every lesson; reserve for high-leverage objectives [~W]
- Placement based on fixed ability grouping rather than task-specific readiness depresses expectations and outcomes for lower-tier learners [~M] — tiers should be fluid and re-visited per task
- If lower tiers reduce cognitive demand rather than scaffolding it, learners receive a diluted curriculum and fall further behind [-M]
- Publicly visible tier labels create status hierarchies that harm belonging and motivation; tiering works best when versions are not visibly ranked
- Design cost is high; tiering every task is unsustainable — reserve it for high-leverage, high-difficulty concepts

#### Implementation Variability
- **Complexity tiers**: same content, increasingly open-ended or multi-step tasks (e.g., single-source summary → multi-source synthesis → original argument)
- **Scaffolding tiers**: same task, varying degrees of support — full worked model, partial prompts, or independent attempt ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md))
- **Abstraction tiers**: concrete/embodied versions versus symbolic or theoretical versions of the same concept
- **Student-selected tiers**: learners choose their entry point, which builds [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) and often leads students to select appropriately challenging work [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- **Tier by complexity**: same task type, more variables, steps, or abstraction at higher tiers
- **Tier by scaffolding**: identical task, with worked examples, sentence frames, or graphic organizers added at lower tiers ([Adaptive Difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md) automates this logic)
- **Tier by process**: same product reached through different routes (concrete → representational → abstract)
- **Tier by product**: different demonstrations of the same standard, often combined with [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)
- **Whole-class open tiering**: offer all versions and let learners self-select, with teacher guidance — avoids labeling entirely

### Target Learners
- Mixed-readiness classrooms where a single task is either overwhelming or trivial for substantial portions of the class
- Struggling learners benefit from scaffolded tiers that keep them working on the same rigorous goal rather than a separate remedial track [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]
- Advanced learners benefit from complexity tiers that prevent disengagement from under-challenge [~M]
- Less appropriate for novices who need a common shared experience before differentiation is meaningful [~W]
- Mixed-readiness classrooms where a single task is either trivial or inaccessible for substantial subgroups
- Advanced learners benefit from tiers that add depth, abstraction, or open-endedness rather than merely more volume [~M]
- Less appropriate for homogeneous groups or for novices who all need the same initial explicit instruction

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill fluency at varying levels of automaticity and support
- Conceptual understanding at increasing depths of abstraction
- Transfer tasks: tiers can vary how far students must generalize a concept to novel contexts
- Conceptual understanding of a shared essential standard across readiness levels
- Procedural fluency, when tiers vary practice difficulty rather than content
- Transfer and application, when upper tiers increase novelty and open-endedness

### Instructions
1. Identify the single non-negotiable learning goal all students will reach.
2. Gather readiness evidence through a pre-assessment or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md).
3. Design the "on-level" tier first, then adjust up (more open-ended, fewer supports) and down (more scaffolding, chunked steps) while keeping the same goal.
4. Assign tiers flexibly and discreetly, or offer tier choice; revisit placement after each formative check [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].
5. Bring tiers back together for shared discussion or synthesis so all students encounter the full range of thinking.

## Examples
- **Parallel math tasks (Marilyn Burns / NCTM tradition)**: all students investigate the same relationship (e.g., patterns in perimeter), with tiers offering manipulatives and sentence frames, open investigation, or a generalization-and-proof challenge.
- **Literature circles with tiered texts**: same theme and essential question, texts at different reading levels, with common discussion prompts so the whole class synthesizes together.
- **[Newsela](https://newsela.com)** — the same news article published at multiple Lexile levels, enabling text-level tiering with shared content and shared questions.
- **[Zearn](https://www.zearn.org)** — digital math lessons with built-in scaffolding tiers (guided digital manipulatives → independent problem sets) aligned to a common lesson goal.
- **Tiered math problem sets**: all students solve problems about proportional reasoning; one tier uses whole numbers and visual models, another uses fractions, a third adds multi-step open contexts. Widely used in [Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI for Math)](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) classrooms, where problem number choices are tiered by number size and structure.
- **Tiered literature circles**: same novel, tiered role sheets — lower tiers provide sentence frames and page references, upper tiers require synthesizing across chapters.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-based pathways effectively tier practice items by difficulty, unlocking harder item tiers as learners demonstrate readiness.
- **[Zearn](https://www.zearn.org)** — digital lessons paired with tiered "Bonuses" and scaffolded "Boosts" around the same grade-level objective.

## Description
Tiered assignments are a differentiation strategy in which the teacher designs two or more versions of a task that target the *same* essential concept or skill but vary in complexity, process, support, or product. All tiers are equally respectful and equally valued; the difference is the degree of challenge and scaffolding, not the status of the work. Tiers are typically assigned based on readiness evidence — prior assessment, diagnostic tasks, or observation — rather than fixed labels.

## Design Implications

Tiering keeps every learner inside their zone of proximal development: the task is hard enough to require effort but achievable with available support, which sustains engagement and avoids the twin failure modes of boredom and overwhelm [Vygotsky, L. S. (1978). *Mind in Society*. Harvard University Press.] [+M]. Because all tiers converge on the same learning goal, tiering preserves curricular coherence while managing cognitive load — each learner's version removes only the barriers that would otherwise exceed working memory capacity [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. 2. Gather readiness evidence with a short diagnostic or pre-assessment ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
3. Design the "middle" tier first — the task you would assign everyone — then adjust up (complexity, abstraction) and down (scaffolding, scaffolds such as worked examples or organizers) while keeping cognitive demand intact.
4. Check each tier for equal respect: every version should require reasoning, not just differing amounts of effort or recall.
5. Assign flexibly and, where possible, let learners choose their entry point; regroup between tasks.
6. Debrief on a shared task or discussion so all learners converge on the same understanding, and use results to re-tier next time.
