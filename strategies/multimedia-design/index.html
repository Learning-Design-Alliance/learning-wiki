Multimedia Design¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (5 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q3–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Multimedia design is the deliberate arrangement of words (spoken or printed) and pictures (static or dynamic) in instructional materials. It is carried out by applying empirically derived principles — segmenting, signaling, coherence, modality, redundancy, and personalization — to videos, slides, animations, e-learning modules, and illustrated texts, so that the presentation aligns with how working memory processes visual and auditory channels.

Design Implications¶

Multimedia design is grounded in Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning: learners process visual and auditory information through separate channels of limited working-memory capacity, so design choices determine whether capacity is spent on essential processing or wasted on extraneous load Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes. [+S]. The strongest-supported moves are removing decorative but irrelevant material Irrelevant seductive details hurt learning. [+S], breaking continuous media into learner-paced segments, and highlighting essential content with cues. Combining words with relevant images reliably outperforms words alone Pairing words with relevant images improves learning over words alone. [+S], consistent with Dual Coding Theory.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Analysis of which content is essential versus incidental before production
  • Graphics that are structurally relevant (explain how something works), not decorative
  • Narration and on-screen text managed deliberately — narration for graphics, text for standalone words
  • Segmentation controls (pause, next) or pre-segmented chunks for complex material

Constraints¶

  • Redundant on-screen text read aloud verbatim from narration hurts learning Redundant on-screen text with narration hurts learning. [~S] — the effect reverses when learners are non-native speakers or the text is short
  • Over-signaling (cuing everything) eliminates the benefit of signaling [~M]
  • Learner-paced segmentation helps novices but can be unnecessary overhead for experts [~M]
  • Decorative graphics and background music consume capacity without aiding learning Irrelevant seductive details hurt learning. [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Video: segment into short clips with embedded questions or Practice prompts at segment boundaries
  • Slides: one idea per slide; graphics + spoken explanation rather than text-dense slides
  • Animation: prefer static sequences with Advance Organizers when the content is not inherently dynamic [~M]
  • Interactive e-learning: combine segmentation with learner control over pacing and replay

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and low-prior-knowledge learners, who lack schemas to compensate for extraneous load Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes. [+S]
  • Non-native speakers, for whom redundant printed text can support rather than hinder [~M]
  • Less beneficial for experts, who suffer the expertise-reversal effect when guidance is excessive

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding of systems, processes, and causal models (how something works)
  • Retention of structured verbal and visual information
  • Transfer to novel problems when graphics depict underlying structure rather than surface features

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the essential content and select or create graphics that explain it structurally, not decoratively.
  2. Apply Chunking — segment continuous media into meaningful, learner-paced parts.
  3. Add signaling (arrows, color, highlighting) to direct attention to essential elements.
  4. Narrate graphics with spoken words rather than duplicating them as on-screen text; manage Attention by keeping each screen focused on one idea.
  5. Pilot with representative learners and check for overload using Check-ins or embedded questions.

Related Strategies¶

  • Segmenting — the pacing strategy that operationalizes multimedia design for video
  • Signaling — cueing essential information within multimedia materials
  • Worked Examples — multimedia design principles apply directly to narrated solution videos
  • Consider a focused more is more approach combining minimized extraneous processing with prompts for generative processing

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy — narrated, segmented problem-solving videos with minimal on-screen text, consistent with modality and coherence principles.
  • PhET Interactive Simulations — University of Colorado simulations pairing dynamic graphics with brief, learner-controlled text prompts.
  • Duolingo — pairs images with audio and short text, segmenting lessons into small units with immediate practice.
