# Culturally Responsive Practice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Culturally responsive practice designs instruction around learners' cultural frames of reference — their languages, community knowledge, values, and identities — rather than treating dominant-culture norms as neutral defaults. It draws on students' funds of knowledge as instructional assets, examines curriculum and discipline practices for implicit bias, and deliberately fosters a sense of belonging so that students from historically excluded groups can engage fully with rigorous content.

## Design Implications

Culturally responsive practice is not a separate activity but a design lens applied to existing instruction: content selection, examples, discourse norms, assessment, and relationships. Its central mechanism is belonging — students learn more when they believe their identity is compatible with success in the setting [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+S]. Connecting new content to students' prior knowledge and lived experience also strengthens activation and comprehension [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M], and giving students meaningful ownership of their learning supports intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine knowledge of students' communities, languages, and interests — gathered through [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), family engagement, and community relationships, not assumptions
- Willingness to examine one's own implicit bias and the dominant narratives embedded in curriculum and discipline practices
- Curriculum materials that include diverse perspectives as intellectual content, not only as decoration
- High expectations paired with support; rigor is not lowered, scaffolding is adjusted

#### Constraints
- Surface-level implementation ("heroes and holidays" — adding cultural holidays or famous figures without changing pedagogy) does not produce the engagement or achievement benefits of deep implementation [-M]
- When teacher and student cultural backgrounds differ sharply and the teacher has not built community knowledge, well-intended attempts can stereotype or misread students [-M]
- Deficit framing — treating cultural difference as something to remediate — undermines the approach entirely [-S]
- Belonging interventions are most effective when students are at risk of identity threat; applying them indiscriminately can feel inauthentic or unnecessary [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Funds of knowledge approaches** (Moll et al.): teachers conduct household visits and design lessons that use community knowledge as academic content
- **Asset-based discourse and discipline**: empathic discipline practices that frame misbehavior as fixable and situational reduce exclusionary discipline [Brief empathic discipline interventions cut suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]
- **Curriculum redesign**: selecting texts, cases, and examples that reflect students' identities and global perspectives
- **Social-emotional integration**: building empathy and intergroup understanding as explicit learning goals [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Students from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, particularly where curriculum and teaching norms reflect a dominant culture different from their own [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+S]
- Students experiencing identity threat or stereotype threat in academic settings
- All students benefit from diverse perspectives and asset-based framing, but the largest gains accrue to historically marginalized groups

### Target Learning Goals
- Belonging and identity safety: students see themselves as legitimate members of the learning community
- Engagement and persistence: content connects to students' lives and purposes [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Critical literacy: students can analyze dominant narratives and multiple cultural perspectives
- Self-regulated learning: students develop agency and independence as learners [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Learn your students.** Use [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), surveys, home/community visits, and one-on-one conversations to build real knowledge of students' funds of knowledge before designing instruction.
2. **Audit your materials and norms.** Review curriculum, examples, [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md), and classroom discourse rules for whose perspectives are centered and whose are missing.
3. **Activate and honor prior knowledge.** Design lessons that begin with students' community and lived experience as legitimate academic resources, using [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) and [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) grounded in familiar contexts.
4. **Offer meaningful choice and voice.** Use [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) norms that include multiple participation styles, and [Collaborative Learning](../elements/collaborative-learning.md) structures that distribute expertise across students.
5. **Give feedback that signals high expectations and belonging.** Feedback should be action-oriented and communicate that the student can meet rigorous standards [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
6. **Reflect and revise.** Examine your own patterns — whose hands go up, whose work is praised, who is disciplined — and adjust practices accordingly.

## Examples
- **Funds of Knowledge project (Tucson, AZ)** — Moll and colleagues' teacher study groups documented household knowledge (farming, mechanics, finance) and built lessons on it, demonstrating that working-class Latino communities hold rich academic resources.
- **Zaretta Hammond's "Culturally Responsive Teaching and the Brain" framework** — organizes practice around the information-processing benefits of culturally familiar scaffolds, emphasizing productive struggle over engagement tricks.
- **International Baccalaureate (https://www.ibo.org)** — requires global contexts and intercultural understanding across its curriculum frameworks, embedding multiple perspectives into content selection.
- **Facing History and Ourselves (https://www.facinghistory.org)** — published curriculum that uses students' identities and moral reasoning as entry points into rigorous historical analysis.
