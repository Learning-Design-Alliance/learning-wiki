# "Headings and Highlight" Strategy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The "Headings and Highlight" Strategy is a collaborative reading technique for digital texts (typically Google Docs) designed to slow reading down and generate dialogue about content. Students first highlight unfamiliar vocabulary, then read paragraphs in pairs, highlighting key ideas and composing a four-word heading for each section. Pairs compare headings with other pairs, justify their choices using text evidence, and consolidate headings as a group — often ending with a class vote or presentation and an individual reflection connecting the headings to the unit's driving question.

## Design Implications

The strategy converts passive reading into generative processing: selecting key ideas, condensing them into a constrained format, and defending the condensation verbally. The four-word constraint forces summarization rather than copying, and the comparison-and-justification phase adds [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and argumentation on top of the marking itself [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. This matters because marking alone is one of the weakest study techniques — rereading and highlighting produce minimal learning without follow-up elaboration [~S] (Dunlosky et al., 2013). The dialogue requirement moves the activity up the ICAP hierarchy from passive to constructive and interactive modes, which predict progressively better learning outcomes [+M] (Chi & Wylie, 2014).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A collaborative document platform (Google Docs or equivalent) with highlighting and heading capability
- Excerpts of appropriately challenging, content-heavy text
- Teacher facilitation to structure pair and group discussion and to keep justifications anchored in text evidence
- Time — the routine is deliberately slower than ordinary reading

#### Constraints
- Depends on access to a shared digital platform; the technique loses its clean-revision affordance on paper
- Time-intensive relative to the content covered; poorly suited to texts that must be read quickly or in volume
- Without skilled facilitation, pair discussion can collapse into agreement-seeking rather than genuine comparison of interpretations
- Highlighting as a standalone behavior is weak — if the heading and justification phases are skipped, the routine degrades to the low-utility marking students would do anyway [-S] (Dunlosky et al., 2013)
- Students with weak vocabulary or decoding skills may spend the unfamiliar-vocabulary phase on too many words, stalling the routine

#### Implementation Variability
- Text complexity and excerpt length can be adjusted; shorter excerpts suit younger or less experienced readers
- Works individually (self-generated headings checked against a partner), in pairs, or as a whole-class consolidation
- The four-word constraint can be tightened (three words) or loosened (a sentence) depending on the summarization skill of the group
- In social studies, headings can be framed around cause, perspective, or change-over-time to align with disciplinary reading habits

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students in content-heavy subjects, especially social studies, where main-idea extraction from dense text is the bottleneck skill
- Developing readers who benefit from slowed, socially supported reading and from seeing peers' interpretations contrasted with their own
- Less beneficial for advanced readers reading easy text, for whom the routine adds little beyond what they do automatically [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and supporting evidence in expository text
- Summarization and abstraction: condensing sections into constrained verbal formats
- Argumentation: justifying interpretations with text evidence during peer comparison
- Metacognitive awareness: reflecting on how headings support comprehension and answer the unit's driving question

### Instructions
1. Distribute the text excerpt in a shared document; students individually highlight unfamiliar vocabulary and clarify meanings.
2. Pairs read paragraph by paragraph, highlighting key ideas as they go ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. Each pair composes a four-word heading for the section ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — the constraint forces condensation).
4. Pairs compare headings with another pair and justify their choices using text evidence ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) at pair scale).
5. Groups consolidate headings across sections into an outline of the whole text ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) built by students rather than given to them).
6. Whole class votes on or discusses favorite headings; teacher highlights where interpretations diverged and why.
7. Students individually reflect on how the heading process aided comprehension and how it connects to the unit's driving question.

## Examples
- A middle school social studies class reads a primary-source excerpt on the American Revolution in Google Docs; pairs produce four-word headings ("Colonists Reject Taxation"), defend them against a rival pair's "No Representation, Resistance," and the class consolidates a student-built outline of the document.
- An ELA class applies the routine to a dense nonfiction article, then uses the consolidated headings as the skeleton for an argumentative essay.
