# "Headings and Highlight" Strategy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 10 studies (4 review, 2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 10 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
The "Headings and Highlight" Strategy is a collaborative reading technique for digital texts (typically Google Docs) designed to slow reading down and generate dialogue about content. Students first highlight unfamiliar vocabulary, then read paragraphs in pairs, highlighting key ideas and composing a four-word heading for each section. Pairs compare headings with other pairs, justify their choices using text evidence, and consolidate headings as a group — often ending with a class vote or presentation and an individual reflection connecting the headings to the unit's driving question.

## Design Implications

The strategy converts passive reading into generative processing: selecting key ideas, condensing them into a constrained format, and defending the condensation verbally. The four-word constraint forces summarization rather than copying, and the comparison-and-justification phase adds [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and argumentation on top of the marking itself [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. This matters because marking alone is one of the weakest study techniques — rereading and highlighting produce minimal learning without follow-up elaboration [~S] (Dunlosky et al., 2013). The dialogue requirement moves the activity up the ICAP hierarchy from passive to constructive and interactive modes, which predict progressively better learning outcomes [+M] (Chi & Wylie, 2014).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A collaborative document platform (Google Docs or equivalent) with highlighting and heading capability
- Excerpts of appropriately challenging, content-heavy text
- Teacher facilitation to structure pair and group discussion and to keep justifications anchored in text evidence
- Time — the routine is deliberately slower than ordinary reading
- A shared digital text (Google Docs or similar) that supports visible, concurrent highlighting
- A text at an appropriate challenge level — difficult enough that main ideas are not self-evident
- Time for paired reading, heading negotiation, cross-group consensus, and individual reflection
- A driving question or unit purpose to anchor the final reflection

#### Constraints
- Depends on access to a shared digital platform; the technique loses its clean-revision affordance on paper
- Time-intensive relative to the content covered; poorly suited to texts that must be read quickly or in volume
- Without skilled facilitation, pair discussion can collapse into agreement-seeking rather than genuine comparison of interpretations
- Highlighting as a standalone behavior is weak — if the heading and justification phases are skipped, the routine degrades to the low-utility marking students would do anyway [-S] (Dunlosky et al., 2013)
- Students with weak vocabulary or decoding skills may spend the unfamiliar-vocabulary phase on too many words, stalling the routine
- Highlighting without the heading task adds little: students who highlight indiscriminately or verbatim show minimal comprehension gains [Highlighting and underlining as typically practiced show low utility for learning outcomes.](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-S]
- The full routine consumes substantial instructional time; truncating the consensus and justification phases removes the elaboration that drives the effect [Generative activities that require learners to select, organize, and integrate material outperform passive reception.](../claims/generative-processing-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- Groups dominated by one strong reader can short-circuit negotiation; assigned roles or individual pre-writing of headings before discussion mitigates this
- Very dense or unfamiliar-vocabulary texts can overload working memory during the heading task; pre-teaching key vocabulary or [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the text into shorter sections helps [Chunking reduces working memory load during learning.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Text complexity and excerpt length can be adjusted; shorter excerpts suit younger or less experienced readers
- Works individually (self-generated headings checked against a partner), in pairs, or as a whole-class consolidation
- The four-word constraint can be tightened (three words) or loosened (a sentence) depending on the summarization skill of the group
- In social studies, headings can be framed around cause, perspective, or change-over-time to align with disciplinary reading habits
- Analog version: printed text, highlighters, headings written on sticky notes and posted for a gallery consensus walk
- Scaffolded version: provide sentence stems for justification ("Mine is better because it captures…") for students new to academic argument
- Asynchronous version: students draft headings in shared docs; the teacher curates a heading comparison for the next synchronous discussion
- Extension: students compare their self-generated headings to the author's actual headings, analyzing what the author emphasized differently

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students in content-heavy subjects, especially social studies, where main-idea extraction from dense text is the bottleneck skill
- Developing readers who benefit from slowed, socially supported reading and from seeing peers' interpretations contrasted with their own
- Less beneficial for advanced readers reading easy text, for whom the routine adds little beyond what they do automatically [~W]
- Middle school, high school, and adult learners building reading comprehension and analysis skills with challenging disciplinary texts
- Emergent multilingual learners benefit from the explicit vocabulary-identification phase, provided headings may be drafted in multiple languages before negotiation
- Less suitable for beginning readers still decoding; the strategy assumes fluent word recognition so attention can go to meaning [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and supporting evidence in expository text
- Summarization and abstraction: condensing sections into constrained verbal formats
- Argumentation: justifying interpretations with text evidence during peer comparison
- Metacognitive awareness: reflecting on how headings support comprehension and answer the unit's driving question
- Identifying and prioritizing main ideas in expository text
- Summarization and abstraction: compressing a passage into a constrained representation
- Argumentation and justification: defending an interpretation against alternatives
- Collaborative meaning-making and metacognitive awareness of one's own reading process

### Instructions
1. Distribute the text excerpt in a shared document; students individually highlight unfamiliar vocabulary and clarify meanings.
2. Pairs read paragraph by paragraph, highlighting key ideas as they go ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. Each pair composes a four-word heading for the section ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — the constraint forces condensation).
4. Pairs compare headings with another pair and justify their choices using text evidence ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) at pair scale).
5. Groups consolidate headings across sections into an outline of the whole text ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) built by students rather than given to them).
6. Whole class votes on or discusses favorite headings; teacher highlights where interpretations diverged and why.
7. Students individually reflect on how the heading process aided comprehension and how it connects to the unit's driving question.

## Description
Students read an excerpt of a difficult article in Google Docs. First, they highlight words they don't know. Then, in pairs, they read a paragraph together and highlight key ideas, discussing why those parts are important. Next, each student individually devises a four-word heading for that portion of the text based on the highlighted main ideas, and pairs compare headings — if they differ, each must justify why theirs is better. The teacher then regroups pairs so four students must reach consensus on the single best heading, optionally culminating in group presentations or a class vote. Students finish with an individual reflection on how the heading helped them understand the reading and answer the unit's driving question.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it converts passive reading into generative processing: selecting ideas (highlighting), organizing them (heading construction), and elaborating through justification and consensus [Generative activities that require learners to select, organize, and integrate material outperform passive reception.](../claims/generative-processing-improves-learning.md) [+S]. The four-word constraint is the critical design move — it forces abstraction beyond what highlighting alone achieves, since unstructured highlighting is among the weakest studied learning techniques [Highlighting and underlining as typically practiced show low utility for learning outcomes.](../claims/highlighting-low-utility.md) [-S]. Collaborative negotiation of headings adds peer explanation and error detection, which deepens comprehension beyond solitary summarizing [Collaborative writing and joint text construction improve text quality and comprehension.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M].
