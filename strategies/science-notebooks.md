# Science Notebooks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Science notebooks are structured written records in which students document observations, record data, make drawings and diagrams, note predictions, and construct explanations over the course of scientific investigations. The notebook functions simultaneously as a working tool (a place to think), a formative assessment source for the teacher, and a genre practice ground for the communication norms of science. Implementation typically pairs notebook entries with investigation cycles: students write before (predictions), during (data, sketches), and after (claims and explanations) hands-on work.

## Design Implications

Science notebooks operationalize the "writing to learn" tradition: transforming observations into written claims forces learners to organize, elaborate, and commit to ideas, which exposes conceptions for revision rather than leaving them tacit [Writing about content improves understanding by forcing elaboration and organization.](../claims/writing-to-learn-improves-understanding.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on the prompts and teacher feedback loops, not the notebook as an artifact — entries used only as record-keeping produce little conceptual gain, while entries used as the basis for discussion and revision do [Assessment that feeds back into instruction improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent entry structure (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning frames, focus questions, dated entries) so students know what kind of thinking each entry demands
- Investigations or phenomena worth writing about — the notebook documents real work, not transcription of lecture
- Teacher routines for reading entries and using them to adjust instruction ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
- Opportunities to revisit and revise earlier entries, so the notebook reflects conceptual change over time

#### Constraints
- Writing mechanics demands can overwhelm younger or struggling writers, shifting cognitive effort away from science content [Cognitive overload from competing task demands degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M] — mitigate with sentence frames, drawings, or shared writing
- Notebooks used as graded compliance artifacts (neatness, completeness) shift attention from reasoning to performance
- Effectiveness drops when prompts ask only for description ("what happened") rather than explanation ("why") — descriptive entries alone show weak conceptual gains [-M]
- Requires sustained teacher time to read and respond; unreviewed notebooks lose their formative function

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured templates** (focus question → prediction → data → claim-evidence-reasoning) versus **open science journals**; structure helps novices, openness supports ownership and autonomy [Supporting autonomy fosters intrinsic motivation and engagement.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- **Multimodal entries** — drawings, annotated diagrams, and data tables alongside prose, which reduces writing load and leverages dual coding
- **Digital notebooks** (e.g., Google Sites, Seesaw, electronic lab notebooks in undergraduate courses) enabling embedded photos, data, and peer commenting
- **Interactive science notebooks** (AVID-style) combining left-page student processing with right-page teacher input

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grades students, where notebook drawing-and-writing formats are well established [+M]
- English language learners, when multimodal entries (diagrams, labeled sketches) reduce language demands while still requiring disciplinary reasoning [+M]
- Undergraduate laboratory students, where notebook-style lab reports build authentic scientific documentation practices
- Less effective as a standalone intervention for students who lack the investigation experiences the notebook is meant to document

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: articulating predictions and explanations that can be tested and revised
- Scientific practices: data recording, evidence-based claims, and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) in written form
- Metacognition: monitoring one's own evolving understanding across an investigation sequence
- Disciplinary literacy: learning the genres and norms of scientific communication [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension of domain texts and tasks.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Open each investigation with a focus question and a prediction entry ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)) so students commit to ideas before data collection.
2. During investigation, students record observations, measurements, and labeled drawings ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. After data collection, students write claims supported by evidence, using a claim–evidence–reasoning frame ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)).
4. Use entries as formative evidence: select excerpts to drive whole-class discussion and identify ideas needing revision ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
5. Have students revisit and revise earlier entries after discussion, making conceptual change visible in the notebook itself.

## Examples
- **[FOSS (Full Option Science System)](https://www.fossweb.com)** — embeds student science notebooks as a core component of every module, with grade-banded entry formats.
- **[NSTA Science Notebooks resources](https://www.nsta.org/science-notebooks)** — published implementation guides and prompt libraries from the science-education community.
- **Seesaw digital portfolios** — used in elementary classrooms for multimodal notebook entries (photos of investigations with recorded explanations) that teachers and peers can comment on.
