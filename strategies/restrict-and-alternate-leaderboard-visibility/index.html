Sequence and restrict absolute leaderboard visibility to protect lower-ranked students' motivation¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's practical implications propose managing how much ranking information an absolute leaderboard exposes: publicly releasing only the top five positions, resetting points fortnightly to level the playing field, and alternating leaderboard types across a semester. The authors state an instructor "may consider publicly releasing only the information of the top five students, instead of everyone, in an absolute leaderboard" so low-ranked students experience less discouragement.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A points-based leaderboard plugin (the study used Moodle with the 'Level Up' plugin) with pre-specified, explicitly announced points-adding rules to support perceived fairness

Constraints¶

  • Recommendations derive from two small quasi-experiments (N = 24 and N = 26) with predominantly female, East Asian postgraduate samples; the article cautions that culture and gender composition limit generalisation

Target Learners¶

  • postgraduate students in fully online courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • sustained engagement and motivation in online course activities

Claims¶

  • On an absolute leaderboard, learning performance does not differ significantly across top, middle, and bottom ranked students [+W]
  • On an absolute leaderboard, course engagement does not differ significantly across top, middle, and bottom ranked students [+W]
  • On an absolute leaderboard, top-ranked online students report significantly higher post-intervention intrinsic motivation than bottom-ranked students [-W]
  • Bottom-ranked students prefer anonymous comparison on an absolute leaderboard but public comparison on a relative leaderboard [+W]
