# Connecting

## Description
Connecting involves relating a current problem or text to prior knowledge and experiences. It asks learners if the current problem reminds them of any other problem they have solved and how they solved it — for example, "Does this remind you of another math problem? What did you do then?" The strategy operationalizes schema theory: new information is comprehensible and memorable only when it can be assimilated into existing knowledge structures [Ausubel's advance-organizer research shows meaningful verbal learning depends on anchoring new material to relevant prior concepts.](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046669) [+S].

## Design Implications

Connecting activates prior knowledge before or during new learning, which improves comprehension and retention because learners encode new material relationally rather than as isolated facts [Contextual prerequisites: the same passage is comprehensible and memorable only when learners possess the relevant schema.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9) [+S]. The connection must be genuine, not superficial — prompts that surface relevant knowledge improve learning, while generic "think about your experience" prompts often activate irrelevant material.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient prior knowledge and experiences to draw connections from
- Prompts that specify *which* prior knowledge is relevant ("How is this like the mixture problems we solved?") rather than open-ended recall
- Time for learners to articulate the connection — [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) or brief written/verbal linking statements

#### Constraints
- Learners lacking relevant prior knowledge cannot connect; the prompt then wastes time or activates misconceptions [Prior knowledge is the strongest single predictor of new learning, so its absence is a genuine bottleneck rather than a minor difficulty.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752(02)00023-9) [-S]
- Inaccurate or partial prior knowledge can be activated and strengthened, entrenching misconceptions — connections to flawed analogies transfer the flaws
- Superficial analogies ("this is like sports because it has rules") consume time without supporting the target schema [~M]
- Prompts that overload working memory by requiring learners to hold both the new problem and a retrieved one can add extraneous load for novices [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher-prompted ("Does this remind you of a problem we've seen?") vs. self-initiated (learners habitually ask themselves the question)
- Personal connections (own life experiences) vs. disciplinary connections (prior problems, texts, or cases in the domain)
- Embedded in worked examples, [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md), or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) rather than delivered as standalone prompts

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who have something to retrieve and benefit from cueing [Prior knowledge is the strongest predictor of new learning.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752(02)00023-9) [+S]
- Learners who benefit from relating content to their own lives — personalization increases task value and engagement
- Novices with thin domain knowledge need teacher-supplied connections (analogies, advance organizers) rather than self-generated ones [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension: interpreting new material in terms of existing schemas
- Transfer: recognizing when a familiar strategy applies to a new problem
- Retention: relational encoding produces more durable memory than rote encoding [Spaced and meaningful retrieval strengthens retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Before new material, surface relevant prior knowledge with a targeted prompt or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) ("Remember the sharing problems we solved last week?")
2. Present the new problem or text, then ask the connecting question: "Does this remind you of another problem? How did you solve it?"
3. Have learners articulate the link aloud or in writing — pair with [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) to make the mapping explicit [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
4. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on the new problem type, and [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on whether the connection learners drew was apt
5. Fade the prompts over time so learners internalize the habit of asking the connecting question themselves

## Patterns That Use This Element
- [Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI for Math)](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) — children's informal problem-solving experience is the explicit foundation for formal instruction

## Examples
- A teacher introducing ratio problems asks, "Does this remind you of the sharing problems we solved last week? What did you do first there?" — learners retrieve the partitive-division schema before tackling the new format.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction](https://www.cgiinstitute.org)** — teachers elicit how students would solve a word problem using their own informal strategies, then connect those strategies to formal notation.
- In a reading class, students relate a character's dilemma to a personal experience before analyzing the text, improving comprehension and recall of narrative structure.
