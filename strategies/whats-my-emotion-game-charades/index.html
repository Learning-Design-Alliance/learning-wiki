What’s My Emotion? Game¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 5 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 0 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

One learner demonstrates an emotion through body language and facial expressions — no words allowed — while the rest of the class guesses the emotion. After each guess, the actor and teacher confirm or correct, name the emotion precisely, and point to the specific cues (posture, eyebrows, movement quality) that signaled it. The game converts emotion recognition from a vocabulary exercise into a perceptual-motor activity with immediate feedback.

Design Implications¶

The game pairs two mechanisms known to support learning: enacting content improves memory for it relative to passive study (the enactment effect) [+M], and guessing followed by immediate corrective feedback strengthens discrimination between similar categories [+M]. Because emotions are graded and overlapping, the feedback conversation — why did that look like frustration rather than anger? — is where most of the learning happens; guessing alone is not sufficient [~M]. Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Space for a learner to perform in front of the group
  • A reference vocabulary for emotions (e.g., an EQ Feeling Chart or the Mood Meter from the RULER approach) so guesses become precise words, not just "happy" or "mad"
  • A classroom climate safe enough for public performance; establish norms before starting
  • A teacher prepared to give specific, cue-level feedback rather than simple right/wrong verdicts
  • An EQ Feeling Chart (e.g., RULER Mood Meter or a simpler happy/sad/angry/scared grid) visible to all students
  • Time for individual reflection before the demonstration phase
  • A volunteer willing to demonstrate; a Check-In routine so reflection feels habitual rather than exposed
  • Norms for respectful guessing — descriptions of what was observed ("your shoulders went up"), not judgments of the person

Constraints¶

  • Learners who are self-conscious, have social anxiety, or are on the autism spectrum may find public performance aversive; forcing participation can produce disengagement rather than learning [-M] — offer observer roles with structured cue-spotting tasks instead
  • Low-intensity or socially complex emotions (disappointment, embarrassment, envy) are poorly conveyed by novices and frequently mis-acted, muddying the discrimination being taught [~M]
  • Emotion recognition is not fully universal: in-group members read expressions of their own culture more accurately than out-group expressions [-M] (Elfenbein & Ambady, 2002), so a single "correct" reading can be culturally biased
  • Without a naming step and feedback, the activity degrades into entertainment with little vocabulary gain [~M]
  • Students who are uncomfortable being observed may experience the public demonstration as exposure rather than play; forcing participation can undermine the sense of safety that makes disclosure possible Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [-M] — participation should be voluntary with an opt-out (e.g., pointing to a chart face instead of acting)
  • Students with limited emotional vocabulary or alexithymia-like difficulties may not accurately identify or demonstrate their feelings; the game can frustrate rather than develop them without scaffolding (chart labels, sentence stems)
  • Cultural display rules vary: the same emotion may be expressed differently across cultures, so "wrong" guesses may reflect decoder assumptions rather than encoder error — worth naming explicitly
  • As a repeated routine, novelty wears off; effectiveness depends on varying the emotion set and deepening the debrief

Implementation Variability¶

  • Small groups instead of whole-class performance for learners uncomfortable with an audience
  • Charades variant: act out an emotion situation ("you just dropped your ice cream") and have peers name both the situation and the feeling
  • Card-driven: draw emotion cards from a chart to keep the emotion set varied and graded in difficulty
  • Reverse round: teacher acts, class writes guesses on whiteboards — raises participation and gives the teacher formative evidence (Assessment)
  • Media variant: analyze photos or film clips of faces and bodies instead of live acting
  • Silent version: all students point to a face on the chart simultaneously; no individual demonstration
  • Small-group version: demonstrations happen in groups of 4–6, lowering the social stakes
  • Reverse round: the teacher demonstrates and students guess, modeling the skill and taking pressure off students
  • Literature link: students act out how a character feels at a story moment, connecting emotion identification to Act It Out comprehension work
  • Older students: add a debrief on display rules — when is it appropriate to show what you feel?

  • Young learners and early-elementary students building foundational emotional vocabulary [+M]
  • Learners who benefit from embodied, movement-based encoding over verbal-only instruction [+M]
  • Mixed groups including multilingual learners — the nonverbal channel lowers the language barrier for participation
  • Less suitable, without adaptation, for learners with acute performance anxiety or strong discomfort with social exposure [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotion identification and precise emotional vocabulary
  • Reading nonverbal cues: facial expression, posture, gesture, proximity
  • Perspective-taking and empathy — enacting an emotion requires simulating how it feels Building empathy through perspective-taking activities improves prosocial outcomes. [+M]

  • Emotional self-awareness: identifying and labeling one's current state
  • Non-verbal communication: expressing and decoding facial expressions and body language
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' internal states from observable cues
  • Classroom climate: normalizing talk about feelings as a routine part of learning

Instructions¶

  1. Set norms and vocabulary. Review the emotion chart and agree on rules (no touching, no mocking guesses). This functions as a brief Check-In and primes the emotion lexicon.
  2. Model a round. The teacher acts one emotion and narrates the cues: "My shoulders slumped, my eyes looked down — that's disappointment." Modeling makes the cue-reading explicit before learners take over.
  3. Act. A volunteer draws an emotion card and performs it silently — Act It Out — holding the expression long enough for everyone to observe.
  4. Guess and justify. Peers name the emotion and, critically, state the cue that supports their guess; require evidence, not just a label.
  5. Confirm and refine. The actor reveals the answer; the teacher resolves near-misses (frustrated vs. angry) and adds precise vocabulary. Whole-group discussion of ambiguous cases deepens the discrimination (Class Discussion).
  6. Rotate and debrief. Rotate actors across several rounds, then close with a short reflection: which emotions were hardest to read, and why?

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the teacher's modeled round makes expert cue-reading visible before learners perform

  • Social Learning Theory — peers acquire emotion recognition by observing demonstrations and their interpretations

Examples¶

  • RULER / Mood Meter (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — the game can draw its emotion vocabulary from the Mood Meter's four-quadrant grid of pleasantness and energy.
  • Second Step (Committee for Children) — published SEL curriculum using face-and-body emotion-identification activities with early-elementary learners; this game is a live-action version of its photo-card exercises.
  • Morning meeting check-in — a Responsive Classroom–style opening where one student acts their current emotion and classmates guess, combining community building with emotion vocabulary practice.
  • RULER Mood Meter (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — a feeling chart on which this activity can be based; RULER classrooms use daily mood check-ins followed by discussion of emotional cues.
  • Second Step (Committee for Children) — K–8 SEL curriculum that includes emotion-card guessing games in which students identify feelings from facial and body cues.
  • Morning Meeting (Responsive Classroom) — the greeting and sharing phases often incorporate emotion identification activities structurally similar to this game.
