Visual Cues¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 5 studies (3 causal, 2 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 5 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Visual cues are graphics, diagrams, annotations, or gestures presented alongside verbal information to direct attention, reinforce meaning, and reduce reliance on auditory processing alone. They work by giving learners a second, permanent representation of content that can be revisited, unlike transient speech.

Design Implications¶

Combining verbal and visual representations improves learning when both channels carry complementary information, consistent with dual coding and multimedia learning research Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. Cues are most effective when they are relevant and integrated with the material they support — arbitrary or decorative graphics add load rather than reduce it Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Visuals that map directly onto the spoken or written content (diagrams of the process being described, gestures matching the concept)
  • Integration of visual and verbal elements — placing labels near the relevant graphic rather than separating them
  • Enough time for learners to process the visual before or while it is narrated; avoid reading on-screen text verbatim aloud

Constraints¶

  • Redundant narration of identical on-screen text can depress learning compared with narration alone [~M]
  • Decorative or irrelevant images consume working memory without aiding comprehension [-M]
  • Not accessible to learners with visual impairments; require tactile, verbal, or screen-reader alternatives
  • Transient visuals (gestures, quickly-erased whiteboard work) do not offer the permanence benefit; static displays or handouts do

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static graphics and graphic organizers (permanent, revisitable)
  • Gestures and signing (transient, but support embodied encoding of concepts)
  • Highlighting, underlining, and color coding on shared texts Experimenter underlining is effective as student underlining. [+W]
  • Advance organizers that preview the structure of upcoming content

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with auditory processing challenges or hearing differences, for whom speech alone is a fragile channel
  • Novices, who benefit from external structure that reduces unguided search Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less beneficial for learners with strong prior knowledge, who may find added visuals redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of verbal explanations and multi-step processes
  • Retention of declarative content through dual representation
  • Attention management: signaling what matters in complex material

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the core structure of the explanation (sequence, hierarchy, comparison) and choose a visual form that matches it — timeline, concept map, or diagram — rather than a generic illustration
  2. Present the visual alongside or before the verbal explanation; use Advance Organizers to preview structure
  3. Signal key elements with color, arrows, or Annotating so attention lands on the relevant feature
  4. Chunk dense visuals into stages, revealing parts as the explanation proceeds Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  5. Follow with learner activity — have students reproduce, complete, or annotate the visual themselves (Practice) rather than only viewing it

Related Strategies¶

  • Graphic Organizers — a structured sub-type of visual cue for showing relationships among concepts
  • Multimodal Presentation — the broader principle of distributing content across channels

Examples¶

  • A teacher uses consistent gestures (e.g., a sweeping arc for "range," a pinch for "point") when explaining graphing concepts, giving students a non-auditory anchor for each term. Resource: Auditory Processing micro-credential, Friday Institute at NC State.
  • A biology instructor narrates over a labeled cell diagram, revealing organelles one at a time as each is discussed, then asks students to complete an unlabeled version.
  • Khan Academy videos pair narrated problem-solving with persistent on-screen writing, so each step remains visible while later steps are explained.
