Promote the flipped classroom in low-resource settings using free digital tools, peer instruction, and group discussions.¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 quant-synthesis), q3 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's conclusions recommend concrete steps for low-resource settings: "institutions should leverage free digital tools and open educational resources; prioritize low-cost, high-impact in-class strategies such as peer instruction and group discussions; and maximize existing infrastructure through community collaboration". This strategy adapts the flipped classroom to settings where expensive technology or new infrastructure is unavailable.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Institutional commitment to leveraging existing infrastructure and community collaboration

Constraints¶

  • Resource availability, faculty preparedness, and student preferences must be considered before implementation

Target Learners¶

  • medical students in low-resource settings

Target Learning Goals¶

  • knowledge acquisition through flipped classroom instruction

Claims¶

  • Flipped classroom instruction is associated with higher final knowledge scores than traditional instruction in medical education (SMD 0.90). [+W]
  • Flipped classroom instruction is associated with higher student satisfaction than traditional instruction in medical education (SMD 0.82). [+W]
  • The flipped classroom effect on knowledge scores is larger in basic science subjects than in clinical or public health subjects. [+W]
  • Publication bias inflates flipped classroom effect estimates for knowledge outcomes but not for baseline knowledge. [-W]

  • Adopt flipped classroom in preventive medicine courses according to institutional resources, with teacher information-technology training
