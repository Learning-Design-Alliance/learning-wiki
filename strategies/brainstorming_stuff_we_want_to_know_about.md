# Brainstorming 'Stuff We Want to Know About'

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners and instructors co-create an ongoing list of questions and topics of interest related to course content, typically generated from a stimulus (a phenomenon, text, image, or event) and maintained publicly across a unit or term. The list functions as both a motivational device — giving learners voice and choice — and an instructional map, since selected questions can drive inquiry projects, case explorations, and discussions.

## Design Implications

Brainstorming learner questions converts curiosity into a durable instructional resource, but its payoff depends on what happens next: questions that are curated, revisited, and pursued produce learning, while questions that are merely collected do not. Autonomy over *what to investigate* reliably raises intrinsic motivation and engagement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], and having choice among learning options improves motivation and performance [Patall et al. meta-analysis on choice.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Generating questions also activates prior knowledge, which improves comprehension of subsequent instruction [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A stimulus rich enough to provoke genuine questions (an anchoring phenomenon, provocative text, or demonstration) rather than an open-ended "any questions?"
- A public, persistent record of the list (chart paper, shared doc, question board) that is revisited and revised, not discarded after day one
- Instructor willingness to curation: mapping learner questions onto curriculum objectives and being transparent about which will be pursued, which answered briefly, and which parked
- A structure for pursuing selected questions — [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md), inquiry projects, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)

#### Constraints
- Unguided pursuit of learner-generated questions is a form of minimal-guidance instruction and is ineffective for novices, who lack the background knowledge to evaluate or pursue questions productively [Kirschner, Sweller & Clark on minimal guidance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [-S] — the list must be paired with instructor scaffolding, not replace instruction
- Questions can drift from curriculum objectives; without explicit curation and mapping, time is spent on tangents with little transferable learning [-W]
- If the list is generated once and never revisited, the activity functions as a one-off engagement trick; the motivational benefit of autonomy depends on learners seeing their questions actually shape instruction [~M]
- Dominant voices can colonize the list; without individual silent generation first, the brainstorm reflects a few learners' interests [-W]
- Question generation and curation consume substantial class time, creating tension with content coverage goals [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **KWL charts** — a lightweight variant where "What do we Want to know?" is one column of a reading-comprehension routine, revisited in the "What did we Learn?" column
- **Question Formulation Technique (QFT)** — learners produce, refine, and prioritize their own questions in a structured protocol, shifting ownership of question quality to students
- **Driving Question Boards** — in phenomenon-based science curricula, the class list is organized around an anchoring phenomenon
