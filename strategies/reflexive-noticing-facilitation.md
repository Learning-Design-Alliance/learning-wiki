# Reflexive Noticing Facilitation

## Description
This strategy operationalizes [Reflexive Noticing](../theories/reflexive-noticing.md) as a facilitation discipline for teacher professional-development (PD) dialogue. Facilitators resist the group's default tendency to "collectively rush to quick (often closed) solutions" when a difficulty or surprising result comes up, and instead explicitly slow down and sustain the puzzle. Three linguistic markers help a facilitator recognize (and a teacher-leader can be coached to produce) each device: **sustained puzzle** talk keeps a "we need to work on this" framing rather than resolving into "it can't be helped"; **dilemma-as-origin-of-change** talk names a contradiction (e.g., "our teaching style may be causing the passivity it's meant to fix") as the focal point for the group's next steps, not evidence of an impossible bind; **discursive consciousness** talk has teachers name their own shared assumptions out loud ("we're used to thinking X, but that isn't true for everyone") rather than only applying those assumptions unreflectively.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A recurring PD or intervention structure (the source study used fortnightly two-hour sessions across a school year) rather than a single session, since the shift from stabilization to possibility discourse developed gradually
- Raw material to reflect on: classroom video, pilot-unit outcomes, or other concrete evidence from real teaching, especially "surprises" where student performance contradicted teachers' expectations
#### Constraints
- Surprising evidence does not automatically produce this shift — the same evidence can instead be re-stabilized (reframed as an exception) if the facilitator does not actively sustain the puzzle; presenting positive surprises is necessary but not sufficient
- Evidence is discourse-level (what teachers said changed) — the source study does not measure whether teaching practice or student outcomes changed as a result
#### Implementation Variability
- The three devices (sustained puzzle, dilemma-as-origin, discursive consciousness) appear to build on each other in the source study's session sequence, but their relative necessity and best order have not been separately tested

### Target Learners
- Teachers in school-based professional-development or intervention settings, especially where a shared "our students can't do X" narrative has become entrenched

### Target Learning Goals
- [Reflexive noticing shifts teacher discourse from stabilization to possibility, while surprises alone often re-stabilize it](../claims/reflexive-noticing-shifts-stabilization-to-possibility-discourse.md)

### Instructions
1. Bring concrete evidence from real classroom trials (video, pilot-unit results) to the session, prioritizing moments that surprise teachers' expectations of their students.
2. When a surprise or difficulty surfaces, explicitly resist immediate resolution — name it as a puzzle worth sitting with rather than an anomaly to explain away.
3. If teachers articulate a dilemma (e.g., "didactic teaching demotivates them, but they need didactic teaching"), reframe it as the object of the group's ongoing work, not as proof the situation is unworkable.
4. Prompt teachers to name their own shared assumptions explicitly ("we're used to thinking...") and invite the group to question whether the assumption holds for all students.
5. Track discourse over multiple sessions for a shift from stabilizing markers ("that's how it goes here," "nothing we've tried helps") to possibility markers (agentic framing of student difficulty as workable).

## Examples
- Nine fortnightly two-hour whole-school intervention sessions in a Finnish middle school (27 teachers, ~300 students, 30% recent immigrants/refugees), using classroom video review and pilot-unit planning as the raw material for reflexive noticing.
