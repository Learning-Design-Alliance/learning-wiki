Accessing Students’ Background Knowledge¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 7 claims (2 for, 5 mixed) · 15 studies (6 review, 5 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 15 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

This strategy involves intentionally eliciting and building on learners' existing knowledge, experiences, and cultural resources before and during instruction. Teachers research students' backgrounds, invite students to connect content to their lives (e.g., discussing a topic with peers in their home language, interviewing family members, sharing traditions), and use structured tools such as Advance Organizers or Analogies to bridge the familiar and the new. The goal is to ensure that new material lands on prepared cognitive ground rather than being encoded in isolation.

Design Implications¶

Prior knowledge is among the strongest predictors of new learning: what students already know determines what they can extract from instruction Prior knowledge as a moderator of learning outcomes. [~M]. Comprehension research shows that passages are recalled far better when they connect to relevant background knowledge, even when the text itself is unchanged Relevant background knowledge dramatically improves comprehension and recall. [+S]. Activation must be relevant — generic warm-ups that do not map onto the target content can consume time without priming the right schemas Activation activities only help when they surface knowledge relevant to the task. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Accurate knowledge of students' backgrounds, gathered through surveys, family interviews, or community engagement — not assumptions
  • A structured activation activity that surfaces knowledge relevant to the target content (Activation, Advance Organizers, Analogies)
  • A plan for what to do with what surfaces: connect, correct misconceptions, or build missing knowledge
  • Sensitivity to students' comfort levels with personal or cultural sharing

Constraints¶

  • Activation of incorrect or partially understood prior knowledge can entrench misconceptions; high-confidence errors are especially resistant to correction High-confidence errors persist unless explicitly addressed. [~M]
  • Putting individual students "on the spot" as cultural representatives can stereotype or embarrass; sharing must be voluntary and varied across students
  • For novices, open-ended "what do you know about…?" prompts can surface disorganized or irrelevant knowledge that adds load rather than structure Chunking and structuring reduce working-memory load; unstructured recall does not. [~M]
  • Time-intensive: gathering background information and preparing culturally responsive materials competes with instructional time

Implementation Variability¶

  • Elicitation formats: Class Discussion, think-pair-share, home-language peer talk, KWL charts, anticipation guides
  • Cultural connection: inviting students to share traditions, foods, music, and family histories across the curriculum throughout the year, rather than as one-off events
  • Family and community links: interviewing family members; using community-based texts and artifacts
  • Bridging tools: analogies from familiar domains, advance organizers, Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation

Target Learners¶

  • English language learners and students from culturally diverse backgrounds, for whom instruction that ignores home knowledge creates an avoidable comprehension gap Cultural background shapes text interpretation and recall. [+S]
  • Students whose prior knowledge exists but is not spontaneously activated — many learners do not retrieve relevant knowledge without prompting Self-explanation and prompted retrieval improve conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Less necessary — and sometimes redundant — for learners who already possess and spontaneously activate well-organized knowledge of the domain Expertise reverses the benefit of added guidance. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of texts, concepts, and phenomena that depend on integrating new information with existing schemas
  • Engagement and motivation through perceived relevance Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
  • Vocabulary and concept development in a second language, anchored to familiar referents

Instructions¶

  1. Gather background information about students (surveys, family interviews, community knowledge) before planning the unit.
  2. Activate relevant knowledge with a structured prompt — Activation via discussion, anticipation guide, or home-language peer talk — chosen so the surfaced knowledge maps onto the target content.
  3. Bridge the familiar to the new using Analogies or an Advance Organizer that gives the new material a structure to attach to.
  4. Model the connection explicitly — briefly Demonstrate how prior experience relates to the new concept so students learn to make such links themselves.
  5. Apply and check — have students Practice the new content in a context that draws on their own experience, and Provide Feedback that corrects misconceptions surfaced during activation.

Examples¶

  • Steffensen, Joag-Dev, and Anderson (1979) — readers recalled a passage about a wedding far better when it described a wedding from their own culture, demonstrating how background knowledge drives comprehension even with identical text.
  • Family history interviews — students interview relatives about memories and experiences, then use those accounts as primary material in history or literacy units.
  • "Mirror books" and culturally relevant texts — curricula such as Rudine Sims Bishop's mirrors-and-windows framework select texts in which students can see themselves, increasing engagement and comprehension.
  • Home-language peer talk — before reading a science text, multilingual students discuss the topic with peers in their home language, surfacing concepts that the teacher then connects to target vocabulary.
