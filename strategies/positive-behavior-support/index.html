Positive Behavior Support¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Positive Behavior Support (PBS) is a proactive, function-based approach to behavior management that defines clear behavioral expectations, explicitly teaches them, and reinforces their occurrence instead of relying primarily on punishment for misbehavior. At scale it is implemented as Schoolwide Positive Behavior Interventions and Supports (SWPBIS), typically in three tiers: universal expectations for all students, targeted small-group supports, and intensive individualized function-based plans.

Design Implications¶

PBS reframes behavior management from reactive discipline to instructional design: expected behaviors are treated as skills to be taught, modeled, and practiced, consistent with Behaviorism principles of clear antecedents and contingent reinforcement. The approach is grounded in functional behavior assessment — problem behavior is assumed to serve a communicative or regulatory function (escape, attention, access), and supports address that function rather than suppress the surface behavior. Punitive responses alone do not teach replacement behavior and can worsen outcomes for the students who receive them most [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A small set (3–5) of positively stated, schoolwide expectations (e.g., "Be respectful, be responsible, be safe")
  • Explicit teaching of what each expectation looks like in each setting, with Coaching and reinforcement of approximations
  • A reinforcement system (acknowledgment, tokens, praise) delivered contingently and consistently by all staff
  • Data collection (office referrals, attendance) to identify students needing Tier 2/3 supports and to evaluate implementation fidelity
  • For individual students, a functional behavior assessment linking supports to the identified function of the behavior
  • A small set of positively stated, schoolwide behavioral expectations (e.g., "be safe, be respectful, be responsible")
  • Explicit instruction of what each expectation looks like in each setting (Modeling and Practice of routines, not just posters)
  • A consistent, low-effort reinforcement system (acknowledgment tokens, praise ratios heavily favoring positive over corrective statements)
  • Functional behavioral assessment and behavior intervention planning capacity for Tier 3 cases
  • Leadership commitment and staff-wide implementation fidelity; effects degrade sharply when only some staff participate [~M]

Constraints¶

  • Punitive or exclusionary responses layered on top of PBS undermine it: suspension does not improve behavior and disproportionately harms marginalized students [-S]
  • Reinforcement systems fail when delivery is inconsistent across staff or when rewards become the sole goal, eroding intrinsic motivation [~M] — praise should emphasize competence and autonomy rather than compliance-for-reward Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Implementation without staff training and fidelity monitoring produces little effect; effects depend on sustained, whole-staff implementation [~S]
  • Function-based individual plans require assessment expertise; generic reward charts applied without functional analysis often fail for students with persistent challenging behavior [-M]
  • Punishment-based responses (reprimands, exclusion) suppress behavior temporarily but do not teach replacement skills and increase avoidance, disengagement, and dropout risk [-S]
  • Token economies implemented without fading plans can undermine intrinsic motivation and produce dependence on external rewards [~M] — reinforcement should shift toward natural and social contingencies
  • PBS implemented as a compliance or discipline program (tracking points, public displays of earned status) can shame students and damage belonging [~M]; public leaderboards and point loss are common implementation failures
  • Effects on academic achievement are indirect and inconsistent; PBS alone does not improve instruction [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Tier 1 (universal): schoolwide expectations, acknowledgment systems, and consistent responses for all students
  • Tier 2 (targeted): check-in/check-out mentoring, small-group social skills instruction, brief daily adult connection Check-ins strengthen relationships and monitoring.
  • Tier 3 (individualized): function-based behavior intervention plans, wraparound supports, person-centered planning
  • Classroom-level adaptations: precorrection (prompting expected behavior before problem contexts), choice-making opportunities, and structured routines
  • Tier 1 (universal): schoolwide expectations, routines, acknowledgment systems
  • Tier 2 (targeted): check-in/check-out mentoring, small-group social skills instruction (Check-In, Coaching)
  • Tier 3 (individualized): person-centered planning, functional communication training, wraparound supports
  • Classroom-level adaptations: Classroom Management routines, precorrection of predictable problem contexts, choice-making opportunities to build autonomy Autonomy support improves motivation and engagement. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • All students benefit from clear, taught expectations, but the largest gains accrue to students with or at risk for emotional and behavioral disorders [~S]
  • Students experiencing exclusionary discipline benefit from empathic, relationship-based responses; brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions. [+S]
  • A sense of belonging supports engagement and outcomes, making relational components (greetings, check-ins) more than procedural niceties Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • All students benefit from predictable, explicitly taught behavioral routines [~S]
  • Students with emotional and behavioral disabilities and those with a history of exclusionary discipline benefit most from Tier 2/3 function-based supports [+M]
  • Students whose problem behavior is maintained by escape from aversive tasks need instructional accommodations (Accommodations) alongside behavior plans — punishing escape-motivated behavior without fixing the task escalates it [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and social-emotional objectives: self-regulation, cooperation, prosocial skills
  • Executive and self-management skills: monitoring one's own behavior against expectations
  • Climate outcomes: increased instructional time and engagement that indirectly support academic goals
  • Behavioral: acquisition of prosocial and self-regulation skills
  • Climate: sense of safety and Belonging, which supports achievement Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • Self-management: students monitoring and evaluating their own behavior (Self-Regulated Learning)

Instructions¶

  1. Define 3–5 positively stated expectations collaboratively with staff and, where possible, students.
  2. Teach expectations explicitly in each setting using Direct Instruction structure: name the behavior, model it, have students practice it, provide feedback.
  3. Establish a continuum of reinforcement — specific, labeled praise and acknowledgment delivered immediately and contingently.
  4. Respond to problem behavior with consistent, instructional (non-exclusionary) consequences that include re-teaching the expectation.
  5. Monitor referral and engagement data; move students showing repeated need into Tier 2 supports such as check-in/check-out.
  6. For persistent cases, conduct a functional behavior assessment and build an individualized plan that teaches a replacement behavior serving the same function.

Examples¶

  • SWPBIS (https://www.pbis.org) — the U.S. OSEP-funded national implementation framework; thousands of schools report reduced office discipline referrals under faithful Tier 1 implementation [~S]
  • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) — a widely validated Tier 2 intervention in which a mentor greets the student each morning, reviews goals, and closes the day with feedback
  • Praise-based acknowledgment systems (e.g., "gotcha" tickets exchanged for privileges) — effective when paired with specific verbal feedback, not as stand-alone token economies
  • PBIS — the OSEP-funded national technical assistance center; provides the standard Tier 1–3 implementation guides used in over 25,000 U.S. schools.
  • Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) — a Tier 2 intervention in which a mentor greets the student each morning, sets daily point goals, and reviews progress each afternoon; meta-analytic evidence supports moderate reductions in problem behavior for escape- and attention-maintained behavior [~M].
  • Functional communication training — teaching a nonverbal student to request a break with a card instead of throwing materials, directly replacing escape-motivated aggression.
