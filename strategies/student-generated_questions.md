# Student-Generated Questions

## Description
Student-generated questions place learners in the role of question-askers rather than question-answerers: students formulate the questions that drive a project, investigation, or discussion, then pursue answers through research, experimentation, or dialogue. The strategy converts curiosity into an explicit artifact — a question — that structures subsequent inquiry and gives students ownership over the direction of learning.

## Design Implications

Question generation is itself a cognitive act: formulating a question requires identifying gaps in one's own knowledge, which supports comprehension monitoring and deeper processing [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Student questioning also functions as a form of cognitive activation — prompting learners to reason rather than receive — which is associated with stronger achievement outcomes [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. However, the quality of student questions varies widely with prior knowledge and instruction; without scaffolds such as question stems or the Question Formulation Technique, questions tend to be shallow, closed, or answerable by recall [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit instruction in question formulation — question stems, taxonomy practice, or protocols like the [Question Formulation Technique](https://rightquestion.org/what-is-the-qft/) — before students are expected to generate productive questions
- Access to resources (texts, data, experts, labs) that can actually support investigation of student questions
- Instructor facilitation that helps students refine, prioritize, and connect questions rather than treating all questions as equally investigable
- A classroom culture where student questions are treated as legitimate drivers of activity, not interruptions

#### Constraints
- Unstructured question generation often yields low-level recall questions; without modeling and criteria for "good" questions, the strategy can consume time without deepening learning [-M]
- Students with low prior knowledge generate fewer and less generative questions, so the strategy can widen gaps if used without scaffolding [~M]
- Divergent student questions can fragment a course; instructors need mechanisms (voting, clustering, curriculum mapping) to converge on investigable questions without extinguishing ownership
- Assessment systems that reward predetermined answers can undermine the strategy — students learn quickly that their questions are not what counts [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class question drives**: students generate and vote on questions that anchor a unit (project-based learning, progressive inquiry)
- **Structured protocols**: the Question Formulation Technique (Rothstein & Santana, 2011) — produce, refine, prioritize, plan use
- **Reciprocal peer questioning**: students generate questions for each other before discussion, with question stems provided (King, 1994)
- **Knowledge-building communities**: students continuously post and improve questions in a shared database (e.g., Knowledge Forum) over extended inquiry
- **Entry-point questioning**: students generate questions at the start of a text or case, then revise them as understanding develops

### Target Learners
- Most effective for learners with moderate prior knowledge, who know enough to identify genuine gaps but still have questions worth asking [~M]
- Students who experience low motivation in teacher-directed formats; ownership of the question increases investment and persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Novices need question scaffolds (stems, exemplars) before generating questions independently; unscaffolded generation favors students who already possess academic questioning habits [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Inquiry and research skills: framing investigable questions, planning evidence-gathering
- Conceptual understanding: question generation forces learners to articulate what they do and do not understand [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Metacognition and self-regulated learning: monitoring one's own knowledge gaps
- Epistemic dispositions: treating knowledge as something built and contested rather than delivered

### Instructions
1. **Stimulate curiosity** with an artifact, phenomenon, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) that creates a genuine knowledge gap ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
2. **Generate questions** individually or in small groups using question stems or a protocol; defer evaluation during generation
3. **Refine and prioritize** — students improve questions for clarity and investigability, then select focus questions (class voting or clustering via [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
4. **Investigate** through research, experimentation, or [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)-supported inquiry, revisiting and revising questions as evidence accumulates
5. **Share and assess** findings against the original questions; evaluate both the answers and the quality of the questions themselves ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) for learning, not just of it)

## Related Strategies
- [Case-based learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — cases provide the authentic context from which student questions naturally arise
- [Collaborative inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md) — student questions drive the shared investigative agenda
- [Flipped classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) — students bring questions from pre-work to class time, making them the agenda for face-to-face sessions

## Examples
- **Question Formulation Technique** ([Right Question Institute](https://rightquestion.org)) — a structured protocol used across K–12 and higher education in which students produce, improve, and prioritize questions about a "QFocus" prompt before a unit or text.
- **Knowledge Building / Knowledge Forum** ([IKIT](http://ikit.org)) — Scardamalia and Bereiter's knowledge-building communities, where students' questions are recorded, improved, and built upon in a shared database over months; research in these environments shows progressive deepening of explanatory questions (Hakkarainen, 2003).
- **Reciprocal questioning in college biology** — King's (1994) guided peer-questioning procedure, in which students use question stems to quiz each other before discussion, improved higher-order discussion and achievement relative to lecture.
- **Project-based science curricula** such as [EL Education](https://eleducation.org) — units are organized around student-developed "guiding questions" that culminate in authentic products for real audiences.
