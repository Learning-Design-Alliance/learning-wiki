Pre-Writing Skills Activities¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 review), q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Pre-writing skills activities develop the component abilities that formal handwriting depends on: hand and finger strength, stable pencil grasp, visual-motor integration, line and shape discrimination, and directional awareness (top-to-bottom, left-to-right). Typical activities include tracing and copying strokes and shapes, mazes and dot-to-dot tasks, playdough and manipulatives for hand strengthening, and multisensory letter formation (tracing in sand, forming letters with body movements). They are carried out as short, repeated, developmentally sequenced sessions before or alongside early handwriting instruction.

Design Implications¶

Pre-writing activities function as part-task practice: they isolate subskills so that working memory during later writing is not consumed by motor execution Part-task practice reduces load for novices. [+M]. This matters because transcription fluency constrains composition — when letter formation is effortful, children produce shorter, poorer-quality text, and direct handwriting instruction (which pre-writing activities prepare for) transfers to composition quality Berninger et al. (1997) [+S]. Handwriting practice itself also supports letter recognition and reading, suggesting the motor and perceptual components are mutually reinforcing rather than separate tracks James & Engelhardt (2012) [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Developmentally sequenced progression: gross motor (shoulder/arm stability) → fine motor (grasp, in-hand manipulation) → visual-motor (tracing, copying) → stroke and shape production
  • Short, frequent sessions with immediate corrective feedback on formation, not just completion
  • Multisensory input (visual, tactile, kinesthetic) to build the motor memory for letter forms
  • Eventual connection to real writing purposes so isolated skills are integrated into composition

Constraints¶

  • Extended tracing and copying without connection to actual letter production can consume instructional time without improving writing outcomes; transcription instruction must include forming letters from memory, not only copying Graham, Harris, & Fink (2000) [~M]
  • Fine-motor drill alone (e.g., hand-strengthening exercises) shows weak transfer to handwriting quality unless paired with actual letter-formation practice [-W]
  • Overly long sessions exceed young children's attention and endurance limits, producing fatigue and maladaptive grasp patterns [~M]
  • For children with significant motor impairments, insisting on handwriting readiness before allowing composition (e.g., via dictation or keyboard) delays writing development; accommodations should run in parallel, not after readiness is achieved [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Multisensory programs (e.g., Handwriting Without Tears, which uses wood pieces, wet-dry-try tracing, and music) versus paper-pencil-only approaches
  • Embedded in play (mazes, clay, lacing) versus delivered as explicit short lessons; explicit formation instruction with modeling produces more reliable gains than play alone [~M]
  • Whole-class, small-group, or one-to-one delivery; one-to-one allows grasp correction that group formats often miss

Target Learners¶

  • Preschool and kindergarten children (roughly ages 3–6) before or during formal handwriting instruction
  • Children with developmental coordination difficulties or delayed fine-motor skills, for whom component skill isolation is most beneficial Part-task practice reduces load for novices. [+M]
  • Older struggling writers whose poor transcription fluency constrains composition; remediation should be brief and paired with composition, not isolated drill Berninger et al. (1997) [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency: automatic, legible letter formation that frees attention for composing
  • Perceptual-motor integration: mapping visual letter forms onto motor programs
  • Foundational literacy: letter knowledge supported by writing movements James & Engelhardt (2012) [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Assess baseline grasp, hand strength, and copying ability to determine the starting level.
  2. Begin with gross- and fine-motor strengthening activities (playdough, tweezers, vertical-surface drawing) for children who need them.
  3. Teach strokes and pre-writing shapes (lines, circles, crosses) through modeling and multisensory tracing before letters.
  4. Progress to letter formation with verbal pathways ("down, up, over") and immediate corrective feedback, fading physical prompts as formation becomes consistent Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M].
  5. Integrate formed letters into name writing and short, meaningful composition so skills transfer to authentic writing.

Related Strategies¶

  • Handwriting Instruction — the formal instruction these activities prepare for; the two should overlap rather than be strictly sequential
  • Multisensory Teaching — the sensory-modality approach most pre-writing programs draw on

Examples¶

  • Handwriting Without Tears — widely used developmental curriculum sequencing from wood-piece letter building through wet-dry-try tracing to pencil-paper writing in kindergarten.
  • Get Set for School — the pre-K component, using singing, manipulatives, and crayon activities to build hand and finger strength before letter instruction.
  • Occupational therapy pre-writing programs — school-based OT sessions using mazes, dot-to-dot, and tracing ladders as individualized pre-writing intervention.
