# Random Calling Name Cards

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Random Calling Name Cards is a classroom questioning technique in which the teacher writes each student's name on a card, shuffles the deck, and draws cards to select who responds to questions. Because selection is visibly random, every student must be prepared to answer, and participation is distributed across the whole class rather than concentrated among volunteers. The deck is typically reshuffled (or drawn without replacement until exhausted) so students cannot predict who is next.

## Design Implications

Random calling converts a whole-class question from an optional invitation into a universal accountability mechanism, dramatically increasing the proportion of students who mentally engage with each question [Active participation structures improve exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. It also produces a more accurate picture of class understanding than volunteer-only questioning, since volunteers are systematically unrepresentative of the class. The technique's effects depend heavily on classroom climate: paired with [Wait Time](wait-time.md) (not verified — write plain text: adequate wait time), warm delivery, and a no-stakes or low-stakes framing, random calling improves participation and perceived learning; used punitively or without support, it raises anxiety and suppresses engagement [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A complete, current deck of name cards (or equivalent randomizer) covering every student
- Questions calibrated so every student can attempt an answer — or a support structure ([Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — not verified; use pair discussion before drawing) that lets students rehearse before being called
- A classroom norm that a wrong attempt is safe: allow "pass," take partial answers, or let a called student nominate a partner after trying
- Teacher follow-up moves: probing, revoicing, and crediting the attempt rather than simply moving on

#### Constraints
- Without a psychologically safe climate, random calling increases anxiety and can reduce voluntary participation over time, particularly for anxious students and those from marginalized groups [~M]
- Calling on unprepared students without scaffolding wastes the question and publicizes failure [-M]
- Random selection is inappropriate for questions requiring specific prior experience or for students with communication accommodations — pair with [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) and offer opt-out routes
- Overuse within a single session turns discussion into interrogation, shifting the interaction from dialogic to recitation [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Draw without replacement** until all names are used, guaranteeing every student is called across a session
- **Warm calling**: announce the question first, allow think time or pair talk, *then* draw the card — the highest-yield variant
- **Lifeline rules**: a called student may ask a peer for a hint or pass once per class
- **Group cards**: draw a card to select which small group reports out, softening individual exposure
- **Student-drawn cards**: handing the draw to a student increases buy-in and makes the randomness visibly fair

### Target Learners
- Whole classes in K–12 and higher education where a few voices dominate discussion [~M]
- Reluctant or quiet students benefit from guaranteed, structured turns — but only when anxiety is managed through think time and low-stakes framing [~M]
- Students whose belonging is fragile need explicit norms that being called is expected and safe, not a spotlight test [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Formative assessment: obtaining accurate evidence of whole-class understanding rather than volunteer-biased signals [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Accountability for pre-class preparation (readings, homework)
- Oral reasoning and academic discourse practice across all students, not just volunteers

### Instructions
1. Create one card per student; shuffle the deck before class.
2. Pose the question to the whole class *before* drawing, and provide [Wait Time](wait-time.md) — not verified; provide silent think time or brief pair discussion so every student drafts an answer.
3. Draw a card publicly; ask the named student to respond.
4. Respond to the answer with probing or revoicing, crediting the attempt; accept "pass" gracefully and return to that student later in the deck.
5. Track which cards have been drawn; reshuffle when the deck is exhausted so all students are called equitably over time.
6. Periodically debrief the norm with the class: random calling is about fair airtime, not catching people out.

## Related Strategies
- Cold Calling (verbal variant without cards) — same accountability mechanism; cards add visible fairness and reduce teacher bias in selection
- Equity of Voice structures — random calling is one tool among several for distributing participation
- No Hands Up — the whole-class norm within which random calling operates

## Examples
- **Doug Lemov's "Cold Call" technique** (*Teach Like a Champion*, Jossey-Bass) — name-based random selection with warm-up (question first, then name), widely used in Uncommon Schools charter classrooms.
- **Kagan Structures** ([Kagan Publishing](https://www.kaganteaching.co.uk)) — team and student-number calling randomize accountability within cooperative learning.
- **University seminar courses** (documented in Dallimore et al.'s research on MBA and undergraduate classes) — instructors using cold calling report higher voluntary participation and higher perceived learning once the norm is established.
