# Calming Tactile Input

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Calming tactile input uses the sense of touch to reduce arousal and support emotional regulation: running hands through a tactile bin of sand, rice, or beans; applying deep pressure through weighted blankets, weighted stuffed animals, or firm hugs; or squishing into a pillow or beanbag chair for full-body input. The strategy is drawn from sensory integration practice and is typically offered as a brief, scheduled, or on-demand regulation break rather than as instruction itself.

## Design Implications

Calming tactile input is best treated as a *regulation support that enables learning*, not a learning strategy in its own right: its value lies in returning an overwhelmed learner to a state where attention and instruction can work. Evidence for sensory-based interventions is weaker and more contested than for core instructional strategies, so it should be paired with explicit self-regulation teaching rather than substituted for it [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Tactile materials (sand, rice, beans, putty), weighted blankets or lap pads, weighted stuffed animals, pillows or beanbag chairs
- A predictable, low-stimulation space or routine where the input can be used without social cost
- An adult or self-monitoring routine that connects the sensory break to noticing one's own arousal state [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- A return path to the learning task — the break should end with re-entry, not become avoidance

#### Constraints
- Some learners have tactile defensiveness; forced exposure to aversive textures can *raise* arousal and distress rather than lower it [~M]
- Systematic reviews find little rigorous evidence that sensory-based interventions improve the underlying sensory processing or academic outcomes they are often claimed to target [~M] — treat them as short-term regulation supports, not treatments
- Unstructured access can function as task escape: without a defined duration and re-entry routine, breaks may reduce time on task [-M]
- Deep pressure (weighted blankets, vests) shows inconsistent effects across individuals; effects are idiosyncratic and must be individually evaluated rather than assumed [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Light touch / texture play**: tactile bins, putty, fidget materials — brief, portable, low-preparation
- **Deep pressure**: weighted blankets, lap pads, compression garments, firm squish into a beanbag — longer duration, more individualized
- **Embedded vs. scheduled**: input can be offered on a fixed schedule, on learner request, or embedded in the task itself (e.g., manipulatives during seatwork)
- **Learner choice**: letting the learner select the input type and duration supports autonomy and improves buy-in

### Target Learners
- Children with sensory sensitivities or sensory processing differences, particularly in autistic learners and learners with ADHD [~M]
- Learners who become easily overwhelmed by noise, crowding, or transitions and need a regulation reset before re-engaging
- Young children in early-childhood settings, where tactile exploration is developmentally familiar
- Not a default for all learners: for most students without regulation difficulties, added tactile input is neutral at best and can be a distraction [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and managing arousal states so that instructional goals become reachable
- Emotional regulation: reducing anxiety that would otherwise block participation
- Prerequisite readiness: enabling engagement with academic goals ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)) rather than serving as a goal itself

### Instructions
1. **Identify the need.** Observe or ask the learner when dysregulation occurs; use a simple check-in routine to build awareness of arousal state ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md)).
2. **Match input to the learner.** Offer a small menu (tactile bin, weighted lap pad, beanbag squish) and let the learner choose; avoid textures the learner avoids.
3. **Set boundaries.** Define a clear duration (e.g., 3–5 minutes) and a visible end signal so the break does not become escape from the task.
4. **Re-enter with support.** Return to the learning task with a low-demand re-entry activity, then resume [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or instruction.
5. **Evaluate individually.** Track behavior and engagement before, during, and after input; keep only what demonstrably helps this learner.

## Related Strategies
- Sensory breaks and movement breaks — the proprioceptive/vestibular counterparts to tactile input
- Calm-down corners — the environmental container in which tactile input is often offered
- Explicit self-regulation instruction — teaching the skills that sensory input temporarily supports

## Examples
- **Tactile bin break**: a first-grade classroom keeps a bin of dried beans with hidden small objects; a student who becomes dysregulated after transitions spends three minutes at it, then rejoins the [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
- **Weighted lap pad during seatwork**: an occupational therapist provides a 5-lb lap pad for a student with autism during writing tasks, paired with a visual timer.
- **Beanbag squish**: a learner applies firm full-body pressure by squeezing into a beanbag chair in a classroom calm-down space before returning to [practice](../elements/practice.md).
- **Temple Grandin's squeeze machine** (Grandin, 1992) — a documented individual application of deep pressure for calming anxiety.
