Primary school teachers should regularly include activities in which children manipulate objects with larger and smaller muscles to develop bodily coordination¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 1 study (1 causal), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that during games and physical activities lessons, primary teachers frequently use activities in which children "manipulate various objects by using their larger and smaller muscles, thus, providing their bodily coordination", with the stated purpose of promoting control over objects and visual perceptions. This follows from the study's finding that coordination-based activities improved motor skills more than regular lessons.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Activities should be planned and long-term, as the study applied them for 10 weeks with children's active participation

Constraints¶

  • The supporting evidence comes from 6-year-old first-grade children; the article does not test the recommendation in other age groups

Target Learners¶

  • 6-year-old first-grade primary school children

Target Learning Goals¶

  • development of motor skills (agility, swiftness, ball control, throwing accuracy, footrace coordination)

Claims¶

  • The control group following the regular Games and Physical Activities course showed no statistically significant pre-to-post change on any motor test [+W]
  • A 10-week coordination-based teaching program produced statistically significant pre-to-post gains on all six measured motor skills in 6-year-olds [+W]
  • At post-test the experimental group significantly outperformed the control group on all six motor tests, at significance levels varying from p<0.05 to p<0.001 [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Design coordination activities as games without losers so all children participate actively
