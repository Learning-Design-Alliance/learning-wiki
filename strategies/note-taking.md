# Note Taking

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Note taking is the practice of recording key ideas during a lecture, reading, or multimedia presentation, typically involving selection of important information, paraphrasing, and organization into a personal structure. It serves two functions: encoding (processing material deeply at the moment of learning) and storage (creating an external record for later review). Variants include linear notes, structured outlines, [Cornell notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Notes), concept maps, and sketchnoting.

## Design Implications

Note taking improves learning primarily when it requires generative processing — paraphrasing, organizing, and connecting ideas — rather than verbatim transcription [~S]. The encoding benefit is largest when learners lack prior knowledge and must actively select and structure content; the storage benefit depends on notes actually being reviewed, ideally with [retrieval practice](../principles/retrieval-practice.md) rather than passive rereading [+S]. Because taking notes while listening competes for limited working memory, instruction should manage load through [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and pauses [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Source material presented at a pace that permits selection and paraphrasing (pauses, slides, or recordings help)
- Instruction in note-taking technique itself — many students, especially novices, take poor notes without explicit teaching
- A follow-up use for the notes (review, summarizing, exam preparation), or the storage function is wasted

#### Constraints
- Verbatim transcription produces weak encoding; learners who transcribe often learn less than those who paraphrase [~S]
- Note taking during fast, dense presentations can overload working memory and *reduce* learning compared with just listening [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Providing full instructor notes or slides can eliminate the encoding benefit entirely — learners given complete materials take no notes and encode less [~S]
- Laptop note taking invites verbatim transcription and off-task multitasking more than handwriting [~M]
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, the encoding benefit shrinks because selection is already effortless [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Guided notes / skeletal outlines**: instructor provides structure; learners fill in — reduces load for novices but risks shallow processing if completion becomes mechanical
- **Note-taking then summarizing**: a second pass that forces consolidation ([Summarization](summarizing.md))
- **Collaborative notes**: shared documents distribute the recording load and expose learners to others' selections
- **Multimedia note taking**: prompts to take notes during video, with pauses, counteract the passivity of video consumption

### Target Learners
- Middle school through university students encountering new conceptual material [+S]
- Novices benefit most from guided structures; experts can self-organize
- Students with working memory or processing-speed difficulties need scaffolded formats ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)) such as guided notes or permission to record

### Target Learning Goals
- Factual and conceptual knowledge retention from lectures and texts
- Study skills and self-regulation: notes are the raw material for later [distributed practice](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Discipline-specific literacy: learning what experts in a field consider worth recording

### Instructions
1. Brief learners on the purpose and format before the session ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
2. Present material in segments with pauses for recording and paraphrasing
3. Require generative formats — paraphrase, questions in the margin, or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — rather than transcription
4. Follow with a consolidation activity: summarize the notes, or convert them into questions for later self-testing
5. Build review of notes into later sessions using spaced retrieval rather than rereading [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]

## Related Strategies
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — the text-based counterpart: marking and commenting on a source rather than recording from a presentation
- [Summarizing](summarizing.md) — a post-hoc compression of notes that deepens the encoding benefit
- [Concept Mapping](../elements/concept-mapping.md) — a graphical note format that makes relationships explicit [Concept mapping improves learning.](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- [Retrieval Practice](../principles/retrieval-practice.md) — the most effective way to use notes after the fact

## Examples
- **Cornell Notes system** — page divided into cues, notes, and summary columns; widely adopted in K–12 study-skills curricula ([https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Notes))
- **Guided lecture notes in introductory STEM courses** — instructor-provided partial outlines that students complete, reducing transcription load while preserving generative processing
- **Perusall** ([https://www.perusall.com](https://www.perusall.com)) — social annotation platform where note taking on readings is visible, shared, and graded
