Source Evaluation¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 11 studies (8 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 2 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Source evaluation is the practice of deliberately assessing where information comes from, who produced it, and whether it can be trusted — before relying on it in research, argument, or decision-making. As a strategy, it means teaching learners explicit evaluative moves (checking provenance, corroboration, and evidence quality) rather than assuming these judgments develop incidentally.

Design Implications¶

Explicit instruction in evaluation techniques substantially improves learners' ability to judge online and print sources Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' source evaluation. [+S]. The most effective approaches teach lateral moves — leaving a source to check what others say about it — rather than relying on internal features like design quality or tone, which novices over-weight and manipulators exploit. Evaluation should be embedded in authentic tasks where the judgment matters (building an argument, answering a research question), not taught as an isolated checklist Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online contexts. [-S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Authentic information tasks with genuinely variable source quality (web research, current events, literature review)
  • Explicit modeling of expert evaluative moves, ideally via Think-Aloud demonstration of how a skilled reader investigates a source
  • Criteria that go beyond surface features: authorship, funding, corroboration across independent sources, evidence quality
  • Opportunities to compare sources making competing claims (Comparing Cases)

Constraints¶

  • Checklist approaches (CRAAP test and similar) transfer poorly to real web contexts, where professional-looking sites routinely masquerade as credible Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online contexts. [-S]
  • Novices judge credibility by surface features (design polish, .org domains, confident tone), so instruction that does not directly confront these heuristics leaves them unchanged
  • Evaluation skills are domain-sensitive; training in one topic area does not automatically generalize without varied practice contexts
  • High working-memory demand when learners must evaluate while also comprehending; scaffold by separating the two phases Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Lateral reading: professional fact-checkers leave a source immediately to search for outside information about it; teach this as the default first move
  • Corroboration protocols: require a minimum of independent sources before a claim enters student work (3-Source Rule)
  • Provenance tracing: follow claims, images, or quotes back to their original context
  • Structured comparison: give learners two sources on the same event and ask which is more reliable and why

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students conducting independent research Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' source evaluation. [+S]
  • Learners in civic education contexts who will encounter persuasive and partisan content
  • Adult learners in professional programs (health, journalism, policy) where information quality has real consequences
  • Younger learners benefit from simplified versions (who made this? how do they know?) but need concrete modeling

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Information literacy: judging credibility and reliability of sources
  • Critical thinking: distinguishing evidence quality from rhetorical confidence
  • Argumentation: selecting defensible evidence for claims (Argument Construction)
  • Civic reasoning: evaluating news, media, and political claims

Instructions¶

  1. Model expert evaluation — demonstrate lateral reading and provenance checking on a live, real-world source using Think-Aloud; include a source that looks credible but fails scrutiny
  2. Contrast cases — present paired sources on the same topic with differing reliability and have learners diagnose the differences (Comparing Cases) Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]
  3. Guided practice — learners evaluate new sources using the modeled moves, with prompts fading over time
  4. Embed in authentic tasks — require evaluated sources as the evidence base for an Argument Construction task or research product, so evaluation has consequences
  5. Corroboration requirement — enforce a minimum independent-source standard (3-Source Rule) in finished work

Related Strategies¶

  • 3-Source Rule — a corroboration requirement that operationalizes evaluation in student work
  • A Finder's Guide to Facts — a framework for locating and assessing factual claims
  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the demonstration method for making expert evaluation visible

Examples¶

  • Stanford History Education Group (SHEG) civic online reasoning curriculum (https://cor.stanford.edu) — classroom lessons built on fact-checker strategies (lateral reading, click restraint, provenance), validated in large-scale assessments
  • News Literacy Project — Checkology (https://checkology.org) — interactive modules on source credibility, misinformation, and evidence standards for secondary students
  • First-year composition courses requiring annotated bibliographies in which students justify each source's authority and evidence quality before drafting an argument
