# Rice Tables And Sandboxes Science Through Inquiry

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Rice tables and sandboxes are sensory play stations stocked with pourable materials (rice, sand, water, beans) plus tools (funnels, sieves, cups, scales) that children manipulate freely or with light prompts. The teacher frames the materials around a science question — flow, volume, density, erosion, separation — and children generate predictions, test them through hands-on manipulation, and describe what they observe, converting open-ended sensory play into guided [inquiry](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md).

## Design Implications

Sensory materials make abstract physical concepts directly manipulable, which supports conceptual change in young learners whose reasoning is grounded in physical action [Hands-on activity supports learning when it is paired with sense-making rather than left unstructured.](../claims/hands-on-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]. The critical design move is the adult's framing: unstructured sand play alone produces engagement but little science learning; adding prediction prompts, comparison questions, and brief discussion turns manipulation into investigation [Guided inquiry outperforms pure discovery for novices.](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A contained table or bin with pourable material and a tool set that affords variation (funnels, sieves, containers of different sizes, ramps)
- A driving question or phenomenon the materials can actually reveal (e.g., "Which pours faster — sand or rice? Why?")
- Adult prompts that elicit prediction and observation before and during play ([Questioning](questioning.md))
- Time for children to represent findings verbally, in drawing, or through [annotating](../principles/annotating.md) simple records

#### Constraints
- Free play without framing produces exploration but rarely measurable science learning [Guided inquiry outperforms pure discovery for novices.](../claims/guided-inquiry-outperforms-pure-discovery.md) [-S] — young children do not spontaneously extract the target concept from materials alone
- Overly directive scripting collapses the activity into [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) and removes the prediction–test cycle that drives learning
- Sensory tables can overload children with sensory or attention difficulties; some learners need reduced materials or structured turns
- Mess, allergy, and safety constraints (choking hazards for children under 3; gluten concerns with some grain fillers) limit material choice

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured inquiry**: teacher supplies the question and method; children predict and observe
- **Open inquiry**: children pose their own questions after an exploration period; teacher records and revisits them
- **Rotating provocation**: the same table is re-dressed weekly (add water to sand, add ramps, add scales) to shift the phenomenon under study
- **Documentation loop**: photos and children's dictations posted at the table support recall and revisiting ([Retrieval practice](../principles/retrieval-practice.md) in an early-childhood register)

### Target Learners
- Preschool through early elementary (ages 3–8), where concrete manipulation precedes symbolic understanding [Hands-on activity supports learning when it is paired with sense-making rather than left unstructured.](../claims/hands-on-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]
- Emergent bilinguals and children with limited verbal skills, for whom the materials provide a shared referent for science vocabulary
- Less effective for older learners, who need more complex systems than a sensory table can model

### Target Learning Goals
- Physical science concepts: flow, capacity, conservation, density, separation, erosion
- Science practices: predicting, testing, observing, describing, comparing
- Science vocabulary and discourse: using comparative and causal language ("heavier than," "because it's finer")

### Instructions
1. Select a phenomenon and stock the table with materials and tools that make it observable and variable.
2. Open with a prediction prompt ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)): "What do you think will happen if…?" and record children's guesses.
3. Let children manipulate freely while an adult circulates, asking comparison and causal questions ([Questioning](questioning.md)).
4. Gather briefly to compare results against predictions, using children's own observations as evidence ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)).
5. Change one variable on a subsequent day (add water, add a ramp) and repeat, so children experience the predict–test–revise cycle more than once.

## Examples
- **Tools of the Mind preschool curriculum** — uses structured dramatic and sensory play with intentional teacher framing, consistent with the guided-play evidence base.
- **PEEP and the Big Wide World (WGBH)** — early science units built around water, sand, and ramp explorations with family extension activities ([peepandthebigwideworld.com](https://peepandthebigwideworld.com)).
- A kindergarten "which pours faster?" rice-vs-sand table where children time pours with a sand timer, record results on a simple chart, and revisit the table after water is added to the sand.
