# Counting Collections

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Counting Collections is a routine in which learners — typically in pairs — count a real collection of objects (buttons, cubes, beans) of a size chosen to stretch their current counting ability. Learners decide how to organize the count (ones, groups of 2, 5, or 10), record their count on paper, and then compare strategies in a whole-class discussion. The routine originated in early-number work by Kathy Richardson and was developed extensively within [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) classrooms.

## Design Implications

Counting Collections converts abstract number concepts into [hands-on activity](../principles/active-learning.md) with a concrete referent, letting children build cardinality, one-to-one correspondence, and base-ten structure through their own actions rather than through told procedures. Because learners choose their own grouping strategy, the routine surfaces diverse methods for the teacher to orchestrate into discussion, making it a formative-assessment-rich activity [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Grouping objects into tens offloads working memory during counting [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], which is precisely the mechanism by which the routine builds place-value intuition.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Large quantities of countable objects, organized into bags or containers sized to each pair's current range
- Recording sheets that prompt learners to show *how* they counted, not just the total
- Teacher circulation time to observe and question individual pairs' strategies
- A discussion structure for comparing grouping strategies (e.g., counting by tens vs. twos vs. ones)

#### Constraints
- Collections that are too large relative to a child's current skill produce frustration and inaccurate counts rather than productive struggle [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M] — collection size must be calibrated to the counting range the child can already manage
- If the teacher supplies a single mandated grouping method, the routine loses its diagnostic and discussion value and becomes rote procedure
- Without a recording step, the count remains ephemeral; learners do not connect the physical count to symbolic representation
- Time-intensive: requires regular, repeated sessions (typically weekly) rather than one-off use to produce gains

#### Implementation Variability
- **Size progression:** collections grow from ~20 objects in kindergarten to several hundred by grade 2–3, following a learning-trajectory logic
- **Skip-counting extension:** learners who group by tens can be asked to count the groups, connecting repeated addition and multiplication
- **Add-on rounds:** a second, smaller collection is added to an already-counted one, prompting counting-on rather than recounting
- **Older learners:** the same routine with larger collections supports multiplicative reasoning and division-with-remainder contexts

### Target Learners
- Early elementary learners (K–3) developing cardinality, one-to-one correspondence, and base-ten structure
- Learners who can rote-count but have not yet connected the count sequence to quantity — the routine makes this gap visible and workable
- Multilingual learners: the physical objects and partner talk lower language demands while keeping mathematical reasoning intact

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual number sense: cardinality, one-to-one correspondence, conservation of number
- Place-value understanding: composing and decomposing quantities into tens and ones
- Counting efficiency: moving from counting-by-ones to skip-counting and multiplicative grouping
- Mathematical discourse: explaining and comparing strategies

### Instructions
1. **Select and distribute collections** — pair learners and give each pair a bag sized just beyond their comfortable counting range (formative data from prior sessions guides sizing).
2. **Count and organize** — learners count the collection any way they choose; encourage organizing into groups (tens, fives) rather than a single pile, which enacts [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of the quantity.
3. **Record** — learners write or draw how they counted and the total, connecting physical action to symbolic representation.
4. **Circulate and question** — the teacher observes strategies, asks "How will you keep track?" and "How could you check?", and notes learners for the share-out.
5. **Discuss strategies** — sequence 2–3 pairs' recordings from least to most efficient grouping, asking the class to compare; this [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) step is where individual counting becomes shared conceptual learning.
6. **Adjust next round** — use observed strategies and errors to size and compose the next round's collections.

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction classrooms** (Carpenter et al., *Children's Mathematics*) — teachers use Counting Collections as a core routine for eliciting children's informal counting and grouping strategies and building instruction from them.
- **Kathy Richardson's *Developing Number Concepts*** — the source framework for collection-based counting stations in K–2 classrooms.
- **Seattle/Tacoma district math routines** — several Washington state districts adopted weekly Counting Collections as a K–2 number-sense routine with district-provided collection kits and recording templates.
