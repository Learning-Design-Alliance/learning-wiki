# Illustrate Through Multiple Media

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Illustrating through multiple media means presenting the same core content through complementary channels — diagrams and animations alongside narration or text, simulations alongside static graphics, video alongside worked demonstrations. The strategy is grounded in dual coding: combining verbal and visual representations gives learners two coordinated memory codes rather than one [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. It is not about adding more media, but about selecting media whose affordances match the content — animation for dynamic processes, static diagrams for structure, simulation for manipulation.

## Design Implications

Multimedia works when the combination reduces extraneous load and enables referential connections between words and pictures; it fails when channels compete or when media are decorative [Pairing words and pictures in ways that manage working memory load improves learning.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Mayer's coherence, signaling, and redundancy principles are the operative design rules: exclude seductive but irrelevant detail, cue attention to essential content, and avoid narrating text that is displayed verbatim [Clark, R. C., & Mayer, R. E. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119239086) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content analysis identifying which concepts are structural (best as diagrams), dynamic (best as animation or video), or procedural (best as [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
- Narration or captions that integrate verbal and visual streams rather than duplicating them
- Segmented, learner-paced presentation so learners can process each medium before the next [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Media selected for explanatory fit: dynamic media for dynamic content, static diagrams when learners need to control inspection pace
- Coordination across formats — narration and graphics presented simultaneously and spatially integrated, not split across screens or sequential blocks
- Alignment with [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md): each medium has a defined role in the explanation, with signaling (cues, highlighting) directing attention to the relevant content
- A follow-on activity where learners apply or generate with the concept ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Redundancy harms learning: simultaneous identical narration and on-screen text overloads visual working memory [Clark, R. C., & Mayer, R. E. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119239086) [-S]
- Decorative graphics and seductive details divert attention from essential content and depress retention [-M]
- High element interactivity content can exceed working memory when split across media that require integration (split-attention effect) [-S]
- The strategy does not work by matching a learner's preferred "learning style" — the learning-styles hypothesis is empirically unsupported [Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) [X]
- Redundant media harm learning: on-screen text read aloud verbatim overloads the same channel and reduces retention compared with narration plus graphics [-S]
- Seductive but irrelevant details — decorative images, background music, interesting tangents — divert attention from essential content and depress learning [-S]
- Learners with low prior knowledge benefit from segmented, learner-paced media; continuous fast-paced video can overwhelm them [~M]
- Translating the same text into multiple formats without integration adds processing cost rather than value [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Static diagrams often outperform animation for complex systems because learners control pacing and can revisit [~M]
- Simulations shift the medium from presentation to manipulation; pair with prompts or they become play [~M]
- Video demonstrations can substitute for live modeling when narration makes reasoning explicit ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Static-first: labeled diagrams with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) before any video, letting learners build structure before processing dynamic content
- Simulation-based: interactive environments (e.g., PhET) where learners manipulate variables and observe outcomes, pairing illustration with guided inquiry
- Learner-generated media: students create their own diagrams, videos, or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to explain a concept, converting illustration into a generative activity

### Target Learners
- Novices, who benefit most from coordinated verbal–visual codes that reduce unguided search [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Learners with low prior knowledge in the domain, for whom well-designed multimedia reduces cognitive load [~M]
- Less beneficial for experts, for whom redundant graphics can impose unnecessary integration costs [~M]
- Learners with limited domain vocabulary, for whom visuals carry meaning that dense text cannot ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Less beneficial for high-knowledge learners, for whom detailed graphics can be redundant with their existing mental models [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of structures, systems, and spatial relationships
- Procedural knowledge via narrated video or animated [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)
- Abstraction from varied representations — presenting contrasting cases across media supports generalization [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Conceptual understanding: building integrated verbal and pictorial models of systems and processes
- Transfer: contrasting representations and simulations support abstraction beyond the presented examples

### Instructions
1. Identify the core concept and choose the medium that matches its structure (diagram, animation, simulation, video) rather than defaulting to text-plus-slides
2. Open with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or brief orientation so learners know what to attend to in each medium
3. Present the concept through the primary medium with integrated narration, using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to bridge to prior knowledge
4. Follow with [Application](../elements/application.md) or [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in a different medium than the presentation, forcing learners to translate between representations
5. Use [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) items that require learners to interpret or produce the visual representation, not just restate the narration

## Related Strategies
- Worked examples — the multimedia version is a narrated video demonstration; same example–practice logic
- Case-based teaching — cases are frequently delivered through video and documents across media
- Flipped classroom — pre-class video is the most common multimedia illustration vehicle

## Examples
- **PhET Interactive Simulations** (https://phet.colorado.edu) — research-validated science simulations paired with guided inquiry activities; studies show simulation-plus-guided-activity outperforms simulation alone [~M]
- **Khan Academy** (https://www.khanacademy.org) — narrated visual problem-solving videos followed by practice, enacting the presentation–practice sequence
- **Anatomy & physiology courses using Netter's atlases with narrated labeling tasks** — static visuals with learner-paced annotation rather than passive animation
- **[PhET Interactive Simulations](https://phet.colorado.edu)** — University of Colorado Boulder physics and chemistry simulations combining dynamic visuals with learner-controlled variables, widely validated for conceptual learning in science.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Narrated visual explanations paired with practice exercises; the drawing-plus-voiceover format follows modality and signaling principles.
- **[3Blue1Brown](https://www.3blue1brown.com)** — Mathematics explainer videos using animation to illustrate abstract structures, demonstrating animation's fit for dynamic and geometric content.
- **Anatomy laboratory instruction** — Combining 3D models (e.g., Visible Body), cadaver demonstration, and labeled diagrams so each representation contributes spatial information the others cannot.

## Description
Illustration through multiple media presents a concept in more than one representational format — for example, an animated diagram with narrated explanation, a physical demonstration, and an interactive simulation of the same phenomenon. The goal is not variety for its own sake but complementary representations: each medium carries the part of the explanation it handles best, and together they support the construction of a richer mental model.

## Design Implications

Multimedia learning research shows that well-designed combinations of words and pictures produce better learning than words alone, because learners can integrate verbal and pictorial mental models [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. The benefit is conditional, however: media must be complementary and coordinated, not redundant or decorative. Adding more channels does not help when the additions duplicate content or overload working memory [Cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) principles — segmenting, signaling, and weeding — determine whether multiple media clarify or clutter [~S]. Note that the common rationale of "catering to learning styles" is not supported: matching media to a preferred style does not improve outcomes [X]; what matters is matching media to the *content* (e.g., animation for dynamic processes) and to learners' prior knowledge.

1. Identify the core explanation and the content's demands — what is dynamic, spatial, or procedural — and choose the medium that fits each part, not one medium per "style."
2. Open with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or brief orientation so learners know what to attend to in the media that follow.
3. Present the primary illustration: narration synchronized with integrated graphics or animation, segmented into learner-paced chunks ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) reduces working memory load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]).
4. Add a second, complementary representation — a simulation, physical demonstration, or worked case — that adds information rather than repeating it.
5. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in which learners apply, explain, or generate their own representation of the concept.
6. [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on learners' explanations or products, using the media formats to correct specific misconceptions.
