# Shadowing (Behavior Adjustment)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 review, 1 causal, 1 theoretical), `q3` · 0 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Shadowing assigns an adult to follow a child at close proximity — within arm's reach — throughout high-risk parts of the day, intervening before unwanted behaviors (biting, hitting, elopement) occur rather than reacting afterward. The adult remains as unobtrusive as possible so the child does not feel singled out or have their play disrupted. The goal is twofold: keep other children safe while the child is supported to develop and rehearse alternative, more appropriate behaviors.

## Design Implications

Shadowing is a preventive, high-supervision form of behavior support: it reduces the opportunities for a challenging behavior to be reinforced by its usual outcomes (peer reaction, escape, adult attention) [~M], consistent with the behavioral principle that behavior maintained by consequences changes fastest when those consequences are consistently withheld or replaced [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md). Because the shadowing adult is present at the moment of escalation, they can prompt and model replacement behaviors in context, which is far more effective than delayed correction [~M]. The strategy is intensive and temporary by design — it should fade as the child's self-regulation and replacement behaviors develop, not become a permanent fixture.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A dedicated adult (ideally the same person each day) who can maintain close proximity without becoming intrusive
- Consistency across settings and staff, so the child experiences predictable responses
- A plan for what the adult does at the moment of risk: pre-teaching signals, prompts for replacement behavior, and immediate positive reinforcement of appropriate behavior
- Communication with families so the approach is understood as support, not punishment
- A dedicated adult who can remain with the child for extended periods without displacing other duties
- Knowledge of the child's typical triggers, escalation signs, and behavior patterns, ideally from a functional behavior assessment
- A plan for what the adult does in the moment: prompt replacement behavior, model coping strategies, reinforce positives
- Discretion — the child should not feel singled out or punished, and peers should not perceive the shadowing as surveillance
- A clear exit criterion (e.g., two weeks incident-free) and a fading plan
- A dedicated adult with the training and availability to remain in proximity throughout the target period
- A shared, specific definition of the target behavior and its triggers among all staff, so intervention is consistent across adults and settings
- A plan for what the adult does *instead* of letting the behavior happen: physical blocking, redirection, and narration of the replacement behavior ("Ask me for the toy with your words")
- A data-collection routine (simple incident tally) so the fade-out decision is criterion-based, not impressionistic
- Communication with families about the purpose and non-punitive framing of the support

#### Constraints
- Highly adult-intensive; most classrooms cannot sustain it for more than one or two children at a time [-M]
- If implemented clumsily, the child feels singled out or stigmatized, which can worsen behavior and harm peer standing [-M]
- Constant close supervision can itself function as attention, inadvertently reinforcing the behaviors it precedes if the adult only engages at moments of escalation [~M]
- Prolonged shadowing without a fade plan creates adult dependence and blocks development of self-monitoring [-M]
- Labor-intensive: requires an adult resource most classrooms do not have, limiting scalability [-W]
- Proximity alone suppresses behavior without teaching it; when the shadow is removed, behavior often returns unless replacement skills were explicitly built [-M]
- Can create adult dependence — the child may learn to regulate only when the adult is present, so support must be systematically faded [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- If the child experiences shadowing as punishment or stigmatization, it can damage the adult–child relationship and increase avoidance or escalation [-M]
- Does not by itself address underlying causes (skill deficits, unmet needs, family stressors); it must be embedded in a broader behavior support plan
- Highly resource-intensive: one adult per child is rarely sustainable beyond a short window [-M]
- If the shadowed child perceives the proximity as surveillance or punishment, it can produce stigma, avoidance, or escalation [-M]
- Over-prevention can inadvertently deny the child the natural consequences and social feedback that drive learning, and can reinforce the behavior if shadowing delivers adult attention contingent on escalation [~M]
- Effectiveness drops sharply when implementation is inconsistent across staff or settings; intermittent shadowing leaves unpredictable gaps the child will exploit [-M]
- Not appropriate for behaviors maintained primarily by escape: constant proximity can function as punishment for escape-maintained behavior or intensify the motivation to escape [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full shadowing** — arm's-reach supervision across all activities, used in acute phases (e.g., a biting cycle)
- **Situational shadowing** — proximity only during identified high-risk times (transitions, free play, snack)
- **Fading schedule** — systematically increasing distance and decreasing prompts as incidents decline, moving responsibility to the child ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) logic applied to behavior)
- **Peer-inclusive framing** — the adult positions as a play partner or helper for the whole group so the target child is not visibly the focus
- **Intensity gradient**: full shadowing (arm's reach, all settings) → zone supervision (same room, reduced proximity) → scheduled check-ins
- **Same-adult consistency**: using one familiar adult builds trust and makes prompts more effective; rotating adults weakens the relationship
- **Data-collection emphasis**: some implementations prioritize structured ABC (antecedent–behavior–consequence) observation to inform a functional behavior assessment, with coaching secondary
- **Peer-invisible framing**: the adult can be positioned as a helper or station-based volunteer so peers do not perceive targeting
- **Full shadowing** (arm's reach, all settings) for acute safety risks such as biting
- **Partial or setting-specific shadowing** limited to high-risk periods (transitions, free play), reducing adult cost
- **Peer-inclusive framing**: the adult positions themselves as a helper or activity partner rather than a monitor, reducing stigma

### Target Learners
- Preschool and early-elementary children with persistent aggressive or self-injurious behaviors such as biting or hitting
- Children whose behavior is triggered by identifiable, predictable antecedents (transitions, sharing, overstimulation), where proximity allows early intervention
- Children who have not responded to whole-class behavior supports alone; individualized, intensive support layered on universal systems is the recommended sequence in multi-tiered behavior frameworks [~M]
- Young children (typically preschool through early elementary) whose behavior poses a safety risk to themselves or others, such as biting, hitting, or eloping [~M]
- Children who have not responded to whole-class behavior supports and need individualized intensity
- Less appropriate for older learners, for whom constant adult proximity is stigmatizing and less effective than self-monitoring approaches [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Young children (typically preschool through early elementary) exhibiting frequent aggressive or harmful behaviors in group settings
- Children whose behavior is maintained by attention or access to tangibles, where antecedent intervention and redirection are most effective [~M]
- Less suitable for older learners, for low-frequency behaviors that do not justify the staffing cost, or for children who respond to proximity with shame or escalation [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Replacement behavior acquisition: asking for turns, using words instead of hands, requesting a break
- Self-regulation: recognizing early signs of escalation and using coping strategies
- Social participation: maintaining peer relationships that challenging behavior has damaged
- Behavioral self-regulation: replacing unsafe behavior with taught coping strategies
- Emotional recognition and communication: identifying triggers and asking for help before escalation
- Classroom safety and participation: maintaining access to instruction and peer interaction while the behavior is addressed
- Replacement social skills: requesting turns, using words instead of aggression, tolerating frustration
- Classroom safety and peer relationship protection as enabling conditions for all other learning

### Instructions
1. **Identify antecedents.** Observe for several days to map when, where, and with whom the behavior occurs; note the consequence that appears to maintain it.
2. **Assign a consistent adult.** Brief them on the antecedent map, the replacement behaviors to prompt, and the reinforcement plan.
3. **Shadow at close proximity** during high-risk periods, positioning as unobtrusively as possible (e.g., joining the play area rather than standing over the child).
4. **Interrupt early and redirect.** At the first sign of escalation, prompt the replacement behavior ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) it if needed) and reinforce it immediately when the child uses it.
5. **Reinforce appropriate behavior generously** during calm moments, not only at crisis points, so adult attention is not contingent on misbehavior.
6. **Fade systematically.** Increase distance, reduce prompts, and shift to check-ins ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) as incidents decline; celebrate the fade with the child so independence is framed as success.

## Description
Shadowing is a behavior-support strategy in which a dedicated adult stays within arm's reach of a child who has exhibited harmful behaviors (e.g., biting, hitting), intervening before the behavior occurs rather than reacting afterward. The adult quietly blocks, redirects, and coaches replacement behaviors without singling the child out or framing the presence as punishment. Shadowing is typically deployed after a couple of incidents and faded once the child has been incident-free for an extended period (commonly two weeks), with the ultimate aim of teaching self-regulation and prosocial alternatives.

Because the adult observes the full antecedent–behavior–consequence chain, shadowing also doubles as continuous functional assessment, revealing the triggers and functions (attention, escape, tangibles) that maintenance of the behavior depends on.
