# Model Positive Connections to Literacy

## Description
Teachers deliberately share their own identities as readers and writers — favorite books, personal writing, reading habits, and honest accounts of struggle and enjoyment — to build a classroom culture in which literacy is socially valued. This can involve sharing favorite books aloud, displaying the teacher's own reading list, writing alongside students, and inviting students to contribute their own favorites to a shared classroom collection. Developers can incorporate ways for teachers to share what they read or write with students in both physical and virtual classrooms.

## Design Implications

The strategy works through social modeling: when a credible, valued adult visibly enjoys and invests in literacy, students update their beliefs about whether reading and writing are worthwhile and whether people like them can succeed at them [Social modeling of attitudes and behaviors is a core mechanism of observational learning.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+M]. Modeling enthusiasm raises task value — students see literacy as worth doing — which is a stronger lever on engagement than skill instruction alone [Perceiving value in a task increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. The modeling must be authentic and reciprocal: a display the teacher never refers to again, or a list that admits only "approved" texts, undermines the effect.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine examples of the teacher's own reading and writing life (favorite books, drafts, reading history, honest struggles)
- A visible, persistent space — physical or virtual — to display shared recommendations and invite student contributions
- Regular classroom routines (book talks, read-alouds, teacher write-alouds) that keep the modeling alive rather than one-off
- An inclusive stance that values multiple literacies (graphic novels, audiobooks, fan fiction, bilingual texts), not only canonical print

#### Constraints
- Teacher preferences may not align with students' interests or cultural backgrounds; a list dominated by the teacher's taste can signal whose reading "counts" [-M] — pairing teacher choices with student curation mitigates this
- Performed enthusiasm without authentic engagement reads as inauthentic to adolescents and can backfire [~W]
- Modeling positive attitudes alone does not build skill; without [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and structured reading opportunities, motivation gains do not translate into growth [-M]
- In classrooms with weak student–teacher relationships, the model carries little observational weight [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher book talks and "what I'm reading now" displays
- Teacher-as-writer demonstrations: sharing drafts, revisions, and abandoned pieces to normalize struggle
- Digital implementations: shared Goodreads-style shelves, class blogs, LMS reading walls where students and teacher post recommendations
- Whole-school versions: staff reading walls, "staff picks" shelves, teachers reading silently alongside students during sustained reading time

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, with strongest effects for reluctant or disengaged readers who lack positive reading role models [~M]
- Adolescents developing reading identity, for whom peer and teacher social signals strongly shape self-concept as a reader [Self-efficacy beliefs predict persistence on academic tasks.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners, when the modeled environment values texts in and across multiple languages

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: building positive associations with reading and writing [Perceiving value in a task increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Community building: establishing a shared identity as a community of readers and writers
- Metacognitive and dispositional modeling: showing what real reading and writing lives look like, including struggle and choice

### Instructions
1. Curate genuine artifacts of your own literacy life — a short list of books you love, a piece of your own writing, an account of a book you abandoned.
2. [Demonstrate](../elements/demonstration.md) — present these to students with specific, personal reasons ("I reread this every year because…"), not generic praise; narrate your own reading decisions and struggles like a [think-aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of your literacy life.
3. Create a shared, visible collection (physical shelf, wall, or digital board) and explicitly invite students to add their own favorites, treating all contributions as legitimate.
4. Sustain the routine — revisit the collection regularly, add new items, and hold brief book talks or write-alouds so the modeling remains a living practice rather than a static display.
5. Connect students to texts they contribute toward — use the shared list to guide recommendations, independent reading, and [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).

## Examples
- A middle-school teacher maintains a "Ms. Rivera Is Reading" whiteboard updated weekly with the book she is currently reading and one honest sentence about it, alongside a student-run recommendation shelf; students add sticky-note reviews and borrow from both sources.
- Donalyn Miller's *The Book Whisperer* classroom routine: the teacher reads alongside students during independent reading, gives frequent book commercials, and sets a class goal of reading 40 books each — teacher participation is visible and non-negotiable.
- A high-school English teacher shares her own draft college essay with visible revisions before students write theirs, modeling that writing is iterative and that even adults struggle productively.
- Virtual classrooms: teachers post a "shelf" of current reads in an LMS announcement or class Padlet, with students adding their own titles asynchronously.
