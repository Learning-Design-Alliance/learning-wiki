# Feelings Check-In

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A feelings check-in is a short, low-stakes opening routine in which learners name or rate their current emotional state — via a mood scale, emoji board, color zones, one-word share, or a quick round-robin. It serves two functions simultaneously: developing learners' emotional vocabulary and self-awareness, and giving the instructor formative information about readiness to learn that can shape pacing and tone.

## Design Implications

Check-ins support learning indirectly but measurably: students who feel a sense of belonging and emotional safety engage more and persist longer [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Naming emotions also supports self-regulation — affect labeling reduces amygdala reactivity and improves subsequent self-control [~M]. The practice works best when it is brief, voluntary in depth of disclosure, and visibly acted upon; a check-in whose results are ignored teaches learners that emotional expression is performative [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, quick format (under 3–5 minutes) so the routine does not displace instructional time
- Anonymity or opt-out options (e.g., sticky notes, digital polls, thumbs scale) so disclosure pressure stays low
- Instructor follow-through: acknowledging what was shared and adjusting where feasible
- A shared emotional vocabulary — a feelings wheel or zone chart helps learners who default to "fine"

#### Constraints
- Mandatory public disclosure can increase anxiety and reduce trust, especially for learners with trauma histories or social anxiety [-M]
- Over time the routine can become ritualistic and lose diagnostic value if responses are never acted on [-W]
- In large classes, individual sharing is impractical; aggregate anonymous formats are needed
- Cultural norms around emotional expression vary; a single format will not fit all groups [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Anonymous aggregate:** digital poll (Mentimeter, Google Forms) showing class-wide mood distribution — preserves privacy while giving the instructor a read
- **Zones of Regulation:** learners point to color-coded zones (blue/green/yellow/red), common in K–8 settings
- **One-word whip-around:** fast verbal round in seminars; builds community but requires psychological safety
- **Private journal entry:** check-in goes only to the instructor, trading community-building for disclosure depth
- **Linked to content:** "How confident do you feel about yesterday's material?" merges the emotional check-in with [formative assessment](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)

### Target Learners
- Younger learners and adolescents still building emotional vocabulary and self-regulation [+M]
- Learners in high-stress contexts (exam periods, transitions, new cohorts) where belonging is fragile [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less useful for expert, self-regulated adult learners who may find the routine patronizing unless framed as course climate data [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: emotion identification and self-awareness
- Self-regulated learning: recognizing one's own readiness and arousal state [Self-regulated learning improves achievement.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Classroom climate: establishing belonging and psychological safety as a precondition for discussion-based work

### Instructions
1. **Open with a fixed, brief prompt** at the same point each session — e.g., "Pick the zone that matches you right now" — using a visual support such as a mood meter or emoji board
2. **Collect responses privately or semi-privately** (poll, sticky note, journal) so disclosure is safe; reserve verbal sharing for groups with established trust
3. **Acknowledge the results aloud** — name what you saw and any adjustment you are making ("Lots of people are tired, so we'll start with a review")
4. **Follow up individually** when a response signals distress, using [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) or referral rather than public attention
5. **Close the loop occasionally** with an end-of-session check-out so learners see the routine as meaningful, supporting [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)

## Examples
- **Yale RULER approach** ([https://www.rulerapproach.org](https://www.rulerapproach.org)) — uses the "Mood Meter" (a color/energy-pleasantness grid) for daily classroom check-ins; a whole-school emotional intelligence curriculum with RCT evidence
- **Zones of Regulation** ([https://zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com)) — color-zone check-ins widely used in elementary and special education settings
- **Higher-ed seminar opening** — a one-click Mentimeter poll ("How are you arriving today?") whose aggregate results the instructor references before launching discussion
