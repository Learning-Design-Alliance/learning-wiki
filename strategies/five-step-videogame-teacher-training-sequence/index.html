Five-step experience sequence for training teachers to use videogames as curricular tools¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article describes a five-step instructional sequence: information about the videogame, preliminary survey, experimentation with the game, final survey, and development of didactic proposals. It was used so that trainee teachers could "assess whether this videogame could be used in the classroom" for early education. The sequence combines attitude measurement with hands-on play and lesson-plan design.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to computers or laptops for students to play the videogame
  • A subject syllabus with ICT-related competences to anchor the activity

Constraints¶

  • All five steps were classroom-based except the fifth, which could be done in a semi-virtual modality

Target Learners¶

  • University students in a Primary Education degree

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Technological and didactic competences for integrating videogames into early education curriculum

Claims¶

  • Trainee teachers shift toward agreement that videogames help develop the curriculum crosswise after experiencing one [+W]
  • Students' endorsement of videogames for developing visual memory and retentiveness rose sharply after the experience [+W]
  • After the experience, 100% of students fully agreed the videogame develops heuristic (trial-and-error) thinking [+W]
  • Students remained largely disagreeing that the videogame supports self-regulation of learning (continuous assessment) [~W]
  • A third of Spanish educators (36.4%) used videogames as didactic tools in 2011-2012, with younger teachers more willing [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Implement classroom videogame interventions with teacher training, curriculum alignment, extended schedules, and administrative support
