Blackout Conversation¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 4 causal), q3–q4 · 5 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Students transform conversational text selections into poetry by blacking out sections to reveal only key terms and phrases, focusing on the meaning, nuances, and silences of each character's dialogue. Working in pairs, students read the dialogue aloud, then independently examine their assigned character's subtext — what they are really saying, hiding, feeling, or withholding. After blacking out the "background noise," partners reread the remaining words together and highlight the essence of the conversation. The strategy adapts blackout poetry (popularized by Austin Kleon's Newspaper Blackout) to dramatic dialogue as a vehicle for social-emotional learning.

Design Implications¶

Blackout Conversation combines constrained text-marking with perspective-taking: the physical constraint of keeping only a small fraction of words forces selective attention to what a character actually says versus what they mean. Restructuring source text into a new, self-authored artifact supports deeper processing than passive reading [~M], and fiction-based perspective-taking activities show modest but reliable effects on empathy and intergroup attitudes Fiction reading improves empathy. [+M]. The paired read-aloud structure adds a dialogic component that supports comprehension and expressive language Dialogic reading improves expressive language. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Text selections featuring dominant character dialogue (drama scripts, novel chapters in dialogue form, interview transcripts)
  • Blackout markers or pens; copies of the text each student can mark
  • Facilitation of paired read-alouds followed by independent subtext analysis
  • Time for partners to compare their revealed "poems" and discuss divergent interpretations

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on text selection: dialogue with flat or purely expository language gives students nothing meaningful to reveal [-W]
  • Students who interpret character emotion superficially (or resist speculating about subtext) produce blackouts that merely summarize rather than interpret [-W]
  • The artistic/poetic framing can shift attention toward aesthetics and away from social analysis; teachers must keep discussion of subtext central [~W]
  • Less suitable for emergent readers still decoding text, for whom the marking task adds extraneous load [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reverse variant: students highlight (rather than black out) the subtext words, then explain what the blacked-out "silences" imply
  • Whole-class variant: two students black out opposing characters' lines, then perform the reduced poems as a distilled dialogue
  • Extension: students write the character's unsaid reply in the margins, converting inference into production

Target Learners¶

  • Middle and high school students, who have the inference skills needed to read subtext
  • Students who benefit from concrete, kinesthetic engagement with text before abstract discussion Graphic organizers support novice comprehension. [+M]
  • Learners developing social awareness who need structured, low-stakes practice interpreting others' intentions Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, empathy, and relationship skills
  • Literary analysis: distinguishing dialogue from subtext, inferring character motivation
  • Close reading: selective attention to word choice and what is withheld

Instructions¶

  1. Assign a dialogue-rich text excerpt and pair students, assigning each a character (Assigned Readings)
  2. Partners read the conversation aloud twice, switching roles, attending to tone and pauses (Act It Out)
  3. Each student independently rereads their character's lines and annotates the subtext — what is really being said, hidden, or withheld (Annotating)
  4. Students black out all "background noise," leaving only the words that carry the character's essential meaning
  5. Partners reread their reduced poems to each other and highlight where their interpretations converge or diverge (Class Discussion)
  6. Debrief as a group: what did the silences reveal about each character's needs, habits, and relationship?

Related Strategies¶

  • Annotating — the broader text-marking practice this strategy constrains and intensifies
  • Acting-Role-Play — embodied alternative for the same perspective-taking goal
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — pre-reading discussion of similar real conversations primes subtext inference

Related Elements¶

  • Annotating — the marking mechanism at the core of the blackout task
  • Act It Out — the read-aloud phase that gives dialogue its performative meaning
  • Check-In — a debrief structure for surfacing emotional interpretations

Tools¶

  • Blackout markers, grease pencils, or digital equivalents (PDF annotation tools with a black highlighter)
  • Printed excerpts from dialogue-heavy texts such as Sharon Draper's Tears of a Tiger

Examples¶

  • Using the dialogue-form chapters of Sharon Draper's Tears of a Tiger (Simon & Schuster, 1994): students black out a conversation between Andy and a friend, revealing what each character is actually asking for beneath the banter.
  • Drama classes blacking out a scene from a play script, then performing the reduced "poem" version to compare against the full scene.
