# Activating Prior Knowledge

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Activating prior knowledge involves prompting learners to recall and share what they already know about a topic before introducing new information. The prompt may be a question, a brief discussion, a brainstorm, a prediction task, or a structured organizer such as a KWL chart. The goal is to bring relevant existing schemas into working memory so new material can be connected to — rather than stored alongside — what the learner already knows.

## Design Implications

Activation works because meaningful learning is associative: new information is retained and comprehended better when it is anchored to existing knowledge structures [Recall of new material improves when relevant prior knowledge is activated first.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. It also serves a diagnostic function — surfacing misconceptions and gaps before instruction begins, so the teacher can adjust. The activation must be *relevant* to the target content; generic warm-ups that do not touch the incoming schema produce engagement without learning benefit [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prompt that targets knowledge genuinely prerequisite or relevant to the new material
- Brief, time-boxed activity (typically 3–10 minutes) so activation does not displace instruction
- A mechanism for the instructor to observe what learners surface (discussion, written response, poll) and respond to misconceptions
- Follow-through: explicit linkage during instruction back to what was activated ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) support this bridging)

#### Constraints
- Activating incorrect or naive conceptions without subsequently addressing them can entrench misconceptions [~M] — activation surfaces errors but does not correct them; pair with [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) or refutation when misconceptions are likely
- If learners have little or no relevant prior knowledge, activation prompts waste time and may frustrate; build background first instead [~S]
- Overly long or open-ended brainstorming can raise extraneous load and dilute focus on the learning goal [Cognitive load management principles favor brief, focused activation.](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Oral**: quick turn-and-talk or whole-class brainstorm before a lesson
- **Written/structured**: KWL charts, anticipation guides, pre-questions embedded in text
- **Predictive**: asking learners to predict an outcome before demonstration or reading — predictions create readiness to resolve uncertainty
- **Analogical**: prompting recall of a familiar parallel domain ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), [Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md))
- **Digital**: entry polls or discussion-board pre-posts in online courses

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge benefit most — enough existing schema to activate, enough gap to need the bridge [Recall of new material improves when relevant prior knowledge is activated first.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Novices with minimal background need background *building* rather than activation; activation alone will not help them [~S]
- Learners with strong expertise may find activation redundant and even interfering, consistent with the expertise-reversal pattern [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of new expository or narrative text
- Conceptual change in science and mathematics, where intuitive conceptions must be surfaced first
- Retention and recall of new material connected to existing schemas
- Metacognitive awareness of what one does and does not yet know

### Instructions
1. Identify the prerequisite knowledge the new material depends on.
2. Choose a brief prompt (question, prediction, anticipation guide) that targets that knowledge.
3. Have learners respond individually or in pairs before whole-group sharing, so all learners retrieve rather than echoing others ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. Note misconceptions and gaps; adjust the upcoming explanation accordingly.
5. During instruction, explicitly connect new content back to the activated ideas ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) provide the structural bridge).
6. Close the loop by revisiting initial responses after instruction so learners see how their understanding changed.

## Examples
- **Anticipation guides in science class**: students agree/disagree with statements like "heavier objects fall faster" before a unit on gravity; the teacher revisits the guide after instruction to confront misconceptions.
- **KWL charts in elementary reading**: learners list what they *Know* and *Want* to know before reading, then record what they *Learned*.
- **Pre-questions in online courses**: platforms such as [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) open lessons with a warm-up question that retrieves prerequisite skills before new instruction.
- **Case prediction in medical education**: students predict a diagnosis from a presenting complaint before the full case is revealed, activating relevant clinical schemas.
