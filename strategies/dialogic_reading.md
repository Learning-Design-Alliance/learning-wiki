# Dialogic Reading

## Description
Dialogic reading is an interactive reading technique where the adult helps the child become the teller of the story instead of passively listening. The adult prompts the child to say something about the book, evaluates the response, expands the response by rephrasing and adding information, and repeats the prompt to ensure learning. This is known as the PEER sequence (Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat). The adult uses prompts from the CROWD mnemonic: Completion prompts (fill-in-the-blank), Recall prompts (questions about past events in the book), Open-ended prompts (focus on pictures), Wh- prompts (what, where, when, why, how questions), and Distancing prompts (relating the book to outside experiences). Dialogic reading is suitable for children's books with rich, detailed pictures, and the process should be enjoyable and child-led.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A picture book, an adult facilitator, and a willingness to engage in interactive conversation. The adult needs to be familiar with the PEER sequence and CROWD prompts.
#### Constraints
- May require training for adults unfamiliar with the technique. Distancing and recall prompts may be challenging for younger children. Success depends on keeping the interaction enjoyable and avoiding pressure.

### Target Learners
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Preschool-aged children, particularly those with limited experience with books and vocabulary.

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Enhance language development, improve vocabulary, develop story comprehension, increase expressive fluency, foster narrative skills

### Affordances
- Increases active engagement with books, supports language and literacy development, enhances verbal fluency, builds connections between books and the real world.

### Personalization
- Prompts can be adjusted based on the child's age and language abilities. The technique is adaptable to various book types and can be tailored to the child's interests.

## Assessment Evidence
- Observe the child's language development, vocabulary acquisition, story comprehension, and expressive fluency. Track the child's responses to different types of prompts and their ability to relate the book to real-world experiences.

## Impact
- Research indicates that children who are read to dialogically demonstrate significant gains in language development compared to those read to traditionally. This approach has shown positive effects across diverse geographic locations and socioeconomic backgrounds.

## Examples
- During a book reading with a picture of a fire engine, the adult points to the fire truck and asks, "What is this?" (Prompt). The child says, "Truck." The adult responds, "That’s right (Evaluation); it’s a red fire truck (Expansion); can you say fire truck?" (Repetition).
