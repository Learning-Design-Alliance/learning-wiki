# Vocabulary Pre-Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Vocabulary pre-teaching identifies a small number of words that are critical to understanding an upcoming text or task and teaches them directly before reading — through student-friendly definitions, examples, contexts, and quick checks. The goal is not exhaustive word study but removing comprehension bottlenecks so that working memory during reading is spent on meaning-making rather than decoding unknown words.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching works because unknown word meanings consume working memory during reading, degrading comprehension of the surrounding text [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Effects are strongest when the pre-taught words are genuinely load-bearing for the passage and when instruction goes beyond a dictionary definition to include multiple contexts and active engagement [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Pre-teaching also functions as [Activation](../principles/activation.md): linking new words to known concepts before reading primes relevant background knowledge [Activating prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Text analysis to identify which words actually block comprehension — typically 5–8 words, not 20
- Student-friendly, conversational definitions rather than dictionary phrasing ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Multiple encounters: example sentences, nonlinguistic representation, or a quick use-it-yourself prompt
- Connection to words and concepts learners already know ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md))

#### Constraints
- Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads learners; comprehension benefits shrink as word lists grow [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [-M]
- Isolated definition-giving without contextual engagement produces word-list knowledge that does not transfer to reading comprehension [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [-S]
- Ineffective when the pre-taught words are marginal to the passage's main ideas — the time cost yields no comprehension gain
- Does not substitute for wide reading; vocabulary breadth is built primarily through volume of independent reading over time [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full pre-teaching**: definition, example, context, and learner-generated sentence before reading — for words central to the text's argument
- **Light-touch preview**: brief pronunciation and gloss only — for words needed for surface fluency but inferable in context
- **During-reading substitution**: teaching words at the moment they first appear, when the surrounding context supplies meaning
- **Morphological pre-teaching**: teaching a root or affix (e.g., *bio-*, *-ology*) that unlocks a word family rather than a single word

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers and English learners, who encounter proportionally more unknown words and benefit most from removing bottlenecks [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Novices in a discipline facing technical terminology (e.g., *osmosis*, *tariff*, *metaphor*) before first exposure to domain texts
- Less necessary for proficient readers with strong background knowledge, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: ensuring the text itself is understandable on first pass
- Word knowledge depth: building initial meanings that later encounters consolidate
- Disciplinary literacy: acquiring the technical lexicon of a field before reading in it

### Instructions
1. Analyze the text and select 5–8 words that are (a) unfamiliar, (b) critical to comprehension, and (c) likely to recur — following the three-tier logic of [Bringing Words to Life](https://www.guilford.com/books/Bringing-Words-to-Life/Beck-McKeown-Kucan/9781462508167).
2. Write a student-friendly definition in plain, conversational language ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
3. Present each word with an example and a non-example, connecting to known concepts ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Require brief active processing: learners answer a quick "Would you…?" question or use the word in their own sentence ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) works for younger learners).
5. During reading, revisit the words at the point they appear ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) the text helps).
6. After reading, schedule spaced review so word knowledge consolidates rather than fading.

## Examples
- **Beck & McKeown's Text Talk** — a published instructional routine in which teachers pre-teach a handful of Tier Two words from a read-aloud, then revisit them in rich discussion afterward.
- **Word Generation** (SERP Institute, https://www.wordgeneration.org) — a middle-school program built around a weekly set of high-utility academic words introduced before discussion and writing tasks.
- **Content-area example**: before a science passage on ecosystems, a teacher pre-teaches *producer*, *consumer*, and *decomposer* with a quick diagram and a "which one are you at lunch?" prompt, then has students apply the terms while reading.
