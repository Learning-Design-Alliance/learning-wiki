Strategy Identification & Reflection¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 14 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 3 review, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 4 of 14 report an effect size

Description¶

Strategy identification & reflection asks learners to explicitly name the cognitive and metacognitive strategies they use when working through a challenge — "I reread the problem," "I drew a diagram," "I broke it into parts" — and then reflect on which strategies helped, which did not, and when each applies. The instructor collects, labels, and shares these strategies so the class builds a common vocabulary of learning processes that can be reused across tasks. It is typically carried out through brief debrief prompts after problem-solving, strategy walls or anchor charts, and structured reflection journals.

Design Implications¶

Naming strategies converts tacit, in-the-moment decisions into explicit, transferable knowledge, which is the core move of metacognitive instruction [Schraw, 1998] [+M]. Reflection after task completion is most effective when it targets the process rather than the outcome, consistent with evidence that process-level feedback drives the largest gains Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. Because strategy reflection adds a second layer of processing on top of the primary task, it must be brief and well-timed to avoid overloading working memory Cognitive Load Management.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A challenging but solvable task that actually requires strategy selection — reflection on a trivial task yields nothing to name
  • A visible, growing record of named strategies (anchor chart, shared document, strategy wall) so the vocabulary accumulates rather than evaporating
  • Structured prompts that ask what was done, why, and when else it would work (Articulation)
  • Instructor modeling of strategy naming during the task itself (Think-Aloud)

Constraints¶

  • Reflection prompts added on top of a high-load task can disrupt the primary learning itself; debrief after completion, not during Cognitive Load Management [-M]
  • Generic prompts ("reflect on your learning") produce generic, low-value responses; without specific strategy vocabulary to draw on, learners default to effort attributions ("I tried hard") [~M]
  • Less effective when learners lack the prior knowledge to recognize that they used a strategy at all — novices often cannot identify their own procedures without instructor modeling first [-W]
  • If the same strategy is always the right answer for a task type, reflection becomes ritual rather than decision-making [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Post-task debrief: 2–3 minute whole-class naming session after problem solving ("What did you try first? What did you do when you got stuck?")
  • Strategy wall / anchor chart: named strategies accumulate visibly and are referenced by name in later lessons
  • Reflection journals: individual written entries using a fixed prompt frame (strategy → why → when else)
  • Peer interviews: learners interview a partner about their approach, practicing the vocabulary receptively before producing it (Learning by teaching improves tutor learning. [+M])
  • Contingent prompting: instructor names strategies for novices and gradually hands the naming responsibility to learners Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and struggling learners, who often apply effective strategies implicitly but cannot retrieve or transfer them without naming Mnemonic instruction improves recall for students with disabilities. [+M]
  • Middle-grade and older learners who can describe their own thinking; younger learners need instructor naming plus simplified vocabulary
  • High-achievers with already-automated strategies may find structured reflection redundant Expertise reversal: guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive knowledge: knowing what strategies exist and when to deploy them [Schraw, 1998] [+M]
  • Transfer: building a reusable strategy repertoire that applies across task types
  • Self-regulated learning: the monitoring-and-evaluation phase of self-regulated study Self-regulated learning
  • Academic language: a precise shared vocabulary for discussing learning processes

Instructions¶

  1. Choose a strategy-rich task — a problem or challenge with multiple viable approaches, so naming strategies is meaningful (Case Studies or open problems work well).
  2. Model the naming — during the task, the instructor verbalizes their own strategies aloud (Think-Aloud), giving learners the vocabulary before they are asked to produce it.
  3. Debrief immediately after the task — ask learners to name one strategy they used and why, using prompts like "What did you do when you got stuck?" (Articulation).
  4. Record and label — add named strategies to a shared strategy wall or chart, refining vague descriptions into precise, reusable labels (Advance Organizers can later organize the collection).
  5. Reference by name in future tasks — prompt learners to choose from the accumulated repertoire before and during new tasks, closing the loop from identification to deliberate deployment (Activation).
  6. Revisit and evaluate — periodically ask which strategies earned their place and which apply only in narrow situations, deepening conditional knowledge.

Related Strategies¶

  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the instructor-side counterpart: modeling strategy use aloud so learners have names to adopt
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — strategy reflection is itself a form of activation, surfacing prior procedures before new learning
  • Learning-by-Teaching — explaining strategies to peers strengthens the explainer's own conditional knowledge
  • Give students explicit prompts to think about group processes during PBL

Examples¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) math classrooms — after children solve word problems, teachers publicly name and chart the strategies children used (direct modeling, counting on, derived facts), building a shared repertoire the class references in later problems (CGI for Math).
  • Reading strategy anchor charts in workshop-model classrooms — teachers at TCRWP-affiliated schools co-construct charts naming comprehension strategies ("back up and reread," "stop and jot") that students are then prompted to deploy by name during independent reading.
  • Programming courses using post-solution debriefs — after debugging exercises, students write a one-line "what I tried" entry to a shared log, giving the class a named toolkit of debugging strategies (binary search for the bug, rubber-duck explanation, minimal reproduction).
