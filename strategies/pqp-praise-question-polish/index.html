PQP (Praise-Question-Polish)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 associational), q2–q4 · 4 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

PQP is a structured peer-feedback protocol in which responders react to a peer's draft through three prompts: Praise (identify specific praiseworthy elements with examples), Question (raise genuine questions the draft leaves unanswered), and Polish (offer concrete suggestions for improvement). Typically, group members read drafts aloud while peers follow along with copies, then write comments on a PQP form. The form gives structure, objectivity, and positivity to peer review, converting the vague instruction "give feedback" into three specific, teachable moves.

Design Implications¶

PQP works because it scaffolds the peer-feedback genre: unstructured peer review often produces vague praise ("I liked it") or unactionable criticism, while the three-prompt form elicits specific, evidence-anchored comments. Structured peer feedback is associated with improved writing quality, particularly when feedback includes concrete revision suggestions Peer feedback improves writing performance, especially when comments are specific and actionable. [+M]. Receiving concrete suggestions increases the likelihood that writers actually revise — and revising in response to feedback, not merely receiving it, drives gains Feedback improves learning primarily when learners use it to revise. [+M]. Giving feedback also benefits the giver: evaluating a peer's work against criteria deepens the reviewer's own understanding of quality Peer assessment can benefit the assessor as much as the assessed. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A PQP form for each responder, with prompts and space for specific examples
  • Copies of drafts for all group members (or shared digital documents)
  • Structured group time and a clear protocol for reading and responding
  • Task-specific criteria so "praise" and "polish" comments connect to the qualities being taught, not general impressions

Constraints¶

  • Without prior training and modeling, learners default to vague or purely social comments; the protocol must be taught, not just distributed [-M]
  • Novice responders may lack the disciplinary knowledge to identify genuine weaknesses, producing confidently wrong "polish" suggestions Peer feedback accuracy depends on reviewer expertise relative to the task. [~M]
  • The praise-first structure can bias responders toward softening or omitting substantive criticism; writers may over-weight praise and under-revise
  • Groups must be small enough (typically 3–4) that every draft receives attention; larger groups dilute response quality

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written vs. oral: comments written on forms, delivered orally in Peer Discussion, or both
  • Digital adaptation: shared documents with comment threads replacing paper forms (e.g., Google Docs peer review)
  • Sentence stems: stems ("A strength I noticed was… because…", "I wondered why…", "One thing to try is…") support learners new to critique
  • Criterion-anchored forms: the form lists the rubric criteria for the task, so each P, Q, and P comment must reference a criterion
  • Author role variation: authors may listen silently, take notes, or respond only to questions — restricting author defense keeps focus on the feedback

Target Learners¶

  • Writers in grades 6–12 and higher education working on drafts of extended pieces
  • Learners developing evaluative judgment — the capacity to judge quality in their own and others' work Peer assessment can benefit the assessor as much as the assessed. [+M]
  • Reluctant reviewers who need a low-stakes, positively framed entry point into critique
  • Less suitable when learners have no draft to respond to (feedback on ideas not yet externalized) or when the work is a one-shot product with no revision opportunity

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Providing specific, constructive, criterion-referenced feedback
  • Self-editing and revision: internalizing the P-Q-P lens for evaluating one's own drafts
  • Listening and academic discourse skills: attending closely to a peer's text and articulating responses
  • Building a classroom culture of iterative improvement and trust around critique

Instructions¶

  1. Prepare the form and criteria. Distribute a PQP form listing the task's quality criteria; model each prompt with a sample text so learners see what "specific" looks like.
  2. Share drafts. Group members (3–4) exchange or follow along with copies as the author reads the draft aloud.
  3. Respond in writing. Each responder completes the form: Praise with quoted examples, Questions about unclear or underdeveloped parts, and Polish suggestions phrased as actionable revisions.
  4. Discuss. Use Peer Discussion to share responses; the author listens, asks clarifying questions, and records revision intentions.
  5. Revise. The author revises the draft using the feedback, then optionally submits the PQP forms alongside the revision so the teacher can see how feedback was used.
  6. Provide feedback on the feedback. Briefly review PQP forms and coach responders on specificity — the reviewing skill itself improves with Feedback.

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer Review — PQP is a scaffolded protocol within the broader practice of peer review
  • Reciprocal Peer Feedback — structures two-way exchange; PQP supplies the comment format
  • Writing Conferences — teacher-led one-to-one counterpart that can model the same P-Q-P moves
  • Evaluate student contributions through praising, encouraging, quoting students' words, and gestures

Examples¶

  • ReadWriteThink — "Peer Review: Narrative" (readwritethink.org) — an IRA/NCTE lesson plan in which students exchange narrative drafts and use a PQP form to respond before revising.
  • Middle school writing workshop: students in writing circles read personal narratives aloud, complete PQP forms, then revise; the teacher collects forms to assess both feedback quality and revision decisions.
  • Higher-ed first-year composition: Google Docs drafts with margin comments labeled P/Q/P, so the instructor can scan feedback specificity at a glance.
