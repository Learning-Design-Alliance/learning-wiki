Repairing Harm¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 5 studies (3 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Repairing harm is a restorative practice in which, instead of receiving a conventional punishment, a student who has misbehaved is tasked with figuring out how to repair the damage caused by their behavior — and then actually carrying out that repair with everyone who was impacted. The process typically moves through three phases: meeting with a facilitator to identify the harm and all stakeholders, considering how the behavior affected others (developing empathy), and creating and executing a repair plan with each stakeholder, which may range from a facilitated apology to concrete restitution such as cleaning, service, or renegotiated agreements. It functions as an alternative to exclusionary or purely punitive discipline.

Design Implications¶

Repairing harm converts a disciplinary incident into a learning event: the student must take the perspective of those affected, which targets empathy and moral reasoning rather than mere compliance [~W]. Because the student generates the repair plan rather than receiving an imposed consequence, the process supports autonomy and ownership rather than external control, consistent with self-determination research on the motivational costs of purely controlling responses Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. School-wide restorative implementations have been associated with improved climate and reduced suspensions, but effects depend heavily on fidelity of implementation and whole-staff buy-in rather than isolated use [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A trained facilitator (administrator, counselor, or teacher) with time for a private conference, and a space where the conversation can occur without an audience
  • Willingness from the student to engage in self-reflection and acknowledge their role; facilitation skills for handling denial or defensiveness
  • Stakeholders (the harmed parties) who are willing to describe impact and receive the repair — the process is hollow if the affected parties are never consulted
  • School-level alignment: if the same incident would otherwise trigger a standard punitive consequence, staff must agree to route it through the restorative process

Constraints¶

  • Requires substantially more adult time than issuing a detention or suspension; under-resourced implementations degrade into a scripted apology exercise with no empathy work [-W]
  • Ineffective — and potentially re-traumatizing — when the student denies the behavior or is coerced into performative remorse; facilitators must not extract apologies before the student genuinely understands the impact [-W]
  • Poor fit as a response to harm with no identifiable victim or repairable damage; forcing a "repair" framing onto such incidents confuses the process
  • School-wide adoption without staff training can increase perceived inconsistency in discipline and erode trust among both students and teachers [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Informal repair conversation — a teacher-level conference after a classroom incident, with a simple repair plan
  • Formal restorative conference — a structured meeting including the harmed parties, following a script of "What happened? Who was affected? What is needed to make things right?"
  • Restorative circles — community-based formats where the whole class or group discusses impact and collectively agrees on repair
  • Restitution-focused variants — repair expressed primarily through concrete action (cleaning, service, restoring property) rather than dialogue

Target Learners¶

  • Students whose misbehavior has identifiable, articulable victims or community impact — the empathy work depends on concrete stakeholders [~W]
  • Students with developing perspective-taking capacity; younger students may need the facilitator to scaffold the "how did they feel?" reasoning explicitly
  • Students motivated by relationships and belonging; for students with weak school attachment, the relational repair may carry less weight Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Empathy and perspective-taking: understanding the ripple effects of one's actions on others
  • Moral responsibility: internalizing accountability rather than complying to avoid punishment
  • Conflict-resolution and communication skills: articulating impact, apologizing, and negotiating repair

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the harm. Meet privately with the student; ask what happened and guide them to name who was affected and how, rather than telling them (Check-ins can open the conversation non-defensively).
  2. Surface stakeholder perspectives. Have the student hear directly from those impacted — through conversation, a restorative circle, or facilitated dialogue (Class Discussion formats adapt to group settings).
  3. Co-construct the repair plan. The student proposes how to make things right for each stakeholder; the facilitator ensures the plan is proportionate, concrete, and student-generated, not imposed.
  4. Carry out and verify the repair. The student executes the plan with each stakeholder; the facilitator follows up to confirm completion and closure (Coaching supports students who struggle to follow through).
  5. Reflect and reintegrate. A brief closing conversation consolidates what the student learned and affirms their restored standing in the community.

Related Strategies¶

  • Restorative circles and community conferences — the group formats within which repairing harm most often occurs
  • Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS) — repairing harm is often layered onto tiered behavior frameworks as a response-to-incident alternative
  • Teacher Effectiveness Training / Conflict Resolution — a complementary process for the persistent conflicts or misbehavior that repairing harm alone may not fully resolve
  • Natural and Logical Consequences — the principle distinguishing repair-focused consequences like this one from punishment

Related Elements¶

  • Check-ins — low-stakes relational routines that build the trust repairing harm depends on
  • Coaching — the facilitation stance: guiding the student to their own repair plan rather than dictating one
  • Class Discussion — the dialogue structure for surfacing impact with multiple stakeholders

Examples¶

  • A student throws food across the cafeteria, spilling milk over tables and floor. Instead of detention, they meet with the dean, hear from the custodian about the added work, and agree to clean the tables, mop the floor, and assist the custodian the following week.
  • A student posts a mocking message about a classmate. The repair process includes a facilitated meeting where the classmate describes the impact, followed by the student posting a correction and co-writing (with the harmed student) agreements for future online conduct.
  • Denver Public Schools — one of the earliest large districts to adopt restorative justice district-wide, using repair-of-harm conferences as an alternative to suspension.
  • Oakland Unified School District — implemented restorative justice including harm-repair conferences; associated with substantial reductions in suspensions in evaluated cohorts.
