# Encouraging Self Advocacy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self advocacy is the ability to understand one's own learning needs, strengths, and rights, and to communicate them effectively — requesting accommodations, asking clarifying questions, negotiating deadlines, or seeking help strategically. Encouraging it as a design strategy means deliberately teaching and creating opportunities for learners to identify what they need and voice it, rather than having all supports arranged invisibly for them. It is typically taught through explicit scripts, role-play, and graduated real-world practice.

## Design Implications

Self advocacy instruction works because it converts help-seeking from an admission of failure into a learnable, rehearsed skill, which raises both use of supports and self-efficacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Interventions that teach goal setting, self-monitoring, and communication scripts reliably increase goal attainment for learners with disabilities [Self-determination interventions improve goal attainment.](https://doi.org/10.1177/001440291207800202) [+S]. Crucially, the strategy only works when the environment actually responds to advocacy — learners who request supports and are ignored learn that speaking up is futile.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Learners have (or are helped to develop) an accurate model of their own strengths, needs, and rights — often via [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) conversations or self-assessment
- Explicit instruction in communication: scripts, sentence frames, and role-play of requesting help or negotiating support ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md))
- Responsive adults and systems: requests must be met or negotiated, not dismissed
- Opportunities for authentic practice with real stakes (e.g., leading their own IEP meeting, emailing an instructor), with [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to reflect on outcomes

#### Constraints
- Fails in unresponsive environments: advocacy without institutional follow-through teaches learned helplessness [-M]
- Requires prior self-knowledge; learners who cannot yet name their needs need [Self-Monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) support first [~M]
- Can be counterproductive if framed as replacing adult support entirely — premature withdrawal of scaffolds before learners can advocate effectively reduces performance [-M]
- Power asymmetries (young learners, stigmatized disabilities) raise the social cost of speaking up; scripts alone do not remove this [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Scripted programs** (e.g., the Self-Advocacy Strategy) teach a memorized sequence: state needs, propose solutions, evaluate outcomes
- **Student-led IEP/504 meetings** embed advocacy in authentic high-stakes contexts
- **Gradual release**: instructor models a request → learner role-plays → learner makes a real request with debrief
- **Digital self-advocacy**: learners select their own [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) in adaptive platforms rather than having them assigned

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults with disabilities transitioning to more independent settings (secondary → postsecondary/work) [+S]
- Learners with weak help-seeking or self-regulation skills generally [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less appropriate as a substitute for direct support when learners lack the metacognitive foundation to know what to ask for [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-determination and autonomy: owning decisions about one's learning [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Metacognitive skill: accurate self-assessment of needs and progress
- Transferable communication and negotiation skills

### Instructions
1. **Build self-knowledge.** Guide learners through structured self-assessment of strengths, challenges, and what has helped before; use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to make this ongoing rather than one-off.
2. **Teach the vocabulary of needs.** Name accommodations, rights, and support options explicitly ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)) so learners have language for what they need.
3. **Model a request.** Demonstrate a full advocacy exchange — describing the difficulty, proposing a specific support, and following up — using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) to narrate the reasoning.
4. **Rehearse.** Use role-play ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)) with scripts before real attempts; provide feedback at the process level [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. **Practice authentically and debrief.** Learners make a real request (to a teacher, employer, or platform), then reflect on what worked and what to adjust next time.

## Examples
- **The Self-Advocacy Strategy** (Van Reusen, Bos, Schumaker, & Deshler, University of Kansas) — a scripted mnemonic-based program in which students with disabilities learn to lead their own IEP meetings; widely replicated in special education research.
- **Student-led IEP meetings** — documented practice in which students present their strengths, needs, and accommodation requests to the full team, with preparation sessions beforehand.
- **Postsecondary disability services offices** (e.g., at most US universities) — require students to self-identify and request accommodations each term, making advocacy a gatekeeper skill; effective programs pair this with explicit coaching in how to make the request.
