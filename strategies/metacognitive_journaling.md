# Metacognitive Journaling

## Description
Metacognitive journaling involves writing about one's own thinking, including tendencies, changes in thinking over time, biases, or blind spots. This can be driven by instructor prompts or self-initiation. Using approaches like the 'Muddiest point reflection' is also useful, helping learners recognize areas where their understanding is unclear, thereby positioning them to direct their learning to expand their knowledge.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires a journal (physical or digital), writing utensils, and dedicated time for journaling. Instructors or facilitators need to provide prompts and feedback to guide reflection.
#### Constraints
- Requires learners to be willing to engage in honest self-reflection. May be challenging for learners who are not accustomed to thinking about their own thinking.

### Target Learning Goals

- Promote metacognition, enhance self-awareness, improve understanding of thinking processes, develop the ability to direct one's own learning

### Affordances
- Enhances self-awareness of one's own thinking processes, biases, and tendencies. Supports the development of self-directed learning skills. Helps learners identify areas where their understanding is unclear.

### Personalization
- Provide a variety of prompts to guide reflection on different aspects of thinking. Allow learners to focus on areas of thinking that are most relevant to their personal and professional goals.

## Impact
- Supports the development of metacognitive skills, which are essential for lifelong learning. Helps learners become more aware of their own thinking and better able to direct their own learning.

## Examples
- A learner might use a metacognitive journal to reflect on their tendencies to procrastinate, analyze the reasons behind their procrastination, and develop strategies to overcome it. They might also use the journal to identify biases that influence their decision-making and develop strategies to mitigate those biases.
