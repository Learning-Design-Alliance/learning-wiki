# Use Books to Teach Emotions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Stories are used as examples of handling emotions in a responsible way. Fiction provides a safe, inviting entry point for conversations about the range of feelings and how to handle each. By reading and discussing how characters' emotions connect with their actions, students begin to articulate how emotions drive behavior — and how behavior can be regulated. The strategy works because narrative offers *vicarious* emotional experience: readers simulate characters' mental states without the social risk of exposing their own [~M].

## Design Implications

Fiction reading is associated with improved emotion understanding and empathy, likely because narratives require readers to infer characters' intentions and feelings [Mar & Oatley's fiction-and-empathy work](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2008.00089.x) [~M]. The effect depends on discussion: simply reading a story about anger does little; guided talk that names the emotion, traces its cause, and generates alternative responses is what builds emotional literacy. Social-emotional learning programs that embed such structured discussion produce measurable gains in emotional regulation and social behavior [Durlak et al. SEL meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Carefully selected books whose characters experience emotions at a level students can recognize and discuss
- Time for reading *and* structured discussion — the discussion, not the reading, carries the learning
- Facilitator skill in naming emotions, asking "why did the character…?" questions, and connecting story events to students' own experience
- A classroom climate where emotional talk feels safe ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) help establish this)

#### Constraints
- Students who do not identify with a character or cultural context may disengage from the discussion entirely [-W] — book selection must reflect the class's backgrounds
- Discussion can drift into plot retelling; without facilitator prompts targeting the emotion–behavior link, emotional content goes unexamined [-W]
- Overly didactic "issue books" with flat characters can feel preachy and reduce engagement [-W]; literary quality matters
- Students experiencing acute emotional distress may find certain themes triggering; facilitators need alternatives ready [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Read-aloud + whole-class discussion** (early elementary): teacher reads, pauses at emotional turning points, asks "What is she feeling? How do you know?"
- **Literature circles** (upper elementary/middle): small groups discuss character emotions with role prompts, then share out
- **Bibliotherapy** (counseling contexts): books selected to match an individual student's situation, read and discussed one-on-one
- **Writing extensions**: students write alternative endings or letters to characters, applying the emotion–behavior analysis themselves ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) the text with emotion labels works well here)

### Target Learners
- All age groups; particularly effective in elementary and middle school, where explicit emotion vocabulary is still developing [~M]
- Students who struggle to name or discuss their own emotions directly — the character provides a safe third-person buffer [~W]
- Less effective for students with strong existing emotional literacy, for whom the discussion may feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: building vocabulary for naming feelings
- Cause–effect reasoning about emotion and behavior: "she felt embarrassed, so she…"
- Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' mental states from cues
- Rehearsal of regulation strategies: generating and evaluating alternative responses the character could have chosen

### Instructions
1. Select a book with a clear emotional arc and a character students can relate to; preview it for cultural fit and potential triggers.
2. Read aloud or assign the text ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)), pausing at key emotional moments.
3. At each pause, ask students to name the emotion, identify its cause, and find the evidence (facial expression, action, dialogue) — making the inference explicit.
4. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) on alternative responses: "What else could he have done when he got angry? What would have happened?"
5. Bridge to students' own experience with an optional, low-pressure prompt ("Have you ever felt like…?") — never require personal disclosure.
6. Extend with reflective writing or role-play ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)) where students rehearse the alternative responses they generated.

## Examples
- Reading *Where the Wild Things Are* (Sendak) and discussing how Max's anger drives his behavior, then generating what he could have done differently
- Reading *Wonder* (Palacio) in middle school to discuss empathy, embarrassment, and bystander behavior
- [Second Step](https://www.secondstep.org) — published SEL curriculum that uses photo-story cards and read-alouds as the anchor for emotion-identification lessons
- [Zones of Regulation](https://zonesofregulation.com) — pairs picture books with a color-coded emotion framework so discussion lands on shared vocabulary
