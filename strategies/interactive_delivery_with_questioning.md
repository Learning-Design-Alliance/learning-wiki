# Interactive Delivery With Questioning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Interactive delivery with questioning replaces extended one-way presentation with a sequence of instructor-posed questions, student responses, and responsive follow-up. The instructor plans questions in advance, elicits answers from many students (not just volunteers), and adapts the explanation in real time to what responses reveal about understanding.

## Design Implications

Questioning converts passive reception into [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md), which reliably improves exam performance relative to lecture alone [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Questions also function as formative assessment: student answers reveal misconceptions the instructor can address immediately, making this a core enactment of [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [+S]. The quality of learning depends less on the number of questions than on their cognitive level and the wait time afforded before answers are taken.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions planned in advance, sequenced from recall toward reasoning and application
- Genuine wait time (3+ seconds) after posing a question and after a student answers
- Mechanisms that distribute participation: cold-calling norms, think-pair-share, response systems, or small-group deliberation before whole-class sharing
- Instructor follow-up that probes reasoning ("How do you know?", "What if…?") rather than merely confirming correctness

#### Constraints
- Defaulting to volunteers skews participation toward high-achieving students and narrows the evidence the instructor receives [~M]
- Rapid-fire recall questioning keeps students in a receptive mode and adds little over lecturing [-M]
- Short wait times (under ~1 second) produce fewer and lower-quality student responses [~S]
- Public cold-calling without established norms can raise anxiety and suppress participation for some learners [~M]
- Questioning consumes substantial class time; coverage-oriented courses may struggle to fit it without trimming content

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class questioning** with cold-call or hand-raising — fast, but participation is uneven
- **Think-pair-share** — every student answers privately before public discussion, increasing participation breadth [+M]
- **Clickers / classroom response systems** (e.g., iClicker, Poll Everywhere, Mentimeter) — anonymous responses surface misconceptions without social exposure
- **Socratic seminar / [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)** — student-to-student questioning shifts the burden of elaboration to learners

### Target Learners
- Students in large-enrollment courses where individual attention is impossible; interactive questioning recovers some responsiveness of tutoring [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Students with partial prior knowledge, whose half-formed ideas are exposed and corrected by answering and hearing peers [~M]
- Less suitable as the sole method for complete novices, who may lack the knowledge base to generate useful answers and need [Explicit Teaching](../patterns/explicit-teaching.md) first [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and misconception repair — questions force learners to articulate and defend ideas [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Monitoring comprehension — questions make gaps visible to both learner and instructor [+S]
- Disciplinary discourse practices — reasoning aloud in the vocabulary of the field

### Instructions
1. Identify the 3–5 key ideas of the session and draft a question for each, ordered from activating prior knowledge to application ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
2. Open with a low-stakes question that every student answers (response system, pair discussion, or written minute paper) rather than a question only volunteers can field.
3. Pose the question, then wait at least 3–5 seconds before taking responses; after a response, wait again before evaluating.
4. Follow up on answers by probing reasoning and asking other students to agree, disagree, or extend ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Adjust the explanation in response to the pattern of answers — re-teach if a majority errs, move on if responses show mastery ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
6. Close by asking students to summarize the key idea in their own words, converting the exchange into a [Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) opportunity.

## Examples
- **Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — ConcepTest questions in physics lectures: students vote individually, discuss with neighbors, revote; the instructor then explains based on the vote pattern. Materials at [https://peerinstruction4cs.org](https://peerinstruction4cs.org).
- **Hake's interactive-engagement survey** — 6,542 students across introductory physics courses showed interactive-engagement courses roughly doubled normalized conceptual gains over traditional lecture courses ([https://doi.org/10.1119/1.19321](https://doi.org/10.1119/1.19321)).
- **Reciprocal Questioning (ReQuest)** — students take turns posing questions to the teacher and each other during text discussion, training question generation as a comprehension strategy.
