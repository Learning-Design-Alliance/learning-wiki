# Studio Critique

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Studio critique (crit) is a structured feedback protocol originating in art and design education in which learners publicly present work-in-progress and peers and the instructor analyze its strengths, weaknesses, and possibilities for revision. The presenter typically describes intent, the group responds with observations and questions, and the presenter leaves with concrete directions for revision. The format makes evaluation criteria explicit and positions feedback as a normal, ongoing part of creative work rather than a terminal judgment.

## Design Implications

Critique operationalizes [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by making success criteria visible and actionable before final evaluation [Assessment designed to inform next steps improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Because learners both give and receive analysis of authentic work, critique functions as a form of [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) [Active learning improves exam performance over lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S], and analyzing peers' varied solutions builds the case-comparison structure that supports transfer [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on facilitation: unstructured crits drift toward taste preferences or personal attack, so protocols (e.g., "I like / I wish / what if," Lerman's Critical Response Process) and norms must be taught.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic work-in-progress to critique — finished work removes the revision loop that gives critique its purpose
- Explicit criteria or a protocol so feedback addresses the work, not the person
- A facilitator who enforces norms, balances airtime, and redirects vague opinions toward specific, actionable observations
- Time and expectation for revision afterward; critique without a revision cycle is performance, not learning
- Psychological safety: presenters must believe critique serves their growth [Interventions that affirm belonging improve outcomes for at-risk learners.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Dominant voices and status hierarchies distort feedback; without structured turn-taking, quieter learners receive and give less [~M]
- Novices often cannot diagnose problems in peers' work or articulate useful feedback, producing generic praise [~M] — feedback quality improves when novices are given criteria checklists or sentence frames
- Public critique can threaten belonging and willingness to persist, particularly for learners from groups underrepresented in the discipline [~M]
- Feedback that is vague ("make it pop") or purely preference-based does not improve revision quality [-M]
- Large groups dilute attention; crits beyond ~8–10 participants per work lose specificity

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full-group crit**: traditional format; highest instructor control, highest exposure
- **Small-group or gallery-walk crit**: multiple works circulate with written comment protocols; scales to large classes
- **Silent/written crit first** (e.g., sticky notes before discussion): reduces anchoring on the first spoken opinion and includes quieter learners
- **Lerman's Critical Response Process**: artist-directed questions replace unsolicited advice; shifts power to the presenter
- **Descriptive-then-evaluative sequencing**: observers describe what they see before judging, delaying interpretation

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have produced a draft but cannot yet self-evaluate against disciplinary criteria [Assessment designed to inform next steps improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Learners developing professional judgment and evaluative skill — giving critique is as formative as receiving it
- Novices need scaffolding (protocols, criteria, modeled feedback) before crit benefits them; experts may find peer feedback less diagnostic than self-critique [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing criteria by applying them to others' work
- Revision and iteration: treating work as improvable rather than finished
- Discourse and professional communication: articulating design reasoning and responding to criticism
- Community norms of a creative discipline ([Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md))

### Instructions
1. Establish norms and a feedback protocol; model critique by critiquing a sample work aloud, making criteria explicit ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
2. Presenter briefly frames the work: intent, context, specific questions for the group
3. Group responds in protocol order — typically description, then analysis, then suggestions — anchored to the stated criteria ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
4. Presenter responds, asks clarifying questions, and takes notes; the facilitator does not defend the work for them
5. Presenter synthesizes feedback into a revision plan and revises ([Application](../elements/application.md))
6. Optionally, revised work returns to a later crit, closing the iteration loop

## Examples
- **Rhode Island School of Design** — the semester-long studio crit is the core assessment structure of its studio curriculum; RISD's [Teaching & Learning in Art + Design resources](https://tla.risd.edu) publish crit facilitation guidance
- **Lerman's Critical Response Process** — Liz Lerman's four-step protocol (statements of meaning, artist as questioner, neutral questions, opinions with permission), widely used in dance and theater training
- **Architecture studio juries** — end-of-project panel critiques in accredited architecture programs, where students defend design decisions before faculty and external practitioners
- **Design school "crit" culture in industry** (e.g., design reviews at IDEO) — the studio crit persists as the professional form learners are being prepared for
