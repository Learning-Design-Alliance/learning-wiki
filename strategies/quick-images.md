# Quick Images

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Quick Images is a routine in which the teacher briefly displays a visual — most commonly a dot arrangement, ten-frame, or rekenrek — for two to three seconds, then hides it and asks learners to reproduce or describe what they saw. Learners then share *how* they saw the quantity ("I saw a 4 and a 3, which makes 7"), making their decomposition strategies visible to the group. The brevity is deliberate: it forces learners to structure the image rather than count one-by-one, pressing them toward subitizing and part-whole reasoning.

## Design Implications

Quick Images exploits the perceptual system's capacity to apprehend small quantities instantly (subitizing) and to estimate larger ones approximately, converting perception into structured number talk [Barth et al., 2003]. The brief exposure functions as a working-memory constraint that is pedagogically useful: without time to count, learners must chunk the display into groups, which is precisely the part-whole structure the routine targets [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. The follow-up discussion — "How did you see it?" — is where most of the learning occurs, as learners articulate and compare multiple decompositions of the same quantity.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A carefully designed image whose arrangement affords the target structure (e.g., doubles, near-doubles, tens-and-ones)
- Brief, controlled exposure (1–3 seconds), then concealment
- A discussion protocol that elicits and records multiple strategies, ideally with the teacher annotating each on a visible copy of the image
- Repeated use across a range of quantities so learners encounter the same total in different arrangements

#### Constraints
- If the image stays visible too long, learners revert to unitary counting and the routine loses its structural purpose [~M]
- Images with too many unstructured items exceed perceptual/approximate-number limits and produce guessing rather than reasoning [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- If the teacher stops after the first correct answer instead of collecting multiple decompositions, learners get answer-confirmation rather than strategy comparison; contrasting multiple "ways of seeing" is what drives the learning [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Low value for learners who have already automatized the target decompositions — the routine becomes busywork [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dot cards / ten-frames / rekenreks** — different tools foreground different structures (five- and ten-anchors vs. linear comparison)
- **Quick Draw** (geometry variant) — briefly shown figures are reconstructed, building spatial and property-based reasoning rather than quantity
- **"Is it more or less?" estimation variant** — larger quantities shown briefly for approximate comparison, targeting the approximate number system
- **Student-created images** — learners design arrangements for a target number, reversing the routine and deepening structural understanding

### Target Learners
- Early elementary learners (K–2) developing cardinality and part-whole concepts [+M]
- Upper-elementary learners who still rely on counting-by-ones and need to transition to composite units [~M]
- Older learners rebuilding number sense in intervention settings, where the routine surfaces hidden counting strategies [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Subitizing: instant recognition of small quantities without counting
- Part-whole reasoning: decomposing and recomposing numbers flexibly
- Automaticity with basic combinations, which frees attention for higher-level problem solving ([Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md))
- Metacognitive articulation: describing one's own perceptual and computational strategy

### Instructions
1. **Select an image** whose arrangement affords the target structure (e.g., a ten-frame showing 8 as 5-and-3 for near-ten reasoning).
2. **Flash the image** for 2–3 seconds, then hide it; ask learners to reconstruct it or state the quantity ([Attention](../elements/attention.md) — the flash must be long enough to perceive, short enough to prevent counting).
3. **Ask "How many? How did you see it?"** and record each learner's decomposition on a visible copy, using color or grouping marks to match their description ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. **Compare strategies** — highlight how different decompositions yield the same total, connecting arrangements to equations (e.g., 5 + 3 = 4 + 4 = 8).
5. **Repeat with variations** of the same quantity across days so structural recognition generalizes beyond a single arrangement.

## Examples
- **Sherry Parrish's *Number Talks* (Math Solutions, 2010)** — the widely used professional resource whose K–5 routines include dot-card and ten-frame Quick Images with full discussion protocols. [https://www.mathsolutions.com](https://www.mathsolutions.com)
- **Quick Draw by Grayson Wheatley** — the geometry variant: figures are flashed and reconstructed from memory, shifting discussion from "what is it?" to "what properties did you see?"
- **DreamBox Learning** — digital lessons use briefly flashed ten-frame and rekenrek displays with immediate reconstruction, automating the flash-hide-discuss cycle. [https://www.dreambox.com](https://www.dreambox.com)
