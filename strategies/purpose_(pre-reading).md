# Purpose (Pre-Reading)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners are given a specific purpose for reading, typically in the form of discussion questions about the content prepared by the teacher. They review these questions before reading and prepare answers while reading the text. Afterward, they discuss the questions with a partner. The strategy focuses attention, activates prior knowledge, and enhances comprehension by converting reading from passive reception into goal-directed search.

## Design Implications

Pre-reading purpose questions function as a form of [Activation](../principles/activation.md): they prime relevant prior knowledge and set an attentional goal before learners encounter the text [Giving learners a purpose or question before reading improves comprehension of goal-relevant content.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. They also act as lightweight [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), giving learners a framework into which new information can be assimilated [Advance organizers improve learning by bridging new content to existing knowledge.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. The post-reading peer discussion adds retrieval and elaboration, which strengthens retention beyond reading alone.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Pre-prepared discussion questions directly related to the reading's central content
- Time for learners to review questions *before* reading begins
- A structured follow-on discussion or [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) so answers are retrieved, not merely located

#### Constraints
- Purpose questions create selective attention: learners retain question-relevant content well but often learn *less* of the incidental content than unguided readers [Purpose questions narrow attention, reducing recall of information not targeted by the questions.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M] — questions should target the most important ideas, not peripheral details
- Literal "find the answer" questions encourage skimming for keywords rather than comprehension; inferential and integrative questions mitigate this
- Effectiveness depends entirely on question quality and relevance; poorly aligned questions misdirect attention away from the text's core ideas

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher-generated questions (classic form) vs. learner-generated questions, which add question-asking skill but require more scaffolding
- Questions posed orally, on a worksheet, or embedded as [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) prompts in the text itself
- Discussion format: think-pair-share, small-group [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or written response

### Target Learners
- Developing readers and novices who lack the background knowledge to judge what matters in a text [Advance organizers improve learning by bridging new content to existing knowledge.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from knowing the communicative goal before processing dense text
- Less necessary for expert readers, who naturally set their own reading purposes

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: extracting and integrating central ideas
- Prior knowledge activation: connecting new text to what learners already know [Activating prior knowledge before a task improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Discussion and academic language: justifying answers with textual evidence

### Instructions
1. Select or write 2–4 discussion questions targeting the text's most important ideas ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))
2. Present the questions before reading and briefly activate relevant background knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
3. Learners read with the questions in view, marking evidence as they go ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
4. Learners answer the questions and discuss with a partner ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. Debrief whole-class, resolving discrepancies between answers and pressing for textual evidence

## Examples
- Before reading a history text on the American Civil War, learners receive "What were the main causes of the Civil War?" and "How did the war change American society?", annotate evidence while reading, then pair-discuss answers.
- In a science course using [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md), pre-reading questions direct attention to the mechanism under study before learners read the background reading.
- Reciprocal Teaching ([https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching](https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching)) embeds purpose-question generation as one of its four strategies, transferring the teacher's role to students.
