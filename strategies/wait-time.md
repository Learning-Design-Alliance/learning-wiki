# Wait Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Wait time is the period of silence between the time a question is asked and the time when one or more learners respond. It involves pausing for a minimum of three seconds after posing a question to allow learners to process and formulate a response, and pausing again after a response so other learners can process the answer. The appropriate duration depends on question complexity, learner ability, and question clarity.

## Design Implications

Wait time converts questioning from rapid recall into genuine thinking time. Rowe's foundational research showed that extending teacher questioning pauses from about one second to three or more seconds produced longer and more correct responses, more student volunteering, fewer "I don't know" answers, and gains in achievement scores [+M]. The mechanism is processing time: formulating a substantive answer requires working-memory operations that cannot complete under pressure to respond instantly [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]. Wait time also functions as a participation equalizer — fast responders dominate when pauses are short, while extended silence invites broader involvement, supporting [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Conscious instructor discipline to hold silence for at least three seconds after asking and after answering — the default teacher pause is under one second
- Questions worth waiting for: higher-order or open prompts justify the pause; trivial recall questions do not
- Tolerance for silence, including nonverbal signals (relaxed posture, scanning the room) that communicate the wait is expected rather than awkward
- A genuine question worth thinking about; wait time applied to rapid-fire recall questions yields little benefit
- Conscious instructor discipline to tolerate silence — counting silently or using a visible timer helps
- A norm-setting move early in the term so students understand silence means thinking, not teacher disapproval
- A follow-up move after the pause (e.g., [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) prompts, think-pair-share, or cold-calling with support) so the pause feeds into discourse rather than dead air

#### Constraints
- Instructors uncomfortable with silence or pressured to cover content rapidly revert to short pauses, eliminating the effect [-M]
- Students accustomed to quick-fire questioning may initially resist or fill silence with off-task talk [~W]
- Wait time adds little for pure recall questions or when learners lack the prior knowledge needed to construct a response at all [~M]
- Overlong or undifferentiated pauses can feel punitive to learners with processing speed differences; duration should be adjusted to question complexity and learner needs
- Instructors uncomfortable with silence tend to rephrase, answer their own question, or call on a student before the processing is complete, eliminating the effect [-S]
- Pressure to cover content quickly leads teachers to abandon wait time during fast-paced lessons; the strategy collapses under time stress unless deliberately protected [-M]
- Students habituated to quick-fire exchange may initially resist, filling silence with off-task talk or "I don't know" until norms adjust [~W]
- Wait time alone, without higher-order questions or follow-up probing, produces longer answers but not deeper learning [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Wait Time 1** — pause after the question, before any student responds
- **Wait Time 2** — pause after a student's response, before the teacher reacts, allowing peers to evaluate and extend the answer
- **Structured think time** — explicit "take 30 seconds to think" or write-first prompts ([Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) style) that legitimize silence in products and large classes
- **Asynchronous wait time** — in video or online products, pausing playback with an embedded prompt to think before revealing an answer
- **Post-response wait time (wait time 2):** pausing after a student answers, before evaluating, so peers can agree, extend, or challenge — shifts discourse toward student-to-student exchange
- **Asynchronous contexts:** embedded pauses in video ("pause and think before the answer appears") or enforced delays in response systems replicate the mechanism
- **Differentiation:** extend the pause for complex or novel questions and for learners who process more slowly; shorten for fluency-building recall

### Target Learners
- All learner levels; documented benefits in elementary and secondary classrooms [+M]
- Learners who process slowly or hesitate to volunteer — extended pauses increase their participation [+M]
- Learners with strong rapid-recall habits, who benefit from being pushed beyond first-available answers [~M]
- All levels; effects documented from elementary through higher education, with strong evidence in secondary science and mathematics discourse [~S]
- Reluctant or slower processors, who are systematically crowded out when response windows are under one second [~M]
- Less relevant for learners who already have fluent mastery, for whom extended pauses on routine questions add delay without benefit [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Depth of response: elaborated, reasoned answers rather than single words
- Conceptual reasoning: questions requiring inference, justification, or connection-making [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Discourse norms: students asking their own questions and responding to peers, not only to the teacher
- Higher-order reasoning and extended verbal explanation rather than single-word recall
- Metacognitive habits: learning to tolerate and use thinking time before responding
- Equitable participation: broadening who answers, not just how much the fastest students answer

### Instructions
1. Plan the question in advance and decide whether it merits wait time; higher-order questions do, rapid-fire review does not.
2. Ask the question once, then hold silence for at least three seconds (Wait Time 1), maintaining nonverbal openness rather than repeating or rephrasing.
3. After a student responds, pause again before evaluating or redirecting (Wait Time 2), inviting peers to agree, extend, or challenge — connecting to [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
4. Follow responses with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) prompts ("What's your evidence?") rather than immediate judgment, and close the loop with [Feedback](../elements/assessment.md) that builds on the extended answers.

## Examples
- A science teacher pauses at least three seconds after asking a question about a scientific concept, resulting in more students volunteering thoughtful, detailed answers and fewer "I don't know" responses.
- Rowe's original studies: teachers trained to extend pauses from ~1 to ~3+ seconds showed changes in their own behavior too — fewer teacher interruptions and more varied questioning.
- An online learning product prompts students to pause a video and think before an answer is revealed, embedding asynchronous wait time in self-paced instruction.
- **Secondary science discourse:** Rowe's original studies showed that when teachers extended pauses past three seconds, student responses lengthened, unsolicited but justified answers increased, and "I don't know" responses dropped sharply.
- **Asynchronous video:** platforms such as [Edpuzzle](https://edpuzzle.com) embed questions that pause playback, giving learners enforced think-time before the answer is revealed — a direct translation of wait time to self-paced media.
- **Instructor training:** Stahl's think-time materials for teacher professional development use audiotaped lesson review to make teachers aware of their own sub-second response habits.

# Wait Time

## Description
Wait time is the deliberate period of silence between the time a question is asked and the time when one or more learners respond, and again after a learner responds before the instructor reacts or moves on. Rowe's foundational research identified a minimum of about three seconds as the threshold below which classroom discourse degrades into rapid recall exchange; the appropriate duration scales with question complexity — roughly 1–3 seconds for recall questions, 4–6 for calculation or multi-step questions, and 6–10 or more for higher-order questions. The second pause, after a student's response, is equally important: it gives other learners time to process the answer and invites elaboration rather than immediate evaluation.

## Design Implications

Wait time works because responding to a question — especially a higher-order one — requires working-memory processing that cannot be completed in the fraction of a second typical of untrained classroom discourse. Rowe found that in ordinary classrooms the average gap between question and answer was about one second, and that extending it to three or more seconds produced measurable changes in both student and teacher behavior [~S]. The effect is well replicated for response length, participation breadth, and student confidence, but evidence for gains in achievement scores is more variable and depends on wait time being paired with higher-order questioning rather than recall-only discourse [~M].

1. Plan the question in advance and classify its cognitive demand to set an appropriate pause length.

3. After a student responds, apply **wait time 2**: pause again before evaluating, inviting peers to respond to the answer.
4. Follow up with probing or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) moves ("What do others think?", "How did you arrive at that?") so the pause converts into reasoning.
5. Establish the norm explicitly with students and practice it; expect initial discomfort on both sides to fade within a few weeks.
