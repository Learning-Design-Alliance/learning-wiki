# Wait Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Wait time is the period of silence between the time a question is asked and the time when one or more learners respond. It involves pausing for a minimum of three seconds after posing a question to allow learners to process and formulate a response, and pausing again after a response so other learners can process the answer. The appropriate duration depends on question complexity, learner ability, and question clarity.

## Design Implications

Wait time converts questioning from rapid recall into genuine thinking time. Rowe's foundational research showed that extending teacher questioning pauses from about one second to three or more seconds produced longer and more correct responses, more student volunteering, fewer "I don't know" answers, and gains in achievement scores [+M]. The mechanism is processing time: formulating a substantive answer requires working-memory operations that cannot complete under pressure to respond instantly [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]. Wait time also functions as a participation equalizer — fast responders dominate when pauses are short, while extended silence invites broader involvement, supporting [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Conscious instructor discipline to hold silence for at least three seconds after asking and after answering — the default teacher pause is under one second
- Questions worth waiting for: higher-order or open prompts justify the pause; trivial recall questions do not
- Tolerance for silence, including nonverbal signals (relaxed posture, scanning the room) that communicate the wait is expected rather than awkward

#### Constraints
- Instructors uncomfortable with silence or pressured to cover content rapidly revert to short pauses, eliminating the effect [-M]
- Students accustomed to quick-fire questioning may initially resist or fill silence with off-task talk [~W]
- Wait time adds little for pure recall questions or when learners lack the prior knowledge needed to construct a response at all [~M]
- Overlong or undifferentiated pauses can feel punitive to learners with processing speed differences; duration should be adjusted to question complexity and learner needs

#### Implementation Variability
- **Wait Time 1** — pause after the question, before any student responds
- **Wait Time 2** — pause after a student's response, before the teacher reacts, allowing peers to evaluate and extend the answer
- **Structured think time** — explicit "take 30 seconds to think" or write-first prompts ([Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) style) that legitimize silence in products and large classes
- **Asynchronous wait time** — in video or online products, pausing playback with an embedded prompt to think before revealing an answer

### Target Learners
- All learner levels; documented benefits in elementary and secondary classrooms [+M]
- Learners who process slowly or hesitate to volunteer — extended pauses increase their participation [+M]
- Learners with strong rapid-recall habits, who benefit from being pushed beyond first-available answers [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Depth of response: elaborated, reasoned answers rather than single words
- Conceptual reasoning: questions requiring inference, justification, or connection-making [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Discourse norms: students asking their own questions and responding to peers, not only to the teacher

### Instructions
1. Plan the question in advance and decide whether it merits wait time; higher-order questions do, rapid-fire review does not.
2. Ask the question once, then hold silence for at least three seconds (Wait Time 1), maintaining nonverbal openness rather than repeating or rephrasing.
3. After a student responds, pause again before evaluating or redirecting (Wait Time 2), inviting peers to agree, extend, or challenge — connecting to [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
4. Follow responses with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) prompts ("What's your evidence?") rather than immediate judgment, and close the loop with [Feedback](../elements/assessment.md) that builds on the extended answers.

## Related Strategies
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — institutionalizes wait time by giving every learner a processing partner before public response
- [Cold Calling](cold-calling.md) — pairs with wait time so that calling on non-volunteers happens after adequate thinking time, not instead of it
- [Questioning Techniques](questioning-techniques.md) — wait time is the temporal half of effective questioning; question quality is the other half

## Examples
- A science teacher pauses at least three seconds after asking a question about a scientific concept, resulting in more students volunteering thoughtful, detailed answers and fewer "I don't know" responses.
- Rowe's original studies: teachers trained to extend pauses from ~1 to ~3+ seconds showed changes in their own behavior too — fewer teacher interruptions and more varied questioning.
- An online learning product prompts students to pause a video and think before an answer is revealed, embedding asynchronous wait time in self-paced instruction.
