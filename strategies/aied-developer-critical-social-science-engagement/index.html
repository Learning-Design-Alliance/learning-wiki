Pursue a productive future for AIED through deeper engagement between application developers and critical voices from the social sciences and history¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that AIED research and development be supported by social and historical sensitivity: developers bring pedagogic, design and technical expertise, while critical social scientists and historians bring expertise in understanding the complex and often unintended social effects of technologies. It states "a productive future for AIED would involve deeper engagement between application developers and more critical voices from the social sciences and history". Education sector professionals are urged to think much harder about how AI is being put to work in different contexts and with what social effects.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Acknowledging potential risks AI could bring, taking seriously its potential to worsen existing problems, and refusing the technochauvinist assumption that AI is the ideal solution

Constraints¶

  • The article notes it is hard to anticipate downstream risks of any new technology, though professionals can consider the longer history of AI and related technologies

Target Learners¶

  • AIED application developers
  • education sector professionals
  • education policymakers

Target Learning Goals¶

  • anticipating unintended social effects of AI in education
  • resisting uncritical adoption of AI in education

Affordances¶

Claims¶

  • AI in education is conceived and practised in three ways — as research methodology, as industry profit source, and as political rhetoric — leading to very different outcomes [+W]
  • The burden of ethical and regulatory compliance for AI in education falls on schools while edtech and Big Tech companies remain largely unaffected [+W]
  • The business model of AI in education is usually based on platformization and datafication, with subscription services and data-driven upselling [+W]
  • AI in accountability-oriented policy risks automated decision-making replacing human judgment and poor-quality data driving high-stakes decisions [+W]
  • Big Tech hyperscalers are introducing cloud and AI infrastructures into education with a business model tending towards monopoly capture [+W]
  • Remote exam proctoring software based on automated facial detection tends to disproportionately flag already-marginalized student groups as suspicious [+W]
