# Frayer Model

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (5 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 9 report an effect size

## Description
The Frayer Model is a four-quadrant graphic organizer used to teach a concept: learners complete sections for (1) a definition, (2) essential characteristics or facts, (3) examples, and (4) non-examples. Because learners must supply the content themselves rather than copy a glossary entry, the organizer functions as a structured generative activity rather than a passive note-taking frame. It was developed by Frayer, Frederick, and Klausmeier (1969) at the Wisconsin Research and Development Center for Cognitive Learning as part of work on concept acquisition.

## Design Implications

The Frayer Model operationalizes concept learning by forcing discrimination between instances and non-instances, which is the core of concept attainment [~M]. Requiring learners to generate their own definition and examples makes the activity generative, which strengthens encoding relative to reading a definition alone [elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The four quadrants also distribute processing across verbal and visual/structural channels, consistent with dual coding benefits [dual-coding-improves-recall](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-chosen target concept — the model works best for concepts with clear boundaries, not for terms that are purely arbitrary labels
- Learner access to resources (texts, discussion, prior knowledge) for generating characteristics and examples
- Teacher or peer feedback on the quality of definitions and especially of non-examples, since a wrong non-example can entrench a misconception
- Time for completion; the model is substantially more effortful than copying a definition
- A concept rich enough to have genuine boundaries — not a simple fact or a single vocabulary word with no near-neighbors
- A teacher- or curriculum-supplied definition anchor, so learners do not entrench misconceptions before feedback
- Time for learners to generate their own quadrants, followed by comparison or feedback against a completed version
- A concept with genuine definitional structure — a category with criteria, not a proper noun or an arbitrary label
- A teacher who has clarified the concept's boundaries first; the model exposes fuzzy teacher understanding immediately
- At least two examples and two *near-miss* non-examples that differ on a defining attribute, not on surface features
- Time for learners to generate and defend entries, ideally through [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of disputed cases

#### Constraints
- Inefficient for high-volume vocabulary coverage; completing one organizer per term takes far longer than other vocabulary routines [-W]
- Weak for arbitrary or technical labels with no underlying concept (e.g., proper nouns, unit names), where "non-examples" are ill-defined [-W]
- If the definition quadrant is completed by copying a dictionary, the generative benefit largely disappears [~M]
- Poorly chosen non-examples (near-misses that are actually examples) can create confusion rather than sharpen boundaries [-M]
- Inefficient for concepts with fuzzy or contested boundaries (e.g., "democracy," "energy") — learners may produce arbitrary examples and non-examples without expert feedback [-W]
- Low value when the target is procedural skill or strategy use rather than conceptual understanding [-M]
- If the definition quadrant is completed first and treated as authoritative, learners may copy it verbatim and skip the discriminating work that drives learning [-W]
- Poorly chosen non-examples (too obviously wrong) fail to sharpen boundaries; near-miss non-examples are required for the contrast to do work [-M]
- Ineffective for concepts without clear defining attributes (e.g., "democracy," "energy" in early grades) — forcing a definition produces pseudo-precision [-W]
- Non-examples chosen for surface similarity are essential; using obviously different non-examples makes the task trivial and produces no discrimination learning [~M]
- Copying teacher-completed models yields shallow encoding; the generative effort is where learning occurs [-M]
- One model per concept is slow; it does not scale to high-volume vocabulary demands, where it should be reserved for high-utility tier-two words or core disciplinary concepts [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Blank organizer completed from reading vs. partially completed organizer with examples given and non-examples to be generated (the latter is easier for novices)
- Individual, pair, or whole-class completion; whole-class completion allows [class-discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) around contested non-examples
- Extended variants add a fifth quadrant (e.g., "visual representation" or "personal connection") or an "essential vs. non-essential characteristics" split
- **Pre-filled vs. generated:** teacher-completed organizers support [advance organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) use before reading; student-completed versions function as generative processing during or after instruction
- **Mathematical adaptation:** quadrants re-cast as definition, characteristics/facts, examples, non-examples for concepts like "prime number" or "function"
- **Collaborative completion:** pairs or groups negotiate quadrants, surfacing disagreements about boundaries for whole-class discussion
- **Digital versions:** shared slides or whiteboard tools allow asynchronous completion and comparison across learners
- **Blank vs. partially completed**: give novices the definition and one example, letting them derive the rest; fade support as expertise grows
- **Student-generated non-examples**: asking learners to invent near-misses is a harder, more generative variant
- **Disciplinary adaptations**: in mathematics, quadrants become "what it is / what it is not / examples / counterexamples"; in science, characteristics split into necessary vs. sufficient
- **Digital versions**: shared slides or whiteboard tools allow collaborative completion and comparison across groups

### Target Learners
- Elementary and secondary learners acquiring discipline-specific concepts and academic vocabulary [+M]
- Struggling readers and learners with language-based difficulties benefit from the explicit structure, which reduces the demands of open-ended note-taking [~M]
- Advanced learners with strong prior knowledge may find the fixed structure redundant; they benefit more from comparing multiple concepts than from deep-processing a single term [~W]
- Elementary through secondary learners acquiring discipline-specific vocabulary and concepts [+M]
- Multilingual learners, for whom the visual structure and explicit non-examples reduce reliance on nuanced definitional language [+W]
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already discriminate the concept reliably — the organizer becomes busywork [~W]
- Novices encountering a new disciplinary concept for the first time, who otherwise anchor on surface features [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying principle.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- English language learners, when paired with [accessible vocabulary and syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and visual examples — the structure makes implicit expectations explicit
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already hold well-differentiated concepts; the organizer becomes redundant busywork [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept acquisition: building accurate boundaries around what a term does and does not include
- Academic vocabulary depth beyond definitional recall
- Discrimination and classification: distinguishing instances from non-instances, which supports transfer to new cases
- Concept acquisition: building accurate, bounded schemas for vocabulary and category terms
- Discrimination learning: distinguishing near-neighbors (e.g., *mass* vs. *weight*, *weathering* vs. *erosion*)
- Retrieval and retention: the generative completion acts as elaborative encoding supporting later recall [+M]
- Concept formation: learning what a term includes, excludes, and why
- Discrimination learning: distinguishing superficially similar concepts (e.g., *mass* vs. *weight*, *prime* vs. *odd*)
- Vocabulary depth: moving from definitional knowledge to flexible, criterion-based word use

### Instructions
1. Select the target concept and present the term in context (a text, problem, or phenomenon), activating prior knowledge [activation](activation.md)
2. Learners write or draft a definition in their own words — not copied — in the definition quadrant
3. Learners list essential characteristics or facts in the second quadrant
4. Learners generate 2–3 examples, ideally drawn from the instructional context
5. Learners generate 2–3 non-examples, choosing near-misses that test the concept's boundary rather than obviously unrelated items
6. Review and refine: compare organizers across peers, resolve disagreements about non-examples, and revise definitions — the discussion phase is where much of the learning occurs

## Examples
- **Science:** Students complete a Frayer Model for "mammal" with non-examples of dolphin-adjacent confusions (shark, tuna) and boundary cases (platypus), then defend their non-example choices in discussion.
- **Mathematics:** A Frayer Model for "function" with non-examples such as relations that assign two outputs to one input; the non-example quadrant does the diagnostic work.
- **Vocabulary programs:** Word Generation (https://www.serpinstitute.org/word-generation), SERP Institute's free academic vocabulary curriculum, uses structured word analysis routines of this kind for middle-school discussion-based vocabulary instruction.
- **Vocabulary instruction in science:** students complete Frayer organizers for *conductor* and *insulator*, then classify ambiguous materials (e.g., graphite) using their characteristics quadrant.
- **Mathematics:** [Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org) style concept lessons use example/non-example sorting consistent with the Frayer structure when introducing definitions such as "polygon."
- **Reading intervention programs:** several commercial vocabulary supplements (e.g., within [Reading A–Z](https://www.readinga-z.com) vocabulary materials) include Frayer templates as standard practice for academic vocabulary.
- **Mathematics**: a Frayer Model for *prime number* — definition ("a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two factors"), characteristics (exactly two factors, greater than 1), examples (2, 7, 13), non-examples (1, which fails the "greater than 1" criterion; 9, which has three factors). The non-example *1* is the critical case that corrects the most common misconception.
- **Science**: *mammal* with the non-example *dolphin-as-fish* reasoning surfaced, forcing learners to use reproduction and warm-bloodedness rather than habitat as criteria.
- **Literacy/vocabulary programs**: widely embedded in tiered vocabulary routines such as those in Marzano's six-step academic vocabulary process (Marzano & Pickering, 2005), where the Frayer Model serves as the "represent and deepen" step.

## Description
The Frayer Model (Frayer, Frederick, & Klausmeier, 1969) is a graphic organizer divided into four quadrants surrounding a target concept: a definition, key characteristics or attributes, examples, and non-examples. Learners complete the organizer themselves — individually or in discussion — rather than receiving it pre-filled, forcing them to discriminate the concept's boundaries rather than merely restate a glossary definition.

## Design Implications

The model works because it requires learners to go beyond memorizing a definition: generating examples and non-examples demands that they test candidate features against the concept, which builds a discriminating schema rather than a verbal label [Elaborative interrogation improves learning by prompting learners to explain why features belong.](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. # The Frayer Model

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 10 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
The Frayer Model is a four-quadrant graphic organizer used to teach a concept rather than an isolated fact. Learners complete quadrants for **Definition** (a precise statement of the concept's category and boundaries), **Essential Characteristics** (attributes every instance must have), **Examples**, and **Non-Examples** (near-misses that share surface features but fail the criteria). Developed by Frayer, Frederick, and Klausmeier (1969) at the Wisconsin Research and Development Center, it operationalizes a "concept of definition" procedure in which word knowledge means knowing boundaries, not just meanings.

## Design Implications

The model forces learners to engage in the two operations that concept-learning research identifies as central: identifying the defining attributes that separate instances from non-instances, and comparing contrasting cases to abstract the underlying rule [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying principle.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The non-examples quadrant is the load-bearing wall: without near-miss non-examples, learners form overgeneralized concepts that include irrelevant surface features. Because the organizer externalizes the concept's structure, it reduces the working-memory burden of holding attributes, instances, and boundaries in mind simultaneously [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness increases when learners generate their own entries rather than copying teacher-provided ones [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Instructions
1. Select a concept-critical term and verify its defining attributes yourself before teaching.
2. Present the term and activate related knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)); consider an [analogy](../elements/analogies.md) to a familiar concept.
3. Co-construct the definition, naming the superordinate category and the concept's boundaries.
4. Have learners list essential characteristics — attributes every instance must have.
5. Provide or elicit examples, then deliberately introduce a near-miss non-example to trigger [cognitive conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) and force criterion-based reasoning.
6. Have learners generate their own examples and non-examples and justify them against the essential characteristics [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
7. Revisit and revise the completed model after subsequent instruction; treat it as a living artifact, not a one-time worksheet.
