# Frayer Model

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Frayer Model is a four-quadrant graphic organizer used to teach a concept: learners complete sections for (1) a definition, (2) essential characteristics or facts, (3) examples, and (4) non-examples. Because learners must supply the content themselves rather than copy a glossary entry, the organizer functions as a structured generative activity rather than a passive note-taking frame. It was developed by Frayer, Frederick, and Klausmeier (1969) at the Wisconsin Research and Development Center for Cognitive Learning as part of work on concept acquisition.

## Design Implications

The Frayer Model operationalizes concept learning by forcing discrimination between instances and non-instances, which is the core of concept attainment [~M]. Requiring learners to generate their own definition and examples makes the activity generative, which strengthens encoding relative to reading a definition alone [elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The four quadrants also distribute processing across verbal and visual/structural channels, consistent with dual coding benefits [dual-coding-improves-recall](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-chosen target concept — the model works best for concepts with clear boundaries, not for terms that are purely arbitrary labels
- Learner access to resources (texts, discussion, prior knowledge) for generating characteristics and examples
- Teacher or peer feedback on the quality of definitions and especially of non-examples, since a wrong non-example can entrench a misconception
- Time for completion; the model is substantially more effortful than copying a definition

#### Constraints
- Inefficient for high-volume vocabulary coverage; completing one organizer per term takes far longer than other vocabulary routines [-W]
- Weak for arbitrary or technical labels with no underlying concept (e.g., proper nouns, unit names), where "non-examples" are ill-defined [-W]
- If the definition quadrant is completed by copying a dictionary, the generative benefit largely disappears [~M]
- Poorly chosen non-examples (near-misses that are actually examples) can create confusion rather than sharpen boundaries [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Blank organizer completed from reading vs. partially completed organizer with examples given and non-examples to be generated (the latter is easier for novices)
- Individual, pair, or whole-class completion; whole-class completion allows [class-discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) around contested non-examples
- Extended variants add a fifth quadrant (e.g., "visual representation" or "personal connection") or an "essential vs. non-essential characteristics" split

### Target Learners
- Elementary and secondary learners acquiring discipline-specific concepts and academic vocabulary [+M]
- Struggling readers and learners with language-based difficulties benefit from the explicit structure, which reduces the demands of open-ended note-taking [~M]
- Advanced learners with strong prior knowledge may find the fixed structure redundant; they benefit more from comparing multiple concepts than from deep-processing a single term [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept acquisition: building accurate boundaries around what a term does and does not include
- Academic vocabulary depth beyond definitional recall
- Discrimination and classification: distinguishing instances from non-instances, which supports transfer to new cases

### Instructions
1. Select the target concept and present the term in context (a text, problem, or phenomenon), activating prior knowledge [activation](activation.md)
2. Learners write or draft a definition in their own words — not copied — in the definition quadrant
3. Learners list essential characteristics or facts in the second quadrant
4. Learners generate 2–3 examples, ideally drawn from the instructional context
5. Learners generate 2–3 non-examples, choosing near-misses that test the concept's boundary rather than obviously unrelated items
6. Review and refine: compare organizers across peers, resolve disagreements about non-examples, and revise definitions — the discussion phase is where much of the learning occurs

## Examples
- **Science:** Students complete a Frayer Model for "mammal" with non-examples of dolphin-adjacent confusions (shark, tuna) and boundary cases (platypus), then defend their non-example choices in discussion.
- **Mathematics:** A Frayer Model for "function" with non-examples such as relations that assign two outputs to one input; the non-example quadrant does the diagnostic work.
- **Vocabulary programs:** Word Generation (https://www.serpinstitute.org/word-generation), SERP Institute's free academic vocabulary curriculum, uses structured word analysis routines of this kind for middle-school discussion-based vocabulary instruction.
