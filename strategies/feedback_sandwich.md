# Feedback Sandwich

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The feedback sandwich delivers feedback in three parts: a positive comment, the corrective feedback, then a second positive comment. The intent is to soften the impact of criticism, reduce defensiveness, and maintain rapport. It is a *delivery* structure rather than a feedback-content model — it says nothing about what information the learner actually needs.

## Design Implications

Feedback improves learning when it tells learners something actionable about the task or the process, not when it manages their emotional state [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The sandwich structure is at best a rapport-management device; the evidence that it improves learning outcomes is weak, and it can actively degrade the feedback by burying the actionable message [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine, specific positive observations — generic praise ("good job") carries no information [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- A clearly identified, actionable correction tied to the task or process
- A learner relationship in which the praise is credible rather than formulaic

#### Constraints
- Learners quickly learn the formula and discount or skip the opening praise, waiting for "the real feedback" — the structure becomes transparent and the positives lose credibility [-W]
- Burying the correction between compliments can cause learners to miss or underweight the actionable message, especially when the praise is elaborate [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]
- Praise directed at the person rather than the work can reinforce performance goals and increase fear of failure, reducing willingness to attempt challenging tasks [~M]
- In high-stakes or repeated feedback contexts (e.g., clinical supervision), the ritual can read as insincere and damage trust [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Task-focused variant:** open with a specific strength that is *functionally related* to the weakness ("Your argument is well organized; the evidence for claim two is thin") so the positive frames the correction rather than padding it
- **Separate-channel variant:** deliver positives and corrections in distinct, clearly labeled segments rather than interleaving them
- **Written vs. oral:** the formula is more transparent and more easily discounted in writing; orally it functions more as conversational politeness than as a learning design

### Target Learners
- Anxious or low-efficacy learners who may disengage from unbuffered criticism [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M] — though the buffer must be genuine to work
- Experienced learners and experts generally prefer direct, unbuffered feedback; the sandwich adds noise without informational value [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning-goal strategy in itself; it serves the *delivery* of feedback aimed at task and process improvement [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Maintaining learner motivation and relationship quality during corrective cycles

### Instructions
1. Identify the one or two most important corrections before composing any praise — the correction drives the design, not the sandwich.
2. Open with a specific, genuine observation about what the learner did well and *why it worked* (task or process level, not person level).
3. State the correction clearly and unambiguously, with a concrete next step.
4. Close by connecting the learner's existing strength to the improvement path ("the clarity you showed in section one is what section three needs"), rather than with a generic compliment.

## Related Strategies
- Action-oriented feedback — replaces the emotional-buffering rationale with an informational one
- Check-ins — a more direct way to manage learner affect around feedback

## Examples
- A teacher reviewing a student's presentation: "Your enthusiasm really engaged the audience. To strengthen your argument, add data to support your second claim — your closing summary shows you already know how to synthesize, so apply that here."
- In clinical education, many programs have moved away from the sandwich toward structured models (e.g., "Ask-Tell-Ask") that elicit the learner's self-assessment first, because trainees report the sandwich feels patronizing when repeated.
