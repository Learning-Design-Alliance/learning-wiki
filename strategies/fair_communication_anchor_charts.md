# Fair Communication Anchor Charts

## Description
Anchor charts are co-created or teacher-authored visual displays that make communication protocols — turn-taking, active listening, respecting diverse perspectives, avoiding interruptions, giving specific feedback — permanently visible in the learning space. By externalizing these norms, the chart functions as a persistent environmental prompt that reduces the working-memory and social burden of remembering "how to talk" during cognitively demanding discussion and teamwork.

## Design Implications

Anchor charts work by converting tacit behavioral expectations into explicit, referenceable structure, which supports self-regulation during collaborative talk [Clear structure and visible expectations support productive classroom dialogue.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~W]. Their effectiveness depends less on the chart itself than on the surrounding practice: norms must be explicitly taught, modeled, and referenced in the moment, and students benefit from participating in drafting the protocols, which builds ownership and accountability [Cooperative learning with individual accountability improves outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Charts should be treated as living documents — revisited, annotated, and revised — rather than static decoration.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short set of positively framed, behaviorally specific protocols (e.g., "Build on others' ideas," not "Don't be rude")
- Explicit teaching and modeling of each protocol before it is relied upon ([Explicit Teaching](../patterns/explicit-teaching.md))
- In-the-moment referencing during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and group work, not one-time posting
- Student involvement in drafting or revising the protocols to build ownership

#### Constraints
- A posted chart without modeling or reinforcement has little effect; visual presence alone does not change behavior [-W]
- Norms imposed without student input can feel coercive and are followed only under surveillance [~W]
- Charts can ossify: protocols that are never revisited or revised lose salience within weeks
- Effectiveness depends on students having the underlying discourse skills; a chart naming "build on ideas" does not teach *how* to do so

#### Implementation Variability
- Co-created with students vs. teacher-authored; co-creation trades polish for ownership
- Talk-move versions (sentence starters like "I agree with ___ because…") vs. rule versions
- Digital equivalents: pinned norms in discussion forums or shared documents for online [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) settings
- Differentiated charts using [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) for multilingual learners or students with language-processing needs

### Target Learners
- All ages; especially valuable for adolescents and older students whose peer-status concerns suppress participation
- Multilingual learners and students with social-communication differences, for whom explicit norms reduce ambiguity [~W]
- Classrooms with status hierarchies that silence lower-status students; charts pair well with structured role assignment from [Complex Instruction](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md) traditions

### Target Learning Goals
- Discourse and collaboration skills: equitable participation, active listening, constructive feedback
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking and respectful disagreement ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Classroom climate: psychological safety as a precondition for [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and discussion-based goals

### Instructions
1. Identify the 3–6 communication behaviors that matter most for the upcoming activity; keep the list short to respect working-memory limits [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
2. Draft the protocols with students, eliciting examples of what each looks and sounds like
3. Model each protocol explicitly, including non-examples ([Explicit Teaching](../patterns/explicit-teaching.md))
4. Create the chart with students and post it at eye level where talk happens
5. Reference the chart during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines; name students' use of specific protocols as they occur
6. Revisit and revise the chart periodically, adding protocols as new challenges emerge

## Examples
- **Accountable Talk (Institute for Learning, University of Pittsburgh)** — a research-based set of talk norms and sentence stems for academically productive classroom discussion, frequently implemented as anchor charts: https://ifl.pitt.edu
- **Complex Instruction (Cohen & Lotan, Stanford)** — group-work norms including "no one is done until everyone is done," posted and referenced to equalize participation status
- A middle-school science class co-creates a chart ("One voice at a time; Disagree with the idea, not the person; Cite the evidence") that the teacher points to during lab debriefs, visibly reducing interruptions over the term
