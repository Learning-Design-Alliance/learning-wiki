# KWL Charts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
KWL charts are advance organizers divided into three columns: what students already **Know**, what they **Want to know**, and what they **Learned**. Before a lesson or unit, students complete the K and W columns — surfacing prior knowledge and generating questions. After instruction, they complete the L column, consolidating new learning and checking it against their initial expectations. The routine is typically carried out through individual writing followed by [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).

## Design Implications

KWL charts operationalize [Activation](../principles/activation.md): prompting retrieval of prior knowledge before new material creates retrieval cues that support integration of new information [Activating prior knowledge improves learning of new material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The W column converts passive reception into goal-directed reading or listening, and the L column closes the loop with self-assessment of learning. As a graphic organizer, the chart also reduces the structure-building burden for novices [Graphic organizers support comprehension for novice learners.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible three-column chart (paper, whiteboard, or digital), completed in the K/W phase *before* instruction and the L phase *after*
- Source material or a lesson to organize — the chart frames a text, unit, or [case study](../elements/case-studies.md)
- Teacher facilitation: students' K entries often contain misconceptions that need gentle surfacing, not silent recording
- Time for discussion so the W column generates genuine questions rather than compliance answers

#### Constraints
- Unfacilitated K columns can *reinforce* misconceptions by having students record and commit to inaccurate prior knowledge [~M] — the teacher must treat K entries as hypotheses to test, not facts to preserve
- Becomes a rote fill-in-the-sheet ritual when the W column is not genuinely used to steer the lesson [-W]
- The L column invites listing of surface facts rather than reflection unless prompts push for "how does this change what I thought?" [~W]
- Low payoff for learners with substantial prior knowledge, for whom the K phase adds little activation value [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **KWL+ / KWHL**: add a fourth column — *How* will I find out (KWHL) or *What more* do I want to know (KWL+) — to extend the routine into inquiry planning and [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- **Group KWL**: complete the chart collaboratively on a shared board, using it as a running anchor chart across a multi-week unit
- **Digital KWL**: shared documents or tools like Padlet allow revisiting and annotating the chart throughout the unit rather than completing it once

### Target Learners
- Novices and intermediate learners encountering an unfamiliar topic, where activation and structure matter most [Graphic organizers support comprehension for novice learners.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- All grade levels; the format scales from early readers (drawn/dictated entries) to adult learners
- Less valuable for expert learners, whose prior knowledge is already well-organized [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Prior-knowledge activation and schema building [Activating prior knowledge improves learning of new material.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Comprehension monitoring and metacognitive reflection
- Question generation and purpose-setting for reading or inquiry

### Instructions
1. **Know**: Before instruction, students individually list what they believe they know about the topic, then share in [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) to surface the range of prior knowledge and misconceptions [Activation](../elements/activation.md).
2. **Want to know**: Students generate questions they want answered; the teacher records these and explicitly connects upcoming instruction to them, giving the lesson visible purpose.
3. **Learn**: During and after instruction, students record what they learned, ideally comparing it against their K and W entries — confirming, revising, or rejecting initial claims.
4. **Reflect**: Close with a brief [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) or written summary: "What surprised you? What changed from what you thought you knew?" Follow with [practice](../elements/practice.md) or application of the new material.

## Examples
- **Elementary science unit on weather**: Students list known weather facts (K), ask "why do tornadoes happen?" (W), and after the unit revise their K list, crossing out misconceptions — making conceptual change visible.
- **[Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/vocabulary/articles/using-kwl-approach-learning)** — publishes classroom-tested KWL procedures and printable templates for literacy instruction.
- **Adult ESL / EFL reading courses**: KWL is widely used to frame expository texts; the W column sets a purpose for reading, and the L column doubles as a summary-writing scaffold.
