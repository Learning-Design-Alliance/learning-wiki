# Rubric-Based Self Assessment

## Description
Rubric-based self assessment asks learners to judge their own draft or performance against explicit, criterion-referenced scoring guidelines before (or alongside) instructor evaluation. The rubric converts tacit quality standards into observable criteria, and the act of applying it trains learners to monitor and regulate their own work. It is typically carried out as: introduce the rubric, model its application, have learners score their own work with evidence, then revise.

## Design Implications

Self assessment works when learners have clear criteria and act on their judgments — it is a core mechanism of [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md), which improves achievement [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The rubric must be written in learner-accessible language; criteria that are technically precise but opaque to students produce compliance scoring rather than genuine evaluation. Self-assigned scores should be compared with instructor or peer scores and discrepancies discussed, because calibration — not just scoring — is what builds accurate self-monitoring.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rubric with specific, observable criteria and level descriptors, shared *before* the work is produced
- Modeling of rubric application on an exemplar ([Worked Examples](../elements/worked-examples.md) or annotated samples) so learners see what "meets the criterion" looks like
- A revision or action step that follows the self-score — assessment without consequence becomes box-ticking
- Evidence requirements: learners must point to specific features of their work justifying each rating

#### Constraints
- Simple checklists applied without judgment are ineffective online; learners tick boxes without evaluating quality [Checklist-style evaluation is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Novices tend to overestimate their performance; uncalibrated self-scores can inflate confidence and reduce effort to revise [-M]
- Vague or holistic rubrics ("good organization") invite leniency bias and self-serving ratings [-M]
- If self-assessment counts directly toward grades under high stakes, scores inflate and the diagnostic value collapses [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Draft-stage self-check** — learners score a draft and revise before submission; strongest form for improving the artifact
- **Post-hoc reflection** — learners score submitted work and compare with instructor feedback; strongest form for building calibration
- **Rubric co-construction** — class translates assignment goals into criteria, increasing ownership and rubric comprehension
- **Self-then-peer** — learners self-score before [peer assessment](../elements/peer-assessment.md), which surfaces discrepancies and moderates leniency

### Target Learners
- Most effective once learners have basic familiarity with the task domain; complete novices lack the knowledge to apply criteria meaningfully [~M]
- Particularly valuable for developing learners in writing, design, and project-based courses where quality criteria are multi-dimensional
- Requires support for younger or lower-prior-knowledge learners: simplified rubrics, exemplars, and teacher-modeled scoring

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: monitoring and evaluating one's own work against standards
- Quality criteria internalization: understanding *what makes* work good, not just producing work
- Revision and iterative improvement of complex artifacts
- Accurate self-efficacy and calibration

### Instructions
1. Share the rubric with the assignment, and unpack criteria with learners using annotated exemplars at different quality levels ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) sharpen criterion boundaries).
2. Model applying the rubric aloud to a sample, verbalizing the evidence for each rating ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Learners score their own draft, citing specific evidence for each criterion rating.
4. Learners identify the lowest-scored criterion and revise targeted to it ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) loop).
5. Compare self-scores with instructor or peer scores; discuss large discrepancies to build calibration.

## Related Strategies
- [Peer Assessment](../elements/peer-assessment.md) — applying the same rubric to others' work; peer and self assessment reinforce each other and peer scoring often calibrates self-scoring
- [Formative Feedback Loops](formative-feedback-loops.md) — self assessment is the learner-driven half of a feedback cycle
- [Portfolio Assessment](portfolio-assessment.md) — rubric-based self evaluation gives portfolios their reflective component

## Examples
- **Writing workshops using a 6+1 Trait rubric** — students score their own drafts on organization and voice before teacher conferencing, then revise the weakest trait.
- **Project-based courses (e.g., PBLWorks/Buck Institute gold-standard PBL)** — students self-assess against public product rubrics at milestone checkpoints and revise before exhibition.
- **Design studio critique** — students present self-scores against a design rubric before peer and instructor critique, making their quality judgments itself an object of feedback.
