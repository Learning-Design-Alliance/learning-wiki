# Mindfulness Practices

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Mindfulness practices bring structured attention to present-moment experience — typically through breathing exercises, brief meditation, body scans, or mindful movement — with the aim of calming the nervous system and strengthening self-regulation. In learning environments they are used as short, repeated routines embedded in the schedule rather than as stand-alone lessons, helping learners manage stress and re-orient attention before and during demanding cognitive work.

## Design Implications

Mindfulness training functions as attention regulation practice: controlled studies show brief mindfulness training improves working memory capacity and reduces mind-wandering, with downstream benefits for reading comprehension and test performance [Mrazek et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535801) [+M]. Effects on academic outcomes are mediated by improved executive attention and emotional regulation, so the practice supports — but does not replace — explicit instruction in content. Benefits depend on regularity and dosage; single sessions produce transient effects at best [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Brief, predictable routines (2–10 minutes) integrated at consistent points in the schedule, not one-off events
- Facilitators with their own practice or training; poorly prepared delivery undermines credibility and uptake
- Voluntary, low-pressure participation norms; framing as attention training rather than discipline management

#### Constraints
- Mandated or punitive use (e.g., as a consequence for misbehavior) reduces buy-in and can be counterproductive [-W]
- Learners with trauma histories may find closed-eye or body-focused exercises distressing; trauma-sensitive adaptations (eyes open, choice of posture, opt-out) are necessary [-M]
- Effects fade without sustained practice; meta-analytic evidence shows weaker and more variable outcomes for one-off or very short programs [~M]
- Time spent on mindfulness competes with instructional time; the tradeoff is only justified when attention and regulation problems are actually limiting learning [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Focused-attention breathing at lesson openings; transition resets between activities; body scans or mindful movement (yoga, stretching) for younger learners
- Digital supports such as [Headspace](https://www.headspace.com) or [Calm](https://www.calm.com) for independent practice
- Educator-facing mindfulness training (e.g., [CARE for Teachers](https://www.care4teachers.com)) targeting teacher stress, which indirectly improves classroom climate

### Target Learners
- Learners with high stress, anxiety, or attentional difficulties, who show the largest gains in regulation and working memory [Mrazek et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535801) [+M]
- Adolescents facing high-stakes assessment periods, where stress management protects performance [~M]
- Less effective as a substitute for structured behavioral support for learners with severe self-regulation challenges [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: noticing and redirecting attention and emotion ([Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md)) [+M]
- Attention management: building the executive control that underlies sustained [Practice](../elements/practice.md)
- Stress reduction: lowering arousal that would otherwise consume working memory resources ([Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md)) [~W]

### Instructions
1. Establish a consistent cue and duration (e.g., three minutes after transitions), framed as attention training, not discipline.
2. Model the practice yourself and guide it actively — brief facilitator [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) in the technique precedes independent use.
3. Start with short, structured exercises (breath counting, sound focus); keep eyes-open options available for trauma-sensitive participation.
4. Connect the practice to academic work: after the exercise, name what attention feels like and set a focus goal for the task ahead.
5. Use brief [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to let learners report focus and arousal levels, building metacognitive awareness of their own states.
6. Fade facilitator guidance over weeks so learners can initiate practice independently, consistent with [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

## Examples
- **[MindUP](https://mindup.org)** — published PreK–8 curriculum pairing daily "brain breaks" (breathing-focused core practice) with social-emotional lessons.
- **[Learning to BREATHE](https://learning2breathe.org)** — mindfulness curriculum designed for adolescents, typically delivered in 6–18 sessions.
- **Mrazek et al. (2014)** — two weeks of daily mindfulness training improved GRE reading comprehension scores and working memory in college students, demonstrating academic transfer.
