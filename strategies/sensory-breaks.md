# Sensory Breaks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Sensory breaks are brief, planned interruptions to academic work (typically 2–10 minutes) that involve physical movement, proprioceptive or vestibular input (stretching, walking, heavy work), or calming activities (breathing, quiet focus). They are carried out at scheduled intervals or when learners show signs of attentional fatigue, and are often offered as a choice from a menu of options.

## Design Implications

Sensory breaks address the limits of sustained attention and self-regulation: vigilance declines over continuous task engagement, and physical activity breaks reliably improve subsequent on-task behavior and attention in classroom studies [~M]. They function as a load-management tool — offloading the effort of maintaining focus so that working-memory resources are available when instruction resumes [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on structure: unstructured or over-stimulating breaks can increase arousal and make re-entry to learning harder.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A predictable routine or signal for when breaks occur, so they do not become negotiation points
- A small menu of break options matched to the learner's regulation needs (activating vs. calming)
- A clear re-entry routine (e.g., a two-minute warning, a transition cue) that returns learners to the task
- Breaks that are genuinely short and distinct from instructional time

#### Constraints
- Breaks that are too long, too stimulating, or poorly timed disrupt momentum and increase time lost to re-engagement [-M]
- Used as escape or reward contingencies, breaks can become avoidance behavior rather than regulation [~M]
- Evidence for academic learning gains is weaker than evidence for on-task behavior; breaks alone do not teach content [-W]
- For learners without attentional or sensory regulation needs, frequent scheduled breaks may fragment instruction without benefit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Movement breaks** (e.g., TAKE 10!, GoNoodle): whole-class physical activity integrated with or between lessons
- **Calming breaks**: breathing exercises, quiet corner, reduced-stimulation space for learners who are over-aroused
- **Individual regulation plans**: scheduled sensory breaks embedded in an [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) plan or IEP, often with a visual break card the learner can request
- **Choice-based breaks**: a [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) menu letting learners select the break type, supporting autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with ADHD, autism, or sensory processing differences, for whom breaks are commonly specified as accommodations [~M]
- Young children, whose sustained attention spans are shorter and for whom recess and movement breaks show the clearest attentional benefits [~M]
- Any learner during long blocks of seatwork; benefits shrink for short, varied tasks
- Learners building self-regulation skills, when breaks are paired with self-monitoring of when they are needed [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention and self-regulation: building the capacity to recognize and manage arousal states
- Behavioral readiness: maximizing on-task time during instruction rather than directly teaching content
- Executive function development: practicing transitions and self-initiated regulation

### Instructions
1. Identify the attentional demands of the learning block and where fatigue predictably occurs; align with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) rather than treating breaks as a separate add-on.
2. Establish a short menu of activating (movement, stretching) and calming (breathing, quiet) options; teach each option explicitly before using it.
3. Schedule breaks at consistent intervals or teach learners to request them via a signal or break card, pairing requests with [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) so learners learn to notice their own state.
4. Keep breaks brief (2–10 minutes) and end with a consistent re-entry routine that returns attention to the task.
5. Fade adult prompting over time, shifting responsibility to the learner as part of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

## Related Strategies
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — breaks work best when the surrounding instruction is segmented into manageable episodes
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — movement embedded in learning tasks can substitute for separate breaks
- [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) — the formal mechanism through which individualized sensory breaks are specified

## Examples
- **[TAKE 10!](https://www.take10.net)** — Classroom program integrating 10-minute physical activity breaks with academic content; field studies report improved on-task behavior.
- **GoNoodle** (https://www.gonoodle.com) — Short video-guided movement and mindfulness breaks used widely in elementary classrooms.
- **Occupational therapy sensory diets** — Individualized schedules of sensory activities (heavy work, deep pressure, movement) embedded in the school day for learners with sensory regulation needs.
