# Shadowing for Behavior Adjustment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Shadowing is a behavior-support strategy in which a dedicated adult stays within arm's reach of a child who has exhibited harmful behaviors (e.g., biting, hitting), intervening before the behavior occurs rather than reacting afterward. The adult quietly blocks, redirects, and coaches replacement behaviors without singling the child out or framing the presence as punishment. Shadowing is typically deployed after a couple of incidents and faded once the child has been incident-free for an extended period (commonly two weeks), with the ultimate aim of teaching self-regulation and prosocial alternatives.

## Design Implications

Shadowing functions as intensive, in-the-moment [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md): the adult delivers immediate antecedent intervention and process-level feedback at the point where the problem behavior is triggered, which is far more effective than delayed correction [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because the adult observes the full antecedent–behavior–consequence chain, shadowing also doubles as continuous functional assessment, revealing the triggers and functions (attention, escape, tangibles) that maintenance of the behavior depends on. Prevention alone is insufficient — the strategy works only when paired with explicit teaching of replacement skills, consistent with applied behavior analysis principles [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) and observational learning of modeled alternatives [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A dedicated adult with the training and availability to remain in proximity throughout the target period
- A shared, specific definition of the target behavior and its triggers among all staff, so intervention is consistent across adults and settings
- A plan for what the adult does *instead* of letting the behavior happen: physical blocking, redirection, and narration of the replacement behavior ("Ask me for the toy with your words")
- A data-collection routine (simple incident tally) so the fade-out decision is criterion-based, not impressionistic
- Communication with families about the purpose and non-punitive framing of the support

#### Constraints
- Highly resource-intensive: one adult per child is rarely sustainable beyond a short window [-M]
- If the shadowed child perceives the proximity as surveillance or punishment, it can produce stigma, avoidance, or escalation [-M]
- Over-prevention can inadvertently deny the child the natural consequences and social feedback that drive learning, and can reinforce the behavior if shadowing delivers adult attention contingent on escalation [~M]
- Effectiveness drops sharply when implementation is inconsistent across staff or settings; intermittent shadowing leaves unpredictable gaps the child will exploit [-M]
- Not appropriate for behaviors maintained primarily by escape: constant proximity can function as punishment for escape-maintained behavior or intensify the motivation to escape [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full shadowing** (arm's reach, all settings) for acute safety risks such as biting
- **Partial or setting-specific shadowing** limited to high-risk periods (transitions, free play), reducing adult cost
- **Faded shadowing**: distance and prompting are systematically reduced as incident-free days accumulate, transferring monitoring responsibility to the child [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- **Peer-inclusive framing**: the adult positions themselves as a helper or activity partner rather than a monitor, reducing stigma

### Target Learners
- Young children (typically preschool through early elementary) exhibiting frequent aggressive or harmful behaviors in group settings
- Children whose behavior is maintained by attention or access to tangibles, where antecedent intervention and redirection are most effective [~M]
- Less suitable for older learners, for low-frequency behaviors that do not justify the staffing cost, or for children who respond to proximity with shame or escalation [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: internalizing monitoring that the adult initially provides externally [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Replacement social skills: requesting turns, using words instead of aggression, tolerating frustration
- Classroom safety and peer relationship protection as enabling conditions for all other learning

### Instructions
1. Define the target behavior precisely and identify likely triggers through brief observation and staff/family interview.
2. Assign a shadowing adult and brief all staff on the behavior definition, intervention script, and data routine.
3. Shadow continuously at arm's reach, intervening before the behavior occurs; narrate and model the replacement behavior in the moment ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. Reinforce the replacement behavior immediately and specifically when the child uses it, and use brief [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) at transitions to preview expectations.
5. Track incidents daily; after two consecutive incident-free weeks, fade proximity and prompting systematically while continuing reinforcement.
6. Review data with the team; if incidents recur during fading, return to the previous support level rather than restarting from zero.

## Examples
- A preschool teacher's aide shadows a four-year-old who has bitten peers during free play for two weeks, physically positioning between the child and peers at signs of escalation, coaching "use your words," and logging incidents until the child goes fourteen days without biting.
- The [Incredible Years](http://www.incredibleyears.com) teacher program embeds the same logic — close adult proximity, proactive redirection, and coached replacement skills — within its classroom management curriculum for young children with conduct problems.
