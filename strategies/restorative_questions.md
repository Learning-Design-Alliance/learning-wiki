# Restorative Questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Restorative questions are a small set of open-ended prompts used in restorative practices to elicit reflection from those who have caused harm and those affected by it. The canonical forms are: *What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who has been affected and how? What do you need to do to make things right?* Rather than assigning blame or prescribing consequences, the facilitator holds the structure while the participants generate the account, the impact analysis, and the repair plan.

## Design Implications

Restorative questions operationalize the principle that learners internalize self-regulation when they are required to articulate their own reasoning and its consequences rather than receive verdicts [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Because the questions demand causal and social reasoning ("who was affected, and how?"), they function as a scaffold for perspective-taking and moral reasoning, with the facilitator fading their support as students take over the dialogue [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Autonomy is central: the person who caused harm retains ownership of the repair plan, which supports intrinsic motivation to follow through [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A facilitator trained to ask without lecturing; the questions lose their function if embedded in an interrogation
- Sufficient relational trust that participants will answer honestly ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and community-building routines establish this baseline)
- Time and privacy for genuine dialogue, not a hallway exchange between bells
- Follow-through structures so agreed repairs are actually completed and revisited

#### Constraints
- In low-trust or punitive-dominant climates, students give strategic or rehearsed answers and the questions become performance, not reflection [-M]
- Ineffective when used as a softer prelude to a predetermined punishment; students detect this and disengage [-M]
- Require facilitator skill and time; whole-staff adoption without training produces inconsistent, sometimes harmful implementation [-W]
- Less appropriate for acute crises or situations involving significant power imbalance (e.g., serious harassment) where immediate protective action must precede dialogue [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Formal conferences**: full scripted dialogue after serious incidents, often with families present
- **Informal "restorative chats"**: two-minute versions of the questions used by teachers in the flow of class
- **Proactive circles**: the same question forms used preventively in community-building, not just reactively after harm
- **Peer mediation**: trained student facilitators run the dialogue, shifting the scaffolding role to peers

### Target Learners
- Adolescents, who are developmentally primed for perspective-taking and identity work and respond better to autonomy-supportive than controlling discipline [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Students with weak self-regulation, for whom externalized reflective structure builds internal monitoring habits [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less effective for very young children without simplified question forms and concrete repair options [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: empathy, accountability, perspective-taking ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Self-regulation: connecting intentions, actions, and consequences
- Community norms: repairing relationships so learning can resume, rather than excluding the learner

### Instructions
1. **Stabilize first.** Ensure everyone is calm; restorative questions do not work in the heat of the moment.
2. **Ask the affective questions to the person harmed**: *What happened? What did you think when you realized it had happened? How has it affected you? What has been the hardest thing for you?*
3. **Ask the person who caused harm**: *What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who has been affected and in what ways? What do you think you need to do to make things right?*
4. **Listen without correcting** the account; use [Active Listening](active_listening.md) — reflect and probe rather than rebut.
5. **Co-construct the repair agreement** and record it; the plan must come from the student, not the facilitator.
6. **Follow up** on the agreement at a set time, treating completion as part of the learning loop.

## Examples
- **International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP) SaferSanerSchools program** — whole-school implementation training built around the restorative questions; evaluated in a randomized trial in Pittsburgh public schools ([https://www.iirp.edu](https://www.iirp.edu)) [+M]
- **Restorative circles in Oakland Unified School District** — district-wide adoption as an alternative to exclusionary discipline, with formal conferences for serious incidents and informal chats for classroom-level conflict
- **Community conferencing in Queensland, Australia** — Morrison's work on restorative practices in schools used the questions in conference settings following bullying and property incidents
