# Reframing Self Talk

## Description
Reframing self talk teaches learners to monitor their internal dialogue, identify automatic negative or catastrophic interpretations ("I'm bad at math," "I always fail under pressure"), and replace them with statements that are accurate, task-focused, and actionable ("I haven't mastered this procedure yet; let me rework step two"). It is typically carried out through explicit instruction, modeled examples, sentence stems or cue cards, and repeated practice during challenging tasks until the replacement dialogue becomes habitual.

## Design Implications

Self talk shapes how learners interpret difficulty: learners who attribute struggle to stable, personal deficits disengage, while those who reframe difficulty as normal, temporary, and controllable persist longer. Reframing works because it interrupts the attribution–emotion–behavior chain before avoidance behavior takes over, and it pairs naturally with [Growth Mindset](../principles/growth-mindset.md) instruction and attribution retraining, both of which show reliable but modest effects on persistence and performance [~M]. The reframe must be *believable* — affirmations learners do not accept as true can backfire [~M] — so effective reframes are realistic and strategy-oriented rather than purely positive ("I can do this if I use my notes" rather than "I'm great at this").

### Context
#### Requirements
- A safe classroom climate where difficulty and error are normalized publicly, not just privately ([Belonging](../elements/belonging.md))
- Explicit modeling of the noticing-and-replacing process by the instructor, ideally with [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) during a genuinely difficult task
- Concrete replacement stems learners can actually use (e.g., "This is hard because it's new, not because I can't")
- Repeated low-stakes opportunities to practice reframing during real academic challenges, not as a one-off lesson

#### Constraints
- Pure positive affirmations that contradict a learner's self-view can increase rumination and lower performance for low self-esteem learners [-M]
- Reframing taught as a standalone lesson without practice in context fades quickly; effects decay without reinforcement during actual difficulty [-M]
- If the task is genuinely beyond reach or support is absent, reframing becomes "toxic positivity" that blames learners for structural problems [-M]
- Less effective when the source of anxiety is a real skill deficit — pair with actual skill instruction ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md), [Practice](../elements/practice.md)) rather than dialogue alone

#### Implementation Variability
- **Sentence stems and cue cards**: written reframes posted or kept at hand for use during independent work
- **Modeled reframing**: instructor verbalizes their own self talk when stuck, showing that experts also experience difficulty
- **Written reappraisal**: brief expressive-writing interventions before high-stakes assessments (e.g., the Ramirez & Beilock test-anxiety writing exercise) externalize worries so working memory is freed
- **Peer reframing**: structured partner language where students reframe each other's statements, building a shared classroom discourse

### Target Learners
- Learners experiencing test anxiety or performance anxiety, where reappraisal and expressive writing improve exam performance by reducing working-memory load [~S]
- Students facing stereotype threat or belonging uncertainty, where attributional reframing (difficulty is common and temporary, not evidence of not belonging) protects outcomes [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+S]
- Adolescents and adults; younger children have limited metacognitive access to their own inner speech and need more scaffolding and external language
- Less useful for learners who already hold accurate, constructive self appraisals — the intervention is redundant and can feel patronizing [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: managing emotional and motivational responses to difficulty
- Dispositional outcomes: persistence, resilience, and willingness to attempt challenging tasks
- Metacognitive awareness: recognizing one's own automatic interpretations as interpretations, not facts
- Indirect academic gains: by sustaining engagement, reframing supports achievement in high-anxiety domains (math, public speaking, testing)

### Instructions
1. **Normalize difficulty.** Open with the message that struggle is a signal of learning, using [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) of unhelpful self talk ("I'm just not a math person") drawn from real, relatable situations.
2. **Model the reframe.** Using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), work through a genuinely hard problem while voicing your own self talk — including the negative thought — and demonstrating the noticing-and-replacing move in real time.
3. **Provide replacement stems.** Give learners a short menu of accurate, strategy-oriented reframes; co-construct additional ones so the language is theirs.
4. **Practice in context.** During challenging [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or before an assessment, prompt learners to catch one negative thought and apply a reframe; use a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) afterward to debrief what they noticed.
5. **Externalize under pressure.** Before high-stakes tests, use a short expressive-writing activity in which learners write about their worries, then reframe them — this combination reduces anxiety's grip on working memory [~S].
6. **Fade the prompts.** Move from teacher-prompted reframing to self-initiated reframing, checking occasionally that the dialogue remains accurate rather than drifting into empty positivity.

## Examples
- **Ramirez & Beilock expressive writing intervention** — students write about test worries for 10 minutes before an exam; anxious students show significant score improvements, especially in high-stakes math testing.
- **Yo Yo Ma's "play it through" reframing** — performance educators teach musicians to replace "don't mess up" with process cues ("listen to the phrase"), a standard technique in performance-psychology coaching.
- **SEL curricula such as Second Step** (https://www.secondstep.org) include self-talk identification and replacement lessons in which students practice turning "I can't" statements into strategy statements during collaborative work.
