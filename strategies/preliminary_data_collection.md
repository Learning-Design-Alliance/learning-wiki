# Preliminary Data Collection

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Preliminary data collection involves gathering contextual data to gain a deeper understanding of the local settings where a problem exists. It combines quantitative sources (achievement records, attendance, discipline referrals disaggregated by subgroup) with qualitative sources (interviews, observations, walkthroughs, student voice) to build a comprehensive picture before designing interventions. The strategy treats data as evidence for problem definition rather than as a record-keeping exercise.

## Design Implications

Data-informed problem framing improves the fit between interventions and local conditions; decisions grounded in multiple data types are more likely to target root causes than surface symptoms [Coburn & Turner's review finds that combining data types and building capacity to interpret them is central to productive data use.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X11425878) [+M]. Because raw data underdetermines interpretation, collection should be paired with structured sense-making — protocols that force examination of disaggregated patterns and disconfirming evidence [Data use improves decisions when paired with routines that surface assumptions rather than confirm intuitions.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X11425878) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to data sources: records systems, observation protocols, survey instruments, and interviewees
- Analytic skills for both quantitative (disaggregation, trend analysis) and qualitative (coding, triangulation) data
- Structured protocols for interpretation, such as [Data-Driven Dialogue](../strategies/data-driven-dialogue.md) or the [Data Wise](../patterns/data-wise-improvement-process.md) cycle
- Time and facilitation for collaborative sense-making, not just collection

#### Constraints
- Data collection is time-consuming and often requires navigating gatekeepers, consent processes, and privacy constraints (e.g., FERPA)
- Quantitative indicators alone can mislead: discipline and achievement data reflect policy and practice, not just learner behavior, and decontextualized numbers invite deficit interpretations [-M]
- Confirmation bias operates strongly: teams tend to select data that supports pre-existing theories of the problem [Data use routines that do not surface assumptions tend to reinforce existing beliefs.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X11425878) [-M]
- Over-collection without a clear problem focus produces paralysis rather than insight

#### Implementation Variability
- Rapid-cycle versions collect a small, targeted dataset in days (e.g., five student interviews plus one week of referral data) within improvement-science approaches
- Participatory versions involve students and community members as co-collectors, e.g., youth participatory action research (YPAR)
- Audit-style versions focus on disaggregated system data to surface inequity patterns before any human data gathering

### Target Learners
- Educator teams, school leaders, and improvement communities diagnosing a local problem of practice
- Students conducting inquiry projects, where collection builds authentic research skills [Case-based and inquiry-oriented learning improves engagement and exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]
- Community researchers and equity audits examining disproportionality

### Target Learning Goals
- Problem definition: moving from vague concerns to specific, evidence-grounded problem statements
- Data literacy: reading, disaggregating, and triangulating quantitative and qualitative sources
- Contextual understanding: how local culture, policies, and practices produce observed patterns
- Question formulation: deriving researchable questions from evidence rather than assumption

### Instructions
1. Define a preliminary problem of practice and identify what evidence would distinguish competing explanations ([Challenge Identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md))
2. Gather quantitative indicators, disaggregated by relevant subgroups, and note anomalies or gaps
3. Collect qualitative data — interviews, [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) conversations, classroom observations — to contextualize the numbers and surface lived experience
4. Triangulate sources and code qualitative data for patterns ([Data Analysis](../elements/data-analysis.md))
5. Facilitate collaborative sense-making with a structured protocol that requires teams to generate multiple interpretations before acting ([Peer Collaboration](../elements/peer-collaboration.md))
6. Summarize findings into a problem statement and revise research questions ([Summarization and Synthesis](../elements/summarization-and-synthesis.md))

## Examples
- A school team reviewing achievement and behavioral data disaggregated by race, then conducting walkthroughs and student focus groups to understand school culture before redesigning discipline practices — surfacing how specific policies reinforced disproportionality.
- [Data Wise](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/data-wise) (Harvard Graduate School of Education) — an improvement process whose "prepare and organize" and "inspect data" phases operationalize preliminary data collection for school teams.
- YPAR projects in which students survey peers and analyze school data to document issues such as restroom access or curriculum representation, then present findings to decision-makers.
