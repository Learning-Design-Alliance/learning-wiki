Create a Character Profile¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 18 studies (8 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 3 of 18 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Creating a character profile helps students form a deeper understanding of a book's characters. Learners compile evidence-based portraits of a character — lists of supporting adjectives, illustrations grounded in textual description, predictions about the character's future, or reflections on how the character makes them feel at a given point in the text. The profile format forces students to move from passive reading to active synthesis of textual evidence.

Design Implications¶

Character profiling converts reading comprehension into a generative task: students must locate, interpret, and organize textual evidence before producing their profile, which supports deeper encoding than re-reading or summarizing alone Generative activities that require learners to construct representations improve comprehension over passive review. [+M]. Because profiles ask students to infer motives, feelings, and traits beyond what is stated explicitly, the activity exercises inferential comprehension and perspective-taking Building empathy through structured perspective-taking activities improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]. The creative components (illustration, adjective selection) also support dual coding, pairing verbal character descriptions with visual representations Pairing verbal and visual representations improves recall. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dedicated time for character analysis, creative materials for illustration, and prompts for reflection
  • A text with sufficiently developed characters to support evidence-based inference
  • Prompts that require textual justification ("What did the character do or say that shows this trait?"), not just opinion
  • An opportunity to share or discuss profiles, so students encounter alternative interpretations (Class Discussion)

Constraints¶

  • Profiles built on unsupported impressions rather than textual evidence can reinforce shallow or inaccurate character readings [-M] — require evidence citations for each trait claimed
  • Students with weak inferencing skills may produce superficial trait lists without scaffolding Contingent scaffolding improves learning outcomes. [+M] — provide trait banks, sentence starters, or modeled examples first
  • Over-emphasis on illustration or decoration can displace analytical work; the visual should encode meaning, not merely decorate Decorative illustrations that do not support the content do not improve learning. [+S]
  • Less effective for texts with flat or archetypal characters, where there is little to infer

Implementation Variability¶

  • Trait inventory: students list adjectives with page-referenced evidence for each
  • Illustrated profile: students draw the character using only details from the text, annotating the drawing with the textual source of each feature
  • Future projection: students predict a child character's adult occupation, citing traits that support the prediction
  • Affective response: students describe how the character makes them feel in a specific chapter and why, connecting emotion to textual events
  • Comparative profiles: students profile the same character at two points in the story to track development, or profile two contrasting characters side by side

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 students reading narrative texts; particularly effective in upper elementary and middle grades where character inference is a curricular focus
  • Emerging readers who benefit from concrete visual and organizational structures for comprehension Chunking information into organized structures reduces working memory load. [+S]
  • Multilingual learners, when paired with trait vocabulary banks and sentence frames Accessible vocabulary and syntax supports comprehension for language learners. [+M]
  • Students needing support with perspective-taking and social inference

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Inferential comprehension: deriving character traits, motives, and relationships from textual evidence
  • Literary analysis: distinguishing explicit description from inference, and supporting claims with evidence
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: understanding characters' feelings and motivations Building empathy through structured perspective-taking activities improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
  • Vocabulary development: applying precise descriptive language

Instructions¶

  1. Select a focal character (individually, or the same character for whole-class comparison).
  2. Gather evidence: students annotate the text for actions, dialogue, and descriptions that reveal character.
  3. Draft the profile: list traits with evidence, or complete a profile template (name, traits, relationships, motivations, key quotes).
  4. Add a creative layer: illustrate the character from textual details, or write a prediction about the character's future, each tied to evidence.
  5. Reflect: students describe how the character makes them feel in a specific chapter and connect that feeling to textual events (Individual Reflection).
  6. Share and compare: students present profiles and discuss divergent interpretations, revising where peers cite evidence they missed (Application).

Related Strategies¶

  • Literature circles — profiles serve as preparation artifacts for role-based discussion of a shared text
  • Character mapping — extends single-character profiles into relationship diagrams across a whole text
  • Act It Out — dramatization draws on the same character analysis; a completed profile can script a performance

Examples¶

  • ReadWorks (https://www.readworks.org) — paired fiction passages with character-focused comprehension question sets that function as abbreviated profiles.
  • CommonLit (https://www.commonlit.org) — free texts with guided reading questions asking students to characterize figures and cite evidence, directly supporting the profile task.
  • A grade-4 unit on Because of Winn-Dixie: students illustrate Opal from textual details, list three traits with page references, and predict her father's future, then compare profiles in small groups.
