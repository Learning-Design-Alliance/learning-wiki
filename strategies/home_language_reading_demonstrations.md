# Home Language Reading Demonstrations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
When newcomer multilingual students arrive in a classroom, the teacher asks them to read a book in their home language — aloud to the teacher, a peer, or a small group — as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of what they already know about reading. The teacher observes fluency, comprehension strategies, print conventions, and expression without needing to speak the language, then uses what is observed to plan instruction. By using the collective skills and knowledge of all students (both ELL and English-L1 students) in the classroom, even a monolingual teacher can tap into these valuable language resources that ELLs have, with confidence that these methods will promote language development in English as well as the home language.

## Design Implications

The strategy rests on the linguistic interdependence hypothesis: literacy-related skills transfer across languages, so reading ability demonstrated in the home language predicts and supports English reading development [Cummins' interdependence hypothesis: L1 literacy supports L2 literacy.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. It also functions as asset-based assessment — the demonstration reveals competence that an English-only assessment would mask, and it positions the multilingual student as an expert rather than a deficit [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to books in students' home languages (school library, public library, digital collections such as [Unite for Literacy](https://www.uniteforliteracy.com) or the [International Children's Digital Library](https://en.childrenslibrary.org))
- A low-stakes, welcoming setting — the student reads to a willing audience, not for a grade
- A structured observation protocol so the teacher can record what to look for (directionality, self-correction, use of pictures, expression) even without understanding the words
- Peer or family members who can answer comprehension questions in the home language when possible

#### Constraints
- Books may be difficult to find in all languages spoken in the classroom, particularly for less commonly taught languages [-W]
- A monolingual teacher cannot directly verify comprehension; observations of fluency can overstate or understate actual understanding [~W] — pair the demonstration with retellings or translations by peers, family, or bilingual staff
- Students with interrupted formal education may have strong oral language but no home-language literacy to demonstrate; the demonstration will not reveal reading skill that does not yet exist [-M]
- If framed as a test rather than a celebration, the activity can embarrass newcomers, especially adolescents sensitive to peer perception [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- One-to-one reading conference with the teacher observing
- Peer reading: the student reads to a bilingual classmate or a younger student, with the teacher observing
- Recorded demonstrations (audio or video) shared with families, inviting family commentary
- Repeated demonstrations over the year to document growth in the home language alongside English growth

### Target Learners
- Newcomer English language learners with some home-language schooling, who can show what reading knowledge transfers [L1 literacy skills predict L2 reading outcomes.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Younger children, who are typically less self-conscious reading aloud than adolescents
- Less applicable to students with interrupted formal education or home languages without a shared written form the teacher can observe

### Target Learning Goals
- Assessment: establishing a baseline of literacy skills in the home language to inform English reading instruction
- Identity and belonging: affirming that home languages and cultures are valued classroom resources [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Oral language development: expressive reading aloud supports language growth in both languages [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Survey the class's home languages and gather books at a range of levels; invite families to contribute favorites.
2. Invite the student to choose a book and an audience ([choice supports motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]).
3. Have the student read aloud while the teacher observes using a non-language-specific protocol (book handling, directionality, self-monitoring, expression) — a form of [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) through [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md).
4. Follow with comprehension conversation via a bilingual peer, family member, or translation tool, so the demonstration includes [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in retelling, not just decoding.
5. Record observations and use them to plan English reading instruction, treating the home-language profile as the foundation rather than starting from zero.
6. Repeat periodically to document biliterate growth.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- A third-grade classroom with speakers of Spanish, Arabic, and Haitian Creole holds a weekly "read in your language" circle; the teacher logs fluency observations and pairs each newcomer with a bilingual reading buddy for retellings.
- A secondary newcomer program uses recorded home-language read-alouds posted to a class site, letting students demonstrate reading to authentic audiences while the teacher builds a biliteracy portfolio.
