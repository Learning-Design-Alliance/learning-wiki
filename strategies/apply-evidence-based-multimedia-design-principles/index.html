Educators should apply evidence-based multimedia design principles, prioritising the largest-effect strategies¶

Description¶

The article closes by highlighting "a range of evidence-based strategies that could be implemented by educators", drawn from the meta-meta-analytic comparison of design principles on a common metric. Educators and instructional designers can implement captioning for second-language video, integrated (contiguous) layouts, signaling, spoken rather than on-screen text when images accompany content, segmenting, personalisation, and removal of seductive details, matching the strategy to the medium's pacing and complexity.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Educators need to know which design principle fits their medium (system- vs learner-paced) and material complexity before applying it

Constraints¶

  • Verbal redundancy only helps when adding text to audio, not audio to text; decorative animations showed no benefit; 3D pedagogical agents showed no benefit

Claims¶

  • Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits for comprehension and vocabulary acquisition [+W]
  • Presenting related material contiguously in space and time yields large learning benefits [+W]
  • Design principle effects are larger in system-paced and in complex media, and learner prior knowledge was not a significant moderator (p = 0.14) [+W]
  • Multimedia design interventions yield small average improvements in cognitive load management (g = 0.22), with personalisation and modality showing the largest effects [+W]
  • Modality effect: hearing text alongside visuals improves learning more than reading text alongside visuals [+W]
  • Multimedia design principles improve learning by g = 0.38 on average across the 11 largest reviews, an effect the authors call moderate, with large differences between principles [+W]
  • Signaling Improves Learning [+M]
  • Signaling principle: highlighting essential words improves transfer from multimedia lessons [+W]
  • Segmenting Improves Multimedia Learning [+M]
