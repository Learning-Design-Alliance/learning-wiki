# Thinking Critically

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Thinking critically is a strategy in which learners systematically question, analyze, and evaluate ideas rather than receiving them passively — identifying assumptions, weighing evidence, considering alternatives, and justifying conclusions. It is carried out through tasks that demand reasoning: structured dialogue, argument construction, case analysis, and explicit instruction in evaluative criteria. Critical thinking is not a generic skill applied uniformly across domains; it operates through domain-specific knowledge and dispositions that must be taught deliberately.

## Design Implications

Critical thinking instruction works best when it is explicit, embedded in subject content, and socially mediated through dialogue — meta-analyses find that immersion in authentic problems combined with dialogue and mentoring outperforms both standalone "thinking skills" courses and passive immersion [Instructional interventions improve critical thinking outcomes most when combining dialogue, authentic problems, and mentoring.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063) [+M]. Structured argumentation activities produce measurable gains in reasoning quality [Argumentation activities improve reasoning and critical thinking outcomes.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M], and prompting learners to consider alternative perspectives reduces bias in judgment [Considering the opposite reduces judgmental biases.](../claims/considering-the-opposite-reduces-bias.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Subject-matter content worth thinking about — critical thinking cannot be practiced on content-free exercises
- Explicit instruction in evaluative criteria (what counts as evidence, what makes an inference valid), not just opportunities to "discuss"
- Structured dialogue formats that require learners to articulate and defend positions ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Tasks with genuine uncertainty or multiple defensible answers, so evaluation is necessary rather than decorative
- Instructor modeling of expert reasoning — making doubts, revisions, and criteria visible ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

#### Constraints
- Standalone critical thinking courses disconnected from content show weak transfer; skills taught in one domain rarely migrate without deliberate bridging [Standalone critical thinking courses produce smaller gains than embedded instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.11.002) [-M]
- Novices lack the domain knowledge needed to evaluate claims meaningfully; asking them to "think critically" about unfamiliar content produces shallow or confabulated reasoning [~S]
- Unstructured discussion can entrench misconceptions when learners share flawed reasoning without corrective feedback [~M]
- Learners from educational backgrounds emphasizing reproduction of taught content may initially resist tasks requiring them to challenge or evaluate material; expectations and norms must be taught explicitly [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Embedded vs. standalone:** infusing evaluative tasks into existing content instruction outperforms separate thinking-skills modules
- **Dialogue intensity:** ranges from structured [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) formats to [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) seminars to written [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)
- **Domain adaptation:** the same architecture (claim → evidence → criteria → justification) applies to evaluating historical sources, experimental designs, or workplace decisions, but the criteria differ by domain
- **Digital contexts:** curricula such as Stanford's civic online reasoning program show that lateral reading and source evaluation can be taught directly with strong effects [Explicit instruction in civic online reasoning improves learners' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults with enough domain knowledge to have something to evaluate; gains are strongest in postsecondary and adult settings [Meta-analytic evidence for critical thinking interventions is strongest in postsecondary populations.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063) [+M]
- Learners who already hold relevant content knowledge but have not been taught to interrogate it — the strategy converts passive knowledge into usable judgment
- Less effective for complete novices, who need foundational knowledge first; premature critical thinking demands overload limited working memory [Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluation and analysis: judging the quality of evidence, arguments, and sources
- Argumentation: constructing and defending justified positions [Argumentation activities improve reasoning and critical thinking outcomes.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Epistemic cognition: understanding how knowledge is warranted and why claims differ in reliability
- Decision-making: applying criteria to reach and justify defensible choices

### Instructions
1. Select content with genuine cognitive tension — a contested claim, ambiguous data, or competing interpretations — sufficient to provoke [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md)
2. Activate relevant knowledge with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or framing question so learners have the raw material evaluation requires
3. Model expert evaluation aloud ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)): demonstrate checking a source, testing an assumption, or revising a conclusion against criteria
4. Engage learners in structured dialogue ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)) with roles, protocols, or assigned positions that require justification, not just opinion
5. Have learners produce an artifact of reasoning — a written argument, decision memo, or annotated evaluation ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)) — and provide feedback against explicit criteria ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
6. Repeat across varied cases in the same domain so criteria become generalizable ([Application](../elements/application.md))

### Affordances
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — critical thinking tasks are inherently active; learners generate, defend, and revise ideas rather than receive them, which is the mechanism behind its exam-performance benefits
- [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) — the strategy operationalizes this principle by posing questions that require learners to go beyond reproduction: evaluate, justify, connect, and challenge
- [Analogical Reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) — comparing multiple cases side by side helps learners abstract the underlying criteria rather than memorizing surface judgments
