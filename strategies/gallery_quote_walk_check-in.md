# Gallery Quote Walk Check-In

## Description
Students read a set of quotes displayed around the room and choose the one that best reflects their current emotional state. They gather beneath their chosen quote, and volunteers explain why it resonates. The activity functions as a low-stakes [check-in](../elements/check-in.md): it surfaces affect, builds emotional vocabulary, and establishes social presence before academic work begins.

## Design Implications

Naming emotions reduces their intensity and improves regulation — the "affect labeling" effect, in which putting feelings into words dampens amygdala reactivity [Lieberman et al. found affect labeling reduces amygdala response.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]. The gallery format scaffolds this labeling by offering curated language students can select rather than generate, lowering the barrier for students with limited emotional vocabulary. Movement and choice also increase engagement relative to seated discussion openers [Active learning improves performance relative to lecture-only formats.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of 6–10 quotes displaying a *range* of emotional valences (not only positive ones), posted around the room
- 3–5 minutes for silent reading and individual reflection before grouping
- A classroom climate where sharing is invited but never coerced — volunteers only
- A brief teacher debrief that normalizes the full range of emotions expressed

#### Constraints
- Students who cannot find a resonant quote, or who do not wish to disclose, may feel excluded or exposed; an "add your own" blank quote station and an explicit right-to-pass mitigate this [-M]
- In classrooms without established psychological safety, public sharing can increase anxiety rather than reduce it; the activity presumes, and should build toward, a supportive climate [Social-emotional interventions work best in safe, well-sequenced programs.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) [~M]
- Repeated verbatim use dulls novelty; quotes must be refreshed or re-themed to retain attention

#### Implementation Variability
- **Silent variant:** students write the quote's number on an index card or sticky note instead of gathering — appropriate for groups early in community-building
- **Digital variant:** quotes posted on a shared slide or board (e.g., Padlet, Mentimeter word cloud) for remote or hybrid classes
- **Content-linked variant:** quotes drawn from the current unit's texts, connecting emotional check-in to disciplinary content
- **Student-curated variant:** students submit quotes in advance, increasing ownership and relevance

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults, who have the emotional vocabulary and self-awareness to map feelings onto quotes; simplify quote language for younger students
- Students who benefit from structured, low-stakes disclosure before discussion-heavy work
- Less suitable for groups in acute conflict or crisis, where individual counseling rather than group disclosure is warranted

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: expanding the vocabulary and precision with which students identify affect
- Community building: establishing shared social presence and normalizing a range of emotional states
- Self-regulation: practicing the labeling step that precedes regulation [Affect labeling reduces the intensity of negative affect.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Post 6–10 quotes around the room, ensuring a range of valences, plus one blank "write your own" station.
2. Give students 3–5 minutes of silent reflection to walk, read, and choose.
3. Ask students to gather beneath their chosen quote.
4. Invite volunteers to share why the quote resonates; enforce a right-to-pass so sharing remains voluntary.
5. Close with a 1–2 minute teacher synthesis that names the range of emotions present and transitions to the lesson.

## Examples
- A high school English teacher posts quotes from the current novel before a seminar discussion; students gather by quote, then the groups discuss whether the quote captures the protagonist's state — linking the check-in to textual analysis.
- A university first-year seminar uses a Padlet-based gallery walk each Monday; the instructor scans the distribution to gauge cohort mood and adjusts pacing accordingly.
