# Teaching Academic Vocabulary

## Description
Explicitly teach academic vocabulary — the specialized terms and language structures learners need to access disciplinary content — rather than expecting it to be absorbed incidentally from reading and listening. Effective instruction introduces terms in meaningful context, contrasts technical definitions with everyday meanings (e.g., "product," "table," "similar"), and scaffolds understanding with visuals, real objects, graphic organizers, and anchor charts. It also treats learners' home languages as a resource, using cognates, translated glossaries, and multilingual group work.

## Design Implications

Vocabulary knowledge is one of the strongest correlates of reading comprehension, and direct instruction of high-utility words reliably outperforms incidental exposure [Teaching word meanings directly improves comprehension of texts containing those words.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+S]. Because working memory is limited, unfamiliar terms act as load-inducing bottlenecks; pre-teaching and visual supports reduce this burden so learners can attend to the concept rather than decoding the language [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Pairing words with images and gestures exploits dual coding pathways, producing richer memory traces than definitions alone [Dual coding improves recall when verbal and visual channels are both engaged.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identification of a small set of high-utility, high-leverage terms per unit (Tier 2 and Tier 3 words), not exhaustive glossaries
- Contextual introduction — terms met inside content, not as isolated lists
- Visual and concrete scaffolds: diagrams, manipulatives, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), anchor charts, word walls
- Explicit contrast between everyday and technical meanings of polysemous words
- Resources in learners' home languages: cognate lists, translated glossaries, permission to use home languages in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and group work

#### Constraints
- Isolated, definitional drill (look-up-and-copy) produces fragile knowledge that does not transfer to reading or discourse [-M]
- Teaching too many words at once dilutes depth of processing; breadth trades off against depth [-M]
- Translated materials may not exist for all home languages; over-reliance on translation can delay engagement with the technical register itself [~W]
- Vocabulary taught once and never revisited decays rapidly without spaced retrieval [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-teaching** key terms before a text or lesson vs. **embedded instruction** at the moment of need
- **Frayer model / four-square organizers** (definition, characteristics, example, non-example) vs. **semantic maps** connecting new terms to known networks
- **Word walls and anchor charts** as persistent environmental supports vs. **personal glossaries** in learners' own words
- **Home-language leveraging**: cognate awareness (e.g., *fraction/fracción*), translated word walls, multilingual peer explanation

### Target Learners
- English language learners, who must acquire content and the academic register simultaneously [Structured vocabulary instruction with home-language supports improves ELL comprehension outcomes.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Learners from low-income backgrounds who typically enter school with smaller school-relevant vocabularies and rely more on direct instruction [+S]
- All learners encountering Tier 3 disciplinary terms for the first time; less needed for learners who already control the academic register [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of disciplinary texts and discourse
- Accurate use of technical terms in explanation and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)
- Word-consciousness: metalinguistic awareness of how academic language works, including multiple meanings

### Instructions
1. Select 5–10 high-leverage terms per unit; prioritize words that appear across contexts and unlock the content.
2. Pre-assess informally which terms learners already know ([Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge, including home-language equivalents).
3. Introduce each term in context with a student-friendly definition, a visual or object, and an everyday-meaning contrast where relevant ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Provide multiple encounters across modalities: say it, see it, act it out ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)), use it in a sentence about the current content.
5. Have learners generate their own examples, non-examples, and "in your own words" definitions ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
6. Post terms on a word wall or anchor chart and require their use in discussion and written work; revisit on a spaced schedule.
7. Draw on home languages: highlight cognates, accept multilingual explanations, and invite learners to say which strategies help them.

## Examples
- **Math word wall** with three sections: key vocabulary, "in your own words" definitions, and multiple representations (symbol, diagram, example) of each term — e.g., *function* shown as equation, table, and graph.
- **Polysemy mini-lesson**: contrasting everyday "similar" (roughly alike) with mathematical "similar" (same shape, scaled) using non-examples.
- **Cognate support for Spanish speakers**: *coeficiente/coefficient*, *ángulo/angle* posted bilingually; learners permitted to draft explanations in Spanish before translating.
- **[Word Generation](https://wordgeneration.org)** (SERP Institute) — a free middle-school program that teaches high-utility academic words through discussion, debate, and reading across subject areas.
