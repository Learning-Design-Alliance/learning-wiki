# Accountable Talk

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Accountable Talk is a framework for academically productive classroom conversation developed at the University of Pittsburgh's Institute for Learning. It holds talk accountable to three things: the **learning community** (responding to and building on others' contributions), **accurate knowledge** (getting facts and reasoning right), and **rigorous thinking** (using evidence and valid reasoning). Teachers establish talk norms, model moves such as revoicing and probing, and press students to explain, justify, and challenge claims rather than simply answer.

## Design Implications

Accountable Talk converts classroom discussion from recitation into collective reasoning, which improves conceptual understanding and reasoning quality [Argumentation improves reasoning and conceptual learning.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the teacher actively scaffolding discourse moves — unstructured "discussion" without norms or pressing rarely produces the same gains [Active learning improves exam performance, but structure matters.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task or question genuinely worth discussing — one with multiple defensible positions or solution paths
- Explicit norms and sentence frames ("I agree/disagree because…", "Can you show evidence for…?") established early
- Teacher facilitation moves: revoicing, pressing for reasoning, waiting, and redirecting questions to peers rather than answering them
- Psychological safety — students must believe that respectful disagreement is expected, not punished

#### Constraints
- Dominant voices and status differences can silence lower-status students unless structures like [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) or assigned roles equalize participation [-M]
- Works poorly when the goal is simple factual recall with one correct answer — the overhead of negotiation adds little [~M]
- Requires substantial teacher skill in facilitation; poorly moderated talk can entrench misconceptions rather than surface and resolve them [-M]
- Large classes and short sessions limit the depth of whole-group exchanges

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-group talk** for building shared norms and modeling reasoning
- **Small-group or partner talk** for higher participation density, often with [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) structures
- **Talk moves toolkit** (Institute for Learning): revoice, add on, press for reasoning, press for precision, challenge
- **Written-first variants** (talk prompts on cards, chat-based discussion) for online or introverted learners

### Target Learners
- K–12 and higher-education learners in discussion-rich subjects (literacy, mathematics, science, social studies)
- Especially valuable for multilingual learners, who get structured language models for academic reasoning [+W]
- Requires sufficient prior knowledge to contribute meaningfully; pair with [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of prior knowledge first

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and conceptual change through [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)
- Reasoning, justification, and evidence-based claims
- Academic language and discourse practices
- Social norms of intellectual community

### Instructions
1. Select an open, discussable task and prepare anticipated student responses.
2. Establish talk norms and sentence frames; model the moves yourself first.
3. Open with an individual or [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) phase so every student drafts a position before public talk.
4. Facilitate with talk moves: revoice ("So you're saying…"), press ("How do you know?"), and hand off ("Who can agree or disagree with that, and why?").
5. Keep the talk accountable to evidence — require reference to texts, data, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) rather than opinion.
6. Close by publicly summarizing the reasoning trajectory and naming the talk moves students used well.

## Examples
- **[Institute for Learning](https://ifl.pitt.edu)** (University of Pittsburgh) — source of the Accountable Talk framework, talk moves toolkits, and professional development materials.
- **Reading apprenticeship in middle-school ELA** — students discuss a shared text, with the teacher pressing for claims anchored to specific passages.
- **Reform mathematics classrooms** (e.g., CGI-informed lessons) — students present solution strategies and critique peers' reasoning during number-talk routines.
