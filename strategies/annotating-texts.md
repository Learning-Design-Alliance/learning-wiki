# Annotating Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 6 studies (3 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 6 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Annotating texts is a reading strategy in which learners actively mark up a text while reading — highlighting or underlining key ideas, writing margin notes, coding with symbols, posing questions, or summarizing passages in their own words. It converts passive reading into generative processing: the reader must decide what matters, why it matters, and how it connects to prior knowledge. Annotation can be learner-generated or instructor-provided (e.g., pre-annotated exemplars or embedded prompts), and ranges from simple marking to structured schemes (claim/evidence/question codes).

## Design Implications

Annotation works because it forces selection and organization of ideas rather than verbatim rereading, but its effectiveness depends on *how* learners annotate: marking alone is weak, while annotation that requires elaboration (paraphrasing, questioning, connecting) is strong [Ponce et al. (2020) meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09523-9) [+M]. Instructor-provided highlighting can help novices by modeling what experts select, but learners who construct their own annotations generally show deeper comprehension than those given pre-made ones [Learner-constructed graphic organizers outperform provided ones.](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [+M]. Underlining and highlighting as practiced by students is among the least effective study techniques when used without elaboration [Dunlosky et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [-S] — the marking itself must be paired with generative written response to pay off.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text worth annotating: dense enough that selection matters, and reusable (print, PDF, or annotation-capable platform)
- An explicit annotation scheme or rubric, especially for novices — what to mark, how many marks, and what written notes should do
- Instruction or modeling of expert annotation before independent use ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of an expert marking a passage)
- A follow-up use for the annotations (discussion, writing, exam prep) so learners see them as functional, not decorative
- A text worth engaging with closely (dense, concept-laden, or argumentative)
- Explicit instruction in annotation moves: what to mark, how to code, what to write in margins
- A purpose or task that the annotation serves (discussion prep, writing, problem-solving)
- For social annotation: a platform and norms for responding to peers' notes

#### Constraints
- Untrained highlighting is near-useless or harmful: learners mark too much, confuse emphasis with understanding, and reread highlighted text instead of self-testing [Dunlosky et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [-S]
- Over-marking eliminates the selection benefit — if everything is highlighted, nothing is [Experimenter underlining can be effective when selective, but student underlining often is not.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M]
- Annotation adds cognitive load for novices who lack the background to judge relevance; without a scheme, marking competes with comprehension [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]
- Copying or paraphrasing annotations from provided notes without engaging the text produces shallow processing [~M]
- Over-marking eliminates the selection benefit — if everything is highlighted, nothing is prioritized; visual salience effects are unreliable guides to importance [Marking text based on salience does not reliably improve learning of the marked content.](../theories/von-restorff-effect-text-marking.md) [~W]
- Novices often highlight details rather than main ideas, because they cannot yet distinguish them
- Heavy annotation demands can overload working memory for difficult texts or struggling readers [Cognitive overload from excessive concurrent demands degrades comprehension.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured codes**: claim/evidence/question or +/!/ ? schemes that standardize what marks mean
- **Margin summarization**: one-sentence gist per paragraph or section, a generative form with stronger evidence than marking alone [+M]
- **Instructor-provided annotation**: pre-highlighted or annotated exemplars as models, faded over time
- **Social annotation**: shared annotation layers (e.g., Perusall, Hypothesis) where learners read and respond to each other's margin notes
- **Digital annotation**: tools like Hypothesis, Perusall, and Kindle notes enable search, aggregation, and reuse of marks
- **Structured codes**: symbols for claim/evidence/question/confusion (e.g., "Say Something" or SOAPStone protocols)
- **Question-annotating**: learners write questions in margins rather than statements, priming discussion
- **Social annotation**: shared markup where learners see and reply to peers' annotations (Perusall, Hypothesis)
- **Teacher-modeled annotation**: instructor annotates a short passage live, thinking aloud, before learners annotate independently
- **Annotation as assessment**: graded annotation quality replaces reading quizzes

### Target Learners
- Intermediate readers who can decode fluently but need to learn disciplinary selection criteria; complete novices need a provided scheme first [~M]
- Students preparing for writing or discussion tasks, where annotations serve as raw material
- Less beneficial for beginning readers, for whom annotation competes with decoding for limited working-memory resources [~M]
- Adolescent and adult readers with decoding fluency, who can devote working memory to comprehension strategies [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension strategies.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Students preparing for discussion, writing, or exams who need a durable record of engagement
- Less suitable as an untaught expectation: learners must be trained in annotation moves before independent use

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and retention of expository text: identifying main ideas and supporting detail [+M]
- Disciplinary reading practices: learning what experts in a field notice and question
- Preparation for synthesis tasks: annotations as scaffolding for essays, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), and [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)
- Comprehension and close reading of complex texts
- Self-regulated learning: monitoring understanding and flagging confusion
- Preparation for discussion, argumentation, and evidence-based writing

### Instructions
1. Model the practice: annotate a short passage on a shared display, thinking aloud about selection criteria ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. Provide a simple scheme (e.g., underline = main idea; margin ? = confusion; margin ! = surprising or important) and a quota (e.g., no more than 10–15% marked) to force selection.
3. Require a generative component: a margin paraphrase, question, or connection for each marked passage — marking alone is insufficient [-S].
4. Have learners use the annotations within 24–48 hours for a discussion, summary, or self-quiz, converting marks into retrieval practice.
5. Fade the scheme: shift from instructor-specified codes to learner-designed annotation as expertise grows, and compare learner annotations against an expert-annotated exemplar to calibrate.
