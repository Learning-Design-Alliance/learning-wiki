# Strengths Based Feedback

## Description
Strengths based feedback leads with an accurate diagnosis of what a learner is doing well — specific strategies, skills, or dispositions that are working — and then frames improvement as an extension or transfer of those strengths. It is not praise: the strength named must be genuine, specific, and connected to the learning goal, and it must be followed by actionable next steps. The approach draws on positive psychology's shift from deficit repair to capacity building [Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.5) and on feedback research showing that effectiveness depends on what level the feedback addresses [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

## Design Implications

Strengths based feedback works because it directs attention to successful *processes* learners can reuse, rather than only to errors to avoid — consistent with evidence that process-level feedback outperforms person-level praise [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Naming a working strategy also supports learner autonomy and competence, which sustains motivation for revision [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]. The critical design decision is specificity: "good job" is inert, while "your topic sentences each state a claim you then evidence — apply that same move to your conclusion" gives the learner a transferable model of their own best work.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine, specific identification of a strength tied to the learning goal — not generic encouragement
- An explicit bridge from the strength to the next improvement ("do more of this, here")
- A climate where feedback is expected and acted on ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Opportunities for learners to act on the feedback, not just receive it

#### Constraints
- Unearned or inflated praise reduces motivation and encourages avoidance of challenge [-S] — learners discount insincere positivity and may disengage from hard tasks to protect the inflated reputation
- Strengths framing that stops at affirmation without next steps produces no measurable learning gain [~M] — the improvement component is doing the work, not the positivity
- Can misfire with learners who expect and trust direct error-focused feedback; abrupt strengths framing can read as evasion or condescension [~W]
- In high-stakes evaluative contexts, learners may read strengths language as a soft prelude to a grade, muting its motivational effect [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written comments on work**: open with the strongest move the learner made, then link revision requests to it
- **Conferencing**: short one-to-one [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) that surface a strength and co-plan one next step
- **Portfolio or strengths inventories**: learners document recurring strengths across tasks and reference them when planning new work
- **Peer feedback protocols**: structured sentence frames ("A strength of your argument is…; you could extend it by…") so peers deliver strengths-first comments reliably

### Target Learners
- Struggling or discouraged learners, for whom deficit-only feedback erodes self-efficacy and engagement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Developing writers and novices who benefit from seeing their own successful strategies made explicit [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Advanced learners may need less strengths framing and more direct challenge; over-scaffolding praise can feel patronizing [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill refinement: extending existing successful strategies to harder cases
- Self-regulated learning: building learners' awareness of what they do well so they can deploy it deliberately
- Motivation and persistence: sustaining engagement through revision cycles

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the work against the learning goal and identify the single strongest genuine move ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Name the strength specifically, describing the *strategy* not the person ("your evidence selection," not "you're a natural writer") [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
3. Bridge to improvement: state one next step that reuses or extends that strength.
4. Deliver the feedback in a low-stakes channel — comments, conference, or [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) — before or alongside grading.
5. Have the learner act on it: revise, apply the named strategy to a new task, or articulate the strength themselves ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
6. Follow up over time with [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) that tracks whether the named strength is being transferred.

## Related Strategies
- Action-oriented feedback — shares the requirement that feedback specify what to do next; strengths based feedback adds the strengths-first entry point
- Feedback loops — strengths based feedback is most effective inside short cycles where learners can act on it immediately
- Writing conferences — a natural delivery format combining strengths identification with co-planned next steps

## Examples
- **Writing instruction**: A teacher comments, "Your second paragraph uses a counterexample to sharpen your claim — that's your strongest move. Try opening your conclusion with one," converting a strength into a revision instruction.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Mastery-based progress indicators surface what learners have already demonstrated, so hints and next-step recommendations build from established skills rather than framing the learner as behind.
- **Strengths-based advising in medical education** — Programs such as those described in the academic medicine literature use "asking what went well" debriefs after clinical encounters before reviewing errors, improving trainee receptiveness to corrective feedback.
