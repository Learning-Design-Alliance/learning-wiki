Present pre-service teachers with a realistic cyberbullying case resolved through successive collective phases¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 2 studies (2 causal), q2–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends confronting trainee teachers with an adapted case of adolescent cyberbullying (sexting) that they resolve collaboratively across phases, since "The proximity of the case to their lives, the emotional attachment to the case, as well as the complexity of its resolution, may allow the emergence of collective intelligence". The case was judged by two experts in digital school coexistence before use.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Case design and question design must fulfil the conditions for the emergence of collective intelligence, including bottom-up factors such as emotional intelligence and cognitive diversity

Constraints¶

  • If the necessary conditions for the emergence of collective intelligence had not been fulfilled, the pre-service teachers might not have achieved the desired learning effect

Target Learners¶

  • pre-service teachers

Target Learning Goals¶

  • identifying cyberbullying
  • confrontation and prevention strategies
  • cyberbullying typology knowledge

Affordances¶

  • Collective Intelligence Framework

Claims¶

  • Pre-service teachers' answer scores on cyberbullying identification, typology, confrontation and prevention questions increase significantly across the seven phases of a Collective Learning session [+M]
  • Self-perceived capacity to act in a cyberbullying case (Question 2) shows no significant change across phases [-W]
  • Significant score differences between phases emerge earlier for confrontation and prevention questions (from Phase 2) than for identification and typology questions (from Phases 3–4) [+W]
  • Top 1 answers for cyberbullying identification and typology questions reach the maximum score of 10, while prevention reaches only 6 [~W]
  • Mean answer scores in the large group rose continually across the interaction phases, especially from phase 4 onward, but stagnated on question 3 [+W]
  • The large online group showed significant score improvements from phase onward on question 1 while the smaller groups did not, indicating collective intelligence capacity emerged in the large group [+W]
