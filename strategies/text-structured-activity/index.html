Text + Structured Activity¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 unmarked) · 13 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 4 of 13 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Students engage with text resources (physical or digital) accompanied by a structured activity designed to ensure active learning and engagement with the material. The text conveys information; the structured activity forces processing of that information — answering questions, applying concepts, annotating, or self-explaining — so that reading does not remain passive. This combination provides instruction in pre-class, in-class, or asynchronous settings, and serves as a low-bandwidth alternative to video-based delivery.

Design Implications¶

Structured activities convert reading from passive reception into generative processing, which is what drives durable learning Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture-only formats. [+S]. The activity must require learners to do something with the text — explain, apply, predict, or connect — rather than merely locate or restate information Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding when learners articulate how examples relate to underlying principles. [+S]. Activities should be tightly aligned to specific sections of the text so learners know what to extract and when.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-selected text at an appropriate reading level, ideally with Advance Organizers signaling its structure
  • A structured activity requiring generative processing: Practice questions, Application tasks, or Individual Reflection prompts
  • Clear instructions specifying what learners should do with the text, not just that they should read it
  • A feedback or debrief mechanism — instructor review, answer keys, or class discussion — so errors are corrected

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured reading assignments ("read chapter 4") produce little learning; without a task, most learners skim or skip [-M]
  • Text-heavy delivery disadvantages learners with weak reading skills or limited language proficiency; comprehension support (Chunking of long texts, glossaries) is required Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Activities that ask only for information location (find-and-copy questions) do not produce conceptual learning [-M]
  • Poorly timed activities — questions before the relevant text section without guidance — can increase extraneous load [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Guided reading questions distributed through the text, answered as the learner reads
  • Reading + response: text followed by short written application or reflection, submitted before class
  • Text + self-explanation prompts: embedded prompts asking learners to explain why a claim holds or how an example illustrates a principle Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding when learners articulate how examples relate to underlying principles. [+S]
  • Text + problem set: reading paired with problems that require applying what was read, in example-problem pairs
  • Annotated reading: learners mark and comment on the text itself, using Annotating tools such as Perusall or Hypothesis

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with limited bandwidth or technology access, since text is low-bandwidth and often offline-capable
  • Self-regulated learners who can pace their own reading; weaker readers need embedded scaffolds and vocabulary support
  • Learners who benefit from rereading at their own pace — text, unlike video, supports rapid review and skimming

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding of declarative content (definitions, explanations, frameworks)
  • Application: transferring concepts from the text to new problems or cases
  • Preparation for higher-effort in-class work (flipped pre-class phase)
  • Retention of factual and procedural information over time, especially when activities are spaced [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Select or write the text, chunked into coherent sections with clear headings (Clear Structure & Presentation)
  2. Add an advance organizer or guiding question framing what learners should extract (Advance Organizers)
  3. Design a structured activity requiring generative processing — application, prediction, or self-explanation — not information location (Practice, Application)
  4. Embed the activity at the point of use: after each section for long texts, or after the whole text for short ones
  5. Provide a feedback loop: answer keys, instructor comments, or in-class debrief (Assessment)
  6. Optionally schedule revisiting of the material at spaced intervals (Spaced Repetition)

Related Strategies¶

  • Flipped Classroom — text + structured activity is the most common low-bandwidth substitute for pre-class video
  • Direct Instruction — the text can serve as the "present content" phase when scripted with worked examples
  • Assigned Readings — the unstructured baseline this strategy improves on by adding the activity component

Related Elements¶

  • Assigned Readings — the text component
  • Practice — the most common structured activity paired with text
  • Application — tasks requiring transfer of text content to new situations
  • Individual Reflection — reflective prompts that consolidate reading
  • Advance Organizers — framing devices that orient learners before reading

Tools¶

  • Perusall (https://www.perusall.com) — social annotation; the annotation prompts function as embedded structured activities
  • Hypothesis (https://web.hypothes.is) — collaborative web annotation layered on assigned readings
  • Google Forms / LMS quizzes — low-effort comprehension-check activities attached to readings
  • Anki (https://apps.ankiweb.net) — spaced-repetition cards generated from text content

Examples¶

  • Flipped pre-class reading with response: an instructor assigns a textbook section plus three application questions due before class; class time opens by discussing the most-missed question. This is the text-based variant of the flipped model.
  • Perusall-annotated readings: students annotate a journal article with questions and comments; the instructor's annotation prompts ("explain why this method was chosen here") structure the engagement.
  • OpenStax + embedded exercises (https://openstax.org): free open textbooks with built-in checkpoint questions after each section, enacting the text + activity pairing at scale.
