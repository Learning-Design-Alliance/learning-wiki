# Culture Responsive Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Culturally responsive instruction adapts standards-based content and teaching methods so that students from diverse cultural backgrounds can access and engage with them. The teacher integrates students' language practices, prior knowledge, community references, and interests into lessons while maintaining rigorous academic goals. Ladson-Billings frames this as culturally relevant pedagogy, which simultaneously pursues academic success, cultural competence, and critical consciousness [Ladson-Billings, 1995](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312032003465) [+M].

## Design Implications

Culturally responsive instruction works by reducing the distance between what students already know and what the curriculum asks them to learn — activating relevant background knowledge so new content attaches to existing schemas [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. It is not a separate curriculum but a way of delivering the same standards through culturally familiar examples, texts, and discourse norms [Gay, 2010](https://www.tcrecord.org/books/Content.asp?ContentId=9205) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine knowledge of students' backgrounds, gathered through [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), family communication, and community engagement — not assumptions
- Willingness to redesign examples, texts, and contexts while holding the learning goal constant
- [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of students' prior knowledge at the start of instruction, using culturally familiar analogies and entry points
- High expectations paired with support; responsiveness must not lower academic demands [Ladson-Billings, 1995](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312032003465) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Superficial "heroes and holidays" approaches — swapping names in word problems or adding cultural decorations — do not improve learning and can read as tokenism [~M]
- Teachers who stereotype or essentialize cultures (treating a background as uniform) risk alienating the very students they aim to reach [-M]
- Effectiveness depends on teacher cultural knowledge; without it, attempts can misfire or oversimplify [-W]
- One-off professional development rarely changes practice; sustained, reflective implementation is required [Aronson & Laughter, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582066) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Curriculum-level: redesigning units around community-based problems and [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)
- Lesson-level: substituting culturally familiar examples, texts, and analogies while keeping the same objective
- Discourse-level: incorporating students' home languages and communication norms, with explicit bridges to academic language ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Relationship-level: building belonging and trust as a precondition for academic risk-taking [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Students whose home culture and language differ from the dominant school culture, who otherwise face a double burden of content plus cultural translation [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit when instruction activates and values home-language knowledge [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- All students in diverse classrooms benefit from multiple entry points, but effects are strongest for historically underserved groups [Aronson & Laughter, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582066) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Standards-based academic content delivered through culturally meaningful contexts
- Identity and belonging: students seeing themselves as capable members of the learning community [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Critical consciousness: analyzing how knowledge, power, and society interact [Ladson-Billings, 1995](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312032003465) [+W]

### Instructions
1. Learn about your students' backgrounds, interests, and community assets through surveys, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), and family engagement.
2. [Activate](../elements/activation.md) prior knowledge at the start of each lesson, using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) drawn from students' lived experience.
3. Redesign examples, texts, and problem contexts to reflect students' communities while holding the learning objective constant.
4. Deliver content through [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) or guided exploration, bridging everyday language to academic language.
5. Provide structured [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Application](../elements/application.md) in culturally meaningful contexts, with feedback that maintains high expectations.

## Examples
- **Algebra in community contexts** — A unit on linear functions uses data students collect about their own neighborhood (transit costs, local businesses) rather than decontextualized textbook scenarios, keeping the same standard while changing the context.
- **[Zaretta Hammond's Culturally Responsive Teaching and the Brain](https://crtandthebrain.com/)** — A widely used framework connecting cultural responsiveness to cognitive science, emphasizing "warm demander" teaching that pairs high expectations with instructional scaffolding.
- **[National Writing Project](https://www.nwp.org)** — Professional development programs where teachers redesign writing instruction around students' community funds of knowledge.
