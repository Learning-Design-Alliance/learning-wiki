Concrete Examples¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 10 studies (6 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Concrete examples anchor abstract ideas in specific, tangible instances — a worked physics problem for the principle of conservation, a real negotiation transcript for game theory, a particular poem for the concept of meter. The strategy is carried out by presenting one or more instances first (or alongside the abstraction), then helping learners map features of the example onto the general principle.

Design Implications¶

Concrete examples reduce the working-memory and knowledge demands of abstraction by giving learners something specific to reason about, which is especially valuable when the concept itself is unfamiliar Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Examples are most powerful when learners first explore cases and then receive the general rule — a sequence that supports schema induction better than rule-first presentation Exploration before explanation improves learning from subsequent instruction. [+M]. Multiple varied examples are generally needed for transfer, since a single example tends to be encoded as a specific story rather than a generalizable structure Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Examples that genuinely instantiate the target concept, not merely adjacent to it
  • Explicit mapping between example features and the abstract principle — learners rarely extract the deep structure unaided
  • Multiple, varied examples when the goal is transfer rather than recognition (Comparing Cases supports this)
  • Connection to what learners already know (Analogies and Activation of prior knowledge)

Constraints¶

  • Overly rich, seductive details in an example can divert attention from the target concept and depress learning Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-M] — concreteness helps only when surface features support, not compete with, the abstraction
  • A single example encourages overgeneralization of its incidental features; learners may treat the example's specifics as part of the rule
  • Concrete materials can impede expert-like abstract reasoning in some domains; purely concrete instantiations sometimes produce worse transfer than abstract representations [~M] (Kaminski et al., 2008)
  • Examples without explicit mapping leave learners to guess the intended correspondence, often anchoring on surface similarity [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Example-first vs. rule-first: presenting cases before the rule (productive failure / "a time for telling") versus interleaving rule and example; example-first tends to build better schemas for novices
  • Concrete-to-abstract fading: starting with physical or situational instances and progressively introducing symbolic notation
  • Interleaved examples and practice: alternating Worked Examples with problems to solve
  • Contrasting cases: pairing correct and incorrect or near-miss examples to sharpen discrimination

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who lack the prior knowledge to parse abstract statements meaningfully Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Learners in domains with high symbolic density (mathematics, statistics, economics) where notation outpaces intuition
  • Less necessary for learners with well-developed schemas, who can often reason directly with abstractions and may find concrete instantiations redundant or distracting [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Concept formation: understanding what a category, principle, or definition means in practice
  • Schema induction: extracting generalizable structure from instances Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M]
  • Transfer: applying principles to novel situations (requires multiple varied examples) Multiple varied cases support flexible transfer. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the abstract concept or principle and its essential features.
  2. Select or construct 2–3 examples that vary in surface features but share the deep structure (Case Studies work well for rich domains).
  3. Present the first example and make the mapping explicit — name which features carry the concept and which are incidental.
  4. Have learners Compare the examples to induce the common structure, or compare an example with a Non-Example.
  5. State or elicit the general rule, then ask learners to apply it to a new, dissimilar case (Application).

Related Strategies¶

  • Analogies — a concrete example drawn from a familiar domain, used explicitly for structural mapping
  • Case-Based Learning — extended, authentic examples as the primary vehicle of instruction
  • Worked Examples — concrete examples applied to problem-solving procedures

Examples¶

  • Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler's math learning studies (2008) — demonstrated that novices learning an abstract mathematical group structure from a concrete "mixing liquids" cover story transferred less well than those learning from abstract symbols, a caution about unexamined concreteness.
  • Desmos classroom activities — introduce function concepts through concrete, manipulable instances (a marbleslide, a Ferris wheel) before formal notation.
  • Physics Education Technology (PhET) simulations — concrete, manipulable instances of abstract physical principles (charge, waves) with explicit visual mappings to the underlying model.
  • Statistics teaching with real datasets — teaching p-values first through a single concrete study (e.g., a coin-flipping or tea-tasting example) before the formal definition.
