# Embrace Errors as Learning Opportunities

## Description
Creating a classroom culture where errors become learning opportunities involves changing the assumption that math ability is binary—either you're good at it or not. Instead of moving on quickly from a wrong answer, teachers can show excitement about the student's thinking process. By identifying what is correct about the student’s approach, even when the answer is wrong, the teacher can show that the student was on the right track. This provides an opportunity for the student to uncover their own mistake or misconception and promotes a growth mindset.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires a shift in classroom culture and teacher mindset. Teachers need to be prepared to facilitate discussions around errors and provide constructive feedback. Worked examples with incorrect solutions can be presented.
#### Constraints
- May initially be uncomfortable for students accustomed to traditional math instruction. Requires careful facilitation to ensure that discussions remain positive and constructive.

### Target Learners

- K-12, higher education, and adult learners who struggle with math confidence

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Cultivate a growth mindset, improve problem-solving skills, reduce math anxiety, encourage participation

### Affordances
- Encourages students to take risks, values effort over correct answers, and supports deeper understanding of mathematical concepts. It allows students to see errors as a natural part of the learning process and reduces anxiety associated with making mistakes.

### Personalization
- Can be adapted for different grade levels by adjusting the complexity of the problems and the depth of the discussion. For students who are particularly anxious about making mistakes, teachers can provide more explicit support and positive reinforcement.

## Examples
- Teachers can use worked examples with wrong answers and have students find the error. Or they can rethink how they use class participation. Teachers can first say, “I don’t want to hear the answer just yet. I want to hear about the first thing you thought about.” Different students will share different ideas, which can help them all see that there can be multiple ways to solve a problem.
