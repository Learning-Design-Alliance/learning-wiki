# Temperature Monitoring & Adjustment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Temperature monitoring and adjustment is an environmental-design strategy: the instructor or facility continuously tracks ambient temperature and adjusts it (via HVAC, ventilation, fans, or window control) to keep the learning space within a range that supports cognition. Environments that are too cold increase distractibility and reduce [Attention](../elements/attention.md), while overly warm environments induce discomfort and drowsiness that degrade concentration. Field research in classrooms places the effective range for reading and mathematics performance at roughly 68–74°F (20–23°C), with performance declining measurably as temperatures rise above it [Wargocki & Wyon found classroom performance in schoolwork tasks declined as temperature rose above ~20°C.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0668.2006.00441.x) [+S].

## Design Implications

Thermal discomfort consumes working-memory and attentional resources that would otherwise support learning, functioning as a source of extraneous load [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Because the effect is physiological and largely invisible to learners, it must be managed by the environment rather than left to self-report — students often do not attribute reduced performance to heat [Cedeño Laurent et al. found students in non-air-conditioned dormitories during a heat wave showed slower reaction times on cognitive tests than peers in air-conditioned rooms.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Reliable temperature monitoring (thermometers, sensors, or smart HVAC with real-time feedback)
- A means of adjustment: HVAC control, operable windows, fans, or portable heaters
- Awareness of the target range (68–74°F for academic tasks) and of task differences — performance on tasks requiring sustained attention degrades faster in heat than on simple tasks
- A feedback loop: periodic [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) or comfort surveys to catch conditions sensors miss

#### Constraints
- Individual thermal preferences vary (sex, clothing, acclimatization), so no single setpoint suits everyone in a shared space [~M] — group settings force compromise
- Older buildings often lack zoned HVAC or operable windows, limiting adjustment options [-W]
- During heat waves, even the adjustment strategies available (fans, dimming) may only partially offset conditions; cognitive effects of heat exposure persist despite mitigation [Cedeño Laurent et al. found students in non-air-conditioned dormitories during a heat wave showed slower reaction times on cognitive tests than peers in air-conditioned rooms.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [-S]
- Over-ventilation to cool a room can introduce drafts and noise, trading one distraction for another [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Passive: scheduled monitoring with manual adjustment (opening windows, fans) — appropriate where automation is unavailable
- Active: smart HVAC systems that adjust setpoints based on occupancy and sensor data
- Learner-mediated: individual fans/heaters, seating choice near or away from heat sources, and clothing flexibility, which partially personalizes a shared thermal environment

### Target Learners
- All learners, but effects are largest for tasks demanding sustained attention and working memory (reading, mathematics) [Wargocki & Wyon found classroom performance in schoolwork tasks declined as temperature rose above ~20°C.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0668.2006.00441.x) [+S]
- Younger children, who regulate body temperature less efficiently and report discomfort less reliably [~M]
- Learners in buildings without air conditioning during warm seasons [Cedeño Laurent et al. found students in non-air-conditioned dormitories during a heat wave showed slower reaction times on cognitive tests than peers in air-conditioned rooms.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning goal in itself: this strategy optimizes the environment so that other instructional strategies can operate — it protects attention and reduces extraneous cognitive load [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- Indirectly supports any goal requiring sustained focus, particularly fluency-building and problem-solving tasks

### Instructions
1. Establish a baseline: measure temperature at learner height (not thermostat height) at several points in the room, since conditions vary within a space.
2. Set the target range (68–74°F / 20–23°C) and adjust HVAC, windows, or fans to reach it before cognitively demanding activities.
3. Pair environmental monitoring with human feedback: brief [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) or comfort surveys, since thermal comfort is subjective and sensors do not capture drafts, humidity, or air quality.
4. Adjust continuously rather than once — temperature drifts with occupancy, sunlight, and outdoor conditions across a session.
5. Offer personalization where the group setpoint cannot satisfy everyone: individual fans, seating options, and clothing flexibility ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)).

## Examples
- **Wargocki & Wyon classroom field studies (Denmark/Sweden)** — controlled interventions in real classrooms showed that lowering temperature from ~25°C to ~20°C improved performance on reading and mathematics tasks [Wargocki & Wyon found classroom performance in schoolwork tasks declined as temperature rose above ~20°C.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0668.2006.00441.x) [+S]
- **Heat-wave natural experiment (Cedeño Laurent et al., 2018)** — college students in dormitories without air conditioning performed worse on cognitive tests during a Boston heat wave than peers in air-conditioned rooms [Cedeño Laurent et al. found students in non-air-conditioned dormitories during a heat wave showed slower reaction times on cognitive tests than peers in air-conditioned rooms.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S]
- **Practical school implementations** — elementary classrooms using fans, window control, and dimmed lighting during heat events; districts installing smart HVAC that adjusts setpoints based on occupancy sensors and real-time temperature feedback.
