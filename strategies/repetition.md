# Repetition

## Description
Repetition is the deliberate re-presentation of key points multiple times within and across a lesson, using simple, plain language that minimizes language-processing demands. Repeating key points gives every student additional opportunities to grasp concepts, while simplified wording removes barriers for learners who process auditory information slowly. It is carried out through verbal emphasis, restatement in varied phrasing, and having learners themselves restate or re-explain the key idea.

## Design Implications

Repetition works because each exposure strengthens the memory trace and gives working memory a second chance to encode material that was missed the first time [Multiple exposures to to-be-learned material improve retention compared with single exposure.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on *what* is repeated: only the highest-priority concepts should be selected in advance, and language should be simplified so repetition does not compound confusion. Repetition is most powerful when converted into retrieval — asking learners to restate or re-explain the point rather than merely hearing it again [Retrieval practice produces stronger long-term retention than restudying.](../claims/retrieval-practice-enhances-long-term-retention.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Careful lesson planning to identify the small set of key concepts worth repeating
- Thoughtful, plain-language phrasing so restatements are easier to process, not just louder ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- A way for learners to signal they did not process the information, or self-monitoring prompts so they notice gaps themselves
- Varied phrasing or modality on repetition (restate, visualize, have students repeat) so the second exposure adds encoding rather than mere re-hearing

#### Constraints
- Verbatim, massed repetition produces boredom and diminishing returns; spaced repetitions spread over time are far more effective than immediate repeats [Spaced repetition improves retention relative to massed repetition.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [~S]
- Repeating the same wording does not repair a comprehension failure — if the learner didn't understand the phrasing the first time, identical repetition adds little [-M]
- Students must be self-aware enough to notice they aren't processing information and request repetition; without this metacognitive check, gaps persist silently
- Over-repetition of low-priority detail dilutes attention to the key points and consumes instructional time [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher restatement with varied phrasing ("In other words…")
- Peer repetition: students explain the key point to another student, converting repetition into [retrieval and elaboration](../principles/active-learning.md)
- Choral or student verbal repetition of the key idea
- Signposted repetition: "This is important — I'll say it twice" to direct attention
- Cross-lesson recurrence: revisiting the same key point in later lessons (spacing) rather than within one sitting

### Target Learners
- Learners with auditory processing challenges, who benefit from a second, simplified exposure [+W]
- Second-language learners and students with limited background knowledge, for whom plain-language restatement lowers processing load [Simplifying language reduces extraneous processing load for novice learners.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Young learners, who generally need more exposures before consolidation
- Less necessary for advanced learners, for whom single exposures with elaboration suffice; excessive repetition can feel patronizing and reduce engagement [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention of foundational facts, definitions, and key concepts
- Comprehension of verbally delivered instructions and explanations
- Automaticity of core vocabulary and procedures that later learning depends on

### Instructions
1. During lesson planning, identify 2–3 key concepts that every student must retain; script simple, plain-language phrasings for each ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Present each key point during [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md), then restate it in different words immediately and signal its importance.
3. Have students repeat or re-explain the point to a partner, turning hearing into retrieval ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md)).
4. Prompt self-monitoring: ask students to note what they didn't understand and request repetition ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
5. Revisit the same key points in subsequent lessons at spaced intervals rather than repeating many times in one sitting.

## Examples
- During lessons, teachers emphasize and restate key points in varied simple phrasing, then students explain the key points to another student. Resource: Auditory Processing micro-credential, [Friday Institute at NC State](https://fridayinstitute.ncsu.edu/)
- Spaced-repetition flashcard systems such as [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) schedule each item's repetition at expanding intervals, the algorithmic version of the same principle
- Direct Instruction programs (e.g., [National Institute for Direct Instruction](https://www.nifdi.org/)) script multiple, precisely worded repetitions of each concept with choral student responses
