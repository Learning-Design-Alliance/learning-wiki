# Rate (and Change) Tasks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Ask learners to rate the level of challenge in an activity on a scale of 1 to 10. If the activity is rated an eight or higher, ask what could be done to make the task a two or three. The rating acts as a rapid [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) that surfaces frustration before it escalates into avoidance, and the follow-up question converts the rating into a concrete plan for modifying the task.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines two mechanisms: it gives the learner a low-cost way to signal that difficulty has exceeded their current capacity, and it gives the teacher or system actionable information for adjusting the task toward the learner's zone of proximal development — the range of difficulty a learner can handle with support [~M]. Because learners' judgments of their own difficulty and performance are often miscalibrated, the rating should be treated as one data source alongside observed performance, not as a definitive measure [Dunlosky & Rawson found learners' confidence judgments are frequently overconfident and poorly calibrated.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A simple, consistent rating scale the learner understands (numeric, emoji, or color-coded)
- Structured tasks that can be decomposed or modified — simplified examples, reduced steps, added [scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md), or alternative entry points
- A routine for acting on ratings quickly; ratings that never produce changes erode learner trust in the process
- A follow-up question ("What would make this a 3?") that elicits specific, actionable suggestions rather than vague complaints

#### Constraints
- Learners may not provide accurate ratings — young children, learners with limited metacognitive skill, or anxious learners may rate everything as too hard (or claim everything is fine to escape the task) [~M]
- Some adjustments are not feasible within fixed curricula, timed assessments, or standardized task sequences [-M]
- Repeatedly lowering difficulty on request can deprive learners of [desirable difficulties](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md); tasks rated 8–10 are sometimes exactly where productive struggle and learning occur, and automatic downshifting removes that opportunity [-M]
- Ratings reflect momentary frustration and motivation as much as objective difficulty, so a single high rating may signal fatigue rather than task design problems [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Numeric scale (1–10)** with a defined action threshold (e.g., ≥8 triggers modification)
- **Emoji or color scales** for pre-readers and younger learners
- **Two-part rating** — difficulty and interest rated separately, since a task can be too easy *and* boring
- **Learner-directed change** — instead of the teacher deciding, the learner proposes the modification, building self-regulation and problem-solving [Zimmerman argues that involving learners in monitoring and adjusting their own learning processes builds self-regulatory skill.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- **System-mediated** — adaptive platforms collect difficulty ratings or struggle signals and adjust item difficulty automatically

### Target Learners
- Learners who avoid work, shut down, or appear distracted when frustrated — the rating gives them a legitimate, non-punitive escape valve before avoidance behavior starts
- Learners with weak self-advocacy skills who cannot yet articulate *why* a task feels impossible; the scale lowers the expressive demand
- Learners with anxiety or low frustration tolerance, for whom early difficulty signaling prevents escalation
- Less reliable with very young learners or those with limited metacognitive development, whose ratings need to be cross-checked against observed performance [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: building the habit of monitoring difficulty and requesting help appropriately [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Task engagement: keeping learners working within a productive difficulty range rather than disengaging
- Metacognitive calibration: over time, comparing ratings to actual performance helps learners judge difficulty more accurately

### Instructions
1. **Introduce the scale.** Teach the 1–10 rating explicitly with anchor examples ("a 1 is something you could do in your sleep; a 10 feels impossible").
2. **Rate before or during the task.** Ask for a rating at task start or at a natural checkpoint, using a quick [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) routine.
3. **Set an action threshold.** Agree in advance what rating triggers a change (typically 8+).
4. **Ask the change question.** "What would make this a 2 or 3?" Elicit a specific suggestion — fewer items, an example first, a [graphic organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md), working with a partner.
5. **Adjust the task.** Modify difficulty, add [scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md), or reduce scope based on the learner's suggestion and your judgment.
6. **Re-rate and reflect.** After the change, ask for a new rating and compare — this builds the learner's calibration and shows that feedback leads to action.

## Examples
- **Elementary classroom:** A teacher uses a traffic-light card system; a student flips to red during multi-step word problems, and the teacher swaps in a version with the first step modeled.
- **Special education / home:** A parent asks a child to rate homework difficulty; a rating of 9 on a writing assignment leads to breaking it into an outline plus two paragraphs, dropping the rating to a 4.
- **Adaptive platforms:** Systems like [DreamBox Learning](https://www.dreambox.com) and [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) adjust task difficulty based on performance signals; explicit difficulty ratings add a self-report channel to this loop.
