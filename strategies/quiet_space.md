# Quiet Space

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A quiet space is a designated classroom area where children can voluntarily reduce auditory, visual, and social input in order to self-regulate. It ranges from a corner with a bean bag chair to a desk with a partition, providing privacy and a refuge for regrouping. A clear communication system — a sign-up sheet, laminated card, or hand signal — enables children to request time in the space without negotiation or stigma.

## Design Implications

Quiet spaces work by lowering environmental stimulation during moments of arousal that would otherwise overwhelm a child's limited self-regulation capacity [Sensory interventions reduce classroom disruptive behavior in children with sensory processing difficulties.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]. The space is a *regulation* tool, not a reward or punishment: its value depends on children being taught when and how to use it, and on the class culture treating the space as normal rather than exceptional. Time in the space should be bounded and followed by a brief re-entry routine so the child returns to instruction rather than avoiding it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A designated area with soft furnishings (bean bag, cushions, headphones) and reduced visual clutter
- A low-friction request system (card, sign-up sheet, signal) so asking for the space is never a social hurdle
- Explicit teaching of the space's purpose and use rules, and peer education so other students respect it
- A time structure (e.g., 5–10 minutes) and a re-entry routine, often paired with a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)

#### Constraints
- Without taught usage rules, the space becomes avoidance: children use it to escape tasks rather than to regulate, and instructional time is lost [-W]
- If peers treat the space as a privilege or the user as "special," it can mark the child and undermine [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [-W]
- Effectiveness varies by individual: some children regulate better with movement or social co-regulation than with solitude [~W]
- A quiet space is not a substitute for [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) addressing the underlying sensory or instructional mismatch [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class norm ("anyone may use the calm corner") vs. individualized plan for a specific child
- Passive refuge (dim, quiet) vs. active regulation station (fidgets, breathing cards, weighted lap pad)
- Physical partition in the classroom vs. scheduled access to a separate resource room

### Target Learners
- Children with sensory sensitivities, autism, ADHD, or anxiety who are easily overwhelmed by classroom stimulation [Sensory processing difficulties predict poorer classroom emotional and behavioral outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Young children still developing independent self-regulation skills
- Less appropriate for children whose dysregulation stems from instructional mismatch or social conflict — the space treats the symptom, not the cause

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing arousal states and taking an independent corrective action
- Emotional well-being: reducing the frequency and intensity of classroom meltdowns or shutdowns
- Re-engagement: returning to instruction with enough attentional capacity to participate

### Instructions
1. **Designate and equip the space** — a low-stimulation corner with soft seating, dim lighting, and noise-reducing headphones; coordinate with [Acoustics and Noise Management](../strategies/acoustics_and_noise_management.md) for the surrounding room.
2. **Teach its purpose explicitly** — model when to use it ("when your body feels too fast or too loud inside"), how long to stay, and how to return; frame it as a tool, not a time-out.
3. **Establish the request system** — a laminated card or hand signal so children can ask without interrupting or being refused publicly.
4. **Set a time boundary and re-entry routine** — a visible timer plus a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) ("How does your body feel now?") before rejoining the activity.
5. **Normalize for peers** — make the space available to all students or explain its purpose to the class so it carries no stigma.
6. **Monitor and adjust** — track frequency, duration, and behavior before/after use; if use is rising or always coincides with a particular task, address that task instead.

## Examples
- **TEACCH-structured autism classrooms** — quiet/calm corners are a standard environmental accommodation alongside visual schedules ([TEACCH Autism Program](https://teacch.com))
- **Conscious Discipline "Safe Place"** — a classroom self-regulation station with breathing tools and feeling cards, taught to all students as a class-wide norm ([Conscious Discipline](https://consciousdiscipline.com))
- **Inclusive early-elementary classrooms** — a "calm corner" with a timer and laminated request card, available to any child, with use logged to identify patterns in task-related avoidance
