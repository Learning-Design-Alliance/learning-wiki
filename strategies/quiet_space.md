# Quiet Space

## Description
Creating a designated quiet space in the classroom allows children to self-regulate and reduce sensory overload by limiting auditory, visual, and other sensory input. These spaces can range from a corner with a bean bag chair to a desk with a partition, providing privacy and a refuge for regrouping. A clear communication system, like a sign-up sheet or laminated card, enables children to easily request time in the space when needed.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A designated area in the classroom, furniture like a bean bag chair, and a system for children to request time in the space.
#### Constraints
- The space must be respected by other students to ensure it remains a safe and calming area. The effectiveness may vary based on individual preferences and needs.

### Affordances
- Offers a physical and mental break from classroom stimuli, helping children to manage their sensory experiences and emotions. Reduces sensory overload, aiding in self-regulation and emotional well-being.

### Personalization
- The quiet space can be customized with items that provide additional comfort, such as pillows or blankets. The method of requesting the space can also be tailored to individual needs.

## Assessment Evidence
- Observe the frequency and duration of use, as well as the child's behavior before and after using the space. Monitor the child's self-regulation and ability to manage sensory input.

## Impact
- Studies show that designated quiet spaces can improve children's ability to manage their emotions and sensory input, leading to increased focus and participation in classroom activities.
