# GoNoodle: Physical Activity & Movement Breaks

## Description
GoNoodle is a free classroom platform offering short (typically 2–5 minute) videos that lead whole-class movement, dance, breathing, and mindfulness activities. Teachers deploy these breaks as transitions between instructional segments, as energizers when attention wanes, or as calming routines before focused work. The strategy — structured physical activity breaks delivered via guided video — can be implemented with or without the platform itself.

## Design Implications

Physical activity breaks leverage the link between aerobic movement and subsequent attention and executive function: brief bouts of moderate activity produce short-term improvements in on-task behavior and selective attention in children [~M]. Breaks also function as [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) at the lesson level — segmenting instruction into discrete blocks with recovery intervals, which reduces sustained-attention demands [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Because GoNoodle videos are student-facing and often student-chosen, they can support autonomy, which sustains intrinsic motivation for the routine itself [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A screen and audio setup visible and audible to the whole class, or a printed movement routine as a low-tech substitute
- A predictable slot in the lesson rhythm (e.g., after 20–30 minutes of seated work or before a transition)
- Clear behavioral norms so the break remains bounded — a start and end signal, and expectations for returning to seats
- Some student choice among activities to sustain buy-in [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Breaks consume instructional time; benefits depend on the break being shorter than the attention dividend it returns — overuse or poorly timed breaks produce net loss [~M]
- High-energy videos immediately before tasks requiring calm focus can leave students dysregulated rather than refocused; energizing and calming breaks serve different points in a lesson [~M]
- Effects on attention are acute and short-lived (minutes, not hours); breaks do not substitute for adequate daily physical education or recess [~M]
- Screen-delivered breaks can add novelty-driven excitement that competes with the return to instruction, particularly for younger students [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Energizing breaks** (dance, aerobic movement) after long seated stretches or during transitions
- **Calming breaks** (breathing, mindfulness, stretching) before tests, independent reading, or writing
- **Student-led breaks** — a rotating student selects and leads, building ownership and leadership
- **Content-linked movement** — videos or teacher-led routines that embed academic content (counting, spelling, geography) into the movement itself [~W]
- Low-tech equivalents: [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md), structured [Active Transitions](../strategies/active-transitions.md), or [Active Recess](../strategies/active-recess.md) formats

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (K–5), who show the largest acute attention benefits and for whom the platform's content is designed [~M]
- Students with ADHD or high activity needs, for whom scheduled movement can reduce off-task behavior during subsequent instruction [~M]
- Whole classes as a universal routine; less individually targeted than accommodations such as [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) for specific learners

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention restoration and readiness to learn — a precondition for academic objectives rather than an objective itself
- Self-regulation: recognizing arousal states and using movement or breathing to modulate them [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Procedural or content reinforcement when movement is paired with academic content (counting, vocabulary, sequences) [~W]

### Instructions
1. Establish the routine and norms: when breaks occur, how students transition to and from them, and the end-of-break signal.
2. Diagnose the class state and choose the break type — energizing after sustained seatwork, calming before focused tasks ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) can surface student state).
3. Run the break (2–5 minutes), keeping it bounded and predictable.
4. Return with a rapid re-entry cue — a two-minute warm-up task or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) for the next segment — so momentum carries into instruction.
5. Periodically offer student choice of activity to sustain motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], and evaluate timing against on-task behavior afterward ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of the routine itself).

## Related Strategies
- [Active Transitions](../strategies/active-transitions.md) — embeds the same movement principle into the transition itself rather than a separate break
- [Active Recess](../strategies/active-recess.md) — extends structured movement to unstructured time; complements rather than replaces in-class breaks
- [Act It Out](../strategies/act_it_out.md) — movement in service of content encoding rather than attention restoration

## Examples
- **[GoNoodle](https://www.gonoodle.com)** — Free classroom platform with channels such as *Moose Tube* (energizing dance), *Flow* and *Think About It* (mindfulness and breathing), and *Maximo* (stretching with vocabulary), used in the majority of U.S. elementary schools.
- **Classroom-based physical activity interventions** — Published programs such as TAKE 10!® integrate 10-minute activity bursts tied to academic content, demonstrating the content-linked variant of this strategy.
