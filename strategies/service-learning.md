# Service Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Service learning is a form of experiential instruction in which learners engage in organized service activities that meet genuine community needs, and then use structured reflection to connect that experience to course content. It differs from volunteering or community service by making the academic learning objectives — not the service alone — central: the experience is designed, scaffolded, and assessed as part of the curriculum [Furco's distinction between service learning and related experiential forms.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00221546.1996.11780257) [+M].

## Design Implications

Service learning works because it situates learning in an authentic context with real stakes, which increases engagement, perceived relevance, and transfer [Authentic audiences improve the quality of student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends critically on the quality and integration of reflection: meta-analytic evidence shows that programs with more reflection (more hours, more types, more frequency) produce larger gains in academic, civic, and social-emotional outcomes [Service learning meta-analysis shows dose-response effect of reflection.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654310389057) [+S]. Without deliberate connection back to disciplinary concepts, the experience remains service, not learning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A community partner with a genuine, learner-executable need, identified collaboratively rather than imposed
- Explicit alignment between service tasks and course learning objectives
- Structured reflection activities distributed across the experience ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md), journals, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), not a single end-of-term report
- Assessment that evaluates learning demonstrated through the experience, not hours served

#### Constraints
- Poorly matched or "make-work" placements reduce learning and can reinforce stereotypes about the community served [-M]
- Reflection that is generic ("write about your feelings") rather than analytically tied to course concepts produces little academic gain [Reflection quality, not quantity alone, drives outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654310389057) [~M]
- Logistical burden is high: partner coordination, transportation, liability, and variable placement quality make fidelity hard to sustain at scale [-M]
- Short-duration or one-off service ("drive-by volunteering") shows weaker and less durable effects than sustained engagement [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Direct service** (tutoring, serving) vs. **project-based service** (designing a product, conducting research for a partner) — the latter aligns more tightly with disciplinary skills
- **Curricular vs. co-curricular** — credit-bearing integration allows assessment of learning; co-curricular programs rely on voluntary reflection structures
- **Discipline-specific models** — e.g., writing courses producing documents for nonprofits, engineering programs doing community-based design

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary students in courses with applicable civic, social, or professional dimensions
- Learners who benefit from concrete experience before abstract conceptualization; service learning enacts [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) by embedding practice in a community of practice
- Students from dominant or privileged groups working across difference need structured support; well-designed programs improve intergroup attitudes [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M], but unstructured contact can entrench stereotypes [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied understanding: using disciplinary concepts to analyze real situations ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Civic learning: civic responsibility, awareness of social issues, commitment to community engagement
- Social-emotional and interpersonal outcomes: empathy, perspective-taking, communication [Service learning meta-analysis shows largest gains in social-emotional outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654310389057) [+S]
- Professional skill development: collaboration, problem-framing, working with ambiguous constraints

### Instructions
1. Identify course learning objectives and find a community partner whose needs align with them; negotiate the scope of work jointly.
2. Prepare learners with background knowledge and expectations before contact ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)), including context about the community and assumptions they should examine.
3. Structure the service so learners perform meaningful, course-relevant work with real accountability to the partner.
4. Build in recurring, prompted reflection that forces analysis through course concepts — not just description ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. Close with a synthesis product and assessment that evaluates the learning, and debrief with the partner to sustain the relationship.

## Examples
- **Campus Compact** (https://compact.org) — national coalition supporting curricular service learning across hundreds of US colleges, with published syllabi and partnership models.
- **Learn and Serve America** (archived; Corporation for National and Community Service) — federally funded K–16 service learning programs evaluated in the meta-analytic literature.
- **Engineering Projects in Community Service (EPICS)** at Purdue (https://epics.org) — multi-semester teams design and deliver engineering projects for local nonprofits, integrating technical coursework with sustained partner accountability.
