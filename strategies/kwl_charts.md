# KWL Charts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
KWL (Know, Want to know, Learned) charts are three-column graphic organizers completed around a lesson or unit. Before instruction, students list what they think they know (K) and generate questions about what they want to know (W); after instruction, they record what they learned (L). Developed by Ogle (1986) as part of a comprehension strategy for expository text, the routine combines [prior knowledge activation](../principles/activation.md), goal setting, and post-hoc reflection in a single reusable structure.

## Design Implications

KWL charts work primarily as [advance organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md): the K and W columns prime relevant prior knowledge and give students a purpose for reading or listening, which directs attention during instruction [~M]. The W column is the pedagogically strongest component — self-generated questions create a knowledge gap that new content can resolve — while the L column functions as a retrieval and [self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S] opportunity. The routine's value depends on the quality of the K discussion: inaccurate prior knowledge that goes unchallenged can persist into the L column.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A three-column chart (Know, Want to know, Learned) on a shared display or individual sheet
- A brief [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) for the K and W phases, so misconceptions surface publicly
- Instructional content that genuinely answers some of the W questions — the chart must connect to what follows
- Time reserved for the L phase; skipping it reduces the routine to a warm-up activity

#### Constraints
- If the K phase is done silently and uncorrected, students may consolidate misconceptions rather than surface them [-M]
- Listing "what you know" can consume instructional time without adding learning when students have little relevant prior knowledge [~M]
- The L column often becomes a copy-out-the-facts exercise; without prompts to compare L against K and W, it produces shallow listing rather than reflection [-W]
- Effectiveness drops when the chart is treated as a compliance worksheet rather than a genuine discussion record [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **KWL+ / KWHL**: add a fourth column — *How* will I find out (KWHL) or *What more do I want to know* (KWL+) — to extend the routine into inquiry planning and metacognitive monitoring
- **KLEW**: replace L with *Learning* evidence and add *Evidence* and *We still wonder* columns, suited to science inquiry
- Digital versions (shared documents, Padlet boards) allow asynchronous completion and make the W questions visible throughout a unit
- Individual charts suit reading comprehension; whole-class charts suit unit launches and [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grade students encountering expository text or a new content unit, where the structure externalizes planning they cannot yet do unaided [~M]
- English learners and students with limited background knowledge, for whom the K discussion makes the knowledge landscape visible before reading [~W]
- Less beneficial for learners with strong prior knowledge, for whom the K phase adds little beyond what they already do spontaneously [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Prior knowledge activation and schema preparation before reading or instruction
- Question generation and purpose setting — students learn to interrogate a topic rather than receive it
- Metacognitive monitoring: comparing the L column against K and W makes students notice what changed and what remains unknown
- Vocabulary and concept development in content-area literacy

### Instructions
1. **Know**: Present the topic and have students brainstorm what they think they know; record responses publicly and flag statements to verify rather than accept ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
2. **Want to know**: Guide students in converting curiosity into questions; keep the list visible during instruction so it functions as a purpose-setting organizer ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. **Teach/read**: Deliver the lesson or text with reference back to the W questions.
4. **Learned**: Have students record what they learned, then explicitly compare L against K — confirming, correcting, and extending earlier entries — and against W — noting which questions were answered and which remain open ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md), [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).

## Related Strategies
- Anticipation guides — a related pre-reading routine that activates prior knowledge through prediction rather than listing
- Question-answering and question-generation routines — the W column is a scaffolded version of self-questioning
- Exit tickets — a compressed, post-only alternative to the L phase

## Examples
- **Ogle's original DRTA-derived routine** (Ogle, 1986): fifth-grade students completed group KWL charts before reading expository passages, with the teacher modeling how to generate W questions from the topic.
- **Science unit launches (KLEW variant)**: teachers using the KLEW chart (e.g., in elementary science methods texts based on Hapgood & Magnusson, 2007) have students tie each "Learning" entry to specific evidence from investigations, connecting the chart to scientific practices.
- **Content-area literacy programs**: widely used in reading workshop models and district literacy frameworks (e.g., Reading Rockets' KWL templates, https://www.readingrockets.org) as a standard pre-reading organizer for informational text.
