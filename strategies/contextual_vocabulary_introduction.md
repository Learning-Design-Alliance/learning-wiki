# Contextual Vocabulary Introduction

## Description
Contextual Vocabulary Introduction presents new words embedded in the stories, passages, or texts learners are actually reading, rather than as isolated word lists. Learners encounter the word in use, infer and discuss its meaning from surrounding text, and analyze the author's word choice and its effect on meaning. The strategy treats vocabulary as inseparable from comprehension: words are learned as they function in discourse.

## Design Implications

Contextual introduction leverages the fact that word meanings are learned incrementally through repeated, varied encounters in meaningful contexts [Incidental word learning from reading context is real but modest per encounter.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.1.95) [~M]. Because a single contextual encounter rarely produces durable word knowledge, the strategy works best when contextual introduction is paired with explicit explanation, discussion, and multiple spaced encounters [Vocabulary instruction combining context with explicit teaching outperforms either alone.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2013.840683) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts selected to contain high-value target words that are important to comprehension ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))
- Brief pre-reading introduction of target words in context, or in-text supports such as glosses or annotations ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Facilitated discussion that connects word meaning to the text's situation and the author's craft ([Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md))
- Multiple subsequent encounters with each word across varied contexts

#### Constraints
- Incidental learning from context alone is unreliable: learners infer a correct meaning from context only about 5–15% of the time, and struggling readers benefit least [Incidental word learning from reading context is real but modest per encounter.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.1.95) [-S]
- Context can support wrong inferences — surrounding text may suggest a plausible but incorrect meaning that then must be unlearned [~M]
- Requires texts rich enough in useful vocabulary; finding such texts at the right difficulty level is a real constraint
- Effectiveness drops when discussion facilitation is weak, leaving word meanings vague or unconsolidated [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Pre-teaching a small set of critical words before reading vs. introducing words at the moment they arise in text
- Embedded glosses, footnotes, or hyperlinked definitions for independent reading
- [Dialogic Reading](../elements/dialogic-reading.md)-style questioning for younger learners vs. analytic discussion of authorial word choice for adolescents
- Text talk / read-aloud routines where the teacher pauses to spotlight and discuss a word in its narrative moment

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through high school readers building academic and literary vocabulary
- Struggling readers need the most support here, but paradoxically gain least from context alone — they require more explicit definitional support alongside context [Incidental word learning from reading context is real but modest per encounter.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.1.95) [~M]
- English learners, for whom contextualized encounters supply the situational grounding that translation lists lack [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Vocabulary acquisition with depth: connotation, register, and usage, not just denotation
- Reading comprehension of the host text [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Word consciousness and analysis of author's craft

### Instructions
1. Select 3–8 high-utility words from the text that are essential to comprehension or richly consequential to meaning; prioritize Tier Two words ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
2. Briefly introduce each word before or during reading in its narrative context — read the sentence, hypothesize the meaning, then confirm or refine it ([Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md))
3. During or after reading, discuss the author's word choice: what would change if a different word were used? ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
4. Have learners use the word in a new sentence or situation tied to the text's ideas ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md))
5. Re-encounter the words in later lessons, discussions, and writing tasks across the following weeks

## Related Strategies

- [Read-Alouds with Dialogic Questioning](../elements/dialogic-reading.md) — the oral-language counterpart for younger learners

## Examples
- **Text Talk** (Beck & McKeown) — teachers read a story aloud, then lead discussion of sophisticated story words in their narrative context rather than pausing mid-story for definitions.
- **Word Generation** (SERP Institute, https://www.wordgeneration.org) — a middle-school program introducing the same high-utility academic words across multiple subject-area passages and discussions over a week, ensuring varied contextual encounters.
- **Bringing Words to Life routines** — robust-vocabulary instruction in which words introduced from a read-aloud text are then discussed, acted out, and applied in new contexts across several days.
