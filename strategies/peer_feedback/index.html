Peer Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer feedback arranges for learners to exchange evaluative comments on one another's work, typically against explicit criteria or a rubric. It functions formatively: the goal is revision and improved understanding, not grading. Giving feedback is itself a learning activity — reviewing a peer's work requires learners to internalize quality criteria and apply them to a concrete case.

Design Implications¶

Peer feedback works because it doubles the learning opportunity: receivers get timely, multiple perspectives on their work, while givers must analyze work against criteria, which deepens their own understanding of quality Peer assessment can improve learning outcomes when structured with clear criteria. [+M]. Feedback is most effective when it answers "Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next?" rather than assigning praise or grades Feedback improves learning when it addresses task, process, and self-regulation levels. [+S]. Structure matters more than volume: rubrics, exemplars, and sentence frames determine whether comments are actionable.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria or a rubric shared with both givers and receivers before review begins
  • Training or modeling of feedback quality (Coaching or an Articulation phase where students practice commenting on an anonymized sample)
  • Time and expectation for the receiver to act on the feedback — revision, not just receipt
  • Psychological safety; anonymous or small-group formats can reduce social threat

Constraints¶

  • Untrained peer feedback tends toward vague praise ("good job") or personal remarks, with little effect on revision Structured peer assessment outperforms unstructured, but unstructured formats show weak or null effects. [~M]
  • Perceived low competence of the peer source reduces uptake of feedback, even when the feedback is accurate [~M]
  • Grading by peers invites collusion, leniency bias, and friendship effects; peer feedback should be formative, not summative [~S]
  • Novices may lack the schema to detect errors in peers' work, propagating misconceptions if feedback is unverified [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written vs. oral: oral feedback (e.g., studio critiques, conference pairs) is richer but harder to document; written comments support revision
  • Anonymous vs. identified: anonymity increases candor but removes accountability and dialogue
  • Two-stage protocols: givers first describe what the work does, then suggest improvements — improves specificity
  • Calibration activities: all students rate the same sample and compare ratings with an expert's, aligning standards before live review

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and higher-education students with enough literacy and domain knowledge to apply criteria [+M]
  • Developing writers and problem-solvers, who benefit from seeing multiple peer approaches to the same task
  • Less reliable for young novices who cannot yet judge quality; teacher-mediated feedback remains primary there [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving product quality through revision (writing, design, code, arguments)
  • Internalizing quality criteria and evaluative judgment — the giver's side is often the larger learning gain
  • Self-regulation: receiving feedback builds habits of interpreting and acting on critique Self-regulated learning strategies improve achievement. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Share the task criteria and rubric; anchor them with an exemplar (Advance Organizers)
  2. Model quality feedback on an anonymized sample, thinking aloud about how criteria apply (Coaching)
  3. Pair or group students and have them exchange work with a deadline for comments
  4. Require receivers to respond — a revision plan or revised draft — so feedback is consequential (Assessment for learning, not of it)
  5. Debrief: collect samples of useful comments and discuss why they helped, building evaluative judgment over successive rounds

Related Strategies¶

  • Formative Assessment — peer feedback is a distributed form of it; the same feedback-quality evidence applies
  • Collaborative Learning — peer feedback structures the critique phase of group work
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — peer critique plays the role expert critique plays in the coaching phase

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) — students first calibrate their ratings against expert-scored samples, then review peers' essays; calibration gates access to the review stage. https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — algorithmically distribute submissions, anonymize reviewers, and flag low-quality reviews for instructor follow-up.
  • Writing workshop models (e.g., Lucy Calkins' Units of Study) — structured peer conferring with sentence stems ("I noticed… I wondered…") in K–8 writing instruction.
