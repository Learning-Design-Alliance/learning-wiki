Ten Frames¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 review, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

A ten-frame is a simple 2×5 array on which counters, dots, or numerals are placed to represent quantities from 0 to 10. Because each cell holds at most one counter, the frame makes the "ten-ness" of numbers visually salient — learners can see 7 as 5-and-2, or as 3-away-from-10 — without counting individual units. Two frames side by side (or a double ten-frame) extend the representation to 20 and ground early place-value reasoning.

Design Implications¶

Ten frames leverage the visual system to offload counting and support subitizing — rapid recognition of small quantities without counting — which reduces working-memory demands during early number work Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. The fixed structure of the frame acts as an anchor: numbers are always seen relative to 5 and 10, which builds the part-whole and benchmark-of-ten reasoning that underpins mental addition and subtraction strategies (e.g., 8 + 5 as 8 + 2 + 3). The representation is most effective when paired with verbalization of the quantity's structure ("7 is 5 and 2") rather than used as a silent display [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A consistent 2×5 frame with one counter per cell — irregular placement destroys the benchmark structure
  • Counters or markers learners can physically place, move, and rearrange
  • Teacher prompts that ask learners to describe what they see ("How many? How do you see it? How many more to make 10?")
  • Progression from filled frames (subitizing) to partially filled frames (part-whole reasoning) to empty frames (construction)

Constraints¶

  • If learners are allowed to count counters one-by-one indefinitely, the frame adds little over loose counters and can become a counting ritual rather than a structural tool [-W]
  • Over-reliance on a single representation can bind number understanding to that image; ten frames should be varied with number lines, rekenreks, and dot arrangements to support flexible abstraction [~M]
  • Less useful for learners who have already internalized base-ten structure — the representation becomes redundant busywork [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Physical frames with two-color counters allow "flip to make ten" activities (e.g., 6 red / 4 yellow)
  • Quick-look routines: flash a filled frame for 2–3 seconds, then ask "How many? How did you see it?" to build subitizing and part-whole language
  • Double ten-frames for numbers 11–20, bridging to place value and regrouping
  • Digital versions (e.g., in DreamBox Learning or Didax virtual manipulatives) permit rapid reconfiguration

Target Learners¶

  • Early elementary learners (K–2) developing number sense and the benchmark-of-ten strategy
  • Learners who still count one-by-one and need a structure that makes 5-and-10 groupings visible [~M]
  • Older struggling learners as a diagnostic and remedial tool for gaps in part-whole reasoning

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Subitizing and quantity recognition without counting
  • Part-whole decomposition of numbers within 10 and 20
  • Foundational mental computation strategies (make-ten, bridging ten)
  • Early place-value understanding

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce the empty frame and establish the one-counter-per-cell convention.
  2. Have learners build target numbers on the frame, then describe arrangements aloud ("8 is 5 and 3").
  3. Run quick-look flash routines to move learners from counting to subitizing.
  4. Pose missing-part problems ("I have 7, how many more to fill the frame?") to build the make-ten benchmark.
  5. Extend to double ten-frames for teen numbers, connecting to place value; fade the frame as learners internalize the structure and can reason with numerals alone.

Related Strategies¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction — ten frames are a common representation within CGI's research-based progression of number strategies
  • Direct Instruction — teacher-modeled use of the frame follows a demonstration-then-practice structure

Examples¶

  • DreamBox Learning — adaptive K–8 math platform that uses ten-frame and open-number-line models in its early number sense lessons.
  • Didax Virtual Manipulatives — free online ten-frame tool with two-color counters for whole-class demonstration.
  • Quick-look number talks — a widely used K–2 classroom routine in which a filled ten-frame is flashed briefly and students explain how they saw the quantity.
