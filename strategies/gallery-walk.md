# Gallery Walk

## Description
A gallery walk sets up stations, posters, or displayed work around a room; small groups travel from station to station, performing a task or responding to a prompt at each stop and typically recording or building on others' contributions (e.g., with sticky notes). Variations include groups producing posters and acting as tour guides, peer critique of drafted work, and stations featuring learner-created video tutorials. The instructor designs the prompts or tasks, sets rotation logistics, and monitors and debriefs.

## Design Implications

Gallery walks operationalize [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) by replacing extended seat time with movement, task performance, and peer discussion [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Their learning value comes less from the movement itself than from the structured tasks and the requirement to articulate, defend, and build on ideas with peers [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability outperforms traditional instruction.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Because each station is a discrete chunk, the format also supports [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — but only if station tasks are tightly scoped and instructions are unambiguous.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Physical space for 4–8 stations with clear sightlines and traffic flow
- Well-scoped prompts or tasks per station, each answerable in 3–7 minutes
- A recording mechanism (sticky notes, response sheets, digital boards) so ideas persist across rotations
- Clear rotation rules, timing signals, and roles within groups
- A synthesis or debrief phase that consolidates what the rotations surfaced ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- In small or crowded rooms, movement and noise degrade on-task behavior; effectiveness drops sharply without explicit norms and monitoring [-W]
- Stations with vague prompts produce social chatter rather than task-focused talk; the collaboration benefit depends on individual accountability structures [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability outperforms traditional instruction.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-M]
- Copying or "voting with the crowd" at stations where earlier groups have already written answers can propagate errors and suppress independent thinking [-W]
- Poorly timed rotations truncate thinking; groups that finish early disengage or those cut off mid-task lose the reasoning work [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Respond-and-build:** groups answer a prompt, then annotate or challenge prior groups' responses at subsequent stations
- **Peer critique:** displayed drafts (writing, designs, solutions) receive structured feedback using protocols like gallery critique or "warm/cool" feedback
- **Student-authored galleries:** groups create the posters themselves, then tour and question each other — shifting the walk toward [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) and elaboration
- **Digital gallery walks:** shared slides or boards (e.g., Padlet, Google Slides) replicate rotation for hybrid or space-constrained settings
- **Jigsaw-compatible:** each group becomes "expert" at one station, then shares out — pairing with [Jigsaw](../strategies/jigsaw.md) structure

### Target Learners
- All ages and subject areas; particularly useful where multiple perspectives on open questions matter
- Learners who benefit from movement and embodied engagement; sustained seated discussion is harder for them [-W]
- Novices need scaffolded prompts and worked models at stations; open-ended critique stations suit learners with more prior knowledge [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through comparing multiple perspectives and solutions
- Peer teaching and articulation: explaining and defending ideas to peers
- Evaluation and feedback skill: critiquing work against criteria
- Collaborative process skills: turn-taking, building on others' contributions

### Instructions
1. Define the goal and 4–8 station tasks or prompts, each scoped to a short rotation ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
2. Prepare materials: posted prompts, artifacts or drafts for critique, sticky notes/markers or digital equivalents
3. Assign small heterogeneous groups and roles (recorder, timekeeper, reporter) to create accountability [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability outperforms traditional instruction.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]
4. Model the expected station behavior once before starting ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
5. Run timed rotations; circulate to probe reasoning and redirect off-task groups ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
6. Debrief whole-class: synthesize themes, surface disagreements between stations, correct propagated errors ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

## Related Strategies
- [Jigsaw](../strategies/jigsaw.md) — complementary rotation structure where groups specialize then share
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — the discussion routine that can run *within* each station
- [Peer Feedback](../elements/peer-feedback.md) — the critique variant of a gallery walk depends on this skill

## Examples
- **EL Education protocols** ([https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)) — published gallery walk and "gallery critique" protocols used in expeditionary learning classrooms, with structured peer-feedback norms
- **Peer review of lab reports** — students post experimental designs or solutions; rotating groups annotate strengths and errors with sticky notes before revision
- **Face-to-face "poster session" in higher ed** — student research groups present posters and rotate as audience members, questioning peers; mirrors disciplinary conference practice
