Interest Inventories¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (5 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Interest inventories are surveys used to assess students' areas of interest. They can be open-ended, where students generate their own topics, or closed, where students select from a predetermined list. The results are used to form learning clubs or to select texts, problems, and project topics aligned with shared interests, connecting instruction to students' background knowledge and personal relevance.

Design Implications¶

Interest inventories operationalize personal relevance, which raises task value and situational interest — both reliable predictors of engagement and persistence Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]. Giving students a voice in topic selection also supports autonomy, a core condition for intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. The inventory itself is only the diagnostic step; its effect on learning depends on whether the information actually changes what students read, write about, or investigate.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An inventory instrument (open-ended, closed, or mixed), administered early enough to inform planning
  • Class time for completion and a system for organizing students into interest-based groups or topic tracks
  • A mechanism for acting on the data: matched texts, project options, or Collaboration structures built around shared interests

Constraints¶

  • Inventories that collect interests but do not change instruction produce no motivational benefit and can erode trust in future choice opportunities [~M]
  • Closed inventories constrain responses to the designer's assumptions; students with interests outside the list are effectively ignored [-W]
  • Interest-based grouping can drift from learning objectives when topics are chosen for passion alone; alignment checks are needed [~M]
  • Some students — especially younger learners or those new to a domain — cannot yet articulate interests in an unfamiliar field, since individual interest typically develops only after sustained engagement [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Open-ended formats ("What do you love doing?") maximize voice but are harder to group from; closed checklists are efficient but narrow
  • Content-specific variants (reading interest inventories, science topic surveys) target a single subject; general inventories inform cross-curricular clubs
  • Inventories can be re-administered periodically, since interests shift as knowledge grows

Target Learners¶

  • Students whose engagement is low under default, non-chosen topics Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]
  • Reluctant readers and writers, for whom choice of text and topic is among the strongest motivational levers [+M]
  • Students with weak academic self-concept, where interest-driven entry points can build early success experiences that support persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Motivation and engagement: establishing personal relevance before instruction
  • Background knowledge activation: surfacing what students already know about their chosen topics (Activation)
  • Sustained inquiry: supporting extended reading, research, and project work within interest-based clubs

Instructions¶

  1. Administer the inventory early in the term; combine open-ended prompts with a closed checklist so every student has a way to respond.
  2. Have students reflect individually on their responses (Activation), then discuss in pairs to surface shared interests.
  3. Analyze responses and form interest-based groups or offer topic menus aligned to the same learning objectives.
  4. Match materials and tasks to inventoried interests — texts, problems, or project topics — while keeping the target skills constant across groups.
  5. Revisit the inventory at natural breakpoints (new unit, new term) to update groupings and topic choices.

Related Strategies¶

  • Choice-based reading workshops — inventories supply the data that makes book choice efficient
  • Project-based learning — inventories seed authentic project topics
  • Learning clubs / literature circles — the grouping structure inventories typically feed

Examples¶

  • Reading interest inventories in elementary classrooms (e.g., the "Reading Interest-A-Lyzer" style surveys from the reading workshop tradition) used to build classroom libraries and match students to independent-reading books.
  • Writing workshop conferring: a quick interest survey at the start of the year gives teachers topic hooks for student writing across the year.
  • Interest-based science clubs: students complete a topic survey, then join clubs (robotics, ecology, space) that pursue the same inquiry standards through different topics.
