# Restorative Circles

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (6 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), `q3`–`q4` · 0 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Restorative Circles bring affected parties — those who caused harm, those harmed, and community members — into a facilitated, round-robin dialogue using a talking piece and sequenced prompts (What happened? Who was affected? What is needed to make things right?). The goal is not punishment but accountability, repair of relationships, and reintegration of the person who caused harm into the community. Circles are used both reactively (responding to specific incidents) and proactively (community-building, norm-setting, academic check-ins).

## Design Implications

Restorative Circles operationalize social-emotional and community-building goals through structured dialogue rather than direct instruction; their effectiveness depends on skilled facilitation, voluntary participation, and a pre-existing climate of trust. Evidence from school implementations is promising but conditional: whole-school restorative practice programs have reduced suspensions in some contexts [Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M], and empathy-focused dialogue improves intergroup attitudes [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. Circles also function as belonging interventions, since structured turn-taking guarantees every voice is heard [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained facilitator who keeps the process non-judgmental and manages power imbalances
- Voluntary participation by the person who caused harm — coerced "accountability" undermines the repair process
- Established community norms and prior relationship-building; circles work best in communities that already practice them proactively
- Adequate time: a harm-focused circle typically requires 45–90 minutes, sometimes multiple sessions
- A talking piece or equivalent turn-taking structure to equalize participation
- A trained facilitator who can hold neutrality and manage strong emotion
- Voluntary participation by the harmed party; circles fail when attendance is coerced
- Sufficient time — a responsive circle typically needs 30–60 minutes, not a passing-period conversation
- Follow-through structures: the group's repair agreement must be revisited and monitored
- A prior culture of [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and community-building so participants have practiced the format before using it under stress

#### Constraints
- Poorly facilitated circles can re-tra victims by forcing confrontation with the person who harmed them [~M] — screening for readiness and power dynamics is essential
- Implementing circles only as a disciplinary replacement, without proactive community-building, tends to fail; teachers without training report circles becoming unfocused or performative [~M]
- Whole-school adoption without staff buy-in and sustained professional development shows weak or null effects on discipline outcomes [~S] — the RAND evaluation of the Pittsburgh restorative practices rollout found suspension reductions but no academic gains and increased teacher-reported classroom disruption in early implementation years
- Not appropriate for all harms: severe violence or cases where victims refuse contact require alternative processes
- Time-intensive relative to traditional discipline; under-resourced schools may implement a degraded version that yields neither repair nor efficiency
- Poorly facilitated circles can re-traum victims by forcing confrontation with the person who harmed them [~M] — victims report lower satisfaction when participation is pressured (Strang et al., 2006)
- Implemented as a substitute for adequate behavioral support rather than alongside it, restorative practices can leave teachers feeling unsupported and can worsen climate [~M] (Acosta et al., 2019)
- Requires significant adult time and training; under-resourced rollouts degrade into unstructured conversations with no repair outcome
- Power imbalances (e.g., a popular student vs. an isolated one, or staff vs. student) can silence less powerful participants unless the facilitator actively manages turn-taking

#### Implementation Variability
- **Proactive/community-building circles**: regular low-stakes check-ins and norm-setting; no incident required
- **Responsive/harm circles**: convened after a specific incident, often replacing or supplementing suspension
- **Academic circles**: content-focused discussions using the same protocol to deepen engagement with material
- **Tiered models** (Wachtel's whole-school framework): universal circles for all students, targeted circles for emerging conflict, intensive circles for serious harm
- **Proactive/community-building circles**: low-stakes weekly rounds on prompts, building the relational capital that responsive circles draw on
- **Responsive circles**: convened after a specific harm, following the classic three-phase question sequence
- **Talking-piece vs. open discussion**: the physical talking piece enforces sequential speaking and slows the exchange; open formats are faster but allow dominant voices to take over
- **Whole-class vs. small conference**: classroom circles address diffuse climate issues; small restorative conferences address specific incidents

### Target Learners
- K–12 students school-wide, with strong evidence in secondary settings where exclusionary discipline is most damaging
- Students at risk of suspension or disengagement, for whom reintegration rather than exclusion changes trajectories [Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]
- Marginalized students disproportionately affected by exclusionary discipline; circles can reduce disparities when implemented with fidelity [~M]
- Less suitable as a standalone intervention for students needing intensive individualized behavioral support
- Less suitable as a first response to serious violence or when the harmed party declines participation

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, empathy, and emotional regulation [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Community and belonging: establishing shared norms and mutual accountability [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Moral and civic reasoning: understanding harm, responsibility, and repair
- Conflict resolution and communication skills
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, emotional regulation, accountable speech
- Civic and moral reasoning: practicing collective decision-making and repair of harm

### Instructions
1. **Prepare**: facilitator meets separately with affected parties to assess willingness, explain the process, and screen for power imbalances or trauma risks.
2. **Open with a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)**: a brief round of low-stakes sharing to establish tone and equalize voices before addressing the issue.
3. **Establish or revisit norms**: participants agree on confidentiality, respect, and speaking only when holding the talking piece.
4. **Sequence the dialogue**: use the classic restorative questions — What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who has been affected and how? What do you need to do to make things right? — moving the talking piece around the circle.
5. **Facilitate active listening**: model and prompt paraphrasing and perspective-taking ([Active Listening](active-listening.md)); the facilitator's role is to keep the dialogue non-judgmental and on protocol.
6. **Build the agreement**: participants co-construct concrete repair actions with timelines and follow-up; the facilitator records the agreement.
7. **Close and follow up**: end with a closing round; schedule a follow-up circle to review whether the agreement held and relationships were repaired.

## Examples
- **Denver Public Schools (Colorado)** — district-wide restorative justice implementation replacing exclusionary discipline for many infractions; documented reductions in suspensions and discipline disparities.
- **Oakland Unified School District (California)** — a well-studied whole-school restorative justice program; African American suspension rates fell substantially after implementation (Karp & Breslin, 2001; later district evaluations).
- **International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP) SaferSanerSchools program** — whole-school professional development model using tiered circles; evaluated in a randomized study of 14 Pittsburgh middle schools (Augustine et al., 2018).
- **Restorative Circles (Dominic Barter, Brazil)** — community-developed model originating in Rio de Janeiro favelas, now adapted internationally for schools and justice systems.
- **Pittsburgh Public Schools** — a 5-year randomized evaluation of district-wide restorative practices (RAND, 2018–2019) found reduced suspensions and improved teacher-student relationships, with mixed effects on climate: [https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2840.html](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2840.html)
- **Oakland Unified School District** — a pioneer of whole-school restorative justice; its published implementation manual is widely adopted: [https://www.ousd.org/restorativejustice](https://www.ousd.org/restorativejustice)
- **Kay Pranis's peacemaking circles** — the talking-piece circle protocol adapted from Indigenous practice, used in classrooms and community settings (Pranis, Stuart, & Wedge, 2003)

### Instructions
1. **Prepare separately.** Facilitator meets briefly with each party to confirm voluntary participation and surface what each wants from the circle.
2. **Open with a check-in round.** Use a [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) question and a talking piece so every participant speaks once before any discussion of the incident.
3. **Name what happened.** Facilitator asks each participant for their account; the goal is shared facts, not adjudication of blame.
4. **Surface impacts.** Participants describe who was affected and how — this is the perspective-taking core of the circle.
5. **Agree on repair.** The group generates and commits to concrete repair actions, recorded and assigned.
6. **Close and follow up.** A closing round; the facilitator schedules a follow-up to review whether the agreement held.
