# Low Floor, High Ceiling Tasks

## Description
A low floor, high ceiling task is an open-ended problem that can be accessed with minimal prior knowledge — anyone can make a start — while containing extensions that challenge advanced learners. Rather than differentiating by assigning different problems to different students, the single task itself accommodates a wide range of entry points and depths of engagement. The approach originated in mathematics education reform (Sullivan, Clarke, & Wallbridge, 2011) and is central to problem-based curricula such as [Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org) and [NRICH](https://nrich.maths.org).

## Design Implications

Low floor, high ceiling tasks enact productive struggle: learners at different readiness levels work on the *same* problem, which preserves a shared classroom discourse and avoids the fixed-ability messaging of tiered assignments [Boaler's research on open, multidimensional mathematics classrooms shows improved outcomes and equity.](https://www.youcubed.org) [+M]. The low floor reduces the risk of [cognitive overload](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) at entry [~M], while the high ceiling sustains challenge for experts, mitigating the expertise-reversal problem that arises when advanced learners receive the same support as novices [~M]. Because the task is open-ended, success depends on the teacher facilitating rather than demonstrating — the role shifts toward [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)-style coaching during work time.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely open task: multiple solution paths, multiple representations, or extendable structure — not a single-step problem with a decorative extension
- Norms for productive struggle and collaboration, so learners persist rather than wait for the answer
- Teacher facilitation skill: questioning, selecting and sequencing student work for discussion, and knowing when to intervene [Contingent, responsive support improves learning outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Planned "ceiling" extensions ready in advance (What if…? prompts, generalization questions, counterexamples)

#### Constraints
- Without skilled facilitation, low floor can become *no* floor: learners who lack entry strategies flounder, and unguided exploration produces minimal learning for novices [-S] — minimal guidance is ineffective for learners without sufficient prior knowledge
- Open tasks impose high working-memory and self-regulation demands; learners with weak foundational skills may experience overload rather than productive struggle [~M]
- Assessment is harder: single-answer grading schemes undervalue the reasoning these tasks elicit, and misaligned tests can undermine the approach [-M]
- Time-intensive; covering a fixed curriculum with many such tasks requires trade-offs in breadth [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Same task, staged prompts**: all learners start identically; extensions are offered as learners finish, keeping the floor intact
- **Parallel tasks**: same context and numbers vary in complexity — a compromise when genuine openness is impractical
- **Task sequences**: a low-floor opener followed by increasingly constrained follow-ups that consolidate the mathematics surfaced by exploration
- **Subject adaptation**: works beyond mathematics — e.g., open design briefs in engineering, inquiry questions in science, open writing prompts in literacy

### Target Learners
- Mixed-ability classrooms, where a single task avoids the stigma and ceiling effects of ability grouping [+M]
- Advanced learners, who can pursue generalization and proof rather than more of the same exercises [+M]
- Novices need an entry scaffold (a concrete material, a simpler case, a sentence starter) or the floor is illusory [-S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and flexible strategy use, rather than procedural fluency alone
- Mathematical (or disciplinary) practices: conjecturing, justifying, representing, generalizing
- Persistence and productive disposition toward struggle [+W]

### Instructions
1. **Select or design the task.** Check the floor: can a learner with minimal background make a first move? Check the ceiling: does it extend to generalization or proof? Repositories like [NRICH](https://nrich.maths.org) and [Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org) tag tasks this way.
2. **Launch without teaching the method.** Present the problem, clarify constraints, and resist demonstrating a solution — the exploration *is* the instruction. Activate relevant prior experience briefly instead ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
3. **Support entry.** Offer concrete manipulatives, simpler cases, or "start by trying small numbers" prompts for learners who stall; keep support contingent rather than preemptive [Contingent, responsive support improves learning outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
4. **Monitor and extend.** Circulate, ask advancing questions ("Can you find all solutions? What if the rule changed?"), and note work worth sharing.
5. **Orchestrate the discussion.** Sequence student solutions from accessible to sophisticated, making multiple strategies visible — the social phase where much of the learning is consolidated [Active learning with discussion improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
6. **Consolidate.** Name the key ideas that emerged and connect them to target concepts, so exploration resolves into structured understanding rather than remaining open-ended.

## Examples
- **[Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org) "Week of Inspirational Math"** — published low floor, high ceiling tasks (e.g., the "Four 4s" and visual number puzzles) with facilitation videos, used in classrooms worldwide.
- **[NRICH](https://nrich.maths.org)** — University of Cambridge task library explicitly tagged for accessibility and depth, with teacher notes on extending to the ceiling.
- **[Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org)** curriculum — problem-based lesson sequences that open with an accessible task and build toward generalization through student discourse.
