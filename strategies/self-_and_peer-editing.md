# Self- and Peer-Editing

## Description
Self- and peer-editing are structured processes in which learners evaluate their own writing against explicit criteria and provide feedback to peers. Self-editing builds awareness of writing conventions and self-monitoring; peer-editing exposes writers to varied perspectives and requires editors to apply criteria analytically. Both depend on a shared rubric or checklist and on the teacher modeling the evaluation process with sample texts before students work independently.

## Design Implications

Editing as a formative activity improves writing quality when it is criterion-referenced and followed by revision [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. A key mechanism is that *reviewing* someone else's text is itself a learning activity: reading and critiquing multiple peer drafts improves subsequent writing as much as receiving feedback does [Receiving and especially giving peer feedback improves writing quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]. Unstructured "read my essay and tell me what you think" exchanges produce little benefit; the checklist, the modeled demonstration, and the required revision step carry the effect.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A specific, learner-friendly checklist or rubric targeting a small number of features per pass (e.g., organization, sentence boundaries, word choice)
- Teacher modeling of the editing process on anonymous sample text before students edit independently ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of applying each criterion)
- A protected, supportive classroom norm for giving and receiving critique
- Time and expectation for [Revision](../elements/revision.md) — feedback that is never acted on does not improve writing

#### Constraints
- Without explicit instruction and modeling, peer feedback tends to be vague praise ("good job") or surface-level correction, with negligible effect on writing quality [-M]
- Younger or lower-proficiency writers can misapply criteria or give incorrect corrections; peer editing supplements, not replaces, teacher feedback [-M]
- Social discomfort or status differences in pairs can suppress honest critique; anonymous or rotating exchanges mitigate this [~M]
- Editing only conventions (spelling, grammar) yields weaker gains than editing for organization, clarity, and audience [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-pass structure:** self-edit first with the checklist, then peer-edit with a partner or small group
- **Focused passes:** one editing session per criterion (ideas, then organization, then conventions) rather than everything at once
- **Anonymous exchange:** papers swapped without names to reduce social risk
- **Author response sheets:** the writer records which suggestions they will adopt and why, making the decision explicit rather than automatic compliance
- **Digital tools:** shared documents with comment features (e.g., Google Docs peer review) allow threaded, criterion-tagged feedback

### Target Learners
- Elementary writers (grades 3–5) learning to see their own drafts objectively, provided criteria are concrete and modeled [+M]
- Adolescent and college writers, where peer review is a standard component of process writing instruction [+S]
- English language learners benefit from seeing how peers solved the same genre demands, though checklists must use [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation of writing: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own text [Self-regulated learning strategies improve writing outcomes.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-strategies-improve-writing.md) [+S]
- Application of genre and convention knowledge through analytical reading of others' work
- Collaborative critique and academic discourse skills

### Instructions
1. **Model:** Teacher demonstrates applying the checklist to an anonymous sample text, verbalizing each judgment ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. **Self-edit:** Students apply the checklist to their own draft, marking specific features ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. **Peer-edit:** Partners or small groups exchange drafts and complete the checklist, writing specific, criterion-linked comments ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)).
4. **Author response:** Writers review feedback and record which suggestions they will adopt and why ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
5. **Revise:** Students produce a revised draft incorporating accepted changes ([Revision](../elements/revision.md)).
6. **Apply:** The criteria are applied to the next writing task, transferring the editing routine ([Application](../elements/application.md)).

## Examples
- **Process writing instruction (Graham & Sandmel, 2011):** Across 29 experimental studies, elementary and secondary students in process writing classrooms — which routinely include peer and self-editing with structured response — outperformed controls on writing quality (effect size ≈ 0.34).
- **Structured peer review in composition courses (Cho & MacArthur, 2011):** College students who reviewed multiple peer drafts of the same assignment produced better revisions of their own writing than students who only received feedback.
- **Classroom checklist routine:** Using an editing checklist, students self-edit their drafts, then meet with a partner to peer-edit; the teacher first models the checklist on sample text, and groups collaboratively edit one shared piece.
