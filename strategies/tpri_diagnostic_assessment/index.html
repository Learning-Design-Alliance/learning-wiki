TPRI Diagnostic Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

The TPRI is an individually administered screening and diagnostic inventory of early reading development, used in kindergarten through grade 3. It consists of a brief Screening section identifying students at risk, followed by an Inventory section that assesses specific domains — phonological awareness, graphophonemic knowledge, word reading, fluency, and comprehension — so instruction can be targeted to each child's profile. Teachers administer it at the beginning, middle, and end of the year, using results to group students and select interventions.

Design Implications¶

The TPRI operationalizes Assessment for Learning: its purpose is not grading but locating each learner on a developmental trajectory so instruction can be adjusted. Early screening matters because reading difficulties compound — students who fall behind in phonemic awareness and decoding tend to fall further behind without targeted intervention Early identification and intensive intervention prevent entrenched reading failure. [+S]. Diagnostic profiles are actionable only when teachers translate them into differentiated instruction; assessment data without instructional follow-through yields little benefit Foorman & Moats, 2004 [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Trained one-on-one administration; fidelity depends on the teacher following standardized protocols and scoring rules
  • A decision framework linking specific inventory results (e.g., failure on blending tasks) to specific instructional responses (Coaching and Check-Ins support this translation)
  • Time allocated for administration (roughly 10–15 minutes per child per wave) and for regrouping students afterward

Constraints¶

  • One-on-one administration is labor-intensive; under time pressure, teachers may rush administration or skip the Inventory for screened-on students, losing diagnostic detail [-W]
  • A screening score alone does not identify why a student struggles; interpreting results requires knowledge of reading development that not all teachers have [~M]
  • Three assessment waves per year can reduce instructional time and produce testing fatigue in the youngest learners [-W]
  • Results reflect a single administration moment; young children's performance varies with attention and fatigue, so a single low score should trigger monitoring, not immediate placement [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Screening-only use (identifying at-risk students) versus full inventory use (informing daily grouping)
  • State-mandated implementation with prescribed intervention menus versus school-level discretionary use
  • Paper-and-pencil administration versus the TPRI's electronic support tools for scoring and data management

Target Learners¶

  • Kindergarten through grade 3 students, particularly those at risk of reading difficulty Early identification and intensive intervention prevent entrenched reading failure. [+S]
  • Struggling readers whose profile of strengths and weaknesses can guide remediation; responsiveness to early intervention distinguishes readily remediated from difficult-to-remediate poor readers Vellutino et al., 1996 [+S]
  • Less informative for fluent readers beyond grade-level benchmarks, for whom the inventory adds little beyond confirming mastery

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Phonological and phonemic awareness
  • Decoding and word recognition (Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S])
  • Reading fluency and listening/reading comprehension

Instructions¶

  1. Administer the Screening section one-on-one at the beginning of the year to identify students at risk on grade-appropriate tasks.
  2. For students who do not screen on, administer the relevant Inventory sections (Assessment) covering phonological awareness, graphophonemic knowledge, and reading accuracy and comprehension.
  3. Score against developmental guidelines and build a profile of each student's specific skill gaps rather than a single aggregate score.
  4. Translate profiles into differentiated small-group instruction and targeted intervention; feedback to students should address the task and process level, not the child globally Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  5. Re-administer at middle and end of year; adjust grouping based on growth, and intensify support for non-responders (Scaffolding and Accommodations as needed).

Related Strategies¶

  • Curriculum-Based Measurement — a complementary frequent-monitoring approach; TPRI provides the deeper diagnostic profile, CBM the growth trajectory
  • Response to Intervention — TPRI screening and progress waves supply the data tiers of an RTI model
  • Running Records — another one-on-one reading diagnostic, focused on oral reading behavior rather than discrete skills
  • Use a multi-stage screening tool (SSBD) to nominate and rank at-risk students for behavioral intervention

Examples¶

  • Texas public schools have used the TPRI as a K–3 reading instrument under the Texas Reading Initiative, with results driving placement in intensive, research-based reading interventions Foorman et al., 1998.
  • A kindergarten teacher screens in fall; a student fails the blending and rhyming tasks, so the teacher forms a small phonemic-awareness group meeting daily for 15 minutes and re-checks at the mid-year wave.
