# Graphic Organizers and Visual Aids

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Graphic organizers and visual aids present content in structured visual formats — concept maps, Venn diagrams, timelines, flowcharts, advance organizers, comparison matrices — that externalize relationships among ideas. They direct attention to the most important information, make structural relationships visible, and offload organization work from working memory onto the display.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers work by imposing structure on to-be-learned material, which reduces the effort learners spend identifying what matters and how it connects [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Visual formats also recruit a second representational channel, improving recall when words and pictures are well integrated [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. Effectiveness depends on the organizer matching the actual structure of the content — a hierarchy for taxonomies, a flowchart for processes, a matrix for comparisons — and on learners being taught how to read and use it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Analysis of the content's underlying structure before selecting an organizer type
- Explicit instruction in how to interpret and complete the organizer ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) work only when learners understand their logic)
- Integration with the text or instruction rather than as a detached add-on; signaling that highlights key relationships supports attention [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Overly complex or decorative graphics add extraneous load and depress learning [~S] — a cluttered organizer is worse than none
- Provided organizers can encourage shallow copying; learners who generate their own organizer often learn more than those given one [~S], though generation costs time and can overload novices
- Poorly matched to content structure (e.g., a linear timeline for a causal system), organizers can instill a misleading model of the domain [-M]
- Learners with strong prior knowledge may find organizers redundant and gain little [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-provided** organizers: efficient for novices; use as a framing device before or during instruction
- **Learner-generated** organizers (concept mapping, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)): deeper processing; pair with [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) prompts to avoid mechanical copying [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- **Partially completed** ("skeleton") organizers: a middle path that structures without doing the work — a form of [Fading](../elements/fading.md)
- **Cooperative** mapping: pairs or groups negotiate the organizer, surfacing disagreements about relationships

### Target Learners
- Novices and learners who struggle with organization, attention, or processing large amounts of text [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Younger learners and English language learners, for whom visual structure reduces language-processing demands
- Less beneficial for experts, who already possess the organizing schema [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Relational knowledge: seeing how concepts connect, compare, and hierarchize
- Text comprehension and summarization: extracting macro-structure from extended material
- Retention of key facts and relationships embedded in a structure
- Less suited to skill automatization or open-ended creative production

### Instructions
1. Identify the core structure of the content (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, causal chain, part–whole).
2. Select or design an organizer that mirrors that structure; keep it sparse — key concepts and labeled relationships only ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
3. Introduce the organizer before or early in instruction as a framing device ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Model completing a portion of it, then have learners complete the rest individually or in pairs, prompting them to explain their placement choices ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)).
5. Fade the organizer over successive tasks as learners internalize the structure ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **[Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org)** — published library of story maps, comparison charts, and concept frames used in K– literacy instruction.
- **[Inspiration Software / Kidspiration](https://www.inspiration.com)** — concept-mapping tools widely used in schools for learner-generated webs and diagrams.
- **Comparison matrices in history instruction** — a grid of events × causes × outcomes that turns narrative text into comparable cases; contrasting cases in matrix form supports abstraction [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
