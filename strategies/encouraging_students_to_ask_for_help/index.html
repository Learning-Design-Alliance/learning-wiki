Encouraging Students to Ask for Help¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (4 review, 2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 0 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy builds a classroom environment in which seeking help is treated as a normal, strategic part of learning rather than a sign of inadequacy. It combines cultural moves (normalizing struggle, modeling help-seeking) with explicit instruction in how to seek help well — articulating what is confusing, what has already been tried, and what kind of help is needed. The aim is adaptive help-seeking: students first attempt independent strategies, then seek targeted assistance that supports rather than replaces their own effort.

Design Implications¶

Help-seeking is a core self-regulatory behavior: students who seek help adaptively cope with difficulty more effectively and persist longer Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]. However, students frequently avoid help due to perceived costs — embarrassment, appearing incompetent, or threat to autonomy — so the design problem is reducing those costs while keeping the help instrumental rather than answer-giving [~M]. Unfiltered help (e.g., bottom-out hints) can become "help abuse," where students click through hints without engaging, undermining learning [-S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit framing that struggle and help-seeking are expected parts of learning, reinforced repeatedly by the instructor
  • Sentence stems and routines that give students the language to specify their need ("I understand X but not Y," "I tried A and it failed because…")
  • Instructor modeling of help-seeking, including the instructor publicly asking for help or revising their own thinking (Think-Aloud)
  • Structured opportunities to practice articulating needs (Class Discussion, Peer Discussion, Check-ins)
  • Help structures that scaffold rather than solve — hints sequenced from general to specific (Scaffolding, Coaching)

Constraints¶

  • One-off "it's okay to ask for help" messaging does not change behavior; students' avoidance is driven by stable perceived social costs, so norms must be built through repeated practice and visible instructor response [-M]
  • If help is too readily available or delivers answers, students offload thinking and learn less than peers who must attempt the problem first [-S]
  • In competitive or high-stakes grading environments, help-seeking carries reputational cost and drops sharply regardless of instructor messaging [-M]
  • Students with low academic self-efficacy may interpret needing help as confirmation of inability, so framing must emphasize help-seeking as a strategy of successful learners, not remediation [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Peer-first protocols: require consulting a peer before the instructor, distributing help-giving and building collaborative norms (Collaboration)
  • Anonymous channels: question queues, sticky notes, or digital backchannels lower the social cost for students unwilling to ask publicly
  • Metacognitive prompting in tools: intelligent tutoring systems can prompt learners to evaluate hints before revealing them, training better help use
  • Help-request artifacts: requiring a written "what I tried / where I'm stuck" slip converts help-seeking into Self-Explanation and often resolves the question before it is asked

Target Learners¶

  • Students hesitant to seek help or lacking self-advocacy skills, who otherwise disengage silently [-M]
  • Novices, who often do not yet know what they need help with; teaching need-articulation is itself part of instruction [~M]
  • Students developing self-regulation, since adaptive help-seeking is one of the coping strategies that distinguishes self-regulated learners Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Less effective as-is for students with strong metacognitive monitoring, who already seek help selectively and need only access, not encouragement [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulated learning: knowing when, whom, and how to ask
  • Metacognition: accurately identifying the point of confusion before requesting help
  • Persistence and academic resilience: treating difficulty as workable rather than as evidence of fixed ability Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Communication: articulating one's own understanding gaps precisely

Instructions¶

  1. Model help-seeking explicitly. Demonstrate encountering difficulty and asking a targeted question aloud (Think-Aloud), including what you tried first.
  2. Normalize struggle publicly. Share your own stories of seeking help; frame errors as information (Feedback culture).
  3. Teach the help-seeking script. Provide sentence stems: "I tried , I expected , but ___ happened." Require this format for instructor questions.
  4. Build in structured practice. Use Check-ins and peer protocols (Peer Discussion) so asking is routine, not exceptional.
  5. Respond instrumentally. When students ask, coach with questions and partial hints rather than answers (Coaching, Scaffolding), fading support as competence grows Contingent scaffolding improves learning. [+M].
  6. Reinforce and assess. Notice and name good help-seeking; track whether requests become more specific over time.

Related Strategies¶

  • Formative Assessment — creates the low-stakes moments in which asking for help carries no grade cost
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — its coaching phase is where instrumental help-seeking responses live
  • Growth mindset framing — reframing difficulty as normal underpins the normative message (plain text; no page yet)
  • Use predicted hint-taking likelihood and hint effects to adaptively decide whether to withhold or provide hints
  • Normalize and de-stigmatize help-seeking behavior in the classroom so academic help-seeking habits carry over to health issues

Examples¶

  • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) — hint sequences in exercises are deliberately ordered from general to specific, an attempt to keep help instrumental; research on such systems shows learners often under-use or abuse hints, motivating explicit help-seeking training.
  • ASSISTments (https://www.assistments.org) — logs student help requests as data, allowing teachers to see who is struggling silently and who is over-requesting.
  • Elementary "ask three before me" routines — peer-first protocols that distribute help-giving and lower the cost of approaching the teacher.
  • College supplemental instruction (SI) sessions — regularly scheduled peer-led help structures that normalize attendance rather than framing it as remediation.
