# Set Clear Objectives

## Description
Setting clear objectives means specifying, in advance and in learner-accessible language, what students should know or be able to do after instruction, then aligning activities and assessments to those objectives. Well-formed objectives name an observable performance, the conditions under it occurs, and the criterion for success — the classic Mager formulation. The strategy operates on two channels: it disciplines the instructor's design decisions (alignment), and it gives learners a goal structure that directs attention and effort.

## Design Implications

Clear objectives function as advance organizers, giving learners a schema for incoming instruction, and as goal-setting devices that improve performance relative to vague "do your best" intentions [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S]. Objectives also make feedback meaningful: feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and process relative to a known goal [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The alignment discipline — objectives, activities, and assessments all pointing at the same performance — is the core of constructive alignment [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Objectives written at the right cognitive level (e.g., using the [revised Bloom taxonomy](https://www.celt.iastate.edu/instructional-strategies/effective-teaching-practices/revised-blooms-taxonomy/)), naming observable behavior rather than mental states ("students will analyze…" not "students will appreciate…")
- A limited number of objectives per lesson or unit; overloading dilutes attention and planning
- Assessments that actually measure the stated objective — misalignment between what is stated and what is tested is the most common failure mode
- Objectives shared with learners in plain language, not standards jargon

#### Constraints
- Objectives framed as outcomes rather than processes can hurt novices, who benefit more from process goals describing *how* to approach the task [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M]
- Overly specific behavioral objectives can fragment learning into checklists and undermine integration of knowledge into coherent wholes [~W]
- Objectives shared without accompanying [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or structure become wallpaper — students skim and ignore them [-W]
- For ill-structured, creative, or exploratory domains, rigid objectives can narrow the solution space prematurely and reduce intrinsic motivation [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-facing vs. learner-facing:** detailed objectives drive design; learner-facing versions should be simplified and framed as "you will be able to…"
- **Essential questions:** some frameworks (e.g., [Understanding by Design](https://www.ascd.org/books/understanding-by-design-expanded-2nd-edition)) replace or supplement objectives with open essential questions for conceptual units
- **Co-constructed objectives:** learners generate or negotiate success criteria, which supports ownership and self-regulation [~W]
- **Competency-based granularity:** objectives decomposed into fine-grained competencies with mastery thresholds, as in [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md)

### Target Learners
- Novices benefit most, because clear objectives reduce unguided search and clarify what matters [Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S]
- Novices additionally benefit when objectives are framed as process goals rather than outcome goals [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [~M]
- Advanced learners may need objectives less; excessive goal specification can constrain their exploration [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural and declarative learning with well-defined success criteria
- Alignment of [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) with instruction (validity of measurement)
- Self-regulation: objectives give learners reference points for self-monitoring and interpreting feedback [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Identify the target performance and write objectives at the appropriate cognitive level, using observable verbs.
2. Share objectives with learners in plain language, ideally paired with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that shows how the lesson fits the larger structure.
3. Design learning activities that give learners [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on exactly the stated performance — not a near or distant variant.
4. Build the [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) directly from the objectives so that what is tested matches what was promised.
5. Return to objectives during feedback and [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md), so learners evaluate their progress against the stated goal rather than against peers or effort.

## Related Strategies
- [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — objectives state *where* learning is going; organizers supply the structure for getting there
- [Formative Assessment](../strategies/formative-assessment.md) — objectives define the criteria against which formative feedback is interpreted
- [Backward Design](../strategies/backward-design.md) — the design discipline that starts from objectives and works backward to activities

## Examples
- **Constructive alignment in course design** — Biggs' framework requires intended learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment tasks to state the same verb level (e.g., "analyze case data" taught through case analysis and assessed with a case-analysis exam).
- **[Understanding by Design](https://www.ascd.org/books/understanding-by-design-expanded-2nd-edition)** (Wiggins & McTighe) — a published curriculum framework built entirely on objectives-first design with essential questions and performance tasks.
- **3rd-grade science** — "Students will classify organisms into groups based on observable characteristics and justify each classification" pairs a sorting activity with a classification rubric, rather than a recall quiz.
