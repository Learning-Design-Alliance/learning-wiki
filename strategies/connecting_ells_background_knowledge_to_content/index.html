Connecting ELLs' Background Knowledge to Content¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q3 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy makes instruction more culturally relevant by deliberately connecting new content to English language learners' (ELLs') existing experiences, cultural knowledge, and funds of knowledge. Teachers first find out what background knowledge students hold on a topic, then look for points of contact between the content and what is familiar — students' home countries, educational systems, artistic and musical traditions, geography, or local ecology. Students are positioned as contributors, sharing their own cultural expertise with the class, which both anchors their comprehension and broadens the whole class's cultural understanding.

Design Implications¶

Activating relevant prior knowledge is one of the most consistently supported comprehension moves in learning research: new information is encoded in relation to what learners already know, and ELLs' knowledge is often present but not triggered by instruction delivered in a second language Activation improves learning outcomes. [+S]. The strategy works because it lowers the dual load of processing unfamiliar language and unfamiliar content simultaneously; when content is anchored to familiar schemas, working memory is freed for language development Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Critically, the connection must be genuine — superficial "cultural dressing" on otherwise unchanged instruction does not produce the engagement or comprehension gains of substantive integration Culturally responsive teaching improves outcomes when it changes pedagogy, not just content surface. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Systematic knowledge of students' backgrounds gathered through Check-Ins, home-language surveys, family interviews, or community visits
  • Willingness to revise curriculum materials, not just add examples (Analogies drawn from students' contexts)
  • Instructional structures that let students contribute expertise: Class Discussion, Case Studies, or home-language drafting before English writing
  • Attention to Accessible Vocabulary and Syntax so the connection itself is comprehensible

Constraints¶

  • Requires substantial planning and research to ensure cultural accuracy; stereotyped or tokenizing connections can alienate students rather than engage them [-M]
  • Assumes the teacher can identify relevant background knowledge — ELLs may hold knowledge that is invisible to teachers trained in Western curricula, and misreading this leads to connections that miss [-W]
  • Heterogeneous classrooms (multiple language backgrounds) make individualized connections costly; group-level connections dilute the effect [-M]
  • If connections stay at the anecdotal level and never connect to the disciplinary content being taught, time is spent without comprehension gains [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher-supplied connections: the teacher introduces analogies and examples from students' cultures (Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation)
  • Student-supplied connections: students share funds of knowledge through presentations, bilingual writing, or drawing before English production
  • Curriculum redesign: units built around community practices (e.g., Moll's funds-of-knowledge household studies feeding into math and literacy lessons)
  • Structured protocols: K-W-L charts, Advance Organizers, and pre-reading activation routines

Target Learners¶

  • English language learners at intermediate proficiency, who have rich background knowledge but cannot yet access it through English-only instruction Activation improves learning outcomes. [+S]
  • Newcomers whose prior schooling used different conventions or content sequences, who otherwise appear to "lack" background knowledge
  • Benefits the whole class through exposure to diverse perspectives, though gains are largest for the ELLs whose knowledge is activated [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of academic content delivered in a second language
  • Vocabulary development anchored to known concepts rather than translation
  • Identity and belonging: positioning ELLs as knowledgeable contributors rather than deficits
  • Cross-cultural understanding for the full class

Instructions¶

  1. Elicit what students already know about the topic, using home language, drawing, or discussion rather than English-only responses (Activation)
  2. Identify genuine points of contact between the content and students' experiences; build Analogies from those points
  3. Introduce new content through the familiar anchor first, then bridge to the standard curriculum framing (Advance Organizers)
  4. Have students apply the content to their own contexts (Application), producing work that draws on both new learning and cultural knowledge
  5. Structure opportunities for students to teach the class about their expertise, with Coaching on academic language for the presentation

Related Strategies¶

  • Accessing Students' Background Knowledge — the elicitation half of the cycle

Examples¶

  • Funds of knowledge units (Moll et al.) — Teachers visit students' households, document the knowledge and skills there (construction, farming, mechanics), and build math and literacy lessons around them (https://hepgjournals.org/doi/10.17763/haer.62.4.3466k53037463q33).
  • SIOP model — The Sheltered Instruction Observation Protocol includes "building background" as a scored component: teachers explicitly link concepts to students' backgrounds and past learning (https://www.pearson.com/en-us/siop-model.html).
  • K-W-L chart before a science unit — Students record what they Know (in any language) about weather patterns in their home regions before studying the host country's climate; the teacher uses these entries as the anchor for new vocabulary.
  • Bilingual drafting — Students discuss a personal narrative topic with a same-language peer, draft in the home language, then translate and expand in English.
