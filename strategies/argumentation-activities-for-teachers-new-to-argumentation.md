# Use structured argumentation activities to help teachers new to argumentation start and sustain argumentation in science courses

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (1 for, 2 mixed, 2 against) · 1 study (1 causal), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article proposes that ready-made learning activities based on argumentation serve teachers encountering argumentation for the first time, showing how to start and continue the argumentation process in science courses. Because teachers are reported as inadequate in starting and continuing argumentation, lacking knowledge about argumentation, and facing problems creating active arguing environments, the activities plus teacher guidance material scaffold the practice. The study's experimental implementation demonstrates this use.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teacher preparation sessions on what argumentation is and how the process progresses, plus a Teacher Guidance Material describing how activities could be applied
#### Constraints
- Students are not used to arguing with others, so preparation courses for students are also needed

### Target Learners
- 7th grade science students
- science teachers new to argumentation

### Target Learning Goals
- Conducting and sustaining classroom argumentation
- justifying opinions with evidence and warrants

## Claims

- [Argumentation-based learning activities did not produce a statistically significant difference in 7th graders' conceptual understanding of Force and Motion compared with regular instruction](../claims/argumentation-no-significant-conceptual-understanding-gain-force-motion.md) [-W]
- [Interviewed experimental students reported feeling themselves in thinking friendly classrooms and generally scientifically accurate understanding, with misconceptions persisting in Work, Simple Machines, and Frictional Force topics](../claims/interview-students-thinking-friendly-classroom-persistent-misconceptions.md) [+W]
- [Neither group showed a statistically significant pre-to-post change in Force and Motion misconceptions over the five-week unit](../claims/no-significant-prepost-fmct-change-either-group.md) [-W]
- [Students in the argumentation group most frequently reported teacher behaviors of respect and tolerance (31%) and letting student interaction (28%), while evidence use and example-giving were reported as insufficient](../claims/teacher-behaviors-develop-thinking-interview-frequencies.md) [~W]
- [Within-group pre-to-post TFCS changes were not statistically significant in either group, though the experimental group's views developed positively and the control group's negatively](../claims/tfcs-prepost-nonsignificant-descriptive-divergence.md) [~W]
