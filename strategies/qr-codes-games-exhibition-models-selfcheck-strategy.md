# Use QR codes for games and quizzes, a virtual exhibition, apparatus apps, computer-model study tasks, and answer-key self-checking in physics lessons

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 review), `q1` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
Beyond quests, the article gives four further worked strategies for physics teaching. In games, quizzes and polls, a task may be offered as a QR code, printed codes can be pasted directly into pupils' notebooks, and test material can take the form of cards with different task variants (Fig. 3). For the topic on heat engines and efficiency, the teacher prepares images of heat machines on their own site, encodes hyperlinks into QR codes, and hangs them with captions on classroom walls so pupils can view the virtual exhibition outside class. QR codes placed on laboratory equipment link to a virtual laboratory or self-study questions, and in the physics room can convey the purpose, use conditions, history and biographies of instrument makers. For computer models, pupils build a graph of the Maxwell distribution function for oxygen at the given conditions, with task variants encoded before the lesson. For self-checking, pupils receive a question list whose correct answers are pre-placed at Internet addresses presented as QR codes.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- the teacher encodes task variants or hyperlinks into QR codes in advance using software or services
- pupils' mobile devices with a reader app are needed to decode the codes
#### Constraints
- the article advises checking that codes read reliably; for quests it notes long tasks create QR reading difficulties

### Target Learners
- pupils (secondary school)
- students in practical classes

### Target Learning Goals
- self-checking of answers
- study of computer models of physical phenomena and processes
- engagement with learning objects beyond lesson time
