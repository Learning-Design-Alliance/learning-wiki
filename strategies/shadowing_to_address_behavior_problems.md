# Shadowing to Address Behavior Problems

## Description
Shadowing assigns a trained adult (aide, counselor, or behavior technician) to stay within arm's reach of a student exhibiting harmful behaviors (e.g., biting, aggression, elopement) to interrupt the behavior chain before an incident occurs. The shadow provides constant direct supervision without singling the student out publicly, models and prompts alternative strategies at the moment of need, and collects observational data on antecedents and triggers. The goal is to keep the classroom safe while breaking the reinforcement cycle sustaining the behavior and teaching replacement skills in context.

## Design Implications

Shadowing works as an intensive, temporary scaffold: it substitutes adult regulation for the student's self-regulation while replacement behaviors are taught, then fades as competence grows. Its effectiveness depends on pairing prevention with explicit teaching of alternatives — supervision alone suppresses behavior without building skills [~M]. Because adult attention can itself reinforce problem behavior, the shadow must attend most richly to positive and replacement behavior, not to incidents [~S]. Framing matters for the relationship: interventions delivered in a warm, empathic way preserve the student–adult bond, while punitive framing damages it and worsens downstream behavior [Brief, empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions by changing how teachers interpret and respond to misbehavior.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained adult with capacity to be present continuously during risk periods; administrative and parental consent and support
- Clear, pre-agreed protocols for what the shadow does at each stage (prevention, prompting, de-escalation, crisis)
- A functional understanding of the behavior — what triggers and reinforces it — gathered through observation before the shadow begins
- A plan to teach replacement behaviors and to [fading](../elements/fading.md) the shadow's presence as the student succeeds

#### Constraints
- Labor-intensive and costly; dedicated one-to-one staffing is often unsustainable and can create dependency if not faded [-M]
- Suppresses symptoms without addressing root causes (skill deficits, trauma, academic frustration, unmet needs) if used as containment only [-M]
- Can stigmatize the student if peers perceive the shadow as surveillance; low-key presence and consistent staffing mitigate this [~M]
- Adult proximity and attention can inadvertently reinforce the target behavior if attention follows incidents rather than prosocial behavior [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full shadowing** (arm's reach, all day) for dangerous behaviors, fading to proximity supervision, then periodic check-ins
- **Time-limited shadowing** during identified high-risk segments (transitions, recess, unstructured time) rather than the whole day
- **Peer-mediated variants** in which structured peer support partially substitutes for adult proximity in less severe cases
- Integration into a school-wide framework such as [Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports](https://www.pbis.org), where shadowing is the Tier 3 intensive tier rather than a stand-alone fix

### Target Learners
- Students in early childhood and elementary settings with frequent, high-intensity harmful behaviors who have not responded to class-wide supports
- Students whose behavior is predictable and antecedent-driven, where proactive interruption is feasible [~M]
- Less appropriate for behavior driven primarily by acute distress or crisis, where therapeutic rather than supervisory responses are needed [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: internalizing replacement strategies the shadow initially prompts and models
- Social skill acquisition: turn-taking, requesting help, tolerating frustration in authentic settings
- Classroom safety and access to learning for the whole class, protecting instructional time for peers

### Instructions
1. **Observe and define.** Collect baseline data on frequency, antecedents, and consequences of the target behavior; define it in observable terms.
2. **Brief the team.** Secure administrator and parent agreement; write the intervention protocol, including what the shadow does *before*, *during*, and *after* risk moments.
3. **Assign a consistent adult.** Use the same shadow across days to build trust and consistency; a stable relationship is itself protective [~M].
4. **Shadow proactively, not punitively.** Stay within arm's reach during risk periods, pre-empting triggers and prompting replacement behaviors; deliver attention and praise contingent on positive behavior, not incidents.
5. **Teach in the moment.** Use [modeling](../elements/act-it-out.md) and guided [practice](../elements/practice.md) of the replacement behavior immediately before and during the situations where the problem behavior occurs.
6. **Check in and review data.** Hold brief daily [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) with the student and weekly team reviews of incident data to adjust the plan.
7. **Fade deliberately.** Systematically increase distance and reduce prompting as the student succeeds, transferring monitoring to the student ([assess performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) before each fade step).

## Examples
- A kindergarten student who bites during unstructured transitions is shadowed by a paraprofessional for three weeks; the shadow pre-teaches a "ask for a break" card, prompts it at each transition, and fades to proximity by week five as incident data drops.
- A behavior technician shadowing a student with elopement risk as part of an individualized [PBIS](https://www.pbis.org) Tier 3 plan, collecting ABC (antecedent–behavior–consequence) data that later drives a function-based intervention.
