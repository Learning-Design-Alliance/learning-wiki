# Where Are the People Like Me?

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners examine media examples — films, television, music, advertising, fashion — to analyze who is represented, who is missing, and how groups are portrayed. Through structured discussion and reflection, they connect patterns of representation to effects on identity, belonging, and social perception. The strategy builds critical media literacy while surfacing the affective consequences of under- and misrepresentation, particularly for learners from marginalized groups.

## Design Implications

The strategy works by making an invisible curriculum visible: media selections learners consume daily encode messages about whose stories matter. Analyzing these patterns activates prior knowledge and lived experience, which supports deeper processing [Activation of prior knowledge improves comprehension and engagement.](../principles/activation.md) [+M]. For learners who rarely see themselves represented, the question "where are the people like me?" is also an identity and belonging intervention — belonging and self-efficacy predict academic persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic media examples chosen to contain analyzable patterns of presence, absence, or stereotyped portrayal
- Facilitation that establishes norms for respectful discussion of identity and difference ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Structured reflection prompts that move learners from observation ("who appears?") to inference ("what message does this send?") to implication ("who does this affect, and how?")
- Optional readings framing representation research (e.g., cultivation theory, stereotype threat) to ground discussion in evidence ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))

#### Constraints
- Without careful facilitation, discussion can devolve into defensiveness or tokenizing analysis; instructors must establish discussion norms before analysis begins
- Learners from represented majority groups may initially dismiss the issue as unimportant, requiring evidence-based framing rather than assertion
- A single session risks producing awareness without transfer; effects on media consumption habits require repeated analysis across varied media types
- For learners whose groups are the ones absent or stereotyped, the activity can be emotionally taxing; opt-out and alternative framing options should exist

#### Implementation Variability
- Learner-selected media: participants bring examples from their own cultural consumption, increasing relevance and personalization
- Comparative audit: learners code a corpus of examples (e.g., 20 film trailers) for representation, turning the activity into quantitative media analysis
- Production follow-up: learners create counter-examples or re-cast media, moving from critique to creation
- Discipline-specific versions: analyzing representation in science textbooks, history curricula, or children's literature rather than popular media

### Target Learners
- Adolescents through adults; particularly effective with learners old enough to analyze media meta-cognitively rather than only respond to it
- Learners from underrepresented groups, for whom naming the pattern can validate lived experience and support belonging [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners from well-represented groups, who benefit from developing perspective-taking and empathy for absent or misrepresented others [Building empathy through structured perspective-taking improves intergroup attitudes.](../principles/building-empathy.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Media literacy: identifying representation patterns, framing, and stereotyping in media texts
- Critical thinking: moving from observation to inference about audience effects
- Social awareness and identity development: connecting representation to belonging and self-concept
- Communication: articulating evidence-based arguments about contested social topics

### Instructions
1. Select or solicit media examples containing analyzable representation patterns; learner-selected examples increase personal relevance.
2. Provide framing readings or a mini-lecture on representation research so analysis is grounded, not purely opinion-based ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)).
3. Pose the guiding question — "Where are the people like me?" — and have learners individually inventory who appears, who is missing, and how groups are portrayed in their example.
4. Facilitate structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) comparing findings across examples, moving from observation to inference about effects on viewers.
5. Close with individual written reflection connecting the analysis to learners' own media consumption and identity.
6. Optionally extend into production: learners create or re-imagine media that addresses the gaps they identified ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) or media creation).

## Examples
- Students audit the ethnic composition of lead characters across a season of a popular streaming series, then discuss what the pattern signals about whose stories are considered central.
- Learners analyze LGBTQ+ representation in music videos and fashion advertising, distinguishing visibility from authentic portrayal.
- A children's literature course applies the question to award-winning picture books, counting protagonists by race and gender, then writing counter-narratives.
