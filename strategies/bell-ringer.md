# Bell Ringer

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A bell ringer (also called a "do now," "warm-up," or "opening hook") is a brief task — typically 3–8 minutes — that students begin immediately upon entering class, before formal instruction starts. It is usually displayed or distributed so students can start without teacher direction, and it serves one of three functions: retrieving previously learned content, activating relevant prior knowledge for the day's lesson, or previewing the coming topic.

## Design Implications

Bell ringers exploit the opening minutes of class, when attention is unclaimed and time is otherwise lost to transitions, to add distributed retrieval or activation opportunities at near-zero instructional cost [Activation improves learning by preparing relevant knowledge for use.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Their learning value depends on the task type: retrieval-based openers (answering questions about prior material) produce durable memory benefits beyond restudying [Retrieval practice produces more durable learning than restudying.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S], while purely procedural busywork produces none. Because the task runs without teacher prompting, it must be self-explanatory and low-load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task students can start independently within seconds, displayed or handed out as they enter
- Content that connects to prior lessons or the day's objective — not disconnected filler
- A predictable routine so students know where to find the task and what to do with it
- A brief follow-up during class (whole-class review, peer check, or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) so the work is not silently discarded

#### Constraints
- If the task requires teacher explanation before students can begin, the routine collapses and the opening minutes are lost anyway
- Novel-content "hooks" that are engaging but unrelated to the objective can improve interest while adding nothing to learning, and may even misdirect attention away from the target content [~M]
- Retrieval-based bell ringers on material students never learned well produce failure experiences rather than retrieval benefits; the material must have been at least initially taught [-M]
- Daily low-stakes grading of bell ringers shifts attention toward compliance and can undermine the formative purpose [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Retrieval opener**: 2–4 questions on yesterday's or last week's content — the highest-evidence option
- **Activation opener**: a question, image, or short scenario that surfaces what students already know about today's topic ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) serve this function in text form)
- **Preview/hook opener**: a provocative question or demonstration related to the coming lesson, used to create a knowledge gap
- **Diagnostic opener**: a quick poll or one-item probe the teacher uses to adjust the day's plan ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- **Skill drill**: brief fluency practice (math facts, vocabulary, conjugations) building [Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md)

### Target Learners
- All ages; the routine is especially valuable for younger students and learners with executive-function challenges, for whom an unstructured class opening is the hardest part of the period
- Struggling students benefit disproportionately from the extra spaced retrieval opportunities that daily openers accumulate [+S]
- Students with strong prior knowledge gain less from activation-type openers, though they still benefit from retrieval-type ones [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention of previously taught content through spaced retrieval
- Preparation for new learning through prior-knowledge activation [Activation improves learning by preparing relevant knowledge for use.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Fluency and automaticity in foundational skills
- Formative information for the teacher about what needs reteaching

### Instructions
1. Choose the function (retrieval, activation, preview, or diagnostic) based on where students are relative to today's objective.
2. Write 1–4 short items or one focused prompt; keep the load low enough that every student can begin within 30 seconds [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].
3. Display it in a fixed location before students arrive; establish the routine explicitly for the first several weeks.
4. Circulate and scan responses while students work, using them as a quick [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) on readiness.
5. Debrief in 2–3 minutes — connect retrieval answers to today's lesson or use activation responses to bridge into new content ([Activation](activation.md)).
6. Vary the format (question, image, error to fix, prediction) to keep attention engaged ([Attention](../elements/attention.md)).

## Examples
- A middle-school math teacher opens each class with two problems from material taught 1–3 weeks earlier, exploiting the spacing effect; this is the core routine in many [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) classrooms.
- An ELA teacher displays a sentence with a common usage error for students to diagnose, building editing fluency daily.
- A science teacher opens a unit on ecosystems with an image of a local pond and the prompt "List everything you think lives here and what it needs" — an activation opener that surfaces prior conceptions, including misconceptions, before instruction.
