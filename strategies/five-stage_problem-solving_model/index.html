Five-Stage Problem-Solving Model¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 causal), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

A five-stage model that scaffolds learners through a complete problem-solving cycle: (1) understand the problem by restating it in their own words, (2) describe the barriers standing between them and the goal, (3) identify multiple possible solutions, (4) try a solution while keeping accurate records of thoughts and procedures, and (5) evaluate the results. The model externalizes the metacognitive and self-regulatory moves that skilled problem-solvers perform implicitly, making them explicit and teachable.

Design Implications¶

The model's value lies in converting problem solving from an unstructured search into a self-regulated cycle. Explicit strategy instruction of this kind improves problem-solving performance, particularly when learners must also monitor and evaluate their own progress [~M]. Stage 4's record-keeping requirement is critical: it supplies the raw material for Stage 5's evaluation and builds the self-monitoring habits that characterize Self-Regulated Learning.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Learners understand the five stages and have a template or graphic organizer for recording work at each stage
  • Problems that are genuinely non-routine — if the answer is obvious, the cycle adds overhead without benefit
  • Time for the full cycle, especially Stages 4–5; truncating evaluation undermines the model's main benefit
  • Instructor modeling of the cycle on a worked problem before learners attempt it (Coaching, Think-Aloud)

Constraints¶

  • Rigid application can become a bureaucratic checklist; learners who follow the stages mechanically without genuine restatement or evaluation gain little [-W]
  • Less effective for well-structured, algorithmic tasks where a direct procedure exists — the cycle adds cognitive load without payoff [~M]
  • Requires sustained learner engagement in self-assessment; adolescents and novices often skip or shallowly complete Stages 2 and 5 without accountability structures [-W]
  • For novices in high-element-interactivity domains, open exploration during Stage 3 can induce overload; pairing with worked examples first mitigates this Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone. [+M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Adapt stage names and prompts to domain: IDEAL (Identify, Define, Explore, Act, Look) in general problem solving; understand–plan–solve–check in mathematics
  • Use with Case Studies so learners cycle through the stages on realistic, ill-structured scenarios
  • Fade the scaffold: begin with a full written organizer, then prompt stages orally, then remove prompts as learners internalize the cycle (Fading)
  • Run collaboratively, with groups dividing stages or debating Stage 3 alternatives (Collaborative Learning)

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who lack an organized approach and default to impulsive or random solution attempts [~M]
  • K–12 and adult learners alike; the cycle is domain-general
  • Learners developing self-regulation; the record-keeping and evaluation stages build monitoring skills [~M]
  • Less necessary for experts, who already execute these moves tacitly and may find the structure constraining [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Problem-solving skill: systematic analysis of non-routine problems
  • Metacognition and self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own reasoning
  • Transfer: a reusable procedure applicable across domains, from mathematics to interpersonal conflict resolution

Instructions¶

  1. Understand — Learners restate the problem in their own words, list given information, and identify what is being asked (Advance Organizers can frame this)
  2. Describe barriers — Learners articulate what is blocking the goal, distinguishing the core obstacle from surface details (Challenge Identification)
  3. Identify solutions — Learners generate multiple candidate approaches before committing, optionally using Analogies to related solved problems
  4. Act and record — Learners implement a chosen solution while documenting their reasoning and steps (Practice with Annotating)
  5. Evaluate — Learners check results against the goal, analyze what worked, and decide whether to cycle back (Individual Reflection, Assessment)

Related Strategies¶

  • IDEAL Problem Solving — a closely parallel five-stage model (Identify, Define, Explore, Act, Look back)
  • Reciprocal Teaching — similarly externalizes expert strategies through structured stages
  • Case-Based Learning — supplies the authentic problems the cycle is applied to
  • Use staged problem-solving reflection assignments to build metacognition around LLM use and surface student help-seeking perspectives

Examples¶

  • Mathematics classrooms: Students restate a word problem in their own words, list knowns and unknowns, sketch a diagram, attempt a solution showing all work, then verify the answer against the original question — Polya's understand/plan/carry out/look-back cycle in practice
  • IDEAL problem solving (Bransford & Stein) — a published curriculum framework embedding the same cycle in science and everyday reasoning tasks
  • Social-emotional learning programs: Students facing interpersonal conflicts work through the stages — defining the problem, identifying obstacles, brainstorming responses, trying one, and reflecting on the outcome — as in problem-solving training within Second Step
  • Engineering design courses: Learners document constraint analysis, alternative concepts, prototype records, and post-test evaluation in a design notebook mirroring the five stages
