# Family Engagement

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Family engagement is a practice in which families and school personnel collaborate to support and improve the learning and development of children. It spans behaviors at home (reading with children, discussing school, monitoring homework), at school (communicating with teachers, attending events, governance participation), and in the community. Effective engagement requires thoughtful planning, sustained two-way communication, modeling high expectations, and treating families as partners rather than recipients of information.

## Design Implications

Family engagement functions as an out-of-school support layer that extends instructional time and reinforces learning goals; its effects on achievement are consistently positive but modest, and strongest when engagement is directly tied to learning content rather than general school involvement [~M]. Engagement practices must be designed as two-way partnerships — schools that merely transmit information see weaker effects than those that build relational trust and shared responsibility [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Commitment from school personnel, active participation from families, and effective communication channels
- Relational trust between teachers and families; communication in families' home languages and responsive to work schedules and access constraints
- Specific, actionable guidance connecting home activities to classroom learning goals (e.g., structured home reading routines rather than generic "help with homework" advice)

#### Constraints
- Requires time, effort, and resources to develop and maintain; may be challenging to engage all families, and effects are weaker for engagement driven by compliance or attendance at events rather than home-based learning support [~M]
- Generic involvement (attending fundraisers, volunteering) shows weaker relationships with achievement than engagement centered on learning activities at home [~M]
- Poorly designed engagement can burden families with unequal time and resources, widening gaps between families with flexible schedules and those working multiple jobs [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Epstein's framework distinguishes six types: parenting, communicating, volunteering, learning at home, decision-making, and collaborating with community — programs typically combine several
- Digital platforms (messaging apps, family portals) enable low-cost, asynchronous communication but risk excluding families without reliable connectivity
- Home-visiting programs (e.g., Parent Teacher Home Visits) build trust through in-person, relationship-first contact

### Target Learners
- Students from early childhood through secondary school, with the strongest documented effects in elementary grades [~M]
- Students from historically underserved communities benefit when engagement is culturally responsive and builds on family funds of knowledge rather than deficit framing [+M]
- Young children developing language benefit specifically from structured home literacy interactions [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+S]
- Students at risk of disengagement benefit when engagement reinforces belonging; family-school partnership supports the same belonging mechanisms as classroom interventions [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic achievement: improved grades, test scores, and graduation rates, especially when engagement targets specific learning content [+M]
- Attendance and behavior: reduced absenteeism and improved conduct [+M]
- Social-emotional development: self-regulation and school attachment [+W]
- Metacognitive and study habits: home routines that scaffold planning and persistence

### Instructions
1. Establish relational trust before academic asks — conduct outreach (home visits, welcome calls) that centers family strengths and goals.
2. Set up regular two-way communication channels with [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) at predictable intervals, translated and accessible to all families.
3. Provide families with specific, curriculum-linked home activities — e.g., structured [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) guidance for reading routines or math games tied to current units.
4. Coordinate with classroom supports such as [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) so home and school expectations are consistent for students with diverse needs.
5. Include families in decision-making (advisory councils, goal-setting conferences) rather than only information transmission.
6. Evaluate engagement by family reach and student outcomes, not event attendance; adjust outreach for families not being reached.

## Related Strategies
- Community-based learning — extends the partnership model beyond the family into neighborhood organizations
- Home literacy routines — the most-studied specific family engagement practice

## Examples
- **Parent Teacher Home Visits** (https://www.pthvp.org) — relationship-first home visiting model used across U.S. districts, with documented gains in attendance and teacher-family trust.
- **Epstein's Six Types of Involvement framework** — used by the National Network of Partnership Schools (https://nnps.jhucsos.com) to structure school-family partnership action plans.
- **Dialogic reading programs** — training parents to use interactive questioning during shared book reading, one of the best-evidenced home learning interventions.
