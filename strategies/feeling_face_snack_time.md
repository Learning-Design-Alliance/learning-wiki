# Feeling Face Snack Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners draw a "feeling face" card depicting an emotion (happy, frustrated, surprised, worried) and, while eating a shared snack, show the matching facial expression and body posture. The facilitator names each emotion, invites brief imitation, and prompts short conversations about when learners have felt that way. Embedding the activity in a familiar, low-stakes routine connects emotional vocabulary to physical experience and peer interaction.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it pairs explicit emotion labeling with embodied enactment and repeated exposure in a predictable routine — conditions that support early emotional vocabulary growth and emotion knowledge, which in turn predict social competence and school readiness [Denham's emotion-knowledge research supports early emotion labeling.](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327935ped1701_4) [+M]. Modeling by the facilitator and peers is central: young children acquire expressive and regulatory behavior substantially through observation [Bandura, A. (1977). *Social learning theory.*](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of feeling-face cards covering a small, age-appropriate emotion set (start with 4–6 basic emotions)
- A routine snack setting with seating that lets all learners see one another's faces
- A facilitator who models each expression first and names the emotion with precise vocabulary ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
- Norms for respectful response — no laughing at or mocking a peer's expression

#### Constraints
- Learners uncomfortable expressing emotions publicly may disengage or comply superficially; participation pressure in front of peers can suppress authentic expression [-W]
- If the facilitator treats it as a performance game only, without naming emotions or connecting them to real experiences, learners may learn poses without learning emotion concepts [-W]
- Learners with sensory sensitivities or feeding difficulties may find the snack component itself dysregulating, undermining the emotional focus [-W]
- Imitation of facial expressions is harder for some autistic learners; forcing mimicry rather than allowing labeling or pointing can create failure experiences [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Point-to-the-face variant:** learners select the card matching a scenario the facilitator describes, instead of performing the expression — lowers social exposure
- **Guess-my-feeling variant:** one learner shows an expression and peers name it, adding a discrimination and feedback loop
- **Story-link variant:** cards are tied to characters in a read-aloud, connecting expressions to narrative causes of emotion
- Cards can be replaced by photographs of the learners themselves, which increases engagement and personal relevance

### Target Learners
- Early childhood and early elementary learners (ages ~3–8) building foundational emotion vocabulary [+M]
- Multilingual learners, for whom paired visual + embodied + verbal input supports new vocabulary [Multiple modalities aid early vocabulary learning.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as-is for adolescents, for whom the routine may feel infantilizing; the card-and-label structure transfers better than the snack framing [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotion recognition and labeling (identifying emotions in self and others)
- Emotional expression and early self-regulation skills
- Social-emotional vocabulary development and empathic perspective-taking [Building empathy through perspective-taking activities.](../principles/building-empathy.md) [+W]

### Instructions
1. Establish snack-time seating so every learner can see peers' faces; review the "kind watching" norm.
2. [Demonstrate](../elements/demonstration.md) first: the facilitator draws a card, names the emotion ("This is *frustrated*"), shows the expression, and gives one example of when it happens.
3. Learners take turns drawing cards; each shows the expression while taking a bite, and peers guess or confirm the emotion ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with immediate peer feedback).
4. Facilitator extends with one prompt per card: "When have you felt surprised?" — keeping turns brief to hold attention.
5. Close by sorting the cards into "comfortable feelings" and "uncomfortable feelings," previewing regulation talk.
6. Repeat across days, rotating in new emotions and using the cards at other moments ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md)) so the vocabulary generalizes beyond snack time.

## Examples
- **[Incredible Years](http://www.incredibleyears.com)** (Webster-Stratton) — the Dinosaur School curriculum uses puppet- and card-based emotion identification embedded in routine circle times, closely paralleling this structure.
- **[Second Step](https://www.secondstep.org)** (Committee for Children) — early-grade lessons use feeling-face cards and "practice the expression" moments during recurring classroom routines.
- A preschool teacher runs Feeling Face Snack Time daily for three weeks, then transfers the cards to the calm-down corner, where children point to a card to communicate how they feel.
