# Interpreting the News

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners select a current news item and examine how it is reported across multiple outlets, comparing framing, sourcing, and emphasis to infer the assumptions and perspectives of each reporter. The activity treats news texts as constructed artifacts rather than transparent windows on events, building media literacy, critical evaluation skills, and perspective-taking through structured comparison and discussion.

## Design Implications

Comparing multiple accounts of the same event is a form of multiple-document analysis that pushes learners beyond single-text comprehension toward evaluating source, purpose, and bias [~M]. Contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying dimensions — framing, omission, word choice — that any single text cannot reveal [Multiple contrasting cases help learners abstract the features that distinguish them.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The strategy works best when learners are prompted to explain *why* accounts differ, not just that they differ [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to several news sources covering the same event (or archived coverage of a past event)
- A structured comparison protocol (framing, sourcing, language, what is emphasized vs. omitted) so analysis is systematic rather than impressionistic
- Facilitation that models evaluative questions before learners analyze independently ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of an expert reading one article)
- A discussion space where divergent interpretations can be voiced and defended with textual evidence

#### Constraints
- Learners without background knowledge of the event's context cannot distinguish bias from accurate reporting; they may attribute disagreement to "everyone is biased" rather than analyzing *how* accounts differ [-M] — a cynical relativism that undermines the goal
- Learners with strong prior attitudes evaluate congenial coverage as more credible (confirmation bias), so unstructured analysis can entrench rather than challenge existing views [-M]
- If the comparison task is purely open-ended, novices attend to surface features (length, tone) rather than structural features like sourcing and framing [-W]
- Requires careful facilitation to keep analysis of viewpoints respectful when topics are politically charged

#### Implementation Variability
- **Lateral reading variant:** learners verify a source's credibility by leaving the article to check what other sites say about the outlet — the technique that most distinguishes expert fact-checkers from novices [+M]
- **Structured controversy variant:** learners are assigned positions to defend from the coverage, then swap ([Debate](../patterns/debate.md))
- **Annotation variant:** learners mark framing devices directly in texts ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)) before discussion
- **Historical variant:** use archived coverage of a past event to reduce emotional reactivity while preserving the analytical structure

### Target Learners
- Secondary and adult learners with enough background knowledge of the topic to evaluate claims rather than just read them [~M]
- Learners developing epistemic cognition — understanding that knowledge claims carry perspective and purpose [~M]
- Less effective for learners with no topical background, who need context-building ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)) before comparison is meaningful

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical evaluation of sources: identifying bias, framing, and omission
- Epistemic understanding: recognizing that texts are constructed from particular perspectives
- Argumentation: defending an interpretation with textual evidence ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Perspective-taking and tolerance for interpretive diversity

### Instructions
1. Select a current news item with coverage across at least three outlets with differing editorial stances; gather the articles as [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md).
2. Build necessary background on the event itself so learners can evaluate rather than merely decode.
3. Demonstrate an expert analysis of one article aloud, modeling questions about sourcing, framing, and omission ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
4. Have learners annotate the remaining articles for framing devices and compare across sources ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
5. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) in which learners explain what each account assumes and emphasize, supporting claims with evidence.
6. Close with reflection: what makes an account trustworthy, and how would the learner verify it? Connect to lateral-reading practice.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- Students compare climate-change coverage across outlets, mapping how each frames causality and which stakeholders are quoted, then discuss how framing shapes public opinion.
- A civics class uses archived coverage of a historical immigration event from ideologically distinct newspapers, annotating word choice before a structured discussion.
- Media-literacy courses built on the Stanford History Education Group's civic online reasoning curriculum ([SHEG](https://cor.stanford.edu/)) use lateral reading of news sources as a core activity.
