# Self-Instruction

## Description
Self-instruction (self-talk, self-statements) is a strategy in which learners verbalize guidance to themselves while performing a task, in order to self-regulate their behavior. Meichenbaum's five statement types structure the practice: problem definition ("What is it I have to do?"), focusing attention ("I need to watch the sign"), strategy explanation ("I divide first, then multiply"), coping ("This is hard, but I can go slowly"), and self-reinforcement ("Good, I got that right"). The developmental goal is internalization: overt speech fades to whispered, then covert, inner speech as the regulation becomes automatic [Vygotsky's private-speech account predicts this overt-to-covert progression.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+M]

## Design Implications

Self-instruction converts executive control from external (teacher prompts) to internal (learner-generated), building the self-monitoring habits that underpin self-regulated learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. It works best when taught through explicit modeling: the instructor performs the task while verbalizing the statements aloud, then has learners imitate the verbalization before fading it. Because verbalizing forces articulation of steps and reasoning, it also produces self-explanation benefits — learners detect gaps and errors in their own procedure [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- The learner must already be capable of performing the target academic or behavioral skill — self-instruction regulates execution, it does not create the skill
- Self-statements must be developmentally appropriate and meaningful to the learner; scripted statements the learner does not own are parroted and dropped
- An instructor or model who demonstrates the statements ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)) before learners adopt them
- Structured [Practice](../elements/practice.md) opportunities in which learners use the statements on real tasks

#### Constraints
- Useless if the learner cannot perform the designated skill — verbal guidance cannot substitute for missing procedural knowledge [-S]
- Overt self-talk can add cognitive load for complex tasks if statements compete with task execution for working memory [~M]; keep statements short and task-relevant
- Young children and novices may not benefit from covert (silent) self-instruction; internalization is a gradual outcome, not a starting condition [-W]
- Scripted, teacher-imposed statements that the learner finds meaningless are quickly abandoned [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Cognitive Modeling (Meichenbaum)** — instructor models the task while verbalizing, then the learner performs with overt statements, whispered statements, then covert speech
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)** — statements embedded in a writing-strategy regimen with goal setting and self-monitoring of performance
- **Coping-focused variants** — statements target emotional regulation ("stay calm, work step by step") for frustrating or anxiety-provoking tasks
- **Peer-mediated variants** — learners exchange prompts with a partner before internalizing them

### Target Learners
- Learners with learning difficulties or ADHD, for whom self-instruction interventions show the strongest effects on task completion and accuracy [+M]
- Young children developing self-regulation, for whom private speech is a natural transitional mechanism [+M]
- Novices who need support with task initiation and persistence [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Less useful for experts, whose procedures are already automatized and for whom verbalization can disrupt fluent execution [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation and metacognitive awareness: monitoring one's own comprehension and progress [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Procedural fluency: verbalizing steps during acquisition supports error detection and correction
- Coping and persistence: managing frustration and maintaining effort on difficult tasks [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Model** — the instructor performs the task while verbalizing all five statement types aloud ([Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md), [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
2. **Guide** — learner performs the task with instructor prompting the statements ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
3. **Practice overtly** — learner performs the task saying statements aloud, then whispered ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
4. **Fade to covert** — statements become silent inner speech; the instructor provides [Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on both task performance and statement use
5. **Reflect and transfer** — learner reviews which statements helped and adapts them to new tasks ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md), [Application](../elements/application.md))

## Examples
- **SRSD for writing (Graham & Harris)** — struggling writers learn self-statements ("Who will read this? What do they need to know?") alongside planning strategies; a widely replicated intervention in elementary writing instruction
- **Meichenbaum's cognitive modeling** — impulsive children taught to talk through matching tasks ("Okay, what is it I have to do?") showed large gains on attention and impulsivity measures
- **Clinical skills training** — a medical student mentally rehearses procedural steps ("check airway, then breathing") during an injury evaluation, then self-reinforces after each completed phase
- **Everyday example** — a young child talks themselves through tying shoelaces, verbalizing each loop and pull until the sequence runs silently
