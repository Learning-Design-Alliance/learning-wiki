# Creating Visuals

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Creating Visuals asks learners to generate their own visual representations — diagrams, sketches, concept maps, infographics, storyboards — of the content they are studying, rather than only viewing instructor-provided graphics. The act of constructing a visual forces learners to select, organize, and integrate information, making it a generative learning activity rather than a passive one.

## Design Implications

Student-generated visuals are effective because drawing requires translating verbal information into spatial form, which promotes integrative processing between verbal and pictorial mental models [Drawing supports learning by forcing learners to construct and integrate verbal and pictorial representations.](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) [+M]. The benefit comes from the *generative processing* the construction demands, not from the aesthetic quality of the product — a rough sketch that forces selection and organization outperforms a polished but copied illustration [~M]. Visuals also externalize understanding, making misconceptions visible to both learner and instructor and creating a surface for [Self-Explanation](../principles/annotating.md) and feedback.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Source material that has a mappable structure (processes, systems, hierarchies, relationships) — visuals of arbitrary lists add little
- Low-stakes framing: the goal is reasoning made visible, not artistic quality
- A prompt that specifies *what* the visual must show (e.g., "show what happens to X when Y changes"), not just "draw it"
- Tools appropriate to the medium: paper, whiteboards, or digital tools (see Examples)

#### Constraints
- Drawing can overload working memory when learners must simultaneously search for the pictorial format and reason about content; providing a partially completed structure or reference images reduces this load [~M] — see [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md)
- Learners with weak prior knowledge may copy surface features without understanding; pairing the visual with [Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) prompts mitigates this [-M]
- Benefits shrink when the material is purely verbal or when learners already have fluent mental models — the expertise-reversal pattern applies [~M]
- Time-intensive relative to reading or discussion; reserve for high-value, structurally complex content

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured formats**: concept maps, flowcharts, timelines, and comparison matrices scaffold learners who cannot yet invent a format
- **Free-form sketching**: best when learners have enough knowledge to choose their own representation
- **Progressive completion**: provide a partial diagram learners finish, fading support over time
- **Group visuals**: collaborative diagramming surfaces disagreements about relationships that individual work hides

### Target Learners
- Novices and intermediate learners encountering relational or systemic content for the first time [+M]
- Learners who benefit from dual-channel encoding of verbal material [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Less effective for learners with strong existing mental models, for whom construction is redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of systems, processes, and relationships [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Abstraction: comparing multiple student visuals or contrasting cases supports generalization [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Communication: translating understanding into a form others can read

### Instructions
1. Select content with a mappable structure and define what the visual must communicate.
2. Activate relevant prior knowledge before construction ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Have learners construct the visual individually or in pairs, using paper or a digital tool.
4. Prompt learners to explain their visual aloud or in writing — the explanation, not the drawing, carries much of the learning benefit [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
5. Compare visuals across students or against an expert version to surface alternative structures and misconceptions.
6. Revise: have learners redraw after feedback or new information, making conceptual change visible.

## Examples
- **[BioRender](https://www.biorender.com)** — students construct biological pathway diagrams from a library of scientific icons, combining structured support with generative construction.
- **[Canva for Education](https://www.canva.com/education/)** — infographic design for summarizing research findings; the constraint of fitting content into a layout forces prioritization.
- **Sketchnoting in history classes** — students produce visual notes combining symbols, spatial layout, and text; the spatial arrangement encodes relationships that linear notes miss.
- **Model-based drawing in science** (e.g., the [Ambitious Science Teaching](https://ambitiousscienceteaching.org) practice of "puzzling phenomena") — students draw initial models of a phenomenon, then revise them as evidence accumulates.
