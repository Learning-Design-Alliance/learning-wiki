# Mentor Text Analysis

## Description
Mentor text analysis asks learners to read an exemplary published or peer text not primarily for comprehension but as a model of craft — noticing how the author structures an argument, opens a narrative, integrates evidence, or controls tone. Learners annotate, name, and discuss these moves, then imitate or adapt them in their own composing. The strategy treats the text as a demonstration of expert performance embedded in authentic work rather than as a decontextualized rule list.

## Design Implications

Mentor texts function as worked examples for writing: they reduce the search problem novices face when facing a blank page by supplying a concrete reference model [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on guided noticing — unstructured reading of a model produces far less transfer than analysis that directs attention to specific craft moves and requires learners to articulate them [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and structured discussion serve this role. Analysis must be paired with composing; studying models without immediate application yields shallow, declarative knowledge of technique rather than usable skill [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short, high-quality text that exemplifies the target craft move clearly enough for novices to detect
- A focused analytic lens — one or two named moves per text, not "notice everything"
- An annotation or marking routine ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)) that makes the noticing visible and discussable
- An immediate composing task that invites imitation or adaptation of the analyzed move

#### Constraints
- Analysis without a follow-on composing task produces recognition without production [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [-S]
- Over-imitation: learners may copy surface features (topic, vocabulary) rather than the underlying move, especially when only one mentor text is used; multiple contrasting models reduce this
- Published "professional" models can be too distant from novice capability, discouraging rather than guiding; peer-written mentor texts at a reachable standard often work better for struggling writers
- Less useful for learners who already control the target craft move, for whom the analysis is redundant [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-text immersion**: read several texts in a genre to induce its conventions before writing (genre study)
- **Single-sentence or paragraph mentors**: micro-analysis of one move, e.g., how an author punctuates a suspense beat
- **Side-by-side comparison**: two texts handling the same move differently, prompting learners to articulate the choice space rather than one "correct" way
- **Student-written mentors**: anonymized peer work as the model, which also builds an audience culture
- **Reverse engineering**: learners outline or "map" a mentor text's structure before drafting their own

### Target Learners
- Novice writers who lack a repertoire of craft moves and would otherwise face unguided search at the blank page [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from seeing target structures in authentic context rather than as isolated grammar rules
- Developing writers who have plateaued at "correct but flat" prose and need named techniques to revise toward
- Less beneficial for advanced writers who already analyze texts independently [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Craft knowledge: acquiring named, reusable techniques for structure, style, and genre convention
- Transfer of reading into writing: building the reader-as-writer habit of noticing how texts work
- Genre understanding: internalizing the features and purposes of a discourse community's texts
- Revision skill: giving learners concrete targets to move toward when improving a draft

### Instructions
1. **Select a mentor text** that clearly exemplifies one target move; keep it short enough for close rereading.
2. **Read once for meaning** so comprehension does not compete with craft analysis on first pass.
3. **Reread with a lens**: learners mark where the move occurs and label it ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. **Name the move**: the class articulates what the author did and why it works, converting the model into transferable language.
5. **Try the move**: learners draft an imitation or adaptation in their own piece immediately, while the model is fresh.
6. **Compare and revise**: students check their attempts against the mentor (and each other's), revising toward the effect rather than the exact wording.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Katie Wood Ray's *Wondrous Words* (1999)** — the foundational treatment of "reading like a writer," in which elementary students study picture books as mentors and invent craft terminology from what they notice.
- **[The Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)** — uses exemplar sentences and short models that students analyze and then imitate through sentence-level activities before composing.
- **[Achieve the Core / Student Writing Samples](https://achievethecore.org)** — annotated on-demand student writing used as mentor texts, with commentary naming what the writer did well against a rubric.
- **Genre study in [NWP](https://www.nwp.org) classrooms** — teachers immerse students in multiple examples of a genre (e.g., op-eds), co-construct a features chart, then draft within the genre.
