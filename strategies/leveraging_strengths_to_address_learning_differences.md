# Leveraging Strengths to Address Learning Differences

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy involves identifying each learner's specific strengths and challenges, then deliberately selecting instructional strategies that route learning *through* those strengths while scaffolding areas of difficulty. It treats learning differences (e.g., in working memory, processing speed, self-regulation, or language) as variations to be designed around rather than deficits to be remediated in isolation, and it builds learners' own awareness of their assets so they can self-advocate and self-regulate.

## Design Implications

Strengths-based differentiation works because it pairs compensatory access with motivational support: when learners experience competence through an existing strength, self-efficacy rises, and self-efficacy predicts persistence on difficult tasks [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. The strategy is inseparable from learner self-knowledge — students who can name their own strengths and challenges are better positioned to monitor and adjust their learning [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Crucially, the strategy is about *matching* support to the learner: support that fits one profile can be useless or harmful for another [Support matched to learner expertise is required; mismatched support can backfire.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Systematic identification of strengths and challenges — via [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), observation, learner interviews, and family input — not assumptions from labels
- A repertoire of aligned strategies so that each identified strength has a concrete instructional use (e.g., strong oral language → [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) tasks before writing)
- Explicit teaching of self-awareness: learners need language and routines for describing their own profiles ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Activities for Student Self-Reflection](../strategies/activities_for_student_self-reflection.md))
- Feedback that addresses the task and process rather than labeling the person [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Strengths-based framing without genuine instructional adjustment becomes empty praise; praise directed at the person rather than the process can undermine motivation and does not build competence [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- Mismatched support is worse than no support: adding scaffolds that do not fit the learner's profile increases load or dependency [Support matched to learner expertise is required; mismatched support can backfire.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Identification takes sustained time and relationship-building; one-off inventories produce shallow or stereotyped profiles
- Over-reliance on a single strength can narrow the learner's trajectory — strengths should open access to challenging content, not become a reason to lower expectations

#### Implementation Variability
- Educator-driven: teacher diagnoses profiles and pre-plans differentiated tasks (Tomlinson's differentiation model)
- Learner-driven: students maintain their own "strengths profiles" and select from strategy menus, building [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- System-level: [Universal Design for Learning](https://udlguidelines.cast.org) embeds the same logic into curriculum design so individual retrofitting is minimized

### Target Learners
- Students with identified or suspected learning differences (e.g., dyslexia, ADHD, autism, anxiety) who experience chronic competence-frustration in one-size-fits-all instruction [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners with weak self-awareness or self-regulation, who benefit from explicit profiling and monitoring routines [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- All learners in heterogeneous classrooms, since every student has a usable profile; the strategy is differentiation, not special education only

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-awareness: learners can accurately describe their strengths, challenges, and needed supports
- Self-regulation: learners select and apply strategies matched to their own profiles [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Access to grade-level content: strengths serve as entry points to the same rigorous goals peers pursue
- Motivation and persistence: competence experiences sustain engagement on difficult work [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Identify profiles.** Gather evidence from [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), observation, and learner/family voice; record strengths and challenges in parallel, never challenges alone.
2. **Share the profile with the learner.** Use [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) prompts ("What's easy for you? What's hard? What helps?") so the learner co-owns the profile.
3. **Align strategies to strengths.** For each challenge, select a route through a strength — e.g., a strong verbal memory paired with weak note-taking might use oral rehearsal and [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) before writing.
4. **Scaffold the challenge directly.** Strengths provide access, not a substitute for targeted support; add [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) for the difficult skill itself.
5. **Build self-regulation routines.** Have learners plan with their strengths, monitor progress, and reflect — moving responsibility from teacher to student over time.
6. **Review and revise.** Reassess profiles periodically; strengths and challenges shift with content and development.

## Examples
- A student with dyslexia but strong spatial reasoning uses concept maps and diagram-based [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) to access a history unit, while receiving structured phonics-based [Practice](../elements/practice.md) for decoding — strengths provide content access, targeted support addresses the difference.
- A student who accurately identifies their own emotions (a self-awareness strength) uses that skill in structured journal reflections to recognize when frustration is building, then applies a pre-taught regulation strategy — leveraging a strength to support self-regulation.
- [CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org) — systematizes the approach: rather than diagnosing each learner individually, designers remove barriers and offer multiple means of engagement, representation, and action so varied strengths all have a route in.
- [Understood.org](https://www.understood.org) — practical strengths-based profiles and strategy libraries for specific learning and attention differences, including IEP planning anchored to documented student assets.
