# Co-Teaching and Collaboration

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Co-teaching pairs a content teacher (e.g., mathematics) with a language specialist (e.g., ELL teacher) who jointly identify target content, key vocabulary, academic language demands, and language-focused tasks such as writing a proof, reading a word problem, explaining problem-solving aloud, or writing numbers as words. The specialist guides the content teacher through analyzing language demands; this analysis becomes integrated into the content teacher's own planning over time. After the lesson, the pair debriefs to identify areas needing clarification and shares insights with colleagues.

## Design Implications

Co-teaching works when it is a genuine instructional partnership rather than parallel teaching: shared planning time and a common framework for analyzing language demands are the strongest predictors of benefit [~M]. Structured models such as the SIOP protocol give the partnership a shared vocabulary for planning, which reduces the coordination cost that otherwise erodes co-teaching quality [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Scheduled, protected collaborative planning time — not just shared classroom space
- A shared framework for identifying language demands (vocabulary, discourse functions, task language) in content lessons
- Clear role division during instruction (e.g., one leads, one models academic language; both circulate)
- Administrative support for the partnership, including compatible schedules and joint accountability

#### Constraints
- Without dedicated planning time, co-teaching degrades into "one teach, one assist," which research links to the least student benefit and role marginalization of the specialist [-M]
- Specialists assigned to many classrooms spread thin cannot sustain the planning depth required [-M]
- If the specialist is treated as an aide rather than a co-instructor, learners receive no additional instructional expertise [-M]
- Benefits are weaker when the partnership lacks a shared model for language-objective writing; vague language objectives produce unfocused lessons [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Station teaching and parallel teaching distribute both teachers' expertise across groups; team teaching integrates them in whole-class delivery
- Consultative collaboration: the specialist coaches the content teacher without co-delivering — lighter to schedule but less intensive
- Co-planning without co-teaching: the specialist helps design language objectives and tasks, which the content teacher delivers alone

### Target Learners
- English language learners in content classrooms, who need simultaneous access to content and academic language [~M]
- Learners with disabilities in inclusive settings, where co-teaching originated [~M]
- Struggling readers in content areas who benefit from explicit vocabulary and discourse support

### Target Learning Goals
- Content objectives (e.g., mathematical concepts and procedures) delivered without language barriers
- Academic language development: vocabulary, discourse structures, and language functions of the discipline (explaining, justifying, defining)
- Transferable learning-to-learn language: talking about one's own problem-solving

### Instructions
1. Jointly identify target content standards and concepts for the lesson or unit.
2. Analyze the language demands: key vocabulary, academic language structures, and the language functions learners need (e.g., writing a proof, explaining problem-solving). Use [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) to prioritize which terms and structures need explicit support.
3. Write dual objectives — content and language — for the lesson.
4. Design language-focused tasks and decide each teacher's role during delivery, drawing on [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) so the specialist builds the content teacher's capacity over time.
5. Co-deliver the lesson, with the specialist modeling academic language use where needed ([Modeling](../elements/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) within [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)).
6. Debrief after the lesson: identify areas needing clarification, adjust subsequent planning, and share insights with colleagues through [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md).

## Examples
- A middle-school math teacher and an ELL specialist co-plan a unit on ratios: they identify "ratio," "proportion," and "per" as key terms, target the language function of comparing quantities, and design a task where learners write comparison sentences before solving problems. The specialist leads a brief vocabulary routine; both teachers circulate during group work.
- The [SIOP Model](https://www.cal.org/siop/) (Echevarría, Vogt, & Short) operationalizes this collaboration: co-teachers use its 30 features to plan lessons with integrated content and language objectives across K–12 settings.
