# Uncluttered Learning Environment

## Description
An uncluttered learning environment removes non-essential visual, auditory, and digital stimuli so that learner attention is directed at the instructional core — the task, the text, the demonstration, or the discussion. It applies the coherence principle at the level of the physical and digital space: everything present should serve the learning goal, and everything else should be removed or minimized.

## Design Implications

Attention is a limited resource; irrelevant stimuli compete with instructional content for working memory and can measurably reduce learning [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md). Children taught in classrooms with heavily decorated walls spent more time off-task and scored lower on a focused learning task than children in sparse classrooms [~S] — the effect is strongest for young learners and those with developing attention regulation. Decorative but irrelevant graphics and sounds do not improve learning and often depress it [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A deliberate audit of the learning space (physical, digital, or document) identifying what serves the learning goal and what does not
- A clear visual hierarchy: one focal point at a time, with [Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) guiding where attention should go
- Consistent placement of essential materials so learners do not spend effort searching for what they need
- A plan for digital distractions — notifications, tabs, and device policies — since off-task laptop use harms both the user and nearby peers [~S]

#### Constraints
- Over-stripping can remove useful supports: advance organizers, anchor charts, and reference displays that learners actively consult are instructional, not clutter — the distinction is whether material is *used* during learning
- Sterile or featureless environments can reduce engagement and perceived warmth, particularly in early-childhood and community settings [~W]
- Learners with strong attention regulation are less affected by ambient clutter, so the cost of aggressive minimization may outweigh the benefit for them [~M]
- Clutter is not the only source of distraction; poorly structured content or unclear tasks produce distraction that tidying the room cannot fix

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical classrooms:** keep walls mostly neutral; display only current, actively used content; rotate displays rather than accumulating them
- **Digital interfaces:** remove decorative imagery, disable non-essential notifications, use a single-column layout, and apply [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) to reduce on-screen density
- **Learning materials:** apply the coherence principle to slides and handouts — cut background music, decorative stock photos, and tangential sidebars
- **Home and remote learning:** designate a dedicated, cleared workspace; this is especially important where shared or multi-use spaces are the default

### Target Learners
- Young children, whose attention regulation is still developing and who are most vulnerable to visual distraction [~S]
- Learners with ADHD or weaker executive function, who are disproportionately affected by environmental distraction [~M]
- Novices generally, who lack the prior knowledge to distinguish relevant from irrelevant stimuli [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Focused skill practice and [Practice](../elements/practice.md) requiring sustained attention
- Reading comprehension and text-based learning where attention must stay on the text
- Assessment conditions that minimize extraneous variance in performance

### Instructions
1. Audit the environment: list every visual, auditory, and digital stimulus present during learning and classify each as instructional, motivational, or extraneous.
2. Remove or suppress extraneous stimuli — decorative graphics, background noise, unused displays, notification streams — consistent with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).
3. Establish one focal point per learning episode and structure materials with [Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) so attention is cued, not scattered.
4. Retain only functional displays (anchor charts, reference tools, current-unit content) that learners actively use; rotate them as units change.
5. Set explicit norms for digital devices and teach learners to manage their own digital environment as part of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).
6. Reassess periodically: clutter accumulates, and what was instructional last month may be extraneous this month.

## Related Strategies
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — reduces within-material density the way decluttering reduces environmental density
- [Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) — the positive counterpart: cueing attention to what matters rather than only removing what does not
- [Coherence in Multimedia](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — the same principle applied to instructional media design

## Examples
- **Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014) classroom study** — Kindergarten classrooms were matched except for wall decoration; children in the decorated classroom spent more time off-task and learned less on the targeted lesson, demonstrating a causal effect of visual clutter.
- **Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Exercises use a deliberately sparse interface: one problem, minimal chrome, no decorative imagery, keeping attention on the task.
- **Montessori classrooms** — Prepared environments with ordered, limited materials on open shelves; each material has a single purpose and a defined place, an institutionalized version of the uncluttered environment.
