# Discussion-Oriented Flipped Classroom

## Description
In a discussion-oriented flipped classroom, first exposure to new content is moved out of class — typically as curated video lectures or other pre-class materials — while in-class time is restructured around facilitated discussion, debate, and collaborative analysis of that content. The variant differs from a general [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) in that the class-time activity is primarily dialogic rather than problem-solving: students arrive with baseline knowledge and use discussion to interrogate, apply, and extend it.

## Design Implications

Pre-class exposure frees class time for the interactive, generative processing that drives deeper learning; active class-time formats outperform lecture on exam performance [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The discussion component works because students arrive with shared background, allowing the instructor to start at analysis and evaluation rather than transmission — but only if pre-class work is actually completed and held accountable [Activation of prior knowledge before instruction improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Discussion quality depends on structured formats and facilitation; unstructured conversation drifts toward the most vocal students and shallow opinion exchange [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Curated, high-quality pre-class media (video lectures, readings) with embedded accountability — pre-class quizzes, [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), or guided notes
- Facilitation skill: the instructor must pose questions that require the pre-class content, not re-teach it
- Structured discussion formats ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) protocols, [Debate](../patterns/debate.md), think-pair-share) rather than open plenary alone
- A participation and preparation grading scheme that makes pre-class work consequential

#### Constraints
- Fails when students arrive unprepared: discussion without shared baseline knowledge collapses into instructor re-lecture [-M] — preparation compliance is the single largest failure point reported in flipped-classroom studies
- Less effective for novices who lack the prior knowledge to discuss meaningfully; the [expertise reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) means minimally guided discussion can overload beginners [~M]
- Discussion can be dominated by confident students; without protocols, equity of participation suffers [-W]
- Requires reliable student access to technology and bandwidth for pre-class video [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-class accountability spectrum**: from ungraded trust to graded pre-quizzes; graded checks reliably raise preparation rates [~M]
- **Discussion structure spectrum**: Socratic seminar, structured academic controversy, [Debate](../patterns/debate.md), jigsaw, small-group [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)
- **Media mix**: instructor-produced videos, external resources (e.g., Khan Academy, Crash Course), or [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) in place of video
- **Partial flips**: flipping only some sessions, or using in-class discussion for selected difficult topics while retaining lecture elsewhere

### Target Learners
- Upper-secondary and higher-education students with sufficient self-regulation to complete pre-class work independently [~M]
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge, who can contribute to and benefit from peer dialogue; complete novices need more direct pre-class structure
- Students in discussion-rich disciplines (humanities, social sciences, professional ethics) where argumentation is a target outcome

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical thinking and argumentation: evaluating claims, defending positions [~M]
- Conceptual understanding through [Cognitive Disequilibrium](../principles/cognitive-disequilibrium.md) — discussion surfaces contradictions that motivate conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]
- Communication skills: articulating and defending viewpoints in real time
- Application and transfer of concepts to cases and current issues

### Instructions
1. Curate or record short pre-class videos (ideally under 10 minutes each, per multimedia principles) and pair with guided questions or an [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) frame.
2. Assign the pre-class work with a low-stakes accountability check (entry quiz, discussion-board post) due before class.
3. Open class with a brief check of understanding — surface misconceptions from the pre-class data rather than re-lecturing.
4. Run the core discussion activity: structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), small-group analysis, [Debate](../patterns/debate.md), or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) that require applying the pre-class content.
5. Close with [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) or a written synthesis to consolidate the discussion outcomes.

## Examples
- **Political science courses** assign video lectures introducing theories (e.g., via YouTube or instructor-recorded segments), then run in-class structured debates applying them to current policy cases.
- **Jensen, Kummer & Godoy's flipped biology course** (University of Washington) replaced lecture with pre-class videos and in-class group discussion and problem-solving, improving exam performance [doi:10.1187/cbe.14-08-0129](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.14-08-0129).
- **Harvard's case-method courses** function as a discussion-oriented flip: students prepare a case before class and class time is entirely discussion-driven (see [Case-Based Learning — Harvard Method](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)).
