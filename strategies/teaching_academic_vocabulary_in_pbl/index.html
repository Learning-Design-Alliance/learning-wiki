Teaching Academic Vocabulary in PBL¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 6 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

In project-based learning (PBL), teaching academic vocabulary means identifying the specialized and high-utility academic words students will need to read, discuss, and produce during a project, then planning deliberate instruction for those words rather than leaving acquisition to incidental exposure. Instruction typically combines explicit teaching of word meanings and morphology, multiple encounters across contexts, and structured opportunities to use the words in authentic project tasks. This is especially consequential for English Language Learners (ELLs), who must build academic language while simultaneously learning content.

Design Implications¶

Vocabulary knowledge is one of the strongest predictors of academic reading comprehension, and word meanings are learned incrementally through repeated, varied encounters rather than single definitions [~S]. Explicit, rich vocabulary instruction produces reliable gains in word learning and comprehension for adolescent learners, including ELLs [+S] (Carlo et al., 2004). In PBL, the project drive provides authentic purposes for using target vocabulary, which supports retention and transfer compared with isolated word lists [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Front-loaded vocabulary selection: distinguish tier two high-utility academic words (analyze, justify, criterion) from tier three domain-specific terms, prioritizing words with the widest payoff (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan, 2013)
  • Explicit introduction of word meanings with student-friendly definitions, Analogies, and examples in the project context
  • Multiple, spaced encounters with each word across reading, discussion, and writing tasks Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]
  • Structured opportunities for students to use the words productively — in Class Discussion, project artifacts, and presentations — with Coaching and corrective feedback
  • Word walls, glossaries, or Annotating routines that keep target vocabulary visible throughout the project

Constraints¶

  • Pre-teaching every unfamiliar word is impossible and crowds out instructional time; over-selection dilutes depth of word knowledge [-W] — depth of processing for a small set beats shallow coverage of many
  • Definitions taught in isolation, without contextual encounters or productive use, produce fragile knowledge that does not transfer to reading or writing [-M]
  • If vocabulary instruction is front-loaded entirely before the project begins, words are encountered before students have a purpose for them, weakening encoding [~W]
  • PBL's open-ended structure can let vocabulary instruction disappear into activity management; without planned checkpoints, ELLs default to everyday registers and academic terms go unused [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Just-in-time mini-lessons: introduce vocabulary at the moment a text or task requires it, tying meaning to immediate use
  • Morphological analysis: teach roots, prefixes, and suffixes so students can decompose unfamiliar academic words independently
  • Frayer models and semantic mapping: students define, exemplify, and contrast target words; contrasting cases supports abstraction of word meaning Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  • Peer talk structures: structured discussion protocols require use of target terms before formal writing
  • Embedded glossaries and digital supports: hyperlinked definitions in project documents for self-service support

Target Learners¶

  • ELLs and students with limited academic language backgrounds, who benefit most from explicit instruction in high-utility academic words [+S]
  • All students in the project, since academic vocabulary is a gatekeeper for disciplinary texts and assessment tasks
  • Advanced students need fewer pre-taught definitions but still benefit from productive-use requirements and morphological analysis [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of disciplinary texts and project resources
  • Disciplinary communication: using precise academic terms in discussion, writing, and presentations
  • Independent word-learning strategies (context clues, morphology, dictionary use) that outlast the project

Instructions¶

  1. Select — during project planning, identify 8–15 high-utility words from project texts, rubrics, and final-product expectations; prioritize tier two words and essential tier three terms
  2. Introduce explicitly — use Direct Instruction with student-friendly definitions, Analogies, and examples drawn from the project context
  3. Distribute encounters — embed target words in project readings, discussion prompts, and task directions so students meet each word repeatedly across days Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]
  4. Require productive use — build discussion protocols, drafts, and presentation requirements that obligate use of target vocabulary, with Coaching and Provide Feedback on accurate usage
  5. Assess formatively — quick checks (rating familiarity, using the word in a sentence) to decide which words need re-teaching before high-stakes project milestones

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — connecting new terms to known concepts anchors word meanings in existing schemas
  • Accessible Syntax — vocabulary and sentence structure together determine text accessibility; teaching words alone is insufficient when academic syntax blocks comprehension
  • Front-load Tier 1 vocabulary before the science lesson and teach embedded scientific vocabulary in the lesson's context

Examples¶

  • EL Education (Expeditionary Learning) curriculum (https://eleducation.org) — PBL modules specify tier two and tier three vocabulary per unit, with word walls, morphological mini-lessons, and speaking protocols that require academic terms during crew discussions and final presentations
  • Science PBL unit on water quality — students pre-learn terms like turbidity, ppm, and hypothesis through Frayer models, then encounter and use them in field data collection, lab reports, and a public presentation to a local water board
  • Carlo et al. (2004) intervention — a research-based instructional program teaching academic vocabulary and morphological analysis to middle-school ELLs through multiple contextual encounters, producing significant gains in word knowledge and comprehension
