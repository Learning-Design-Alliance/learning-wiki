    Cognitively Demanding Tasks¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Cognitively demanding tasks require a high level of cognitive engagement to complete: learners must determine what the problem is asking, decide how to use the information provided, and make connections among ideas in ways not prescribed by a memorized procedure. High-demand tasks often admit multiple solution paths or multiple correct answers, which surfaces diverse student thinking and creates rich material for discussion. The construct originates in research on academic work (Doyle, 1983) and was developed most fully in mathematics education through the "cognitive demand" framework of Stein and colleagues, which distinguishes "doing mathematics" and "procedures with connections" from lower-demand "procedures without connections" and memorization.

    Design Implications¶

    Selecting or designing a high-demand task is only the first step; the demand must be maintained through enactment. Studies of classroom implementation show that the cognitive demand of a task frequently declines during instruction — through proceduralization, teacher takeover of the difficult reasoning, or time pressure — and that learning gains track the demand actually maintained, not the demand as written Tasks that maintain high cognitive demand during enactment produce greater learning than tasks whose demand declines. [+M]. Because high-demand tasks present learners with a complete, complex problem rather than a sequence of sub-steps, they function as whole-task practice that builds the schemas needed for transfer Whole-task practice supports transfer better than isolated part practice for complex skills. [+M]. When multiple solution paths are compared, learners abstract the underlying structure more effectively Comparing multiple contrasting cases supports abstraction of deep structure. [+M], and requiring students to explain their reasoning consolidates conceptual understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A task that genuinely requires reasoning — multiple entry points, non-obvious solution paths, or multiple defensible answers
    • Classroom norms that make it safe to share unfinished or divergent thinking (Class Discussion, Collaboration)
    • Teacher questioning that presses for explanation and justification rather than supplying the method
    • Adequate time; high-demand tasks cannot be rushed to a single "right answer"

    Constraints¶

    • Demand declines when teachers demonstrate a procedure before students attempt the task, converting "doing mathematics" into "procedures without connections" [-M]
    • Struggling learners without adequate support may experience unproductive frustration; the expertise reversal effect means minimal guidance suits novices poorly Guidance that benefits novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners. [~M]
    • Requires substantial teacher preparation — anticipating student strategies and planning facilitating questions — that is often underestimated
    • Assessment systems that reward speed and single answers undermine the strategy by penalizing the exploratory work these tasks require

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Launch–explore–summarize structure: teacher launches the task without giving away the method, students explore (individually or in groups), then the class summarizes by comparing strategies
    • Low-floor, high-ceiling adaptations: open entry points and extensions let one task serve heterogeneous readiness levels
    • Part–whole sequencing: brief part-task practice [+M] on prerequisite procedures before the whole task reduces extraneous load for novices
    • Written vs. oral justification: exit tickets, journals, or argumentation routines vary how reasoning is externalized

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners who have sufficient prerequisite procedures to deploy flexibly but have not yet connected them conceptually Whole-task practice supports transfer better than isolated part practice for complex skills. [+M]
    • Learners with some prior knowledge; complete novices may need worked examples or part-task preparation first Guidance that benefits novices can hinder more knowledgeable learners. [~M]
    • The strategy is documented most extensively in K–12 mathematics, but the demand framework applies to any domain with procedural vs. conceptual knowledge

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Conceptual understanding: connecting procedures to underlying principles Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]
    • Problem solving and adaptive reasoning: selecting and justifying strategies
    • Mathematical discourse: communicating and critiquing reasoning
    • Transfer: applying ideas to novel, non-routine situations Whole-task practice supports transfer better than isolated part practice for complex skills. [+M]

    Instructions¶

    1. Select or adapt a high-demand task. Check that it cannot be solved by recalling a single taught procedure; look for multiple paths or answers. (Whole-Task Performance)
    2. Anticipate student strategies. Predict solution approaches, misconceptions, and the questions that will keep the demand high rather than resolve it for students.
    3. Launch without demonstrating. State the problem and expectations; do not model a solution method, which lowers the demand before students begin.
    4. Support exploration. Circulate, press for explanations ("How do you know?", "Will that always work?"), and use Cognitive Conflict when results contradict expectations.
    5. Summarize by comparing. Orchestrate discussion that contrasts multiple solution paths so students abstract the shared structure Comparing multiple contrasting cases supports abstraction of deep structure. [+M].
    6. Require justification. Close with written or oral explanation of reasoning to consolidate understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S].

    Related Strategies¶

    • Productive Struggle — the disposition this strategy aims to cultivate; demand is only productive when struggle is supported, not abandoned
    • Number Talks — a routine built on comparing multiple mental strategies for a single problem
    • Low-Floor High-Ceiling Tasks — a task-design adaptation that personalizes demand within one problem

    Related Elements¶

    • Whole-Task Performance — the element this strategy enacts: practicing the complete, complex task rather than isolated sub-skills
    • Application — high-demand tasks require applying concepts to novel situations rather than executing rehearsed steps
    • Cognitive Conflict — contradictory results within a task create the disequilibrium that drives conceptual change
    • Case Studies — the same demand logic applied to narrative problems in other disciplines

    Tools¶

    • Illustrative Mathematics — openly licensed K–12 tasks vetted for alignment with the Standards for Mathematical Practice
    • NRICH — University of Cambridge rich problems designed with low floors and high ceilings
    • Three-Act Tasks (Dan Meyer) — multimedia problem sequences that withhold information to force question-posing

    Examples¶

    • "Buying Lunch" task — students determine combinations of lunch items purchasable within a budget; multiple valid combinations generate discussion of combinatorial reasoning and constraints.
    • Illustrative Mathematics tasks — e.g., "The Spider and the Fly" asks students to find the shortest path on a cube's surface; no taught procedure applies, forcing spatial reasoning and justification.
    • NRICH "Cuboid" problems — open investigations with many correct answers, used in UK classrooms for sustained small-group exploration.
