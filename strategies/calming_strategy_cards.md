# Calming Strategy Cards

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Calming strategy cards are small, portable prompts (illustrated or written) that remind learners of specific emotional regulation strategies to use when anger, anxiety, or overwhelm begins to escalate. Cards are introduced and rehearsed during calm periods, personalized by learner choice, and then used in the moment as external cues that substitute for executive-control processes that degrade under emotional arousal. With repeated, supported use, the strategies can become internalized and accessible without the cards.

## Design Implications

The strategy works by offloading regulation onto an external cue at precisely the moment when working memory and inhibitory control are compromised by arousal [Executive functions are acutely vulnerable to stress and emotional arousal.](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750) [~M]. Because the cards are only prompts, their effectiveness depends on prior rehearsal: strategies practiced during calm, low-arousal states are far more likely to be retrieved and executed during escalation than strategies merely discussed [Social-emotional learning programs that include practice and feedback improve emotional regulation outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564) [+S]. Learner choice among strategies matters — autonomy over which coping strategy to adopt supports motivation to actually use it [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of pre-made or co-created cards, each depicting one concrete, actionable strategy (not vague labels like "calm down")
- Discussion during calm periods about what anger and escalation feel like, and which options fit which situations
- Rehearsal of chosen strategies under mild, then more realistic, arousal conditions ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Adult [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and reinforcement during early real-world use, fading as independence grows

#### Constraints
- In the acute phase of escalation, learners often cannot read, interpret, or initiate card-based strategies [-M] — cards must be introduced early in the escalation curve or paired with adult prompting
- Strategies chosen by adults rather than by the learner are used less reliably [-M]; imposed coping strategies show weaker uptake than self-selected ones [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]
- Cards without rehearsal become wallpaper: visible but never used [-S]
- Effectiveness varies with the match between strategy, learner, and situation (a quiet-corner card is useless in a setting with no quiet space) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Format: illustrated decks for pre-readers, text cards, keyring-sized portable sets, or digital prompts
- Scope: a class-wide "calm corner" library versus a personalized wallet of 3–5 learner-selected cards
- Integration: paired with a [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) routine so learners rate their state before choosing a card
- Blank cards let learners add idiosyncratic strategies, increasing ownership and fit

### Target Learners
- Children in early and middle childhood, when explicit emotion-regulation instruction has the strongest preventive effects [Universal SEL programs produce measurable gains in emotional regulation and behavior.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564) [+S]
- Learners with emotional and behavioral disorders, ADHD, or anxiety who need external structure for self-regulation [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less effective as a standalone support for learners whose escalation is driven by unaddressed environmental triggers (e.g., sensory overload, academic frustration) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: inhibiting escalation and initiating a coping response
- Emotional literacy: naming feelings and matching strategies to states
- Metacognitive monitoring: recognizing early signs of dysregulation [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Self-efficacy: building the belief "I can manage this," which supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. During a calm period, discuss what anger and anxiety feel like in the body, and brainstorm possible cooling-down options ([Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior coping experience)
2. Present the card deck; the learner selects and personalizes 3–5 strategies that feel doable for them
3. Rehearse each chosen strategy physically and repeatedly while calm ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
4. Establish when and where cards will be available, and a low-stakes signal for using them
5. During real escalation, prompt early ("Is it card time?") rather than waiting for full dysregulation ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
6. Afterward, debrief: which card was used, did it help, what would work better next time ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
7. Fade adult prompting as the learner initiates card use independently

## Related Strategies
- [Self-Monitoring](../strategies/self-monitoring.md) — cards presuppose noticing one's own escalation; explicit self-monitoring routines supply that trigger
- [Mindfulness Breaks](../strategies/mindfulness-breaks.md) — a common card content; brief mindfulness practice reduces anxiety in school settings
- [Fidget Tools](../strategies/fidget-tools.md) — a tangible strategy frequently depicted on cards; effective as a regulation aid, counterproductive when treated as a toy

## Examples
- **Zones of Regulation (Kuypers, 2011)** — a widely used curriculum in which learners sort feelings into color-coded zones and select tools (cards) matched to each zone; cards are rehearsed in calm-state lessons before real use. ([https://www.zonesofregulation.com](https://www.zonesofregulation.com))
- **Calm Classroom / MindUP** — whole-class mindfulness routines that supply the rehearsed strategies later referenced on individual cards ([https://mindup.org](https://mindup.org))
- A learner keeps a keyring of three cards (deep breaths, get water, ask for a break) and uses the "ask for a break" card during tests when anxiety rises.
