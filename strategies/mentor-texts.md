# Mentor Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 12 studies (6 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 12 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
A mentor text is a short, high-quality exemplar — a published essay, poem, lab report, code snippet, or student-written piece — that learners study closely to understand how a particular craft move works before attempting it in their own work. The strategy treats the text as a coach rather than a content source: learners read like writers, identifying techniques, structures, and stylistic choices they can borrow. Instruction typically follows a cycle of reading, noticing, imitation, and independent application.

## Design Implications

Mentor texts are demonstrations of craft rather than content, and their effectiveness depends on making the expert's choices visible through guided noticing and annotation [Example-based learning benefits from integrating cognitive and social-cognitive perspectives.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Imitation of a model followed by independent application outperforms either reading or instruction alone for writing skill [Explicit strategy instruction combined with modeling improves writing quality.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+S]. Studying multiple contrasting mentor texts supports abstraction of the underlying technique rather than surface imitation [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Short, high-quality texts that clearly exhibit the target craft move (structure, voice, evidence use, genre conventions)
- Guided noticing: prompts or [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling that directs attention to *how* the text works, not just what it says
- An immediate writing or production task where learners imitate or adapt the observed move
- Texts at or slightly above learners' comprehension level, so attention goes to craft rather than decoding
- Access to high-quality writing samples (professional or exemplary student work), selected to match the target genre and technique
- Structured analysis activities — annotation, discussion, naming of "writer's moves" — rather than passive reading ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Immediate imitation opportunities: students apply an identified move in their own writing shortly after studying it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Time for reading, analysis, and discussion, and a classroom culture where students share and compare drafts

#### Constraints
- Imitation without guided noticing produces surface copying rather than transfer of technique [-M]
- A single mentor text can anchor learners to one structure or voice; contrasting multiple texts reduces this [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- Texts far above learners' reading level consume working memory on comprehension, leaving little for craft analysis [-M]
- For learners already skilled in a genre, additional exemplar study adds little and may feel redundant [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Students may copy surface features of a mentor text without understanding the underlying technique, producing pastiche rather than transfer [-M]
- Exemplary professional models can intimidate struggling writers or feel unattainable; pairing them with flawed or developing examples lowers the comparison bar [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Techniques studied in isolation often fail to transfer to independent drafting without explicit prompts to apply them [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Published literature** — professional essays, poems, or articles as models of genre and voice
- **Student-written mentors** — peer work normalizes imperfection and makes improvement trajectories visible
- **Contrast sets** — two or three texts handling the same move differently, inviting comparison
- **Annotated mentors** — teacher- or author-annotated versions that mark craft moves explicitly
- **Cross-disciplinary use** — lab reports, proofs, code, design critiques, and clinical write-ups as mentors in non-ELA domains
- **Whole-class study**: one shared text analyzed together, with the teacher making the deconstruction visible ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
- **Small-group or independent study**: students select texts matched to their current writing goal, supporting personalization and autonomy
- **Student-authored mentor texts**: peers' work as models, which signals attainability and builds community
- **Multimodal texts**: song lyrics, comics, or infographics for genre and craft analysis beyond prose

### Target Learners
- Novice writers and producers who lack a repertoire of structural and stylistic options [Explicit modeling of writing strategies improves quality.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+S]
- Learners who can comprehend the mentor text but cannot yet generate the moves independently
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already possess genre flexibility [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- English language learners, who benefit from concrete models of disciplinary and genre conventions [+M]
- Advanced writers benefit less from single models and more from contrasting texts that support stylistic experimentation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Craft knowledge: understanding how texts achieve effects (structure, voice, cohesion, evidence use)
- Genre acquisition: internalizing the conventions and purposes of a form
- Reading-to-write transfer: building the habit of reading analytically as a writer
- Identity and motivation: seeing that real authors and peers struggle, revise, and make deliberate choices
- Writing craft: sentence variety, organization, voice, use of evidence and detail
- Genre knowledge: understanding the conventions and purposes of forms (narrative, argument, report)
- Critical reading: analyzing how authorial choices affect a reader
- Writing confidence and risk-taking: seeing that published techniques are learnable, not innate

### Instructions
1. Select a short mentor text that prominently exhibits the target craft move; keep it brief enough for close study.
2. Read it aloud or have learners read it once for meaning before any craft analysis.
3. Model noticing with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): reread passages and name what the author is doing and why ("Notice how this sentence is short — it lands the point").
4. Have learners annotate the text themselves, marking craft moves ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)), and discuss findings as a class.
5. Optionally show a second, contrasting mentor handling the same move differently to support abstraction [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].
6. Assign an imitation task: learners try the move in their own piece, then share and compare against the mentor.
7. Return to the same mentor across the unit as learners revise ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with revisiting deepens internalization).

## Examples
- **Units of Study in Writing (Lucy Calkins, Teachers College Reading & Writing Project)** — workshop curriculum built on reading published authors as mentors, then imitating their moves in student writing (https://www.unitsofstudy.com)
- **The New York Times Learning Network student mentor texts** — publishes winning student essays with commentary on what makes them work, used as models for subsequent contests (https://www.nytimes.com/spotlight/learning-network-mentor-texts)
- **Science writing instruction** — using published journal abstracts as mentors for students drafting their own abstracts, comparing structure across two or three exemplars before writing
- Using a [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com) feature article as a mentor text for narrative writing, focusing on how the author "shows" rather than "tells."
- The [WritingFix](http://writingfix.com) project and Dorfman & Cappelli's *Mentor Texts* (Stenhouse, 2007) provide published lesson sequences built around picture books as craft models.
- Examining successful college application essays to study voice and risk-taking in personal narrative.
- Using student writing from prior cohorts as mentor texts, signaling that exemplary craft is attainable by peers.

## Description
Mentor texts are examples of high-quality writing — professional or student-authored — that learners study and imitate to improve their own writing. The strategy involves deconstructing a text to identify effective "writer's moves" (sentence structure, organization, voice, use of detail), then emulating those moves in students' own drafts. Mentor texts can be multimodal: essays, poems, newspaper articles, song lyrics, comic strips, or manuals.

## Design Implications

Mentor texts are an example-based learning strategy for writing: they substitute a studied model for unguided invention, reducing the search burden on novice writers [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. Comparing several mentor texts that address the same goal supports abstraction of the underlying technique rather than surface imitation [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].
