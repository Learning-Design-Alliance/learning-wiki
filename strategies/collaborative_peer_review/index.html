Collaborative Peer Review¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners produce their own thinking and writing first, then exchange drafts with peers to provide and receive structured feedback for revision. Unlike cooperative writing — where group members each complete a segment and assemble the parts — peer review keeps authorship individual while making the evaluation collaborative. The reviewer role is as important as the receiving role: critiquing another's work builds the criteria knowledge learners then apply to their own drafts.

Design Implications¶

Peer review works because giving feedback forces learners to articulate quality criteria and compare a draft against them, which deepens understanding of what good writing looks like Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [+S]. Reviews must be scaffolded — rubrics, checklists, and worked examples of good comments — because unstructured "looks good to me" feedback produces little learning Scaffolding peer feedback with criteria and training improves feedback quality and revision. [+M]. Reviewers should comment on ideas, organization, and audience fit, not just surface errors; teachers should still review drafts, since peer feedback is a supplement to, not a substitute for, expert feedback [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuine draft to review — peer review requires something substantive to respond to, so it must follow individual drafting (Individual Reflection or independent writing time)
  • Review criteria: a rubric or checklist that names what reviewers should look for and how to phrase comments
  • Modeling of feedback quality: show annotated examples of helpful vs. unhelpful comments before the first round (Think-Aloud or Non-Examples of feedback)
  • Time and structure for revision based on feedback, so the loop actually closes
  • A channel for exchange — in-class pairs/triads, learning management system workshops, or dedicated platforms (see Examples)

Constraints¶

  • Untrained reviewers default to vague praise or surface-level copyediting; without criteria and training, peer feedback has little effect on revision quality [-M]
  • Anonymity and psychological safety matter: learners may withhold honest critique of friends or fear retaliation, so norms for constructive tone must be established and monitored [-W]
  • Emergent writers and English language learners may lack the vocabulary to critique; sentence frames and word banks partially mitigate this but add preparation load [~M]
  • Reviewer comments can be wrong or contradictory; learners need guidance in weighing feedback rather than applying it mechanically [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Calibrated peer review: learners first rate sample essays and compare their ratings to expert ratings, training their judgment before reviewing peers
  • Anonymous vs. named review: anonymity increases candor; named review builds community and allows follow-up dialogue
  • Asynchronous online workshops (e.g., LMS discussion, Peergrade, Eli Review) allow more reviews per student and time for thoughtful comments
  • Reciprocal structured exchange: each student gives and receives a fixed number of criterion-referenced comments, balancing workload Cho & Schunn's reciprocal peer review design [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • All writers with a complete draft; particularly valuable for English language learners, who benefit from seeing multiple peer approaches and from low-stakes interaction before teacher evaluation [+W]
  • Intermediate learners gain most: novices may not yet produce reviewable drafts or evaluate comments, while experts need peer feedback less Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional support. [~M]
  • Learners who struggle with revision benefit from concrete, criterion-referenced comments rather than grades

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: revision of ideas, organization, and clarity [+M]
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing criteria for quality work — the reviewer role drives this
  • Critical thinking: diagnosing problems in someone else's argument transfers to self-evaluation
  • Collaboration and communication: giving specific, constructive, respectful feedback

Instructions¶

  1. Draft independently. Students complete a full draft on their own before any collaboration — peer review presupposes individual authorship.
  2. Establish criteria. Introduce or co-construct the rubric/checklist; model its use on a sample essay (Advance Organizers, Think-Aloud).
  3. Train reviewers. Have students critique an anonymous sample draft and compare their comments to exemplar feedback; calibrate before reviewing real peers.
  4. Exchange and review. Pair or group students (Class Discussion or an online workshop); each reviewer produces criterion-referenced comments — at least one strength, one specific suggestion per criterion.
  5. Revise. Authors triage feedback, decide what to act on, and produce a revised draft (Coaching from the teacher during this phase).
  6. Reflect. Brief Check-In on which feedback was used and why, building evaluative judgment for the next cycle.

Related Strategies¶

  • Writing Conferences — teacher-led one-to-one feedback that complements peer review with expert judgment
  • Peer Tutoring — a related structure where the more capable partner leads rather than exchanging reciprocal critique

Related Elements¶

  • Class Discussion — whole-class debrief of common issues found across drafts
  • Coaching — teacher circulates during review and revision to correct misdirected feedback
  • Articulation — writing feedback requires reviewers to verbalize criteria, the core learning mechanism
  • Assessment — assess the quality of feedback given, not only the revised product

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Collaborative Learning — peer review is a structured form of collaboration with individual accountability
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the review-and-revise cycle enacts the articulation and reflection phases
  • Formative Assessment — peer feedback is formative assessment distributed among learners

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — a web platform where students first calibrate against expert-scored samples, then review peers' writing; widely used in large STEM writing assignments.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style LMS workshops — anonymous distribution of drafts with rubric-anchored comment fields; each student reviews 2–4 peers.
  • Elementary ELL writing workshop — students use a picture-supported checklist and sentence frames ("I like how you…", "Can you add more about…") to review a partner's story, then revise with a word bank.
