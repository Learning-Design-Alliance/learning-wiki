# Maintaining High Expectations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (5 for) · 9 studies (3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical, 1 causal, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 9 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
In culturally responsive instruction, achievement expectations are determined individually rather than applied uniformly. Students receive praise in proportion to their accomplishments — not lavish praise for simple tasks — and when a student struggles, the instructor guides them positively through the initial steps rather than lowering the demand. The strategy communicates the belief that every student can succeed at challenging work while calibrating support to current performance.

## Design Implications

High expectations function as a motivational signal: students infer their own capability partly from the difficulty of the work and feedback they receive. Specific, challenging goals produce higher performance than vague or easy ones [Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M], and self-efficacy built through mastery experiences predicts persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. The key is pairing demanding goals with process-level guidance, so that struggle is framed as solvable rather than as evidence of inability.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Individualized assessment of each learner's current performance and next achievable challenge ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
- Feedback that is specific, task- and process-focused, and proportional to actual accomplishment ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
- Guided entry points for struggling students — modeling or breaking down the first steps — rather than task abandonment ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Consistent communication that difficulty signals the instructor's confidence, not doubt
- Clear learning goals against which "high" can be defined and communicated
- Ongoing formative assessment to calibrate expectations to each student's current level ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- A positive relationship base that lets students read high demands as belief in their capability, not rejection

#### Constraints
- Inflated praise for trivial success ("Great job!" for minimal effort) can signal low expectations and reduce motivation, particularly for students with low self-efficacy [-M]
- Perceived as unfair or demotivating when applied without adequate support structures; high expectations without scaffolding widen achievement gaps for underprepared learners [~S]
- Requires time and effort to understand individual student needs; difficult to sustain in very large classes without systematic assessment routines
- Teachers' implicit biases can distort individualized expectations downward for some groups; without structured, evidence-based assessment, "individual judgment" reproduces inequity [-M]
- Praise for low-level accomplishments signals lowered expectations and can depress achievement; undifferentiated praise undermines the credibility of later praise [-M]
- Expectations not matched to current performance (too far beyond a student's zone) produce failure cycles and learned helplessness [~S]
- Students who have historically experienced low expectations may initially resist increased demands; withdrawal of support at the first resistance collapses the strategy
- Requires substantial time for differentiation and individual conferencing; under-resourced classrooms often default to uniform low demands

#### Implementation Variability
- Mastery-based structures make individual expectations operational by advancing students on demonstrated competence rather than seat time
- Formative feedback cycles ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)) let expectations be renegotiated as performance changes
- In large classes, rubrics and staged task versions approximate individualized expectations at scale
- Individual goal-setting conferences in which students co-construct targets ([Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M])
- Tiered tasks with a common high ceiling, so all students work toward the same demanding endpoint via different routes
- Revision-required assessment policies: work below standard is not accepted as final, but returned with process-level feedback
- Publicly tracking growth against personal baselines rather than normative comparisons

### Target Learners
- Students at risk of underperforming, who benefit most from the combination of challenge and guided support [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners who have internalized low expectations from prior schooling; proportional praise rebuilds accurate self-assessment
- Novices, who need process-level guidance rather than outcome-level pressure [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Students in culturally diverse classrooms, where teacher expectation biases disproportionately depress opportunities for marginalized students [+M]
- Advanced students who are otherwise under-challenged by uniform, low-floor expectations

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-efficacy and persistence: building the belief that effortful work leads to success
- Accurate self-assessment: praise calibrated to real accomplishment teaches learners to judge their own work honestly
- Problem-solving independence: guided first steps that fade toward self-directed completion
- Achievement growth across all content domains — the strategy is domain-general
- Self-regulation and persistence: students internalize demanding standards over time
- Growth mindset: framing ability as developable through effortful engagement with challenge

### Instructions
1. Establish each learner's current performance level through diagnostic or formative assessment ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
2. Set a specific, challenging but attainable goal for each learner — difficult enough to require effort, close enough to current ability to be reachable [Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M].
3. When the learner struggles, guide them through the initial steps — model, prompt, or break down the task — rather than reducing the goal ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Give praise proportional to genuine accomplishment, and make feedback process-focused: what the learner did and how to improve next [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. Have learners reflect on progress toward their own goals ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)), reinforcing accurate self-evaluation.
6. Raise the expectation as competence grows, so challenge tracks development rather than remaining static.

## Description
Maintaining high expectations means holding every student accountable to demanding, individually calibrated achievement standards rather than lowering demands for students perceived as struggling. Expectations are set relative to each student's current performance and growth trajectory: students receive praise in proportion to genuine accomplishment, not lavished on trivial tasks. When a student stalls, the teacher engages positively and guides the student through the initial steps needed to complete the task, rather than reducing the task itself.

## Design Implications

High expectations work through the combination of demanding goals and strong support — the "warm demander" stance. Challenging, specific goals produce higher performance than easy or vague goals [Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+M], and teacher expectations shape student self-efficacy, which in turn predicts persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. The critical design condition is that expectations are paired with actionable feedback and scaffolding; demandingness without support produces disengagement rather than achievement.
