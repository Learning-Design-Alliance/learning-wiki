Repeat Key Points (Processing Speed)¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 1 mixed, 1 unmarked) · 11 studies (4 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 11 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

This strategy reinforces essential information by restating key points multiple times and in multiple forms — verbal repetition, rephrasing, visual summaries, and worked demonstrations. It is primarily an accommodation for learners with slower processing speed: because these learners need more time to encode and consolidate incoming information, deliberate redundancy prevents critical content from being lost before it is consolidated. The repetition must be varied — verbatim looping adds little, while rephrasing and re-modality presentation deepen encoding Levels-of-processing research shows deeper, varied encoding improves retention. [+S].

Design Implications¶

Repetition supports learners whose working-memory and processing-speed constraints cause them to miss or lose information presented once at pace. Multiple, varied exposures also align with Spaced Repetition [+S] and with multimedia principles: presenting the same point in words and pictures supports dual-channel encoding Presenting content in complementary verbal and visual channels improves learning. [+S]. The design risk is redundancy that adds extraneous load rather than reducing it — repetition should restructure and re-present, not simply lengthen.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Identification of the small set of genuinely key points worth repeating — repetition of everything dilutes the signal
  • Varied formats for each repetition: rephrase, visual/verbal pairing, summary, application (Chunking, Advance Organizers)
  • Adequate time between repetitions for consolidation; pauses and checks for understanding (Check-Ins)
  • Consistent signal that "this is the key point" so learners know what to attend to (Clear Structure & Presentation)

Constraints¶

  • Verbatim repetition without variation produces shallow encoding and disengagement; learners may tune out familiar-sounding content [-M]
  • Repeating everything equally removes the emphasis that directs attention to what matters Emphasis and salience direct learner attention to what is highlighted. [~M]
  • Redundant on-screen text that duplicates narration can increase cognitive load rather than support it Redundant presentation of identical verbal content across channels can impair learning. [-M]
  • For learners with adequate processing speed, heavy repetition is unnecessary and slows instruction [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Within-lesson: state the point, rephrase it, show it visually, and have learners restate it (Practice, Self-Explanation)
  • Across lessons: revisit key points at spaced intervals rather than clustering repetitions (Spaced Repetition [+S])
  • Modality shifts: pair spoken repetition with written summaries, diagrams, or gestures (Dual Coding Theory)
  • Learner-generated repetition: have students restate or teach the point to a peer, converting passive repetition into retrieval practice

Target Learners¶

  • Students with identified processing-speed challenges (e.g., ADHD, specific learning disabilities) who need extended time and multiple exposures — this strategy is a core Accommodation in that context
  • English language learners and novices who benefit from rephrasing and Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners, for whom repetition of mastered content is redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of essential facts, definitions, and procedures (Spaced Repetition [+S])
  • Consolidation of foundational concepts before higher-order work
  • Reduced working-memory demand during initial encoding (Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M])

Instructions¶

  1. Identify 2–4 key points per lesson segment; signal them explicitly ("This is the key idea…") (Clear Structure & Presentation)
  2. Present each key point, then rephrase it in different words and pair it with a visual (Chunking)
  3. Pause and check understanding before moving on (Check-Ins)
  4. Have learners restate or apply the point themselves (Practice)
  5. Revisit key points in subsequent lessons at spaced intervals (Spaced Repetition)
  6. Coordinate with broader supports such as Accommodating Processing Speed Challenges

Related Strategies¶

  • Accommodating Processing Speed Challenges — the broader accommodation package this strategy belongs to
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — anchoring repeated points to existing schemas improves encoding
  • Chunking — reduces the amount that must be processed per exposure

Examples¶

  • Explicit instruction scripts (e.g., Direct Instruction programs such as Reading Mastery) — build in scripted repetition and unison responding so key content is encountered multiple times in varied formats
  • UDL-aligned course design — key points appear in the lecture, the slide summary, the posted outline, and the exit ticket, each in a different form (CAST UDL Guidelines)
  • IEP accommodations — "restate and rephrase directions; provide written copy of key points" is a standard processing-speed accommodation in K-12 special education
