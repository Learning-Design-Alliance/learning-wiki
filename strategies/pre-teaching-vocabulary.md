# Pre-Teaching Vocabulary

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Pre-teaching vocabulary involves identifying the words most critical to understanding an upcoming text or lesson and explicitly introducing them — with student-friendly definitions, examples, and quick activities — before learners read or engage with the material. The goal is not exhaustive word study but targeted preparation: removing the specific lexical barriers that would otherwise derail comprehension. It is typically carried out through brief direct explanation, discussion of examples and non-examples, and quick checks for understanding.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching reduces the extraneous cognitive load of decoding unfamiliar words while simultaneously trying to construct meaning from a text [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Vocabulary instruction produces reliable but modest gains in comprehension, with the strongest effects when words are taught deeply (multiple encounters, varied contexts) rather than briefly defined [Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Because working memory cannot juggle novel word meanings and text-level inference at the same time, front-loading key terms functions as a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and pairs naturally with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) as a preparation phase.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — drawn from the text and chosen for how central they are to its meaning (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan's "Tier Two" criterion)
- Student-friendly explanations with examples, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), or visuals rather than dictionary definitions
- Brief active engagement: learners say, sort, or apply the words, not just hear them
- A follow-up encounter with the words in context ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md), discussion, or writing)

#### Constraints
- Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M] — comprehensiveness is the enemy of effectiveness
- Brief, definition-only pre-teaching yields weak comprehension gains; effects depend on depth of processing and multiple subsequent encounters [Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Pre-teaching can preempt productive word-learning from context: learners who never struggle with an unfamiliar word in context lose opportunities to infer meaning, a skill that itself needs development [~W]
- Word selection is consequential — pre-teaching peripheral words consumes time without aiding comprehension of the text's main ideas

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full pre-teaching**: explicit introduction of all critical words before reading (suitable for dense informational texts)
- **Selective pre-teaching**: teach only words unguessable from context; leave inferable words for during-reading strategy work
- **During-reading support instead**: margin glosses, footnoted definitions, or just-in-time explanation — often as effective for digital texts where interruption is cheap
- **Rich instruction**: extended routines such as word walls, semantic mapping, and [Frayer-type](../elements/graphic-organizers.md) four-square analysis across multiple days

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through high school readers encountering discipline-specific or academic vocabulary beyond their current level
- Struggling readers and English learners, for whom unaddressed vocabulary gaps compound into comprehension failure [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M] — the same novice-protection logic applies to lexical scaffolds
- Less necessary for proficient readers with strong background knowledge of the topic, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: enabling learners to construct meaning from complex texts
- Vocabulary acquisition: building durable word knowledge through multiple encounters
- Background knowledge activation: connecting new terms to what learners already know [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Select** 5–10 words that are (a) central to understanding the text and (b) unlikely to be known; skip words learners can infer from context.
2. **Explain** each word in student-friendly language with an example, [Analogy](../elements/analogies.md), or image — avoid dictionary-style definitions ([Dual Coding](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) supports recall [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]).
3. **Engage** learners actively: have them sort examples/non-examples, rate their familiarity, or use the word in a sentence.
4. **Check** quickly that learners can recognize or use the words before reading begins.
5. **Revisit** the words during and after reading through discussion and writing, so learners meet them repeatedly in context.
