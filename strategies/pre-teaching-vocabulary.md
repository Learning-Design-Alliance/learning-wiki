# Pre-Teaching Vocabulary

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (6 for, 1 against) · 19 studies (8 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 4 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 7 of 19 report an effect size

## Description
Pre-teaching vocabulary involves identifying the words most critical to understanding an upcoming text or lesson and explicitly introducing them — with student-friendly definitions, examples, and quick activities — before learners read or engage with the material. The goal is not exhaustive word study but targeted preparation: removing the specific lexical barriers that would otherwise derail comprehension. It is typically carried out through brief direct explanation, discussion of examples and non-examples, and quick checks for understanding.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching reduces the extraneous cognitive load of decoding unfamiliar words while simultaneously trying to construct meaning from a text [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Vocabulary instruction produces reliable but modest gains in comprehension, with the strongest effects when words are taught deeply (multiple encounters, varied contexts) rather than briefly defined [Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Because working memory cannot juggle novel word meanings and text-level inference at the same time, front-loading key terms functions as a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and pairs naturally with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) as a preparation phase.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — drawn from the text and chosen for how central they are to its meaning (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan's "Tier Two" criterion)
- Student-friendly explanations with examples, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), or visuals rather than dictionary definitions
- Brief active engagement: learners say, sort, or apply the words, not just hear them
- A follow-up encounter with the words in context ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md), discussion, or writing)
- Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — that appear in the text and recur across contexts
- Multiple meaningful encounters with each word before and during reading, ideally with [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) such as [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of usage
- A follow-on reading or lesson where learners encounter the words in context
- Careful word selection: prioritize words that are (a) essential to comprehending the text, (b) likely unknown, and (c) broadly useful (e.g., Beck et al.'s "Tier Two" academic vocabulary)
- Student-friendly definitions — conversational explanations with examples, not dictionary paraphrases
- A follow-up context (the text or task) in which learners actually encounter and use the words
- Text analysis to identify which words actually block comprehension — typically 5–8 words, not 20
- Student-friendly, conversational definitions rather than dictionary phrasing ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Multiple encounters: example sentences, nonlinguistic representation, or a quick use-it-yourself prompt

#### Constraints
- Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M] — comprehensiveness is the enemy of effectiveness
- Brief, definition-only pre-teaching yields weak comprehension gains; effects depend on depth of processing and multiple subsequent encounters [Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Pre-teaching can preempt productive word-learning from context: learners who never struggle with an unfamiliar word in context lose opportunities to infer meaning, a skill that itself needs development [~W]
- Word selection is consequential — pre-teaching peripheral words consumes time without aiding comprehension of the text's main ideas
- Teaching words in isolation from context produces knowledge that often fails to transfer to comprehension; definitions alone are insufficient [-M]
- Effects on general vocabulary growth are small when words are taught only once and never revisited; without [Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md), gains fade [-M]
- Learners with strong vocabularies gain little from pre-teaching words they can infer from context — the approach shows an expertise-reversal pattern for known words [~W]
- Word meanings taught in isolation, without later reinforcement in context, show weak and fast-decaying effects [-M] — single pre-teaching sessions rarely produce durable word learning
- Pre-teaching can remove productive struggle: when the goal is inferring meaning from context, supplying definitions beforehand eliminates the practice the activity was designed to provide [~M]
- Effects on general comprehension are smaller than effects on word-specific knowledge; pre-teaching a handful of words rarely moves whole-text comprehension unless the words are truly pivotal [-W]
- Ineffective when the pre-taught words are marginal to the passage's main ideas — the time cost yields no comprehension gain
- Does not substitute for wide reading; vocabulary breadth is built primarily through volume of independent reading over time [~M]

#### Implementation Variability

- **Selective pre-teaching**: teach only words unguessable from context; leave inferable words for during-reading strategy work

- **Rich instruction**: extended routines such as word walls, semantic mapping, and [Frayer-type](../elements/graphic-organizers.md) four-square analysis across multiple days
- **Full pre-teaching**: all critical words taught before reading with rich activities — appropriate for dense, unfamiliar texts

- **Rich instruction**: extended discussion, multiple contexts, word associations (e.g., Beck & McKeown's Text Talk approach) — slower but deeper
- **Brief definitional pre-teaching**: quick definitions plus one example — appropriate for words needed only for one reading
- **Multimedia pre-teaching**: images, gestures, or video for concrete words, especially for multilingual learners
- **Morphological pre-teaching**: teaching root words and affixes that recur across the upcoming text, extending coverage beyond the selected words
- **Full pre-teaching**: definition, example, context, and learner-generated sentence before reading — for words central to the text's argument
- **Light-touch preview**: brief pronunciation and gloss only — for words needed for surface fluency but inferable in context

- **Morphological pre-teaching**: teaching a root or affix (e.g., *bio-*, *-ology*) that unlocks a word family rather than a single word

### Instructions
1. **Select** 5–10 words that are (a) central to understanding the text and (b) unlikely to be known; skip words learners can infer from context.
2. **Explain** each word in student-friendly language with an example, [Analogy](../elements/analogies.md), or image — avoid dictionary-style definitions ([Dual Coding](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) supports recall [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]).
3. **Engage** learners actively: have them sort examples/non-examples, rate their familiarity, or use the word in a sentence.
4. **Check** quickly that learners can recognize or use the words before reading begins.
5. **Revisit** the words during and after reading through discussion and writing, so learners meet them repeatedly in context.

## Description
Vocabulary pre-teaching introduces the meanings of selected words *before* learners read a text, watch a demonstration, or begin a task. The instructor selects a small set of high-impact words, provides student-friendly definitions and examples, and often engages learners in brief active processing (e.g., generating examples, connecting to known concepts) so the meanings are available when the words appear in context.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching removes a common bottleneck in comprehension: when too many words are unknown, working memory is consumed by decoding meaning instead of building a situation model [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. Effects are strongest when the taught words are genuinely critical to understanding the passage and when instruction goes beyond dictionary definitions to include multiple encounters and active use [~M]. Because vocabulary is learned incrementally, pre-teaching works best as the first exposure in a cycle of repeated, spaced encounters rather than a one-time event.

# Vocabulary Pre-Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review), `q3`–`q4` · 3 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Vocabulary pre-teaching identifies a small number of words that are critical to understanding an upcoming text or task and teaches them directly before reading — through student-friendly definitions, examples, contexts, and quick checks. The goal is not exhaustive word study but removing comprehension bottlenecks so that working memory during reading is spent on meaning-making rather than decoding unknown words.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching works because unknown word meanings consume working memory during reading, degrading comprehension of the surrounding text [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Effects are strongest when the pre-taught words are genuinely load-bearing for the passage and when instruction goes beyond a dictionary definition to include multiple contexts and active engagement [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Pre-teaching also functions as [Activation](../principles/activation.md): linking new words to known concepts before reading primes relevant background knowledge [Activating prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Text analysis to identify which words actually block comprehension — typically 5–8 words, not 20
- Student-friendly, conversational definitions rather than dictionary phrasing ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Multiple encounters: example sentences, nonlinguistic representation, or a quick use-it-yourself prompt
- Connection to words and concepts learners already know ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md))

#### Constraints
- Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads learners; comprehension benefits shrink as word lists grow [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [-M]
- Isolated definition-giving without contextual engagement produces word-list knowledge that does not transfer to reading comprehension [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [-S]
- Ineffective when the pre-taught words are marginal to the passage's main ideas — the time cost yields no comprehension gain
- Does not substitute for wide reading; vocabulary breadth is built primarily through volume of independent reading over time [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full pre-teaching**: definition, example, context, and learner-generated sentence before reading — for words central to the text's argument
- **Light-touch preview**: brief pronunciation and gloss only — for words needed for surface fluency but inferable in context
- **During-reading substitution**: teaching words at the moment they first appear, when the surrounding context supplies meaning
- **Morphological pre-teaching**: teaching a root or affix (e.g., *bio-*, *-ology*) that unlocks a word family rather than a single word

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers and English learners, who encounter proportionally more unknown words and benefit most from removing bottlenecks [Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing.](../claims/vocabulary-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Novices in a discipline facing technical terminology (e.g., *osmosis*, *tariff*, *metaphor*) before first exposure to domain texts
- Less necessary for proficient readers with strong background knowledge, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: ensuring the text itself is understandable on first pass
- Word knowledge depth: building initial meanings that later encounters consolidate
- Disciplinary literacy: acquiring the technical lexicon of a field before reading in it

### Instructions
1. Analyze the text and select 5–8 words that are (a) unfamiliar, (b) critical to comprehension, and (c) likely to recur — following the three-tier logic of [Bringing Words to Life](https://www.guilford.com/books/Bringing-Words-to-Life/Beck-McKeown-Kucan/9781462508167).
2. Write a student-friendly definition in plain, conversational language ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
3. Present each word with an example and a non-example, connecting to known concepts ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Require brief active processing: learners answer a quick "Would you…?" question or use the word in their own sentence ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) works for younger learners).
5. During reading, revisit the words at the point they appear ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) the text helps).
6. After reading, schedule spaced review so word knowledge consolidates rather than fading.
