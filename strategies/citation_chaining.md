# Citation_Chaining

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Citation chaining is a search strategy in which learners use the citation network of a known relevant source to find related literature: **backward chaining** follows the reference list to earlier foundational work, and **forward chaining** uses tools like Google Scholar's "Cited by" to find later work that builds on it. The strategy treats sources as nodes in a scholarly conversation rather than isolated results, teaching learners to reconstruct how ideas developed and where disagreement lies.

## Design Implications

Citation chaining shifts information seeking from keyword luck to structured inquiry, building the disposition to ask "who is this author in dialogue with, and who has responded?" It supports source evaluation because a source's position in the citation network — foundational, contested, retracted, or marginal — is itself evidence of quality and influence [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]. It also naturally surfaces multiple perspectives on a question, since forward chaining reveals both supporting and critical responses to a source.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A seed source of known relevance and reasonable quality
- Access to a citation index or database with forward citation search (Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science)
- Explicit criteria for judging which branches of the chain to follow (recency, venue, citation context)
- A method for recording the chain — a citation map, annotated bibliography, or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) workflow

#### Constraints
- Backward chaining skews a literature review toward older work; without deliberate forward chaining, learners miss current state-of-the-art [-W]
- Citation counts measure influence, not accuracy — heavily cited claims can be wrong, and learners who treat citation counts as a quality verdict over-trust flawed sources [~W]
- Forward chaining in interdisciplinary topics can explode into hundreds of citing works; without scoping rules (e.g., limit by venue, date, or citation context), learners experience overload
- Retracted or superseded papers remain in citation networks; chaining alone does not detect retraction status [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Backward-only chaining** — tracing a review article's references to find primary sources; fast but historically biased
- **Forward-only chaining** — starting from a seminal paper and following "Cited by" to track a debate's evolution
- **Snowball sampling** — iterating chaining until no new relevant sources appear; the standard method in systematic-review searching
- **Citation-context reading** — reading *how* a citing paper characterizes the cited work, which teaches that citations can support, qualify, or contradict

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and graduate students beginning independent research [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]
- Learners who already have one credible source but lack domain vocabulary for effective keyword searching
- Less useful for novices with no seed source; pair with [Ask Experts](../principles/ask-experts.md) or librarian guidance to obtain one

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: evaluating source quality and situating claims in a literature
- Epistemic cognition: understanding scholarship as an evolving, contested conversation
- Research skill: conducting literature reviews and systematic searches

### Instructions
1. **Select a seed source** — a relevant, credible article identified through a database, reference work, or [Ask Experts](../principles/ask-experts.md).
2. **Chain backward** — scan the reference list; identify the 2–3 most-cited or most-relevant prior works and skim them.
3. **Chain forward** — use "Cited by" in Google Scholar or Scopus to find later work engaging the seed source.
4. **Read citation contexts** — for each citing work, find where it cites the seed and note whether it supports, extends, or challenges it.
5. **Evaluate and prune** — apply source-evaluation criteria (venue, methods, retraction status) and keep only branches worth following; see [A Finder's Guide to Facts](a_finder%27s_guide_to_facts.md).
6. **Map the conversation** — record the chain as a diagram or annotated bibliography, noting agreements and disputes; enforce a [3-Source Rule](3-source_rule.md) minimum of independent sources per key claim.
7. **Iterate** — treat the strongest new sources as seeds and repeat until the conversation's shape is clear.

## Examples
- **Systematic reviews in health sciences** — Cochrane-style reviews use backward-and-forward citation searching ("snowballing") as a supplement to database searches to ensure no relevant trial is missed.
- **Google Scholar "Cited by" in a first-year seminar** — students start from a single assigned article, build a citation map of 10–15 sources, and write a one-page history of the debate, distinguishing foundational, supporting, and critical papers.
- **Connected Papers (https://www.connectedpapers.com)** — visualizes a seed paper's citation graph as a similarity map, making backward and forward chains explorable in one interface; used in research-methods courses to teach network thinking.
