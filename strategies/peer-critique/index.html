Peer Critique¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 9 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Peer critique is a structured feedback arrangement in which learners review one another's drafts, solutions, or performances against explicit criteria and deliver actionable suggestions for revision. It is carried out through protocols (e.g., gallery walks, "warm/cool" feedback, rubric-guided review) that specify what reviewers look for, how comments are phrased, and how authors respond.

Design Implications¶

Peer critique converts assessment into a learning activity: reviewing others' work requires applying criteria, which deepens the reviewer's own understanding of quality Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]. Benefits accrue to both roles — authors receive multiple perspectives and revision targets, while reviewers strengthen evaluative judgment by comparing diverse solutions Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]. Critique only improves work when it is specific, criteria-anchored, and followed by time to revise; unstructured "comment on your partner's essay" exchanges produce vague praise and little change.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria or a rubric that reviewers are trained to apply (Assessment)
  • Training and modeling of feedback quality — critique the work, not the person
  • Time and expectation for revision; critique without revision is commentary, not learning
  • Psychological safety: norms that frame critique as helping, not judging
  • Explicit criteria, a rubric, or annotated exemplars so reviewers share a definition of quality
  • Training and modeling of feedback quality — often by critiquing a sample work as a whole class first
  • A revision opportunity: critique without revision becomes a dead-end exercise
  • Psychological safety norms (critique the work, not the author)

Constraints¶

  • Untrained reviewers default to vague praise ("good job") or personal taste, which does not improve work Checklist-style evaluation without training is ineffective in online settings. [-M]
  • Peer grades correlate weakly with expert grades for complex, open-ended work; use peer feedback for improvement, not high-stakes judgment [~S]
  • Power and status differences (friendship, perceived ability) bias the candor and uptake of feedback
  • Novices may lack the domain knowledge to detect errors, propagating misconceptions when critique substitutes for expert feedback [~M]
  • Untrained reviewers default to vague praise or personal comments, which produce no measurable improvement [-M]
  • Peer grades are unreliable for high-stakes evaluation; peer critique is a learning activity, not an assessment instrument, unless calibrated [~M]
  • Strong status differences or friendship dynamics distort feedback honesty [-W]
  • Novices may lack the domain knowledge to detect substantive flaws, limiting critique depth to surface features [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written asynchronous (e.g., peer review in LMS or Peergrade/PeerStudio workflows) — scales, but loses dialogue
  • Oral protocols (critical friends groups, gallery walks) — richer negotiation of meaning, higher facilitation cost
  • Calibrated review — reviewers first grade an instructor-annotated exemplar and compare their judgment to expert judgment before reviewing peers; substantially improves feedback accuracy
  • Two-stage — anonymous first pass on criteria, then author–reviewer dialogue
  • Warm/cool feedback protocols (e.g., EL Education's critical friends): separate praise from improvement suggestions
  • Anonymous or blind review to reduce social bias, common in platforms like Peergrade and SWoRD/Peerceptiv
  • Calibrated review: reviewers first rate exemplars with known quality and get accuracy feedback before reviewing peers
  • Gallery walk / silent critique with sticky notes for fast, low-stakes cycles

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to recognize quality but still benefit from seeing peers' approaches Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]
  • Writing-intensive and project-based courses where drafts and revision are natural
  • Less suitable for absolute novices, who cannot yet evaluate work against criteria and need expert feedback first [~M]
  • Most effective in middle grades through higher education; requires enough metacognitive maturity to evaluate against criteria [~W]
  • Particularly valuable for learners who overestimate their own work quality — reviewing others builds the evaluative judgment they lack for their own drafts [+M]
  • Reviewers with moderate (not minimal) expertise give the most useful feedback [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing what "good work" looks like in a domain
  • Revision and iterative improvement of products
  • Communication: articulating specific, actionable, respectful feedback
  • Self-regulation: using external criteria to monitor one's own work Self-regulated learning.
  • Evaluative judgment: internalizing what quality looks like in a domain
  • Revision and drafting processes in writing, design, and project work
  • Communication and professional feedback practices

Instructions¶

  1. Publish the criteria or rubric and anchor a whole-class example to it before any peer exchange.
  2. Model critique on an instructor-provided sample, thinking aloud about how criteria map to specific, actionable comments.
  3. Pair or group learners and exchange drafts; reviewers apply the rubric and give a fixed number of specific strengths and suggestions.
  4. Have authors summarize the feedback and state their revision plan rather than accepting or rejecting comments wholesale.
  5. Build in protected revision time, then optionally repeat the cycle on the revised draft.

Related Strategies¶

  • Comparing Cases — reviewing multiple peers' solutions works as a contrasting-cases comparison that sharpens criteria
  • Self-Assessment — critique criteria transfer to evaluating one's own drafts
  • Gallery Walk — a common peer critique format for visual and project work
  • Two Stars and a Wish — a lightweight critique protocol for younger learners
  • Critical Friends Protocol — a formalized structured critique cycle

Examples¶

  • Elaborated Feedback in Writing Courses — Calibrated Peer Review (https://calibratedpeerreview.org) has students first review instructor-seeded calibration essays; only after their judgments align with expert scores do they review classmates, addressing the novice-accuracy problem.
  • Studio Critique in Art and Design Education — Formal group critique protocols (describe → analyze → interpret → judge) make criteria explicit and separate observation from evaluation.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio in MOOCs and university courses — Rubric-guided anonymous peer review at scale, with agreement checks to flag low-quality reviews.
  • EL Education (Expeditionary Learning) — crew-level critique cycles and "kind, specific, helpful" norms are embedded in project-based curricula (eleducation.org); students revise expedition products through multiple critique rounds
  • Peerceptiv (formerly SWoRD) — calibrated peer review for writing at scale in university courses (peerceptiv.com)
  • Studio critique in art and design education — the canonical professional form: public, criteria-anchored critique followed by revision of the piece
