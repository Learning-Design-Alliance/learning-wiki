Use the SEE-SAY-SIGN method to teach sight words: simultaneously see the word card, say the word, and sign it¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's ASL treatment procedure has the intern explain that the sign makes a "picture in your mind" of the word's meaning, help the child make a personal connection, then use the SEE-SAY-SIGN method: the child simultaneously sees the word card, says the word, and signs it, first with the intern and then independently. The child then uses the word in a sentence and locates, reads, and signs it in context in a shared book. Sessions lasted about 10 minutes weekly for five weeks, one word per week.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Initial instruction of the correct ASL signs must be done explicitly and directly by the teacher

Constraints¶

  • The article's evidence comes from a five-week inquiry with eleven struggling first graders and should not be generalized beyond similar settings without further testing

Target Learners¶

  • struggling first graders acquiring sight vocabulary
  • ELL students in early primary grades

Target Learning Goals¶

  • automatic recognition of high-frequency sight words
  • word retention through multi-modal associations

Claims¶

  • ELL first graders who learned ASL signs with sight vocabulary moved from zero pretest words to perfect posttest scores [+W]
  • First graders taught sight words with ASL signs achieved higher posttest accuracy than students taught without ASL [+W]
  • All eleven participating first graders gained sight vocabulary, with 89% knowing four or all five taught words at posttest [+W]
  • An ELL student spontaneously signed sight words when seeing them in context, which appeared to support word recognition [+W]
  • Interns observed extremely high enthusiasm for ASL vocabulary learning, including home practice and increased self-confidence [+W]
