# Temperature Regulation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Temperature regulation is the deliberate management of ambient temperature in learning environments so that thermal discomfort does not consume cognitive and attentional resources needed for learning. Field research in schools shows that both overheating and excessive cooling degrade performance on reading and mathematics tasks, with the steepest effects above roughly 24°C [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [+S]. Because thermal discomfort operates as a continuous low-level distractor, it is best treated as a baseline environmental condition to be engineered in advance rather than a teaching strategy to be applied mid-lesson.

## Design Implications

Thermal discomfort competes for attention and working memory, adding extraneous load that reduces capacity for the learning task itself [Cognitive overload degrades learning when extraneous demands consume working memory.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Effects are roughly linear near the comfort zone: each degree of unwanted warming above ~20°C measurably reduces speed and accuracy on schoolwork, and moderate cooling is a low-cost intervention with measurable achievement returns [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [+M]. Heat exposure is especially damaging: students in non-air-conditioned rooms show slower reaction times and reduced cognitive throughput during heat waves [Heat exposure without air conditioning reduces cognitive performance in students.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Thermostatic control at the room level, not just the building level — teachers need authority to adjust their own classroom temperature
- Ventilation integrated with temperature control; lowering temperature by simply shutting off ventilation trades one problem for another, since air quality independently affects performance [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [+M]
- Monitoring: a simple thermometer per room, since perceived and actual temperature often diverge
- Attention to seasonal timing — the highest-risk periods are early-autumn heat waves in unconditioned buildings [Heat exposure without air conditioning reduces cognitive performance in students.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Retrofitting HVAC is expensive; the cost-effectiveness case rests on comparing installation cost against achievement gains, which favors simple measures (moderate setpoint reduction, shading, fans) over full rebuilds [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [~M]
- Centralized building controls that lock teachers out of adjustment leave classrooms stuck at uncomfortable setpoints; without local control the intervention cannot respond to real conditions [-M]
- Cooling by reducing outdoor air supply degrades air quality and can erase the temperature benefit [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [-M]
- Individual differences in thermal comfort (clothing, metabolism, acclimatization) mean no single setpoint suits everyone; a fixed temperature helps on average but some learners remain uncomfortable [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Passive measures first: blinds, shading, ceiling fans, and dress-code flexibility can hold temperatures in range without mechanical cooling
- Scheduled ventilation cycles that pre-cool rooms before occupied periods
- In hot climates without air conditioning, shifting high-stakes assessment to cooler parts of the day [Heat exposure without air conditioning reduces cognitive performance in students.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+W]

### Target Learners
- All K–12 students, with the strongest documented evidence in primary-school reading and mathematics tasks [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [+S]
- Students in under-resourced buildings lacking air conditioning, who bear disproportionate heat exposure [Heat exposure without air conditioning reduces cognitive performance in students.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+S]
- Adult learners in office-style environments show the same pattern on cognitive work [Optimal office productivity occurs around 21–22°C, declining as temperature rises.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2006.04.005) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning goal in itself, but a precondition: protects sustained attention, working memory, and test performance across all subject areas
- Particularly relevant to speeded tasks — reading fluency, mathematics computation, and standardized assessment [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish a target range of 68–74°F (20–23°C) for instructional spaces and verify it with a room thermometer, not building-level reports.
2. Delegate adjustment authority to the classroom teacher so corrections happen within minutes, not weeks.
3. Pair any temperature intervention with adequate outdoor air supply; never cool a room by starving its ventilation [Wargocki & Wyon demonstrated classroom temperature reductions improved academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001) [-M].
4. Use passive measures (shading, fans, flexible dress codes) before capital-intensive retrofits.
5. Schedule cognitively demanding activities and assessments during the coolest part of the day in unconditioned buildings [Heat exposure without air conditioning reduces cognitive performance in students.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605) [+W].

## Examples
- **Wargocki & Wyon's school field studies (Denmark, Sweden, UK)** — controlled interventions lowering classroom temperature from 25°C to 20°C improved performance on reading and mathematics tasks equivalent to a substantial fraction of a school year's progress ([Building and Environment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2013.01.001)).
- **PLOS Medicine heat-wave study (2018)** — college students in dormitories without air conditioning performed significantly worse on cognitive tests during a heat wave than peers in cooled rooms ([https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002605)).
- **ASHRAE Standard 55 thermal comfort guidelines** — the engineering standard schools and districts reference when specifying HVAC setpoints and comfort ranges ([https://www.ashrae.org](https://www.ashrae.org)).
