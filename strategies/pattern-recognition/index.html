Pattern Recognition¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Pattern recognition as a strategy presents learners with multiple, deliberately varied instances of a concept, procedure, or problem type and asks them to identify what the instances share and how they differ. Rather than telling learners the defining features up front, the strategy has them extract the underlying structure themselves — surface features vary, deep structure repeats. It is carried out through example sets, comparison tasks, sorting or classification activities, and prompts that ask "what makes these the same kind of problem?"

Design Implications¶

Learning a concept means learning which features matter and which do not; multiple varied examples are the primary way learners acquire that discrimination Multiple varied examples improve transfer by highlighting invariant structure. [+M]. Comparison across cases is more effective than studying examples one at a time, because juxtaposition makes the common structure perceptually and cognitively salient Comparing multiple analogs improves analogical transfer. [+S]. However, learners often fail to notice patterns spontaneously; prompting them to compare, explain, or self-explain is usually necessary Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. [+S]. A productive sequence is exploration before explanation: letting learners struggle with varied cases first prepares them to learn from the subsequent telling Preparation for future learning: contrasting cases before instruction improves learning from lecture. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A set of at least 2–3 examples that share deep structure but differ in surface features
  • Prompts that direct attention to comparison ("How are these alike? What category do they belong to?")
  • A follow-up task requiring classification or application to a novel case (Application)
  • Feedback that names the pattern once learners have attempted to articulate it

Constraints¶

  • Examples that are too similar teach learners to match surface features, not structure — near-transfer only Multiple varied examples improve transfer by highlighting invariant structure. [-M]
  • Unguided discovery of patterns can overload novices; without prompts or prior knowledge, learners may extract the wrong regularity Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]
  • Learners with high prior knowledge may find guided comparison redundant and benefit more from independent abstraction [~M]
  • If the pattern is stated before examples are studied, learners may simply confirm it rather than discriminate features [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Contrasting cases: side-by-side pairs differing on one critical dimension, isolating the feature that matters
  • Classification tasks: learners sort a mixed set into categories, forcing active pattern extraction
  • Invent-a-case: learners generate their own example fitting the pattern, testing whether they have abstracted it
  • Delayed naming: pattern is explored first, labeled afterward ("A time for telling")

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who otherwise anchor on surface features when classifying problems Comparing multiple analogs improves analogical transfer. [+S]
  • Intermediate learners building flexible, multi-case representations of a domain Multiple varied examples improve transfer by highlighting invariant structure. [+M]
  • Less effective as pure discovery for complete novices without comparison prompts Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Concept formation: learning category boundaries and defining features
  • Problem-type identification: recognizing which solution schema applies to a novel problem
  • Transfer: applying structure learned in one context to superficially different contexts
  • Discrimination: distinguishing genuine instances from plausible non-instances

Instructions¶

  1. Select 3–5 cases sharing deep structure but varying surface features; include at least one borderline or non-instance to sharpen boundaries.
  2. Present cases side by side rather than sequentially, so comparison is easy.
  3. Ask learners to identify what the cases have in common and generate a rule or label (Analogies can scaffold the mapping to a known structure).
  4. Have learners classify new, unseen cases, including near-miss non-instances (Case Studies work well as source cases).
  5. Name and consolidate the pattern after learners attempt articulation, then connect it to the target procedure or concept.

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-based learning — cases are the raw material from which patterns are extracted
  • Concept attainment — the classic pattern-recognition routine for concept learning

Examples¶

  • CGI for Math — teachers learn to recognize problem types (result-unknown, change-unknown, etc.) by sorting and comparing varied word problems, then map student strategies to each type.
  • Physics problem sorting (Chi and colleagues' tradition) — novices sort mechanics problems by surface features (pulleys, springs); training with comparison shifts sorting to deep structure (energy conservation vs. Newton's laws).
  • Harvard Business School case method — repeated case discussion across sessions builds recognition of recurring managerial decision patterns.
