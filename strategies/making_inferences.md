# Making Inferences

## Description
Making inferences requires readers to draw conclusions from information in a text that the author does not explicitly state. Readers combine textual clues with their own background knowledge to "read between the lines," building a coherent situation model of what the text describes. Because authors routinely leave connections unstated, comprehension depends on the reader actively generating these bridging inferences rather than passively decoding stated propositions [Constructing inferences during narrative text comprehension.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.3.371) [+S].

## Design Implications

Inference-making is a teachable skill, not merely a byproduct of reading volume: explicit instruction in identifying clues, activating relevant knowledge, and verbalizing the inference improves comprehension, especially for weaker readers [Activation of background knowledge for inference making.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2012.699578) [+S]. Instruction works best when teachers model the reasoning process aloud and then fade support as learners generate and justify their own inferences.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts that genuinely contain implicit information worth inferring — not everything should be stated
- Instructor modeling of inference-making ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or narrated reasoning) before independent practice
- Sufficient background knowledge to combine with textual clues; [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of that knowledge before reading
- Opportunities to generate inferences and receive feedback on their plausibility ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners lacking relevant background knowledge cannot fill the gaps the author left; the strategy fails when the knowledge base is absent rather than merely inactive [~S]
- Inaccurate or biased background knowledge produces confident but wrong inferences — learners may impose a familiar schema on text that contradicts it [~M]
- Overly literal readers who treat inference questions as recall questions, or learners who infer wildly without textual anchoring, both need explicit criteria for what counts as a warranted inference [-W]
- Working-memory demands are high: holding textual clues in mind while retrieving and integrating prior knowledge can overload novice readers; [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the text into shorter segments mitigates this [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Bridging inferences (connecting ideas across the text) versus elaborative inferences (adding world knowledge beyond the text) — bridging inferences have the stronger evidence base for comprehension gains [+S]
- Graphic organizers, clue-and-conclusion frames, or question generation ("Why did the character do that? What must be true?")
- Applied beyond reading: [Case Study](../elements/case-study.md) analysis, data interpretation, and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) all require the same clue-plus-knowledge reasoning

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers in upper elementary through secondary school, who often decode well but fail to build coherent situation models [Activation of background knowledge for inference making.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2012.699578) [+S]
- Adult learners improving reading comprehension and critical thinking
- Less effective as a standalone intervention for learners whose difficulty is word recognition rather than comprehension — decoding must be largely automatic first [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: constructing meaning from implicit information
- Critical thinking: evaluating evidence and justifying conclusions
- Knowledge integration: connecting new text information with existing knowledge [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Select a text with genuine implicit content and identify the target inferences in advance.
2. Activate relevant background knowledge before reading ([Activation](../principles/activation.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. Model inference-making aloud on a short excerpt, verbalizing the clue, the knowledge brought to bear, and the resulting conclusion ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
4. Have learners generate inferences individually or in pairs, citing the textual evidence supporting each ([Individual Response](../elements/individual-response.md)).
5. Discuss and evaluate inferences as a group, distinguishing well-supported from speculative ones ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
6. Fade support across texts until learners generate and self-check inferences independently.

## Related Strategies
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) — supplies the knowledge component that inferences combine with textual clues
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — marking clues and questions in the text externalizes the inference process
- [Question Generation](../strategies/question-generation.md) — learners who pose "why" and "how" questions prompt their own inferencing

## Examples
- In a story where a character is repeatedly late and unprepared, learners infer disorganization from the accumulated details plus their own experience — then must cite which details warrant the conclusion.
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown) — small-group reading routine in which students take turns predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing, generating bridging inferences with peer feedback.
- **Elbro & Buch-Iversen's inference training** — Danish poor readers taught to activate background knowledge and verbalize inferences showed significant comprehension gains over controls.
- [Case-based learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) in business or medicine — diagnosing the underlying problem from incomplete case facts is inference-making applied to professional reasoning.
