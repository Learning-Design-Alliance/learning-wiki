Use outcome- and semester-specific empirical benchmarks from prior RCTs to plan minimum detectable true effects and to interpret new community college evaluation findings¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 7 claims (6 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 quant-synthesis), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

Description¶

When planning an RCT, researchers should size the sample so the minimum detectable true effect is plausible given the distribution of true effects from past evaluations, choosing benchmarks specific to the outcome and timing of measurement. For interpretation, a new effect estimate and its standard error can be converted to an adjusted empirical Bayes estimate and located on the published distribution. The article illustrates this with the Detroit Promise Path evaluation, whose 1.8-credit effect corresponds to "an adjusted empirical Bayes impact estimate of 1.6 credits earned" at the 83rd percentile after two semesters.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • MDTE calculations must be specific with respect to outcome and timing
  • other design parameters are necessary to calculate the minimum detectable true effect

Constraints¶

  • Benchmarks from prior studies inform what is realistically attainable but do not necessarily inform what effects are practically meaningful to decision-makers

Target Learners¶

  • community college students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • enrollment persistence
  • credit accumulation
  • credential attainment

Claims¶

  • For credits earned in semester 1, the estimated distribution of true intervention effects has a mean of 0.48 credits, a cross-intervention standard deviation of 0.55 credits, and a 75th percentile of 0.60 credits [+W]
  • The Detroit Promise Path effect of 1.8 credits earned after two semesters sits at the 83rd percentile of the distribution of community college intervention effects, indicating a quite large effect [+W]
  • Mean effects of community college interventions on marginal (per-semester) enrollment and credits earned decrease over time [+W]
  • Mean effects of community college interventions on cumulative credits earned increase over the first six semesters after random assignment, reaching 1.14 credits by semester 3 [+W]
  • Distributions of estimated intervention effects overstate the spread of true effects; adjusted empirical Bayes estimates reduce the spread by about 16% for cumulative semesters enrolled [+W]
  • Cross-intervention variation in community college intervention effects is substantial: for first-semester credits earned, the true-effect standard deviation (0.55 credits) exceeds the mean effect (0.48 credits) [+W]
  • Average effects on community college degree completion are small (0.9–1.7 percentage points) and benchmarking is limited by two outlier interventions with effects of roughly 16 and 18 percentage points [~W]
