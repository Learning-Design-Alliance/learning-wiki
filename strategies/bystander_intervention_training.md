# Bystander Intervention Training

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Bystander intervention training teaches community members (students, employees, soldiers) to notice problematic situations — harassment, sexual assault risk, bias incidents, bullying — interpret them as requiring action, assume responsibility, and intervene safely. Programs typically combine awareness of the bystander effect with a repertoire of intervention options (direct, distract, delegate, delay) and rehearsal through scenario practice. The approach shifts prevention responsibility from potential victims and formal authorities to the whole community.

## Design Implications

The strategy is grounded in Latané and Darley's five-step decision model: intervention fails at any of noticing, interpreting, taking responsibility, knowing what to do, or acting. Effective training targets each step explicitly, especially the interpretation and responsibility stages where pluralistic ignorance and diffusion of responsibility operate. Because intervention is a social-behavioral skill under stress, programs that include scenario rehearsal outperform awareness-only formats [Bystander training improves intervention attitudes, efficacy, and self-reported behavior.](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcop.20159) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear decision framework (e.g., the 5 Ds: Direct, Distract, Delegate, Delay, Document) so learners have procedural options, not just exhortation
- Realistic scenarios matched to the learners' actual social contexts ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
- Rehearsal of intervention scripts through [Role Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md) or discussion, since willingness to act depends on self-efficacy, not just knowledge
- Institutional follow-through: reporting channels and norms that make intervention safe and supported

#### Constraints
- One-shot awareness sessions produce attitude change that decays without booster sessions or practice [Multi-session programs show stronger and more durable effects than single sessions.](https://doi.org/10.1002/ab.21530) [~M]
- Training that emphasizes only heroic direct confrontation can increase perceived risk of intervening and reduce actual intervention among learners who fear social backlash [~M]
- Effects on observed behavior are harder to demonstrate than effects on attitudes and self-report; programs embedded in broader institutional climate change show the strongest behavioral evidence [~M]
- In high-power-difference situations (e.g., intervening with a superior), the trained scripts may not transfer without adaptation for that context [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Workshop format** (e.g., Bringing in the Bystander): multi-session, discussion- and scenario-based, common in universities
- **Norms-based campaigns** (e.g., Green Dot): combines training with visible social-norm messaging across a whole institution
- **Embedded/online modules**: shorter, scalable, but weaker without live rehearsal
- **Workplace adaptations**: focus on harassment and bias, often paired with reporting infrastructure

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults in school or campus settings, where peer norms strongly shape intervention behavior [+S]
- Whole communities rather than targeted individuals — the model depends on shifting perceived norms so intervening is seen as expected [Community-level programs show broader climate effects than individual-only training.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2011.08.012) [+M]
- Members of groups with existing prosocial motivation benefit most; learners with low initial empathy may need [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) components first [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural skill: selecting and executing a safe intervention tactic
- Attitude change: accepting responsibility and rejecting passive-bystander norms
- Self-efficacy: confidence to act under social pressure
- Community climate: establishing intervention as a shared norm

### Instructions
1. **Activate the problem** — present data and cases establishing that passive bystandership is common and harmful ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. **Teach the decision model** — explicitly cover the five steps and the barriers at each (ambiguity, diffusion of responsibility, evaluation apprehension)
3. **Model interventions** — demonstrate multiple tactics (direct, distract, delegate) so learners see that intervention need not mean confrontation
4. **Rehearse with scenarios** — practice scripts in [Role Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md) with graduated difficulty, including ambiguous situations where the problem is not obvious
5. **Plan for real contexts** — learners identify situations in their own environment and select tactics they would realistically use ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
6. **Follow up** — booster sessions, check-ins, and visible institutional support for those who intervene

## Examples
- **[Bringing in the Bystander](https://www.unh.edu/prevention-innovations-research-center/bringing-bystander)** (University of New Hampshire) — a widely evaluated multi-session campus program with scenario discussion and skill practice.
- **[Green Dot](https://www.livethegreendot.com)** — a norms-based bystander program implemented in high schools and colleges; a cluster-randomized trial found reductions in violence rates at program schools.
- **[Step Up!](https://www.stepupprogram.org)** — a prosocial behavior and bystander program used in athletics and campus settings, organized around the five decision steps and intervention tactics.
