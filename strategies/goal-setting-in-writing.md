# Goal Setting In Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 mixed, 1 against) · 6 studies (3 review, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 6 report an effect size

## Description
Goal setting in writing asks learners to establish a specific, attainable target before and during composing — for example, "include three counterarguments," "write a clear topic sentence for each paragraph," or "cut my draft by 20%." Goals can target the product (features of the final text), the process (planning, revising behaviors), or self-regulation (sustained effort, monitoring). The strategy is typically paired with [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) against the goal and teacher or peer feedback.

## Design Implications

Goal setting works because it directs attention and effort toward concrete features of text rather than the diffuse aim of "write well," and it converts writing from a knowledge-telling task into a self-regulated one [Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory: specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) [+S]. In writing specifically, goal setting is one of the most consistently effective elements of adolescent writing instruction, with effect sizes comparable to strategy instruction [Graham & Perin meta-analysis of writing instruction.](https://dww.wested.org) [+S]. Goals are most powerful when combined with [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) instruction, where learners set goals, monitor progress, and revise strategies.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Goals that are specific and observable ("include two pieces of evidence per paragraph") rather than vague ("write a good essay") [Specific goals outperform "do your best" goals.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) [+S]
- Goals matched to current skill level — moderately challenging but attainable
- A way to check progress against the goal: rubrics, checklists, or [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) during composing
- Feedback or self-evaluation that closes the loop between goal and revision
- Clear understanding of the writing purpose, audience, and genre so goals map onto quality criteria
- Self-assessment tools (rubrics, checklists) learners can apply to their own drafts
- A feedback mechanism — teacher, peer, or structured conference — tied to the stated goals
- Consistent monitoring: goals revisited at planning, drafting, and revision points

#### Constraints
- Overly difficult goals depress performance and motivation when learners lack the skills to reach them [Goal-setting theory: assigned goals must be attainable to sustain commitment.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) [-S]
- Too many simultaneous goals overload novice writers, who have limited working memory for planning while composing [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M] — limit to one or two goals per draft
- Product goals alone can encourage surface compliance (hitting counts, inserting required phrases) without improving reasoning; pairing with [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) [~M] or process goals mitigates this
- Goals imposed without learner input yield weaker effects than collaboratively or self-set goals [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]
- Vague or purely quantitative goals ("write 200 words") do not improve quality and can encourage padding [-M]
- Overly difficult goals depress motivation and self-efficacy for struggling writers; goals must be attainable at current skill levels [Specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [~M]
- Goals set *for* learners without their involvement produce weaker ownership and less goal-directed revision [~W]
- Without monitoring or feedback, stated goals are quickly abandoned during drafting [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Product goals** target text features (structure, evidence, audience awareness) — most common in genre instruction
- **Process goals** target behaviors ("spend 10 minutes planning before drafting") — central to [Self-Regulated Strategy Development](https://srisd.com) approaches
- **Self-set vs. assigned goals** — self-set goals build ownership; assigned goals are faster to deploy and safer for novices who cannot yet judge what to work on
- **Goal + rubric** — pairing goals with rubric criteria makes evaluation criteria visible and supports [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)
- Teacher-set goals for novices, shifting to learner-set goals as expertise grows
- Product goals (genre features) vs. process goals (planning, revising behaviors) [Process-focused goals outperform outcome-focused goals for learners without developed skills.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Individual goals vs. shared class goals with peer support for planning and revision
- Short-cycle goals per draft vs. longer-term goals across a writing unit

### Target Learners
- Struggling writers and students with learning disabilities, who benefit most from explicit, concrete targets [Goal setting combined with strategy instruction improved essay elements for adolescents with disabilities.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.2.250) [+S]
- Novice writers who otherwise default to knowledge-telling without a revision agenda
- More experienced writers benefit less from assigned goals and more from self-set goals tied to their own revision priorities [~W]
- Developing writers building self-regulation, since goal-directed self-monitoring is a core self-regulation skill [Self-monitoring improves self-regulated performance.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Attaining goals builds writing self-efficacy, which predicts persistence on difficult writing tasks [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Text quality: improving specific features such as organization, elaboration, or argumentation
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising as habitual composing behaviors
- Revision: giving learners a concrete agenda for evaluating and improving drafts
- Writing quality: genre-appropriate content, organization, and revision
- Self-regulated writing: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own composing process
- Motivation and self-efficacy as a writer

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the learner's current draft or writing profile to identify one or two high-leverage focus areas.
2. Co-construct a specific, challenging-but-attainable goal with the learner, phrased in terms of observable text or process features.
3. Provide a mini-lesson or model showing what the goal looks like when met ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) if available).
4. During composing, prompt learners to check progress against the goal ([Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md)).
5. After drafting, have learners self-evaluate against the goal and revise, supported by teacher or peer feedback.
6. Fade teacher-set goals toward learner-set goals as self-regulation develops.

## Examples
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)** — Graham and Harris's framework has students set goals for including genre elements (e.g., "state my opinion, give three reasons with facts") before writing persuasive essays; extensively validated across dozens of studies ([SRSD Online](https://srisd.com)).
- **Page-Voth & Graham (1999)** — middle school students given specific goals for essay content (number of supporting arguments and counterarguments) wrote essays with more and higher-quality elements than students given "do your best" goals.
- **The Writing Revolution** — Hochman and Wexler's method sequences sentence-level goals ("because/but/so" sentences) so each activity has a single explicit target before paragraph- and essay-level goals are layered on ([The Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)).
- **Graham & Perin's meta-analysis (2007)** — identified goal setting for writing, alongside summarization and collaborative writing, as one of the most effective instructional practices for adolescent writers, based on effect sizes from experimental and quasi-experimental studies.
- **Zimmerman & Kitsantas' revision studies** — students given process goals (apply one revision rule at a time) learned revision skills better than those given outcome goals ("make it better"), and self-recorded progress added further gains.
- **[The Write Tools / SRSD-based curricula](https://srsdresearch.org)** — Self-Regulated Strategy Development lessons open each genre unit by having students set goals for what their text must accomplish, then graph progress across drafts.
- **Google Docs + teacher comment threads** — teachers post the student's stated goal at the top of the draft and anchor feedback comments to it, making progress toward the goal visible across revisions.

## Description
Goal setting for writing gives learners explicit, task-specific targets — tied to the purpose, audience, and genre of the piece — that guide planning, drafting, and revision. Learners work with teachers or peers to set these goals, track progress against them, and use self-assessment and feedback to decide what to improve next. Goals can target the product (e.g., "state a counterargument in paragraph 3") or the process (e.g., "plan before drafting"), and both forms work best when they are specific and attainable rather than vague or purely quantitative [Specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals.](../claims/specific-difficult-goals-lead-to-higher-performance.md) [+S].

## Design Implications

### Instructions
1. Analyze the writing task with learners: purpose, audience, and what quality looks like in this genre ([State Objectives](../elements/state-objectives.md)).
