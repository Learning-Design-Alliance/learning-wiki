Self-Reinforcement¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (4 review, 3 theoretical, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q1–q4 · 1 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Self-reinforcement involves individuals selecting a reinforcer and rewarding themselves for reaching or exceeding a set criterion. The learner — not the teacher — administers the reward, which shifts the locus of behavioral control from external management to self-management. Rooted in Social Learning Theory and Behaviorism, it is a core component of Self-Regulated Learning: learners observe their own performance, compare it against a standard, and deliver consequences to themselves Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M].

Design Implications¶

Self-reinforcement converts reinforcement contingencies from teacher-controlled to learner-controlled, which builds a transferable self-management skill rather than dependence on external rewards. Because learners set and evaluate their own criteria, the strategy also develops self-monitoring and goal-setting habits that persist after the reinforcer is removed Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]. Involving students in selecting reinforcers supports autonomy, which strengthens intrinsic motivation over time Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear, objective, measurable goal set jointly by teacher and student (Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M] — early goals should specify what to do, not just outcomes)
  • Student selection of the reinforcer, e.g., from a menu or rank-ordered list
  • Agreed evaluation procedures so the student can judge criterion attainment independently
  • Student administration of the reinforcer upon meeting the criterion
  • Ongoing Practice opportunities on which the contingency operates

Constraints¶

  • What is reinforcing varies by age, personality, culture, and gender; a menu chosen by the teacher may hold no value for the student [-M]
  • If criteria are vague or self-judged leniently, students can reward themselves without genuine performance gains [-M]
  • Over-reliance on tangible reinforcers can undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks students already find interesting Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [~M] — prefer informational, task-based reinforcers
  • Requires students to delay gratification; very young learners or those with attention difficulties may need teacher verification before self-administration [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reinforcement menu or rank-ordering: student chooses from teacher-approved options
  • Self-administered points or tokens exchanged later, versus immediate self-rewards
  • Criterion levels can be raised progressively as performance improves, fading the contingency toward natural consequences
  • Pairing with Feedback so self-evaluation is calibrated against accurate performance information

Target Learners¶

  • Students who need help staying on task and sustaining motivation Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Learners developing self-regulation, for whom administering their own contingencies builds monitoring and evaluation skills Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Students preparing for independent adult living, where self-managed reinforcement replaces external supervision

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and attentional goals: increasing time on task and task completion
  • Self-regulation: goal setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Motivational goals: building persistence and self-efficacy through criterion attainment Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Set a goal for receiving rewards. Negotiate a clear, objective criterion with the student; prefer process-oriented criteria for novices (Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]).
  2. Select a reinforcer. Ask the student to suggest reinforcers or choose from a menu; student choice supports autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M].
  3. Determine evaluation procedures. Agree how performance will be measured and who verifies criterion attainment; provide Feedback to calibrate self-judgment.
  4. Administer the reinforcer. The student delivers the reward upon meeting the criterion, during regular Practice sessions; gradually raise criteria and fade the contingency to build durable self-management.

Related Strategies¶

  • Self-monitoring — the evaluation component on which self-reinforcement depends; learners must track performance before rewarding it
  • Goal setting — supplies the criterion that triggers self-administered reinforcement
  • Token economies — the teacher-administered counterpart; self-reinforcement transfers control of the same contingency to the learner

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — the performance context in which criteria are met and rewards earned
  • Feedback — accurate performance information keeps self-evaluation honest
  • Enhance retention and transfer — fading self-reinforcement toward natural consequences supports durable self-management

Examples¶

  • Self-managed math fluency practice: A student sets a criterion of 30 correct facts per two-minute practice sprint, self-scores, and awards five minutes of free reading time upon meeting the criterion; the criterion rises weekly.
  • Writing workshop: A student selects from a reinforcement menu (choice of next writing topic, seat location) contingent on completing a draft meeting a co-set checklist, self-evaluating against the checklist before claiming the reward.
  • Study skills intervention: A college student in a self-regulated learning course logs study sessions, compares them to a weekly goal, and self-rewards with a chosen leisure activity — building the self-management habits emphasized in Self-Regulated Learning curricula.
