# Effort-Based Praise

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Effort-based praise (also called process praise) directs feedback at the learner's effort, strategies, choices, and progress rather than at stable traits like intelligence or talent. Saying "you tried several approaches until one worked" attributes success to controllable actions; saying "you're so smart" attributes it to a fixed ability. The distinction matters because learners infer their implicit theories of intelligence from the praise they receive: trait praise pushes them toward an entity theory (ability is fixed), while process praise pushes them toward an incremental theory (ability grows with effort and strategy) [~M]. Process praise is associated with greater persistence, willingness to tackle challenges, and resilience after failure, whereas trait praise is associated with performance avoidance after setbacks [~M].

## Design Implications

Process praise works because it makes the *causal attribution* for success or failure controllable — and controllable attributions sustain motivation when difficulty rises. It aligns with the broader finding that feedback targeting the task and process levels supports learning, while feedback directed at the person adds little [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. It also supports learners' sense of autonomy and competence, both of which fuel intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Observation of the learner's actual effort, strategy use, and progress — praise must reference something specific and true
- A focus on the learning process rather than only the outcome ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
- Pairing praise with strategy guidance: "you worked hard" is most effective when followed by "here's what to try next," since effort praise alone can become empty encouragement

#### Constraints
- Effort praise directed at a failing learner ("you tried hard, but...") can signal low ability expectations and undermine motivation [~M] — effort praise works best when paired with success or with concrete strategy advice
- Generic, insincere, or indiscriminate praise loses informational value; learners discount praise they perceive as unearned [-W]
- Overuse creates praise dependence, where learners work for approval rather than learning goals [~W]
- Praising effort without addressing ineffective strategy use reinforces unproductive persistence; the target should be *effective* effort and strategy, not effort per se [~M]
- Effects on academic achievement are smaller and less consistent than effects on motivation and mindset; large-scale replications of mindset interventions show modest, heterogeneous effects [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Verbal praise in face-to-face instruction; written comments on work; rubric language that names process criteria; automated feedback in adaptive systems that praises strategy selection rather than speed or streaks
- Parent and teacher language at home and in early schooling shapes implicit theories long before formal instruction

### Target Learners
- Learners who attribute failure to fixed ability and avoid challenge after setbacks [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Novices, who benefit from process- and strategy-level feedback over person-level feedback [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- All learner levels benefit from specific process feedback, though effects are strongest when learners are forming their beliefs about ability

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivational: persistence, challenge-seeking, resilience after failure
- Metacognitive: adopting and refining learning strategies [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Long-term: incremental beliefs about ability that support sustained engagement [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Observe the learner's work closely enough to name a specific process — a strategy tried, a revision made, a misconception worked through ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
2. Attribute the outcome to that controllable process: "Your diagram helped you spot the error" rather than "You're good at this"
3. When the outcome is poor, pair acknowledgment of effort with concrete strategy guidance — never effort praise alone for failure
4. Keep praise genuine and proportionate; reserve it for processes the learner actually controlled
5. Reinforce the same attribution language in rubrics, peer feedback, and family communication so learners receive a consistent message, so the attribution carries beyond the immediate task ([Enhance Retention and Transfer](../elements/enhance-retention-and-transfer.md))

## Assessment Evidence
- Track changes in learner motivation and persistence. Measure performance improvements following effort-based praise. Assess the extent to which learners adopt a growth mindset.

## Impact
- Research demonstrates that effort-based praise enhances motivation and performance, while intelligence-based praise can undermine it (reference Dweck's research on mindset). Learners who receive effort-based praise are more likely to embrace challenges and persist through setbacks (reference research on resilience).
- The specific mechanism: telling a student they are smart when they produce a good result implicitly commits them to that same explanation when they later do poorly — if a good result means "you're smart," a poor result on the very same logic means "you're not smart" (Dweck, 2000). Effort-based praise avoids setting up this trap because it never ties the outcome to a fixed personal trait in the first place.

- Rubric and comment banks that name process criteria (e.g., NWEA and CASEL teacher language guides)

## Examples
- Instead of "You're so smart!" say "I can see you worked really hard on this, and it paid off."
- Instead of "You're a natural at this!" say "I like the way you tried different strategies until you found one that worked."
- In mathematics classrooms, praising a student's choice to draw a diagram or check their work reinforces transferable strategies, not just this answer.
- Writing conferences that name what a young writer *did* ("your revision added the detail that made the character vivid") model process attribution for peers listening in.
