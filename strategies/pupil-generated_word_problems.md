# Pupil-Generated Word Problems

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners author their own word problems — selecting quantities, operations, contexts, and constraints — and then exchange, solve, and critique one another's problems. Problem posing reverses the usual direction of instruction: instead of decoding someone else's problem, learners must construct the semantic structure themselves, which forces them to attend to how quantities, relationships, and question sentences fit together.

## Design Implications

Generating problems requires learners to analyze the deep structure of word problems rather than pattern-match surface features, and this structural awareness transfers to problem solving [Problem posing improves both posing skill and problem-solving performance.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The activity functions as a form of generative self-explanation: deciding what makes a problem solvable, what makes it hard, and what makes it realistic requires articulating relationships that passive solvers often skip [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Posing is most effective when paired with solving and critique, not used as a standalone activity.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A recently taught concept or operation to anchor the problems (posing works best when it extends, not precedes, instruction on the target skill)
- Structured prompts or constraints (e.g., "must involve two steps," "use a money context") so problems align with learning objectives
- Time and a protocol for sharing, solving, and discussing peer problems ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Unconstrained posing produces trivial or unsolvable problems; learners may write problems that rehearse the wrong skill or embed incoherent quantities [-M]
- Learners with weak basic skills often cannot pose problems at all, since posing presupposes some fluency with the operations involved [-M]
- Peer-authored problems vary widely in quality and difficulty, which can waste time or mislead if the teacher does not curate them [-W]
- Some learners are reluctant to share their problems or unmotivated to solve peers' problems without an authentic audience or purpose

#### Implementation Variability
- **Semi-structured posing:** learners complete a problem stem ("Tom has ___ marbles. He gives ___ to ___. How many…?") — lowers the load for novices
- **Transformation tasks:** learners rewrite a given problem (change the operation, add a step, make it multi-step) — a middle ground between solving and full generation
- **Pose-then-solve:** learners must solve their own problem, which catches incoherent problems and closes the loop
- **Problem-swapping gallery:** problems posted and solved by peers, with the author giving feedback on solution errors

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle school learners who have basic fluency with the target operations [+M]
- Learners who rely on surface-feature keyword strategies ("altogether means add"); posing exposes why keywords fail [~M]
- Less suitable as an introduction for novices who have not yet solved problems of the target type — pose after, not before, initial instruction [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Structural understanding of word problems: how givens, relationships, and questions combine
- Discrimination between problem types and operations (which structure demands which operation)
- Real-world connection: mapping everyday contexts onto mathematical relationships
- Communication: justifying why a problem is solvable, realistic, and appropriately difficult

### Instructions
1. Anchor the task in a recently taught concept and review one worked example, naming its structural parts ([Problem Presentation](../elements/problem-presentation.md))
2. Give posing constraints tied to the objective (operation, number of steps, context) and have learners draft one or two problems ([Application](../elements/application.md))
3. Have learners solve their own problems first to verify solvability
4. Exchange problems for peers to solve, then discuss discrepancies between intended and actual solutions ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md))
5. Curate the best problems for later practice, quizzes, or an authentic audience (another class, a class problem book)

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction classrooms** use problem-posing extensions after children solve story problems, asking them to invent new problems for the same number relationship ([Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) for Math](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md))
- A grade-4 class studying multiplication poses "shopping" problems (3 items at $1.25 each), swaps them, and the teacher selects several for the unit test — learners see their own problems become assessment items
- **Illustrative Mathematics**-style curricula include "write your own problem" tasks as lesson warm-ups, often with a stem to complete
