# Website Domain Analysis

## Description
Learners are taught the differences between .com, .gov, .org, and .edu domains to assess the reliability of websites. This is typically taught as a mini-lesson in which students learn the typical purpose and potential biases of each domain type, then apply a checklist or rubric when evaluating sources. Domain analysis is a heuristic form of source evaluation: it gives learners a fast, low-effort rule of thumb for triage before deeper evaluation.

## Design Implications

Domain analysis provides a concrete, teachable framework that supports critical literacy and reduces the cognitive load of evaluating unfamiliar web sources [chunking reduces working memory load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. However, research on civic online reasoning shows that domain-based heuristics are substantially weaker than strategies used by professional fact-checkers, who evaluate sources by leaving the page and checking what other sites say about it — so-called "lateral reading" [~S]. Domain analysis should therefore be positioned as a first-pass filter, not a sufficient evaluation strategy, and should be paired with instruction in lateral reading and corroboration.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to a variety of websites with different domain types, including counterexamples (.org advocacy sites, .com sites with strong editorial standards)
- A framework for guiding the analysis (e.g., a checklist or rubric covering author credentials, currency, evidence, and bias)
- Explicit instruction on what each domain suffix does and does not guarantee — e.g., that .org indicates a nonprofit, not neutrality, and that anyone can register a .com or .org

#### Constraints
- Domain heuristics are frequently wrong: .org sites include advocacy organizations with strong biases, and .edu sites host student pages and personal opinions [-S]
- Teaching domain analysis in isolation can create overconfidence — learners believe a suffix check settles credibility, which suppresses deeper evaluation [-M]
- Professional fact-checkers largely ignore domain cues in favor of lateral reading; students taught only vertical (on-page) evaluation, of which domain analysis is a part, perform far worse on credibility tasks [~S]
- Domain suffixes vary by country (.co.uk, .ca), complicating the heuristic for international contexts

#### Implementation Variability
- Mini-lesson followed by guided [practice](../elements/practice.md) with mixed sets of real websites
- Comparison activities in which learners contrast two sites on the same topic with different domains and different reliability
- Extended into full source-evaluation units that add lateral reading, author verification, and claim corroboration
- Delivered via [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) or embedded in an inquiry project where learners must justify source choices

### Target Learners
- Middle school, high school, and adult learners new to online research
- Novices benefit from the concrete, rule-based structure; learners with strong media literacy background may find the heuristic redundant and should move directly to lateral reading strategies [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluating the credibility and reliability of online sources
- Critical literacy: recognizing that source type signals purpose and potential bias
- Information literacy dispositions: habitual, justified source selection in research tasks

### Instructions
1. Deliver a short [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) mini-lesson defining each domain type (.com, .org, .gov, .edu) and its typical purpose, publisher, and characteristic biases.
2. Model the analysis on one or two contrasting websites, thinking aloud about what the domain does and does not tell you.
3. Provide a checklist or rubric that situates domain analysis among other criteria: author credentials, currency, evidence quality, and presence of bias.
4. Have learners [practice](../elements/practice.md) by classifying and evaluating a mixed set of real websites, then justifying their reliability judgments.
5. Extend the lesson by showing cases where the domain heuristic fails (e.g., a biased .org site), and introduce lateral reading as a stronger strategy.

## Related Strategies
- Lateral reading — the professional fact-checking strategy that research shows outperforms domain-based heuristics; natural successor to this strategy
- Checklist-based source evaluation — the broader rubric framework within which domain analysis sits

## Examples
- A middle school ELA research unit in which students evaluate paired websites on climate change — one .edu, one .org advocacy site — and must justify which source they would cite and why.
- [Common Sense Education's digital citizenship curriculum](https://www.commonsense.org/education/digital-citizenship) includes lessons on judging website credibility that incorporate domain awareness alongside author and evidence checks.
- A first-year composition course where students annotate their bibliographies with a domain analysis plus a lateral-reading note on what other sources say about each publisher.
