# Discussion Norms

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Discussion norms are explicit, co-developed or teacher-established agreements about how classroom discussion operates: how students respond to one another, how disagreement is expressed, how evidence is used, and how airtime is shared. They function as the procedural infrastructure that makes [Discussion-Based Learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md) productive rather than chaotic or dominated by a few voices.

## Design Implications

Discussion quality depends less on the topic than on the interactional rules governing it. Norms such as "build on or challenge the previous speaker's reasoning" and "support claims with evidence" shift discussion from serial answering toward accountable dialogue, which is associated with stronger reasoning and comprehension outcomes [Accountable Talk-style discussion improves reasoning and learning.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Norms also regulate the social risk of speaking: expectations that ideas are treated as revisable and that errors are treated as material for thinking increase willingness to participate, particularly for students who do not already hold high status in the classroom [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A small set (typically 3–6) of positively framed, behaviorally specific norms ("cite the text," "respond to the person before you"), not vague values ("respect each other")
- Teacher modeling and rehearsal of each norm, with public naming when norms are (or are not) followed
- Talk formats that make norms enforceable — [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md), structured roles, or talking protocols — so participation is not left to volunteering
- Periodic revisiting: norms erode without maintenance, especially in long courses

#### Constraints
- Norms imposed without student input or rationale are followed superficially and decay quickly [~M] — co-construction increases ownership, though the teacher must still hold the line on academic norms
- "Safe" norms that suppress disagreement (e.g., "only say nice things") reduce the productive conflict that drives conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [~M]
- Norms alone do not equalize participation; without status interventions, high-status students still dominate [~S] — pair norms with structured roles or equitable turn-taking protocols
- Overly rigid norms can suppress authentic inquiry and reduce discussion to ritual compliance [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Co-constructed norms (students draft and ratify) vs. teacher-set norms with rationale — the former builds ownership, the latter is faster and more reliable early on
- Domain-specific norms (mathematical: "convince us with a representation") vs. general discourse norms
- Written chart, digital pinboard, or rubric form; in online forums, norms are often embedded in posting guidelines and moderation policies

### Target Learners
- All ages, but especially adolescents and adults in seminar or [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) settings where peer-to-peer talk carries the instruction [+M]
- Multilingual learners and students unfamiliar with academic discourse conventions, who benefit from explicit norms making the "hidden rules" of discussion visible [+M]
- Mixed-status classrooms where unstructured discussion systematically advantages confident speakers [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Argumentation and reasoning: norms requiring evidence and response to others directly train [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) moves [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Academic language development: rehearsing norms gives structured practice with disciplinary registers
- Collaborative and social skills: turn-taking, active listening, productive disagreement

### Instructions
1. Introduce the purpose of norms with a rationale tied to learning, not compliance.
2. Draft 3–6 norms, ideally with student input; phrase them as observable behaviors.
3. Model and rehearse each norm explicitly, using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) to show what "building on an idea" sounds like.
4. Launch discussion in a structure that operationalizes the norms — e.g., [Accountable Talk](accountable_talk.md) sentence stems or assigned roles.
5. Monitor and publicly name norm-following ("I noticed you asked for evidence — that's our third norm").
6. Debrief periodically on how the norms are working and revise them.

## Examples
- **Accountable Talk (Institute for Learning, University of Pittsburgh)** — a published norm framework organized around accountability to the learning community, accurate knowledge, and rigorous reasoning: [https://ifl.pitt.edu](https://ifl.pitt.edu)
- **Complex Instruction (Cohen & Lotan, Stanford)** — pairs participation norms with status treatments and role assignments to equalize group talk: [https://complexinstruction.stanford.edu](https://complexinstruction.stanford.edu)
- **P4C (Philosophy for Children)** — communities of inquiry built on explicit norms of reason-giving and respectful challenge, with documented gains in reasoning measures.
