# Peer Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Peer teaching places learners in the instructional role: they explain material, answer questions, diagnose misunderstandings, and give feedback to classmates. Common formats include reciprocal tutoring, jigsaw arrangements, cross-age tutoring, and structured "teach-back" activities. The strategy exploits the fact that preparing to teach — and teaching itself — forces reorganization, elaboration, and monitoring of one's own understanding.

## Design Implications

Peer teaching benefits both parties, but the larger and more reliable gains often accrue to the *tutor*, who must retrieve, organize, and articulate knowledge [Learning by teaching improves both tutor and tutee learning.](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]. The mechanism is generative processing: expecting to teach and actually teaching drive learners to engage in elaboration and self-monitoring that passive study does not [Teaching others is a more effective generative strategy than studying for oneself.](../claims/teaching-others-generative-strategy.md) [+M]. Tutees benefit most when tutoring is structured — with protocols, role scripts, and [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) routines — rather than left to unguided interaction [Cooperative learning works when group rewards are paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient prior knowledge in the tutor to explain accurately; tutors need preparation time and source materials
- Structured roles and protocols (e.g., who explains, who questions, when to switch) rather than open-ended "help each other"
- A mechanism for catching tutor errors — instructor spot-checks, answer keys, or [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — since uncorrected misconceptions propagate
- Individual accountability so tutees cannot free-ride on the interaction [Cooperative learning works when group rewards are paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Unstructured peer tutoring can transmit errors and misconceptions with confidence; tutors rarely have the diagnostic skill to detect a tutee's faulty mental model [-M]
- Tutees with large knowledge gaps relative to the tutor benefit less — near-peer gaps work better than large ones [~M]
- Tutors with weak mastery may experience cognitive overload from managing both content and the social demands of teaching [-W]
- Gains for the tutor depend on actually explaining and responding to questions, not merely presenting; scripted recitation without interaction reduces the effect [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal tutoring**: partners alternate tutor/tutee roles, distributing the generative benefit and keeping knowledge gaps manageable
- **Jigsaw**: each learner masters one segment and teaches it to a home group; builds interdependence and accountability
- **Cross-age tutoring**: older students tutor younger ones; larger gap simplifies content for the tutor but requires careful task design
- **Teach-back / explain-to-a-peer prompts**: brief, low-preparation versions embedded in a lesson ([Learning by Teaching](../strategies/learning-by-teaching.md))
- **Teachable agents**: students teach a computer agent, which removes social pressure and guarantees the "tutee" asks predictable questions [+W]

### Target Learners
- Tutors with moderate prior knowledge — enough to explain, little enough to benefit from reorganization [Learning by teaching improves both tutor and tutee learning.](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]
- Struggling learners gain as tutors: the requirement to explain exposes gaps that re-reading conceals [+M]
- Tutees who are only moderately behind the tutor; very low-knowledge tutees need adult or expert instruction [-M]
- Tutors' self-efficacy gains can improve persistence in the domain [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and verbalizable knowledge — explaining forces integration of ideas
- Metacognitive monitoring: tutors discover gaps when their explanations fail
- Communication and self-regulation skills alongside content objectives

### Instructions
1. Prepare tutors: give them source material, an answer key, and time to rehearse the explanation ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
2. Assign structured roles and a protocol — explain, question, check, switch ([Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md))
3. Have the tutor explain the concept or procedure in their own words, using examples ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)) where helpful
4. Require the tutee to ask at least one genuine question and to solve a problem themselves — the tutor checks, but does not solve for them ([Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md))
5. Rotate roles or switch partners so both learners occupy the tutor position
6. Debrief: instructor samples explanations publicly and corrects errors, closing the quality-control loop ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — students alternate leading small-group discussions of a text using predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing roles; a well-validated peer-teaching structure for reading.
- **Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson)** — each student becomes an "expert" on one segment and teaches it to their home group; widely used in K–12 social studies and science.
- **Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS)** — a structured reciprocal tutoring program for reading and math used across elementary grades, with scripted pairing and role switching ([https://interventioncentral.org](https://www.interventioncentral.org)).
- **Physics "teach your neighbor" prompts (e.g., Peer Instruction, Mazur)** — students explain their answers to a neighbor between clicker votes; the explanation phase is where conceptual change largely occurs.
