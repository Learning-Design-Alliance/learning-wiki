# Teach Metacognitive Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 against) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), `q3`–`q4` · 1 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Teaching metacognitive strategies means directly instructing learners in how to plan an approach to a task, monitor their comprehension and progress while working, and evaluate outcomes and revise strategies afterward. It is carried out through explicit explanation, [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of expert self-regulation, guided practice with prompts, and progressive fading of support until learners apply the strategies unprompted.

## Design Implications

Metacognitive strategy instruction reliably improves achievement, with the strongest effects when instruction is explicit, sustained, and embedded in subject-matter tasks rather than delivered as generic study-skills training [~S]. Strategies must be taught with declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge — what the strategy is, how to execute it, and *when and why* to use it — because learners who know a strategy but not its applicability rarely deploy it [~M]. Modeling through [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) makes invisible self-regulatory moves (detecting confusion, checking work, revising a plan) observable and imitable.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit naming and explanation of each strategy, including when it applies (conditional knowledge)
- Expert modeling via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) so monitoring and evaluation moves are visible
- Embedded practice in authentic content tasks, not decontextualized study-skills lessons
- Prompts or checklists that cue planning, monitoring, and evaluation ([Reflection](../elements/reflection.md), [Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md))
- Gradual [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) and fading as learners internalize regulation
- A named, concrete repertoire of strategies (e.g., self-questioning, summarizing, planning before writing, checking work against criteria)
- Modeling of the strategies by the instructor, ideally via think-alouds that expose expert monitoring and decision-making
- Sustained practice embedded in authentic content tasks, with prompts or checklists that scaffold strategy use until it becomes self-initiated
- Opportunities for structured reflection (e.g., post-task evaluation: "What worked? What would I change?")

#### Constraints
- Generic, content-free "study skills" courses produce weak and non-durable effects; strategies must be taught within a discipline [~S]
- Instruction that stops at declarative knowledge ("knowing about" strategies) without guided practice does not change behavior [-M]
- Over-prompting can create dependence on external cues and add [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) burden for novices, who may juggle strategy execution and content simultaneously [~M]
- Younger learners and novices have limited capacity to benefit from sophisticated self-monitoring; effects strengthen with age and expertise [~M]
- Generic, decontextualized "learning skills" courses show weak transfer; strategies must be practiced within domain tasks to stick [-S]
- Strategy instruction adds time and cognitive load; for novices juggling heavy intrinsic load, an additional metacognitive routine can overload working memory and depress performance [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S] — introduce one strategy at a time
- Overly rigid strategy checklists can produce mechanical application without comprehension, and can impede learners who already have effective idiosyncratic approaches [~M]
- Effects fade if scaffolds are removed abruptly; prompts must be faded gradually as learners internalize the strategies [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Strategy-specific (summarizing, self-questioning, graphic organizing) vs. general regulation cycles (plan–monitor–evaluate)
- Teacher-delivered vs. reciprocal teaching formats, where learners alternate leading strategy application in groups
- Embedded prompts in learning software (e.g., adaptive hints, reflection prompts) vs. live instructor modeling
- **Embedded vs. stand-alone:** integrating strategy talk into regular lessons (reciprocal teaching in reading, "plan-monitor-evaluate" prompts in problem-based learning) versus separate study-skills modules — embedded approaches show stronger transfer
- **Prompt-based vs. modeled:** written prompts and checklists scale cheaply in online environments; live think-aloud modeling is richer but resource-intensive
- **Individual vs. collaborative:** paired or group metacognitive dialogue (e.g., reciprocal teaching roles) distributes monitoring across learners and works well for younger students

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners and low achievers gain the most, partly because they often lack strategies that stronger students acquired incidentally [~S]
- Adolescents and adults benefit more reliably than young children, whose developing self-regulation limits transfer [~M]
- Less effective for learners who already use robust strategies — instruction may be redundant or interfere with established routines [~M]
- Struggling learners and low achievers benefit most, partly because they often lack strategies that higher achievers use spontaneously [Dignath & Büttner, 2008 [+S]]
- Primary and secondary students show robust gains; effects for younger children require more scaffolding and simpler strategy repertoires [~S]
- Less beneficial for learners who already use effective strategies — instruction may be redundant or disruptive [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own study and problem solving
- Reading comprehension and writing: strategy-based text processing and revision
- Transfer: learning *how* to learn, applicable across tasks and domains
- Long-term retention through self-directed use of effective techniques such as self-testing
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own studying and problem solving
- Comprehension monitoring: detecting breakdowns in understanding while reading or listening
- Transfer and strategy selection: knowing *when* a strategy applies, not just *how* it works
- Long-term retention and study efficiency (e.g., replacing rereading with retrieval practice and spaced review)

### Instructions
1. Name and explain the strategy, including when and why it works (conditional knowledge).
2. Model it with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), verbalizing monitoring ("this doesn't make sense — I'll reread") and evaluation moves.
3. Guide joint practice on content tasks, using prompts, checklists, or [Reflection](../elements/reflection.md) frames to cue each phase.
4. Fade prompts progressively ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md)) as learners apply strategies independently.
5. Have learners evaluate strategy effectiveness and adapt it to new tasks, reinforcing conditional knowledge.

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown) — small groups rotate leading four comprehension strategies (predicting, questioning, clarifying, summarizing) with teacher modeling fading over ~20 sessions; large comprehension gains for struggling readers.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-tracking dashboards prompt learners to monitor progress and choose what to practice next, externalizing the planning and evaluation cycle.
- **[Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org)** downloadable posters — classroom materials that teach six evidence-based study strategies with when-to-use guidance.
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — small-group reading instruction where students rotate through the roles of questioning, clarifying, summarizing, and predicting; large comprehension gains in classroom studies.
- **Cognitive Apprenticeship (reading/writing workshops)** — instructors think aloud while composing or analyzing text, then coach students through the same strategies with fading support.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-tracking dashboards and hint structures prompt learners to monitor their own progress and choose when to seek help.
- **Exam wrappers** (e.g., used in physics courses at the University of Michigan) — post-exam reflection forms asking students to analyze how they prepared and what to change, feeding directly into the next study cycle.
