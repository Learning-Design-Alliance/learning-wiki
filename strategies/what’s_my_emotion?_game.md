# What’s My Emotion? Game

## Description
One learner demonstrates an emotion through body language and facial expressions — no words allowed — while the rest of the class guesses the emotion. After each guess, the actor and teacher confirm or correct, name the emotion precisely, and point to the specific cues (posture, eyebrows, movement quality) that signaled it. The game converts emotion recognition from a vocabulary exercise into a perceptual-motor activity with immediate feedback.

## Design Implications

The game pairs two mechanisms known to support learning: enacting content improves memory for it relative to passive study (the enactment effect) [+M], and guessing followed by immediate corrective feedback strengthens discrimination between similar categories [+M]. Because emotions are graded and overlapping, the feedback conversation — *why* did that look like frustration rather than anger? — is where most of the learning happens; guessing alone is not sufficient [~M]. Emotion recognition accuracy varies systematically across individuals and relates to interpersonal effectiveness [Emotion recognition accuracy varies systematically and relates to interpersonal effectiveness.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.2.203) [+M], and it is trainable through structured practice with feedback [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Space for a learner to perform in front of the group
- A reference vocabulary for emotions (e.g., an EQ Feeling Chart or the [Mood Meter](https://moodmeterapp.com) from the RULER approach) so guesses become precise words, not just "happy" or "mad"
- A classroom climate safe enough for public performance; establish norms before starting
- A teacher prepared to give specific, cue-level feedback rather than simple right/wrong verdicts

#### Constraints
- Learners who are self-conscious, have social anxiety, or are on the autism spectrum may find public performance aversive; forcing participation can produce disengagement rather than learning [-M] — offer observer roles with structured cue-spotting tasks instead
- Low-intensity or socially complex emotions (disappointment, embarrassment, envy) are poorly conveyed by novices and frequently mis-acted, muddying the discrimination being taught [~M]
- Emotion recognition is not fully universal: in-group members read expressions of their own culture more accurately than out-group expressions [-M] (Elfenbein & Ambady, 2002), so a single "correct" reading can be culturally biased
- Without a naming step and feedback, the activity degrades into entertainment with little vocabulary gain [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Small groups** instead of whole-class performance for learners uncomfortable with an audience
- **Charades variant**: act out an emotion *situation* ("you just dropped your ice cream") and have peers name both the situation and the feeling
- **Card-driven**: draw emotion cards from a chart to keep the emotion set varied and graded in difficulty
- **Reverse round**: teacher acts, class writes guesses on whiteboards — raises participation and gives the teacher formative evidence ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- **Media variant**: analyze photos or film clips of faces and bodies instead of live acting

### Target Learners
- Young learners and early-elementary students building foundational emotional vocabulary [+M]
- Learners who benefit from embodied, movement-based encoding over verbal-only instruction [+M]
- Mixed groups including multilingual learners — the nonverbal channel lowers the language barrier for participation
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for learners with acute performance anxiety or strong discomfort with social exposure [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotion identification and precise emotional vocabulary
- Reading nonverbal cues: facial expression, posture, gesture, proximity
- Perspective-taking and empathy — enacting an emotion requires simulating how it feels [Building empathy through perspective-taking activities improves prosocial outcomes.](../principles/building-empathy.md) [+M]
- Social-emotional learning competencies more broadly; well-implemented SEL programming shows measurable gains in skills, attitudes, and behavior [Social-emotional learning programs improve social skills, attitudes, and academic performance.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Set norms and vocabulary.** Review the emotion chart and agree on rules (no touching, no mocking guesses). This functions as a brief [Check-In](../principles/check-ins.md) and primes the emotion lexicon.
2. **Model a round.** The teacher acts one emotion and narrates the cues: "My shoulders slumped, my eyes looked down — that's disappointment." Modeling makes the cue-reading explicit before learners take over.
3. **Act.** A volunteer draws an emotion card and performs it silently — [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) — holding the expression long enough for everyone to observe.
4. **Guess and justify.** Peers name the emotion and, critically, state the cue that supports their guess; require evidence, not just a label.
5. **Confirm and refine.** The actor reveals the answer; the teacher resolves near-misses (frustrated vs. angry) and adds precise vocabulary. Whole-group discussion of ambiguous cases deepens the discrimination ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
6. **Rotate and debrief.** Rotate actors across several rounds, then close with a short reflection: which emotions were hardest to read, and why?
