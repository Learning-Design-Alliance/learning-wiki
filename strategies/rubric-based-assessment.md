# Rubric Based Assessment

## Description
Rubric based assessment evaluates work against explicit, task-specific criteria that describe what quality looks like at several performance levels, rather than relying on holistic judgment. The rubric is shared with learners in advance so it functions simultaneously as an evaluation tool and a description of the target performance.

## Design Implications

Rubrics make quality criteria visible, converting tacit evaluative standards into learnable content and supporting [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on specificity and use: analytic rubrics with concrete descriptors produce more reliable scoring and more useful [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) than generic or holistic ones [~M]. Rubrics also enable self- and peer-assessment, which supports self-regulated learning when learners apply criteria to their own drafts before submission [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Criteria that map to the actual learning goals, not surface features (length, formatting)
- Descriptors concrete enough that two raters would agree on a score
- Learner access to the rubric *before* the task, not only at grading time
- A follow-up opportunity to revise or apply the criteria ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Vague or overly generic descriptors ("good organization") produce unreliable scoring and do not guide improvement [-M]
- Rubrics can narrow performance toward compliance with listed criteria, reducing creativity and risk-taking in open-ended work [~M]
- Long, complex rubrics add extraneous load for novices, who may not yet parse the criteria meaningfully [~M]
- Benefits diminish for learners who already internalize quality standards; detailed criteria can be redundant for experts [The guidance advantage of structured support reverses as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Analytic rubrics** — separate criteria scored independently; best for formative feedback and skill diagnosis
- **Holistic rubrics** — single overall scale; faster scoring, weaker feedback signal
- **Single-point rubrics** — describe only the proficient level, with space for notes above and below; reduces clutter and emphasizes the target rather than grade bands
- **Co-constructed rubrics** — learners help draft criteria, deepening understanding of quality but requiring negotiation time

### Target Learners
- Novices who lack internalized models of what quality looks like in a domain [+M]
- Learners developing self-regulation, who benefit from criteria they can apply to their own work before submission [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility from teacher to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already hold accurate quality standards [The guidance advantage of structured support reverses as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Complex performance and product assessment: writing, projects, presentations, portfolios
- Self-regulated learning: internalizing evaluative criteria for self-monitoring
- Consistency and fairness in scoring across raters and sections

### Instructions
1. Define criteria directly from the learning goals of the task; drop criteria that do not reflect them.
2. Write concrete descriptors for each performance level, describing observable features rather than adjectives.
3. Share the rubric before the task and walk through an example scored with it ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame this preview).
4. Have learners self- or peer-assess a draft against the rubric before submission ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
5. Deliver [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) anchored to specific rubric criteria, and allow revision.
6. Fade rubric detail as learners gain expertise, moving toward learner-generated criteria [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility from teacher to learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **[Exemplars](https://www.exemplars.com)** — publishes performance tasks with rubrics and annotated student work samples, used in K–12 mathematics and science to make quality criteria concrete.
- **Writing portfolios in AP courses** — the College Board publishes the analytic scoring rubrics for AP English and history essays in advance; teachers score practice essays with them throughout the year.
- **Undergraduate capstone projects** — AAC&U's [VALUE rubrics](https://www.aacu.org/initiatives/value) provide validated analytic rubrics for outcomes like written communication and critical thinking, widely adapted for program-level assessment.
