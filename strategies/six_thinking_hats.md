# Six Thinking Hats

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Six Thinking Hats technique, developed by Edward de Bono, is a parallel thinking process that separates thinking into six clear functions and roles, each identified with a colored symbolic "hat": White (facts and information), Red (feelings and intuition), Black (caution and critical judgment), Yellow (benefits and optimism), Green (creativity and alternatives), and Blue (process control and metacognition). By mentally wearing and switching hats together — everyone thinking in the same mode at the same time — participants focus or redirect thoughts, conversation, or a meeting, rather than arguing from opposing positions simultaneously.

## Design Implications

The technique's core mechanism is separating modes of thought that normally compete in unstructured discussion. Adversarial discussion forces participants to defend positions while also generating ideas and evaluating them; parallel thinking sequences these modes so each gets full attention. This reduces the evaluation apprehension and production-blocking effects documented in group idea-generation research [Group brainstorming underperforms nominal groups due to blocking and evaluation apprehension.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.53.3.497) [+M]. Structured role assignment also legitimizes dissent and perspective-taking that unstructured groups suppress [Structured dissent procedures improve group decision quality compared with consensus-seeking alone.](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.752) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A facilitator (or trained self-manager in the Blue Hat role) to sequence and time the hat-switching
- Brief training in the meaning of each hat so all participants share the same code
- A real task with genuine stakes — an open problem, decision, or design question — not a topic with a single predetermined answer
- Discipline in staying within one hat at a time; the structure collapses if participants drift back to mixed-mode argument

#### Constraints
- The hat framework is a heuristic, not an evidence-validated cognitive model; treating the six categories as scientifically derived thinking types overstates the basis [-W]
- Rigid, ritualistic application adds process overhead that outweighs benefits for small groups or simple decisions [~M]
- Groups with strong status hierarchies may still defer to leaders regardless of hat, undermining the equalizing intent [-W]
- Poor fit for tasks requiring deep domain analysis where the hats' coarse categories (e.g., "facts" vs. "feelings") do not map onto the needed disciplinary reasoning [-W]
- Sustained gains depend on repeated use; one-off workshops show little durable change in group thinking habits [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Full six-hat sequence for major decisions; two- or three-hat sequences (e.g., Black then Green) for quick reviews
- Individual use as a personal idea-generation and self-critique checklist before writing or deciding
- Hats distributed as physical props or digital cards to make mode-switching concrete for younger learners
- Combined with [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) formats: hats used in preparation, adversarial structure used in the discussion itself

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult learners with enough metacognitive maturity to monitor their own thinking mode [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Groups that default to premature criticism or dominant-voice dynamics; the Black Hat slot channels critique productively [Structured dissent procedures improve group decision quality compared with consensus-seeking alone.](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.752) [+M]
- Less suitable for young children without substantial scaffolding, or for learners who already use structured reflection routines [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Perspective-taking: deliberately adopting emotional, critical, and generative stances toward the same problem [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive regulation: naming and switching thinking modes [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Collaborative decision-making and idea evaluation: separating idea generation from idea judgment

### Instructions
1. Frame the problem and appoint a Blue Hat facilitator to manage the sequence — connect to [Challenge Identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md)
2. Run the White Hat round: gather only facts, data, and gaps in knowledge
3. Run the Green Hat round: generate alternatives without evaluation, deferring all judgment
4. Run the Yellow and Black Hat rounds in sequence: benefits first, then risks — this ordering prevents premature dismissal and mirrors the generate-then-evaluate separation that improves group ideation [+M]
5. Allow a brief Red Hat round for intuitions and concerns not yet articulated
6. Close with Blue Hat synthesis: summarize, decide, or define next steps — connect to [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md) and [Decision-Making](../elements/decision-making.md)
7. Debrief the process itself, not just the outcome, so learners internalize the mode-switching habit

## Examples
- **Product development teams** — Siemens and other organizations have used Six Thinking Hats in design reviews: a Green Hat round generates feature alternatives before a Black Hat round stress-tests feasibility, keeping critique from killing ideas prematurely.
- **Business education** — MBA and management courses use the hats to structure case discussions, requiring students to produce White Hat evidence summaries before advocating positions in [case-based learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md).
- **School settings** — Teachers use simplified three-hat versions (facts / feelings / ideas) to structure pre-writing discussions, giving reluctant contributors a legitimate non-critical mode to speak in.
