# Reflective Journals

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (5 for) · 11 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 3 theoretical, 2 causal), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 11 report an effect size

## Description
A reflective journal is a recurring written record in which learners describe an experience or learning event, analyze it against concepts or goals, and derive implications for future action. Entries are typically prompted by structured frameworks (e.g., Gibbs' cycle: description → feelings → evaluation → analysis → conclusion → action plan) and may be private, shared with an instructor, or assessed. The strategy converts experience into articulated knowledge by forcing elaboration, self-monitoring, and planning into written form.

## Design Implications

Reflective journals operationalize self-regulated learning: the writing act externalizes monitoring and planning processes that otherwise remain implicit, supporting learners in tracking their own progress and strategy use [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+S]. Their effectiveness depends on prompt quality — generic "write what you think" prompts yield descriptive, low-level entries, while prompts that demand analysis against theory and explicit action planning push writing toward higher reflective levels [Prompts that require justification and theory-linking raise reflective-writing depth.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Journals also work as a formative channel: instructor responses to entries deliver process-level feedback precisely when it can shape subsequent effort [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Instructions
1. **Set purpose and audience.** Explain what the journal is for (learning, not evaluation) and who will read it; state explicitly that honest confusion is valued.
2. **Choose or introduce a framework.** Teach a reflection structure (e.g., Gibbs' cycle) and model one entry aloud so learners see analysis, not summary.
3. **Provide analytic prompts.** Each cycle, supply prompts that force theory-linking and planning ("What concept explains what happened? What will you do differently?") rather than open description.
4. **Schedule regular entries.** Brief, frequent entries (weekly or after each significant experience) outperform long, infrequent ones; pair entries with [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) or [Action planning](action_planning.md) to close the loop between reflection and next steps.
5. **Respond with process-level feedback.** Comment on strategies and reasoning, not surface features [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]; dialogue journals or [5-minute writing conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md) make this sustainable.
6. **Fade the scaffolds.** Move from supplied prompts to learner-generated questions as reflective skill grows [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
7. **Assess for depth, not compliance.** If grading is required, use a rubric on levels of reflectivity (e.g., Kember's coding scheme) applied to a sample of entries, and keep most entries low-stakes.

Reflective journaling is a writing technique in which students record their understanding, reactions, and analysis of an event, experience, or concept — encompassing thoughts and emotions, not just content.
