# Interest Inventories

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (5 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Interest inventories are surveys used to assess students' areas of interest. They can be open-ended, where students generate their own topics, or closed, where students select from a predetermined list. The results are used to form learning clubs or to select texts, problems, and project topics aligned with shared interests, connecting instruction to students' background knowledge and personal relevance.

## Design Implications

Interest inventories operationalize personal relevance, which raises task value and situational interest — both reliable predictors of engagement and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Giving students a voice in topic selection also supports autonomy, a core condition for intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. The inventory itself is only the diagnostic step; its effect on learning depends on whether the information actually changes what students read, write about, or investigate.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An inventory instrument (open-ended, closed, or mixed), administered early enough to inform planning
- Class time for completion and a system for organizing students into interest-based groups or topic tracks
- A mechanism for acting on the data: matched texts, project options, or [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) structures built around shared interests
- A developmentally appropriate inventory tool (survey, interview, drawing-and-label task for young learners)
- Time to administer and time to analyze results before planning
- Genuine willingness and curricular flexibility to incorporate findings into examples, project topics, and demonstration formats
- A follow-up mechanism (e.g., periodic [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md)) to update interests, which shift over time

#### Constraints
- Inventories that collect interests but do not change instruction produce no motivational benefit and can erode trust in future choice opportunities [~M]
- Closed inventories constrain responses to the designer's assumptions; students with interests outside the list are effectively ignored [-W]
- Interest-based grouping can drift from learning objectives when topics are chosen for passion alone; alignment checks are needed [~M]
- Some students — especially younger learners or those new to a domain — cannot yet articulate interests in an unfamiliar field, since individual interest typically develops only after sustained engagement [~M]
- Superficial personalization (swapping surface examples while keeping tasks identical) produces weak and short-lived effects; deep integration into task structure is required for measurable gains [+M]
- Interest inventories are not learning-style instruments — matching instruction to self-reported "learning styles" does not improve outcomes and should not be the purpose of the inventory [X]
- Over-reliance can narrow exposure: always routing learners to existing interests limits development of new ones; interest is partly *developed* through instruction, not merely discovered [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Open-ended formats ("What do you love doing?") maximize voice but are harder to group from; closed checklists are efficient but narrow
- Content-specific variants (reading interest inventories, science topic surveys) target a single subject; general inventories inform cross-curricular clubs
- Inventories can be re-administered periodically, since interests shift as knowledge grows
- **Whole-class surveys** at the start of a term to inform example and topic selection
- **Project-based menus** where inventory results feed into [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) or negotiated project topics
- **Reading interest inventories** (e.g., the Elementary Reading Attitude Survey) to match texts to readers
- **Individual conferences** replacing written surveys for younger learners or low-literacy contexts
- **Digital dashboards** (e.g., adaptive platforms) that continuously capture interest signals rather than one-time snapshots

### Target Learners
- Students whose engagement is low under default, non-chosen topics [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Reluctant readers and writers, for whom choice of text and topic is among the strongest motivational levers [+M]
- Students with weak academic self-concept, where interest-driven entry points can build early success experiences that support persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Elementary and secondary learners, where interest–engagement links are strongest and interests are still forming [+M]
- Less critical for advanced learners already pursuing self-defined goals, where interest is typically well established [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: establishing personal relevance before instruction
- Background knowledge activation: surfacing what students already know about their chosen topics ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
- Sustained inquiry: supporting extended reading, research, and project work within interest-based clubs
- Metacognitive goals: helping learners articulate what they enjoy and why, supporting self-knowledge and learner agency

### Instructions
1. Administer the inventory early in the term; combine open-ended prompts with a closed checklist so every student has a way to respond.
2. Have students reflect individually on their responses ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)), then discuss in pairs to surface shared interests.
3. Analyze responses and form interest-based groups or offer topic menus aligned to the same learning objectives.
4. Match materials and tasks to inventoried interests — texts, problems, or project topics — while keeping the target skills constant across groups.
5. Revisit the inventory at natural breakpoints (new unit, new term) to update groupings and topic choices.

## Related Strategies
- Choice-based reading workshops — inventories supply the data that makes book choice efficient
- Project-based learning — inventories seed authentic project topics
- Learning clubs / literature circles — the grouping structure inventories typically feed
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) — interests are a form of prior knowledge; inventories surface material that later serves as an activation hook
- [Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md) — the structural mechanism through which inventoried interests become learner decisions
- [Action Planning](action_planning.md) — learners translate interests into goals and steps

## Examples
- Reading interest inventories in elementary classrooms (e.g., the "Reading Interest-A-Lyzer" style surveys from the reading workshop tradition) used to build classroom libraries and match students to independent-reading books.
- Writing workshop conferring: a quick interest survey at the start of the year gives teachers topic hooks for student writing across the year.
- Interest-based science clubs: students complete a topic survey, then join clubs (robotics, ecology, space) that pursue the same inquiry standards through different topics.
- **[CORI (Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction)](http://www.corilearning.com)** — Guthrie and colleagues' long-running program uses interest surveys and self-selection of texts within thematic units; studies show increased reading engagement and comprehension growth [+M]
- **Elementary Reading Attitude Survey (ERAS)** — a widely used pictorial inventory (the "Garfield survey") measuring reading attitudes in primary grades, used to match students with texts
- **Relevance writing intervention** — Hulleman & Harackiewicz had high school science students write briefly about how course material related to their own lives and interests, improving interest and grades for low-expectancy students [+M]
- **Project menus in PBL classrooms** — teachers use beginning-of-term interest surveys to build [choice boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) where every option hits the same learning targets but through different interest-linked topics

# Learning Interest Inventories

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learning interest inventories are structured surveys, questionnaires, or interview protocols that surface what learners care about, how they prefer to engage with content, and what conditions help them find joy in learning. Instructors use the results to select examples, design tasks, and offer choices that connect curriculum to individual interests. The inventory is a diagnostic tool, not an intervention itself — its value comes entirely from how the information is acted on in task design.

## Design Implications

Interest inventories operationalize the link between personal relevance and motivation: connecting tasks to learners' existing interests raises task value, which in turn increases engagement and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Relevance also directs attention — learners attend more closely to material framed around their interests [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. The strongest documented effects come not from the inventory itself but from what follows it: giving learners meaningful choices and relevance-oriented tasks [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

- A developmentally appropriate inventory tool (survey, interview, drawing-and-label task for young learners)

- Genuine willingness and curricular flexibility to incorporate findings into examples, project topics, and demonstration formats
- A follow-up mechanism (e.g., periodic [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md)) to update interests, which shift over time

#### Constraints
- An inventory that is administered but not acted on can feel performative and may reduce trust; the motivational benefit depends on learners seeing their input reflected in instruction [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]

- Less critical for advanced learners already pursuing self-defined goals, where interest is typically well established [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivational goals: increasing task value, engagement, and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Affective goals: building positive attitudes toward a domain and willingness to attempt challenging work [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive goals: helping learners articulate what they enjoy and why, supporting self-knowledge and learner agency

### Instructions
1. **Administer the inventory** early in the term using a short, low-stakes survey or interview; keep questions concrete (favorite activities, topics, media, ways of showing what they know)
2. **Analyze and cluster** results to identify shared interests and useful individual differences; note interests that can anchor examples versus those that can anchor project topics
3. **Redesign tasks with choice**: build menus, project options, and demonstration formats that draw on inventoried interests — choice itself is the active ingredient [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
4. **Connect content to interests explicitly** during instruction ("we're using your survey data to pick these examples"), so learners see the connection rather than inferring it
5. **Revisit periodically** through [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) or brief re-surveys, since interests develop and change; treat the inventory as a living record, not a one-time snapshot
6. **Assess against interests** by allowing multiple demonstration formats (writing, presentation, build, performance) so learners can show competence through preferred channels [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)
