# Perspective-taking and Empathy

## Description
Perspective-taking and empathy involve the instructor deliberately considering each student's viewpoint, circumstances, and challenges, and communicating in ways that signal genuine understanding and non-judgment. In practice this means asking about students' interests and backgrounds, listening as if one might be wrong, modeling perspective-taking aloud, and using solution-focused rather than blame-focused problem-solving when difficulties arise.

## Design Implications

Teacher–student relationship quality is consistently associated with engagement, achievement, and reduced dropout, with the strongest effects for at-risk and disengaged learners [Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with higher engagement and achievement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298563) [+M]. Perspective-taking is the mechanism by which those relationships are built and repaired: it reduces attribution errors (interpreting a missed deadline as laziness rather than a caregiving obligation) and increases the accuracy of instructional decisions. Empathy must be paired with structure — warmth without clear expectations does not produce the same benefits [Affective teacher–student relationships relate to engagement and achievement, but effects vary by student characteristics.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311421793) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Regular, low-stakes opportunities to learn about individual students (interest inventories, check-ins, conferences)
- Commitment to [Active Listening](../strategies/active_listening.md) and non-judgmental interaction
- Explicit modeling of perspective-taking language ("I can see why you might read it that way…") so students acquire the practice themselves, connecting to [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)

#### Constraints
- Requires sustained time and attention; difficult to maintain under high stress, large class sizes, or limited resources [-W]
- Perspective-taking can backfire when it becomes projection — assuming one knows the student's view without checking produces misattuned responses [-W]
- Perceived as inauthentic or performative, empathy efforts can erode rather than build trust [-W]
- Empathy alone, without accountability and clear structure, does not improve outcomes and may reduce challenge [Affective teacher–student relationships relate to engagement and achievement, but effects vary by student characteristics.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311421793) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Brief structured routines (greetings at the door, two-minute check-ins) versus deeper one-on-one conferences
- Written channels (reflection journals, exit tickets) for students who do not disclose verbally
- Whole-class perspective-taking activities (structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), role reversal) versus individual relationship-building

### Target Learners
- Students who feel marginalized or at risk of disengagement benefit most from strong teacher relationships [Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with higher engagement and achievement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298563) [+M]
- Students with low academic self-confidence, for whom teacher support sustains persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Educators themselves, when perspective-taking is modeled as a professional practice rather than only directed at students

### Target Learning Goals
- Socioemotional goals: building trust, belonging, and classroom climate
- Communication goals: accurate interpretation of student behavior and needs
- Metacognitive and relational goals: students internalizing perspective-taking as a peer and professional skill

### Instructions
1. Gather perspective data early: interest inventories, brief conferences, or [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) that surface students' goals and constraints.
2. Practice [Active Listening](../strategies/active_listening.md) — paraphrase the student's position before responding, and ask clarifying questions rather than assuming intent.
3. Reframe problem-solving as joint and solution-focused: name the obstacle from the student's point of view, then negotiate next steps without blame.
4. Model perspective-taking publicly during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) ("Let me try to state the opposing view fairly first"), making the skill observable.
5. Verify your inferences — check whether your read of a student's situation is accurate before acting on it.

## Examples
- **Gehlbach et al.'s "birds of similar feathers" intervention** — teachers completed a perspective-taking exercise emphasizing similarities with students, improving teacher–student relationships and achievement: [https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000042](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000042)
- **Restorative-practice check-ins** — opening class with a brief round in which students share how they are arriving, followed by non-judgmental teacher responses.
- **Writing conferences** — a teacher asks "Walk me through what happened when you got stuck" before offering feedback, surfacing the student's process rather than judging the product.
