# Student Feedback on the Class

## Description
This strategy involves deliberately gathering students' perceptions of the class — what is helping them learn, what is confusing, what the workload and climate feel like — and using that information to adjust instruction. It is carried out through short anonymous surveys, mid-semester check-ins, exit prompts, or structured discussion, with the teacher visibly responding to what they hear. Unlike summative course evaluations collected at the end of term, the feedback is formative: it arrives while the teacher can still act on it.

## Design Implications

Student feedback functions as a formative assessment loop applied to teaching itself: it gives the instructor information about the gap between intended and experienced instruction while there is still time to close it [Feedback is most effective when it targets the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The same principle that makes feedback powerful for learners applies to teachers — specific, timely, actionable information outperforms global judgments. Mid-semester collection matters because end-of-term evaluations arrive too late to benefit the students who provided them, and early-term adjustments compound over the remaining instruction.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A low-stakes, ideally anonymous channel for feedback (paper slips, online forms, structured [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md))
- Specific, answerable questions ("What is one thing that helps you learn in this class? What is one thing that confuses you?") rather than global ratings
- Visible follow-through: the teacher reports back what they heard and what they will change, which sustains student willingness to respond honestly
- Psychological safety — students must believe criticism carries no penalty

#### Constraints
- Feedback quality degrades when students lack the pedagogical vocabulary to articulate problems; sentence starters and structured prompts partially mitigate this
- Popularity bias: students reward agreeableness and grade leniency, so raw ratings can pressure teachers toward lower standards rather than better instruction [~M]
- Low response rates or responses dominated by a few voices produce a skewed picture; anonymity and quick formats raise participation
- Feedback without visible response teaches students that their input is performative, suppressing future honesty [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mid-semester small-group analysis (SGID):** a facilitator interviews student groups about the class and reports themes to the instructor
- **Exit tickets / one-minute papers:** brief end-of-class prompts asking what was clear and what was confusing
- **Anonymous digital forms:** Google Forms, Poll Everywhere, or LMS surveys collected weekly or at unit boundaries
- **Co-designed feedback:** students help choose the questions, increasing ownership and relevance

### Target Learners
- Students in any K–12 or higher-education setting; the practice benefits them indirectly through improved instruction and directly through increased agency and voice [+W]
- Particularly valuable for students whose needs diverge from the instructional default — multilingual learners, students with [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md), or those reluctant to raise issues publicly
- Teachers are the direct users: the strategy builds a habit of treating teaching as an object of [Self-Monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improved instructional alignment: teaching decisions track what students actually experience
- Classroom climate and engagement: students perceive their perspective as valued, supporting participation and belonging
- Teacher metacognition: surfacing blind spots between intended and received instruction

### Instructions
1. Choose a moment of genuine instructional uncertainty — a new unit, a new grading period, or a change in format — when feedback is most actionable.
2. Design 2–4 specific questions with sentence starters or multiple-choice scaffolds to lower the barrier for students who struggle to articulate criticism.
3. Collect responses anonymously through a quick [Check-In](../principles/check-ins.md), exit prompt, or digital form.
4. Summarize themes and share them back to the class, stating explicitly what you will keep, change, and why — modeling how feedback is received ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) applies to the teacher here).
5. Make one visible change, then check whether students notice it in the next round of feedback, closing the loop.

## Examples
- A high-school teacher runs a two-question anonymous Google Form every Friday ("What helped your learning this week? What got in the way?") and opens Monday's class by naming one adjustment.
- University teaching centers use the Small Group Instructional Diagnosis (SGID) protocol: a neutral facilitator asks student groups to reach consensus on what supports and hinders their learning, then debriefs the instructor mid-semester.
- [Pertinent feedback structures in LMS platforms](https://www.instructure.com/canvas) such as Canvas surveys let instructors collect anonymous mid-course feedback with item-level analytics.
