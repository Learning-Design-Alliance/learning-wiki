# Home Reading Programs

## Description
Home reading programs organize reading practice outside school by supplying children with appropriately leveled books, guidance to families on how to read with children, and routines for tracking and communicating progress between home and school. Common formats include take-home book bags with matched texts, structured dialogic reading guidance, and logging systems that prompt regular reading sessions.

## Design Implications

The core mechanism is volume of successful reading practice: programs that ensure children read texts they can decode accurately build the fluency and word-recognition automaticity that frees attention for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Program impact depends heavily on text difficulty matching — books that are too hard convert practice into frustration, while too-easy texts limit growth [~M]. Guidance quality matters as much as book provision: unstructured "read for 20 minutes" mandates produce weaker effects than coaching families in specific interactional moves such as questioning, pausing, and prompting during shared reading [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A supply of decodable or leveled texts matched to each child's current reading skill
- Brief, concrete guidance for families on how to support reading (prompts, questions, when to supply a word)
- A low-burden tracking or communication routine ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) so teachers can adjust text levels
- Regular opportunities at school to apply and extend what is practiced at home ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Programs that only distribute books without family guidance show small or null effects on reading outcomes [-M]
- Texts assigned above a child's independent reading level suppress fluency growth and motivation [-S]
- Logging and compliance requirements can turn reading into a chore; extrinsic incentives for reading volume can undermine intrinsic motivation to read [~M]
- Families with limited literacy, time, or English proficiency need adapted guidance; one-size-fits-all home routines widen rather than narrow gaps [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Book-bag models** (e.g., leveled take-home collections rotated weekly) emphasize text matching
- **Dialogic reading programs** train caregivers in interactive questioning techniques, with the strongest evidence in preschool and early-elementary populations
- **Digital platforms** (e.g., [Reading Eggs](https://readingeggs.com), [Epic!](https://www.getepic.com)) supply self-leveling libraries and audio support, reducing demands on caregiver time
- **Summer reading programs** target the seasonal decline in reading skill experienced disproportionately by lower-income students

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers building decoding accuracy and fluency through high-volume successful practice [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Preschool and early-elementary children in dialogic reading programs, where caregiver interaction quality drives vocabulary growth [~S]
- Struggling readers, provided texts are tightly matched to independent level; mismatched texts are actively harmful [-S]
- Less effective for fluent adolescent readers, whose needs shift to text quality, discussion, and motivation rather than volume of easy practice [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Fluency and automatic word recognition through repeated successful reading
- Vocabulary growth through wide exposure to varied texts
- Reading motivation and habit formation — sustained voluntary reading depends on positive home reading experiences [~M]

### Instructions
1. Assess each child's independent reading level and select texts they can read with high accuracy ([Adaptive Difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md)).
2. Provide families with brief, modeled guidance on supportive reading interactions — pausing, prompting, praising, and discussing — rather than correction-heavy supervision ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md)).
3. Establish a predictable rotation routine for exchanging books and a lightweight log or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) to monitor volume and difficulty fit.
4. Adjust text levels based on check-in data and connect home reading to classroom instruction so practice reinforces taught skills ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
