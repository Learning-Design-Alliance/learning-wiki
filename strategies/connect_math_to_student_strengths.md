# Connect Math to Student Strengths

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy anchors mathematical content in contexts, tasks, and representations that align with what individual students already do well or care about — sports statistics, art and design, music, building and making, gaming, social data. Rather than teaching math as an isolated formal system, the instructor surfaces each learner's strengths and interests and uses them as entry points: contexts for problems, sources of analogies, and arenas for application. It is carried out through interest surveys, choice among problem contexts, and tasks that let students deploy a strength (visual, narrative, spatial, interpersonal) while learning the same underlying mathematics.

## Design Implications

Personal relevance raises situational interest and engagement, which supports persistence and effort in a domain many learners experience as threatening [Personalized, interest-based contexts improve performance and learning in math.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031882) [+M]. Connecting new content to existing strengths also functions as [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge, giving learners a familiar structure to map new concepts onto. The mathematics itself must remain the target; relevance is a bridge, not a substitute for [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Application](../elements/application.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine picture of student strengths and interests (surveys, conferences, observation), not assumed stereotypes
- Problem contexts that preserve the mathematical structure — the interest connection must be load-bearing, not decorative
- Multiple representations of the same concept so different strengths lead to the same learning goal ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), visual models, hands-on building)
- Opportunities for students to apply math within their strength domain ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))

#### Constraints
- Superficial "wrapper" personalization — swapping names or contexts without changing the task — yields little benefit and can distract from the mathematics [Personalized contexts help only when the relevance is substantive, not cosmetic.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031882) [~M]
- Seductive-but-irrelevant details can depress learning by diverting attention from the target concept [-M]
- Interest-based framing can reinforce fixed self-views ("I'm a visual person, not a math person") if strengths are treated as limits rather than entry points [-W]
- Over-scaffolding to a strength can block productive struggle; guidance should fade as competence grows [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Context choice boards**: students select among equivalent problems set in different interest domains ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md))
- **Strength-based modeling**: use a class's collective strengths (e.g., athletics data, music ratios) as shared problem contexts
- **Project-based application**: students identify the math inside their own strength domain and build a product around it
- **Adaptive personalization**: technology platforms select problem contexts matching learner interest profiles

### Target Learners
- Students with low math self-efficacy who disengage from decontextualized instruction; building on existing competence supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners with strong non-mathematical strengths (spatial, artistic, narrative) who benefit from concrete, familiar entry points [Block play predicts math achievement.](../claims/block-play-predicts-math-achievement.md) [+M]
- Less necessary for students already intrinsically interested in mathematics, for whom added context may be redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: mapping abstract structures onto familiar domains
- Attitudinal goals: shifting math self-concept and reducing math anxiety
- Transfer and application: recognizing mathematics in authentic situations

### Instructions
1. **Survey strengths and interests.** Collect data on what students are good at and care about; treat this as instructional information, not icebreaker content.
2. **Map the math.** Identify the target concepts and where they genuinely appear in each interest domain (ratios in music, probability in games, geometry in design).
3. **Offer contextual choice.** Present equivalent problems in multiple interest contexts via [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) so all students reach the same goal through different doors.
4. **Activate the strength.** Open with [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) or [Activation](../elements/activation.md) routines that connect the familiar domain to the new concept before formal notation.
5. **Apply authentically.** Have students use the math within their strength domain for a real purpose or audience ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).
6. **Fade the bridge.** Progressively move toward abstract, decontextualized representations so understanding transfers beyond the original interest [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **[Teaching to Interests with Adaptive Platforms](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031882)** — Walkington's algebra studies personalized word problems to students' interests (sports, music, gaming) and found improved performance and learning relative to generic contexts.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) for Math](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)** — builds instruction on the strategies students already bring to problems, treating their informal strengths as the foundation for formal mathematics.
- **[Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org)** (Stanford) — publishes open math tasks anchored in art, data science, and real-world contexts designed to connect diverse strengths to deep mathematical ideas.
