# Game-Based Incentives for Quiet

## Description
Game-Based Incentives for Quiet is a classroom management strategy that converts a behavioral expectation — a fast, quiet transition — into a game. In a typical implementation ("Silent 20"), students have 20 seconds to return to their seats and be completely silent; success moves the class forward on a shared game board (e.g., a large facsimile of *The Game of Life*), and reaching the final square earns a group reward such as a popcorn party. The mechanism is a group contingency with delayed reinforcement: the target behavior is immediate, but the payoff accumulates over days or weeks.

## Design Implications

The strategy applies operant conditioning principles to classroom routines: a clearly specified behavior, an observable criterion, and a contingent reinforcer [Behaviorist reinforcement contingencies shape observable classroom behavior.](../principles/behaviorism.md) [+S]. Its distinctive feature is the *group* contingency — the whole class earns or loses progress together — which recruits peer monitoring and positive peer pressure as an additional behavior-change mechanism [Group contingencies with individual accountability improve behavior and achievement outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]. Because the reinforcer is extrinsic and delayed, effectiveness depends on the reward remaining motivating and on students seeing a clear connection between daily behavior and board progress.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible game board and progress tracker that students can monitor themselves
- Explicitly taught expectations: what "quiet" means, the time limit, and what counts as success
- A reward that is genuinely motivating to this class, refreshed periodically to prevent satiation
- Consistent, immediate teacher feedback on whether the criterion was met ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) at the end of each timed transition)

#### Constraints
- Rewards that are not motivating for all students, or that lose novelty, cause the contingency to collapse [-M]
- Group contingencies can produce scapegoating or peer hostility toward students who repeatedly prevent the class from earning progress [-M] — mitigate with a criterion the class can realistically meet and by crediting improvement rather than perfection
- Heavy reliance on extrinsic rewards can undermine students' intrinsic motivation for self-regulated behavior over time [Extrinsic rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M] — pair the game with rationale-giving ("we quiet down quickly so we have more time for…") and fade the board as routines become habitual
- Students with self-regulation difficulties (e.g., ADHD) may be unfairly blamed when the group misses the criterion [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interdependent group contingency** (classic Silent 20): the whole class must meet the criterion — maximizes peer monitoring, highest scapegoating risk
- **Independent contingency**: each student or table earns their own track on the board — reduces peer blame, weaker collective momentum
- **Randomized criterion**: roll a die or draw a card after each transition to determine whether progress counts — unpredictability sustains attention to the routine [~M]
- **Mystery reward**: the class reward is revealed only when the board is complete, adding anticipation
- Adjust the time limit (15–60 seconds) to the actual difficulty of the transition and the class's current baseline

### Target Learners
- Late elementary and middle-grade students, who respond well to visible progress structures and collective goals [Group contingencies with individual accountability improve behavior and achievement outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]
- Classes that need to establish routines quickly at the start of a year or after a schedule disruption
- Less appropriate for older adolescents, for whom public game boards can feel infantilizing [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral: rapid, quiet, orderly transitions between activities — the procedural routines that protect instructional time
- Social: collective responsibility and peer accountability for shared classroom norms
- Not an academic learning goal; the strategy buys time and attention that other strategies convert into learning

### Instructions
1. Define the target behavior concretely ("in your seat, materials out, voice off") and demonstrate it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) the transition once with the class before starting the game).
2. Introduce the game board and reward; state the time limit and criterion explicitly.
3. Run the timed transition; at the end, give immediate, specific feedback on whether the criterion was met ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
4. Move the marker on success; keep the board publicly visible so students track their own progress.
5. Deliver the reward promptly when the final square is reached, then reset — and gradually lengthen the required streak or fade the board as the routine becomes automatic.

## Related Strategies
- [Classroom Physical Activity Breaks](classroom-physical-activity-breaks.md) — the transitions this strategy manages often follow movement breaks; the two pair naturally
- [Positive Narration](positive-narration.md) — narrating students who meet the criterion reinforces the behavior without waiting for the board

## Examples
- **"Silent 20" with a *Game of Life* board** — the canonical version: a poster-sized board on the wall, one square earned per successful 20-second transition, popcorn party at the finish.
- **Table-based variant** — each table group has a row on a chart; tables meeting the criterion color a square, reducing whole-class blame dynamics.
- **Random-criterion variant** — after each transition, the teacher draws a card that does (or does not) award a square, keeping students attentive even when the teacher's attention is elsewhere.
