# Restorative Practices Circles

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Restorative circles are structured, facilitated group dialogues in which participants sit in a circle, speak in turn (often with a talking piece), and respond to a sequence of prompts. Circles serve two main functions: *proactive* circles build community and shared norms before conflict arises, and *responsive* circles repair harm after an incident by focusing on what happened, who was affected, and what is needed to make things right — rather than on rule violation and punishment.

## Design Implications

Circles operationalize social-emotional learning through structured peer dialogue: equalized turn-taking and the talking piece slow the exchange, forcing listening before speaking and reducing the dominance effects typical of open discussion. Proactive circles function as repeated [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) that build relational trust and belonging [Students' sense of belonging predicts engagement and achievement, and targeted interventions improve both.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Responsive circles shift discipline from exclusion to accountability; school-level restorative implementation is associated with reduced suspensions, particularly when paired with empathic discipline training for staff [A brief online intervention encouraging empathic discipline reduced student suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained facilitator who can hold the process neutral and manage disclosure
- Clear norms (confidentiality, respect for the talking piece, right to pass)
- Sufficient time — circles typically need 20–45 minutes and lose integrity when rushed
- Consistency: one-off circles after incidents rarely change climate; effects depend on regular proactive use

#### Constraints
- Poorly facilitated responsive circles can re-traumate victims by forcing confrontation before readiness [-M]
- If implemented as an add-on while punitive structures remain intact, students perceive restorative processes as soft punishment and disengage [~M]
- Requires significant facilitation skill; under-trained staff default to lecture or interrogation, converting the circle into a disguised disciplinary hearing [-M]
- Whole-class circles consume instructional time; benefits must be weighed against opportunity cost in high-content-pressure courses [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Proactive community circles**: regular (e.g., weekly) check-in and values circles with no incident focus
- **Responsive/harm circles**: convened after an incident, including affected parties and sometimes families
- **Academic circles**: content-focused (e.g., discussing a text or shared error analysis) using the same protocol
- **Sentence stems and low-risk prompts** for younger or lower-trust groups, fading toward open prompts as norms solidify

### Target Learners
- K–12 students school-wide, with strongest documented effects in secondary settings with high suspension rates [A brief online intervention encouraging empathic discipline reduced student suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]
- Students from marginalized groups disproportionately affected by exclusionary discipline, for whom circles reduce removal from instruction [~M]
- Learners needing perspective-taking practice; structured dialogue improves intergroup attitudes [Empathy-building interventions improve intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: self-regulation, perspective-taking, conflict resolution
- Community: shared norms, trust, and belonging [Students' sense of belonging predicts engagement and achievement, and targeted interventions improve both.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive and moral reasoning: articulating impact, responsibility, and repair

### Instructions
1. **Prepare the space**: arrange chairs in a circle with no head position; establish or revisit norms (confidentiality, right to pass, respect the talking piece).
2. **Open with a low-risk round**: use a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) prompt so every voice is heard early and facilitator can gauge the room.
3. **Sequence prompts from description to meaning**: move from "what happened?" through "who was affected and how?" to "what is needed to repair the harm?" — never skip the affective middle stage.
4. **Use the talking piece**: only the holder speaks; this enforces [Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) and slows reactivity.
5. **Co-construct the agreement**: close by having participants state specific commitments to repair and future behavior; write them down and schedule a follow-up circle to review.
6. **Maintain the practice**: run proactive circles regularly so responsive circles draw on established trust rather than starting cold.

## Examples
- **[International Institute for Restorative Practices](https://www.iirp.edu)** — provides the widely used whole-school implementation model and facilitator training; its SaferSanerSchools program has been evaluated in randomized trials across Pittsburgh schools.
- **Oakland Unified School District** — a district-wide restorative justice program (see [https://www.ousd.org](https://www.ousd.org)) that replaced much of its suspension process with circles; documented large reductions in suspensions and racial discipline disparities.
- **Academic circles in EL Education schools** ([https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)) — "crew" circles used weekly for community-building and reflective goal-setting alongside project-based learning.
