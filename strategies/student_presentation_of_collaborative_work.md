# Student Presentation of Collaborative Work

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
After completing a collaborative problem-solving task, groups project their work and present their final responses to the class. Learners take turns explaining how they arrived at their answers and why they believe they accomplished the task of building the best answer they could. The presentation phase converts group work from a private activity into a public performance of reasoning, making each group's thinking visible and available for peer critique.

## Design Implications

Presenting group work requires learners to reconstruct and verbalize their reasoning, which functions as a form of [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and deepens conceptual understanding [Explaining material to oneself improves conceptual learning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The public, audience-facing structure also raises accountability within groups and gives the class access to multiple solution approaches for comparison — a form of contrasting cases that supports abstraction of general principles [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A display mechanism (projector, whiteboard, document camera) and completed group work samples
- Sufficient time for multiple groups to present and receive feedback
- A supportive classroom climate with norms for constructive peer feedback; some learners are hesitant to present publicly, and forced presentation without support can produce anxiety rather than learning
- Clear presentation prompts (e.g., "what did you try first?", "what changed your mind?") so groups explain *process*, not just the final answer

#### Constraints
- Presentations dominated by one group member leave others as passive observers, undermining the collaborative learning that preceded them [Group rewards and individual accountability both contribute to cooperative learning effects.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [~M] — assign rotating speaking roles to prevent this
- If every group presents the same solution to the same problem, later presentations become redundant and attention drops; vary problems or assign distinct roles/cases per group
- Weak or incorrect group solutions presented publicly can propagate misconceptions unless the instructor surfaces and addresses the error during debrief [Erroneous examples can build conceptual knowledge when errors are explicitly discussed.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [~M]
- Time cost is substantial; with large classes, use gallery walks, poster sessions, or selected spokespersons rather than full-class presentations for every group

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gallery walk / poster session** — groups post work and rotate, presenting informally to small audiences; scales to large classes
- **Selected spokesperson** — one member presents while the group fields questions, rotating roles across sessions
- **Jigsaw-style reporting** — each group presents a distinct piece of a larger problem, so the class assembles the full picture
- **Peer feedback protocols** — structured formats such as "two stars and a wish" or [Peer Review](../elements/peer-review.md) rubrics focus audience comments on reasoning quality

### Target Learners
- Learners in upper elementary through adult education; the structure adapts across grade levels by adjusting presentation length and scaffolding
- Learners who benefit from verbalizing reasoning — explaining to peers produces the self-explanation effect [Explaining material to oneself improves conceptual learning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Anxious or low-confidence presenters need graduated exposure (small-group first, then whole-class); successful presentations build self-efficacy that supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Communication and argumentation: articulating and justifying a solution to an audience
- Consolidation: re-explaining one's own reasoning strengthens conceptual understanding [Explaining material to oneself improves conceptual learning.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Metacognitive reflection: reconstructing the problem-solving process, including false starts and revisions
- Evaluation: comparing alternative solution approaches across groups

### Instructions
1. Organize learners into small groups and assign a collaborative problem-solving task ([Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md))
2. Circulate during group work, noting which groups' approaches will make productive presentations
3. Have each group project or post its work and present its solution, explaining *how* and *why*, not just the answer ([Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md))
4. Structure audience questions and peer feedback against clear criteria ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. Debrief as a class: compare approaches, surface errors respectfully, and highlight transferable strategies
6. Close with brief individual reflection on what the group would do differently ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

## Related Strategies
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — a lower-stakes precursor that builds the same explain-to-peers skill in pairs before whole-class presentation
- [Peer Instruction](peer-instruction.md) — pairs the public presentation of reasoning with immediate peer evaluation
- [Gallery Walk](gallery-walk.md) — a scalable variant that replaces whole-class presentation with rotating small-audience feedback

## Examples
- A 6th-grade science class completes a collaborative investigation, then each group presents its findings — the steps taken, challenges faced, and how conclusions were reached — while classmates question the evidence
- In a complex-instruction mathematics classroom (Cohen's [Cooperative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) design), group reporters present solutions at the document camera while the teacher holds groups accountable for every member's ability to explain the work
- University engineering courses using project-based learning (e.g., [PBL at Aalborg University](https://www.en.aau.dk/about-aau/profile/profile/education/pbl)) culminate in group presentations of designs to peers and external reviewers
