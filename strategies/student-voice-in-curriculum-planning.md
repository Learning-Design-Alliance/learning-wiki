# Student Voice In Curriculum Planning

## Description
Student voice in curriculum planning means learners participate in substantive decisions about the content, sequence, pacing, or assessment of a course or unit — not merely offering feedback on a plan already made. It ranges from structured consultation (surveys, focus groups that shape revisions) to genuine co-design, where students negotiate topics, projects, or assessment criteria alongside the instructor. The defining feature is that student input has *consequential* influence; token consultation without visible change undermines the mechanism.

## Design Implications

Giving learners authentic decision rights increases ownership and intrinsic motivation, which in turn supports engagement and persistence [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. The effect depends on the input being consequential and on learners having enough context to contribute meaningfully — voice without information or power produces cynicism rather than engagement.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine decision space: the instructor must be willing to change the plan in response to student input, and to say so explicitly when input cannot be acted on
- Scaffolding for participation: students need exposure to the goals and constraints of the curriculum before they can contribute usefully ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines)
- Transparent feedback loops: documenting what was heard, what changed, and why — closing the loop is what distinguishes voice from survey theater
- Structures that surface diverse perspectives, not just the most confident voices ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) protocols, anonymous input channels)

#### Constraints
- Tokenistic consultation — asking for input that is then ignored — actively damages trust and motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]
- Students without prior knowledge of a domain may prefer what feels familiar or easy, so unstructured voice can narrow the curriculum away from essential but unfamiliar content [~M]
- Co-design increases instructor preparation time substantially; under-resourced implementations tend to revert to tokenism
- In high-stakes standardized contexts, the negotiable space may be too small for meaningful voice, making the exercise feel hollow

#### Implementation Variability
- **Consultation model**: instructor retains design authority; students advise on pacing, examples, and topic emphasis
- **Co-design model**: students co-construct units, project briefs, or assessment rubrics with the instructor
- **Negotiated curriculum**: a fixed core with student-selected extensions ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) — a lower-cost entry point
- **Partnership programs**: institution-level initiatives where students work with faculty on course redesign (e.g., Students as Partners programs)

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult learners, who have the metacognitive maturity to reason about curriculum trade-offs [~M]
- Disengaged or marginalized students, for whom consequential voice can restore a sense of belonging and agency [~W]
- Less appropriate for complete novices in tightly sequenced foundational skills, where learner-selected content can omit prerequisites [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: planning and evaluating one's own learning trajectory
- Motivation and engagement: ownership of learning goals
- Civic and participatory competence: practicing deliberation and shared decision-making
- Content goals are served indirectly — voice is a motivational lever, not a content-delivery method

### Instructions
1. **Frame the negotiable space.** Explicitly tell students what is fixed (required outcomes, standards) and what is open (sequence, topics, assessment formats, examples).
2. **Gather input with structure.** Use [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines, anonymous surveys, or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) protocols so quieter students contribute; ask students to justify proposals against the learning goals.
3. **Negotiate and decide visibly.** Discuss trade-offs with students, make the decision, and record what changed and why.
4. **Build voice into assessment.** Involve students in drafting or selecting rubric criteria and in [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) design, so evaluation itself reflects agreed goals.
5. **Close the loop repeatedly.** At each unit boundary, revisit what worked, using [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) data alongside student reflection.

## Examples
- **Students as Partners (International)** — a global movement documented by Cook-Sather and colleagues in which undergraduates co-design courses and curriculum with faculty; many institutions (e.g., McMaster's Student Partners Program) run formal partnership programs.
- **Negotiated curriculum in project-based schools** — schools such as those in the Big Picture Learning network (https://www.bigpicture.org) build each student's learning plan around student-identified interests, negotiated with advisors.
- **Unit-level co-design** — a science teacher presents the required standards for a unit, then students vote on the phenomenon or context through which the standards are taught and help draft the project rubric.
