# Dialogic Facilitation of Student Resources

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
When students spontaneously invoke an everyday experience or example during a whole-class science discussion (e.g., a student wonders whether a fictional superhero's spider bite transferred spider genes), the productiveness of that resource depends less on the example itself than on how the teacher positions the class around it. In an **authoritative** pattern, the teacher treats the student as a "listener" — someone posing a question to be answered — and quickly supplies the canonical explanation, closing down exploration of the student's underlying reasoning. In a **dialogic** pattern, the teacher instead positions the student (and their peers) as "sources" — accountable holders of information whose contribution is elaborated, tested, and responded to by other students rather than immediately validated or corrected by the teacher. This strategy names and operationalizes the dialogic pattern as a repeatable facilitation move.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A whole-class discussion format (not small-group or individual work) where a critical mass of students can respond to one another's contributions
- A teacher willing to let scientific complexity and disagreement stand temporarily unresolved, rather than closing each exchange with an authoritative answer
#### Constraints
- Evidence comes from a single classroom's genetics unit; whether the pattern holds for topics with less everyday-experience overlap (e.g., more abstract or counter-intuitive content) is untested
- Requires the teacher to give up some of the pacing efficiency of direct correction — dialogic sequences in the source study took visibly longer than authoritative ones
#### Implementation Variability
- Can be applied opportunistically (whenever a student volunteers an everyday example) or built into lesson planning by deliberately prompting students to bring in experiences before a topic is formally introduced

### Target Learners
- Whole classes engaged in discussion-based science instruction where students already have relevant everyday experiences or ideas to draw on
- Link to sub-claim: [Positioning students as sources increases productive participation in science discourse](../claims/positioning-students-as-sources-increases-productive-participation-in-science-discourse.md)

### Target Learning Goals
- Supporting students in externalizing, testing, and refining their own conceptual reasoning through argument and counter-argument, rather than through direct correction alone
- Link to sub-claim: [Positioning students as sources increases productive participation in science discourse](../claims/positioning-students-as-sources-increases-productive-participation-in-science-discourse.md)

### Instructions
1. When a student invokes a personal experience, media reference, or other everyday resource relevant to the topic, resist immediately validating or correcting it.
2. Ask open-ended questions that invite the student to elaborate their own reasoning (e.g., "What made you think of that?") rather than closed questions seeking a right/wrong judgment.
3. Explicitly invite other students to respond to the contribution — agree, disagree, extend, or complicate it — before offering any teacher framing. Use [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md).
4. Acknowledge competing conceptual framings that emerge as legitimate and worth holding simultaneously, rather than immediately collapsing them into the canonical account.
5. When summarizing, foreground the range of student perspectives raised rather than replacing them with a single authorized version.

## Examples
- A 9th-grade genetics unit in a Norwegian classroom in which a student's reference to a soccer player's hair loss (Wayne Rooney) was used to explore heredity versus environment, with the teacher inviting peers to respond to and extend the reasoning rather than supplying an immediate answer.
