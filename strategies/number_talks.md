# Number Talks

## Description
A Number Talk is a 10–15 minute routine in which the teacher poses a computation problem (e.g., 18 × 5), students solve it mentally, and the teacher records and juxtaposes multiple student strategies. The goal is not the answer but the reasoning: students articulate, compare, and evaluate different solution paths, building number sense and flexible mental computation.

## Design Implications

Number Talks operationalize [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) in mathematics: students generate and explain strategies rather than reproduce a taught procedure, and explaining one's reasoning to peers improves conceptual understanding [Explaining reasoning improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Publicly recording several strategies creates contrasting cases that help students abstract underlying properties (e.g., the distributive property) rather than memorizing one method [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. Because problems are solved mentally, the routine also constrains working-memory load by design — problems are chosen so that efficient decomposition or transformation strategies beat rote procedures [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Carefully sequenced problems that make target strategies likely to emerge (e.g., problems with friendly numbers)
- A classroom culture where errors are treated as sites of learning and students feel safe offering partial ideas
- Teacher facility with anticipating student strategies and representing them clearly (open number lines, arrays, partial products)
- A consistent protocol: silent think time, signal when ready, share, teacher records and names strategies

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends heavily on teacher facilitation skill; poorly facilitated talks collapse into answer-checking or a single student dominating [-M]
- If the teacher steers toward one "right" strategy, the routine loses its contrasting-case value and becomes [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) in disguise [~M]
- Large classes and short periods compress discussion; without adequate think time, only fast students participate [-M]
- Less suitable for introducing entirely new content — students need enough prior knowledge to generate at least one strategy [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Problem strings**: sequences of related problems (e.g., 2 × 15, 4 × 15, 8 × 15) that highlight a property
- **Dot talks / quick images**: briefly shown dot arrangements or ten-frames for early grades, surfacing subitizing and decomposition
- **Fraction and decimal talks**: same protocol applied to rational number for upper grades
- **Student-led talks**: older students facilitate, deepening [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) demands

### Target Learners
- K–8 students building number sense; the routine scales to high school with fractions, integers, and algebraic expressions
- Students who rely on memorized procedures benefit from seeing flexible, meaning-based strategies [~M]
- Multilingual learners benefit from the visual recording of strategies, which anchors oral explanation

### Target Learning Goals
- Mental computation fluency grounded in place value and properties
- Conceptual understanding of operations and number relationships
- Mathematical communication and strategy comparison [Explaining reasoning improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Select and sequence a problem** aligned to a target strategy; anticipate 2–4 likely solution paths.
2. **Pose the problem and allow silent think time**; students signal readiness with a quiet hand signal.
3. **Collect answers** first, deferring evaluation, so discussion focuses on method rather than correctness.
4. **Elicit and record strategies** publicly, naming each (e.g., "making tens," "partial products") — this is [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) structured around [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of reasoning.
5. **Facilitate comparison**: ask students how strategies are alike and different, and which is efficient for this problem — the teacher acts as [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) rather than evaluator.
6. **Close by connecting** the strategies to the underlying property or big idea, and note which students used which strategies to inform tomorrow's problem choice ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) for learning).

## Examples
- A Grade 4 class solves 18 × 5 mentally; the teacher records 10 × 5 + 8 × 5, 20 × 5 − 2 × 5, and repeated addition on the board, then asks which strategies would work for 19 × 5.
