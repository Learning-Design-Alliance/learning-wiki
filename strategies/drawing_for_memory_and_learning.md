# Drawing for Memory and Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Drawing for memory and learning asks learners to create their own pictures of to-be-learned content — sketching a concept, process, or relationship — rather than only reading, writing, or viewing provided images. The learner must translate verbal information into a spatial, pictorial representation, which requires elaborative processing of meaning. The strategy is distinct from being shown a diagram: the generative act of drawing is what drives the benefit.

## Design Implications

Drawing combines verbal and visual codes and forces generative processing, producing a reliable memory advantage over writing definitions or listing properties — the "drawing effect" [~S]. The effect is robust across materials, ages, and drawing skill, but it is strongest for concrete, imageable content and when learners draw from meaning rather than copying [~S]. Because drawing consumes working memory, it can hurt learning when the material is complex or the drawing task is poorly constrained [Drawing imposes extraneous load when content is complex.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content that can be meaningfully depicted (objects, processes, spatial relations, causal chains)
- Sufficient time — drawing is slower than writing, so tasks must budget for it
- Clear prompts specifying *what* to depict (e.g., "draw how X causes Y"), not just "draw this"
- For complex content: partial templates, provided elements, or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) that scaffold what to draw

#### Constraints
- Drawing abstract, non-imageable content (e.g., abstract definitions, syntax rules) yields little or no benefit and can impair performance by adding extraneous load [~M]
- Copying or tracing provided images does not produce the effect — the benefit comes from generative translation, not the motor act [~S]
- Under severe time pressure, drawing can underperform writing because learners spend effort on the picture rather than the meaning [-M]
- For learners already using effective elaborative strategies, drawing may add little beyond [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)-style processing [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Free-form sketching vs. structured drawing (fill in a partially drawn diagram, connect provided elements)
- Drawing from text vs. drawing from observation vs. drawing a predicted process
- Individual drawing vs. collaborative drawing with discussion of what to include
- Digital sketch tools (e.g., whiteboard apps) vs. paper — evidence suggests the effect holds across media [~W]

### Target Learners
- Learners across a wide age range, including young children and older adults — the effect is unusually age-robust [~S]
- Novices studying concrete science and vocabulary content
- Learners with weaker verbal encoding who benefit from a second representational channel [Drawing recruits dual verbal and visual codes.](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention of concrete facts, vocabulary, and labeled structures
- Understanding of processes and causal sequences (e.g., life cycles, mechanisms)
- Integration of verbal and pictorial representations of the same content

### Instructions
1. Present the to-be-learned content in verbal form (text, explanation, or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) source material).
2. Prompt learners to draw a picture that captures the *meaning* — instruct them to depict relationships and processes, not decorate.
3. Require learners to label or briefly explain their drawing, connecting pictorial elements back to the verbal content [Requiring explanation of the drawing deepens processing.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
4. For complex material, provide a partial template or key elements so drawing effort goes to meaning rather than composition.
5. Follow with retrieval practice: learners redraw from memory or explain their drawing without looking.

## Related Strategies
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — a lighter-weight generative encoding strategy; drawing is the pictorial extension
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) — drawing from memory of prior experience combines both strategies
- [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) — pairing drawn representations with verbal explanation compounds the generative benefit

## Examples
- **Wammes et al.'s drawing paradigm** — participants studied word lists by drawing each item versus writing it; drawing produced consistently higher free recall and recognition across dozens of experiments ([Wammes, Meade & Fernandes, 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-016-1113-3)).
- **Fiorella & Mayer's generative drawing** — students reading about how a bicycle pump works who drew annotated diagrams of each step outperformed students who only read or studied provided diagrams.
- **Science sketching curricula** — programs such as the [Science Sketches](https://www.science-sketches.org) initiative and university "sketch your science" activities ask students to draw and narrate scientific concepts for peers, combining drawing with explanation.
