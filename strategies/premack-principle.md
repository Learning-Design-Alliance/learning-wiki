# Premack Principle

## Description
The Premack Principle states that a more probable behavior can reinforce a less probable behavior: access to an activity the learner already prefers is made contingent on completing a task the learner avoids. In practice, the instructor specifies an "if–then" contingency — "First finish your worksheet, then you may use the art station" — so that the preferred activity functions as a reinforcer for the target task. It derives from operant conditioning and the behavioral analysis of reinforcement [Premack, D. (1965)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022195).

## Design Implications

The principle works because response probability itself predicts reinforcement value: behaviors learners choose freely are effective rewards for behaviors they avoid [+M]. It converts abstract reward systems into concrete, activity-based contingencies that require no tokens or points, making it one of the simplest applied behavior techniques. Its effectiveness depends on the "then" activity genuinely being high-probability for the *specific* learner at that moment — preference is individual and shifts over time, so contingencies must be monitored and adjusted [~M]. Because it is an extrinsic contingency, overuse can undermine interest in the target activity itself [Rewards perceived as controlling can reduce intrinsic motivation for the target activity.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A reliably identified high-probability activity (observed via preference assessment or free-choice data)
- A clear, observable criterion for completing the low-probability task
- Immediate or near-immediate access to the preferred activity contingent on completion
- Consistent follow-through — the contingency must not be honored when the criterion is unmet

#### Constraints
- If the "then" activity is not actually preferred in that moment, the contingency loses reinforcing power [-M]
- Satiation erodes effectiveness: unlimited access to the preferred activity outside the contingency removes its value [-M]
- Emphasizing the contingency can shift learners' attribution of effort to the reward, reducing spontaneous engagement with the target task once rewards stop [~M]
- Poorly specified criteria invite negotiation and bargaining, which can function as escape from the task [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **First/then schedules** — verbal or visual "first X, then Y" boards, common in early childhood and special education
- **Grandma's rule** — home/classroom phrasing that frames the preferred activity as the natural next step rather than a bribe
- **Activity schedules** — sequences in which several low-probability tasks are chained before a single preferred activity
- **Learner-selected reinforcers** — students choose from a menu of preferred activities, increasing perceived [autonomy](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) within the contingency

### Target Learners
- Young children, for whom activity-based contingencies are more concrete than points or grades [+M]
- Learners with autism or intellectual disabilities, where first/then visual contingencies are a core component of structured teaching approaches [+M]
- Reluctant writers, readers, or practicers who avoid specific high-effort tasks but have identifiable preferred activities
- Less appropriate for learners already intrinsically motivated by the task, where the contingency adds no value and may crowd out existing interest [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral regulation: increasing time on task, task completion, and transitions
- Fluency building: sustaining [practice](../elements/practice.md) on repetitive skill work (math facts, decoding, instrument practice)
- Not well suited to conceptual understanding or creative production, where quality depends on engagement rather than compliance

### Instructions
1. Identify the low-probability target behavior and define a clear completion criterion (observable, countable).
2. Conduct a brief preference observation to confirm the high-probability activity is currently preferred.
3. State the contingency before the task begins, using first/then language: "First ___, then ___."
4. Deliver the preferred activity immediately upon meeting the criterion; withhold it if the criterion is not met, without extended negotiation.
5. Monitor response rates; rotate or refresh preferred activities to prevent satiation.
6. Fade the contingency as task engagement becomes self-sustaining, shifting toward naturally occurring consequences and [autonomy](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) support.

## Related Strategies
- [Token economies](../strategies/token_economy.md) — generalize the same contingency structure using symbolic reinforcers exchangeable for preferred activities
- [Behavior contracts](../strategies/behavior_contracts.md) — formalize the if–then contingency in writing with learner input
- [Choice boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) — embed learner choice into the reinforcement menu, softening the controlling aspect of the contingency

## Examples
- **Early literacy block**: students complete 15 minutes of decodable text practice before accessing the classroom reading corner with high-interest books — using preferred reading as the reinforcer for skill-focused reading.
- **TEACCH structured teaching**: visual first/then schedules pair non-preferred work tasks with preferred sensory or leisure activities, a standard component of structured classrooms for learners with autism.
- **Instrument practice**: a teacher structures lessons so a student's preferred pop piece is rehearsed only after completing technique drills.
