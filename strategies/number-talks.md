# Number Talks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A Number Talk is a 10–15 minute routine in which the teacher poses a computation problem — typically one with multiple solution paths — and students solve it mentally, then share and defend their strategies while the teacher records each method on the board. The goal is not the answer but the reasoning: students compare strategies, evaluate efficiency, and build number sense through structured [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md). The teacher acts as facilitator and scribe, not evaluator, using questioning to elicit and connect student thinking.

## Design Implications

Number Talks operationalize [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and [Cognitive Activation](../principles/cognitive-activation.md) by requiring students to generate and justify solution methods rather than reproduce a taught procedure. Mental computation removes paper-and-pencil supports, forcing attention to number relationships and place value rather than algorithm execution [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M] — well-chosen numbers let students decompose and recombine quantities within working memory limits. Publicly comparing multiple strategies supports abstraction of underlying properties (commutativity, distributivity) [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Problems deliberately selected to invite multiple efficient strategies (e.g., 16 × 25, 198 + 47)
- A classroom norm that errors are sites of learning and that all strategies are recorded before evaluation
- Teacher fluency in notation for recording strategies (open number lines, arrays, partial products) and in neutral facilitation questions ("Who can explain why that works?")
- Consistent routine structure: problem posed → silent think time → signal when ready → strategies shared and annotated

#### Constraints
- Fails when the teacher steers toward one "correct" method, converting the discourse into recitation [-M] — premature evaluation suppresses strategy generation
- Problems requiring single-step recall or with only one viable path produce no strategy comparison and collapse into answer-checking [-M]
- Dominant voices can crowd out reasoning from struggling students; without turn-and-talk or hand-signal wait structures, participation narrows [-W]
- Students with very weak fact fluency may spend all capacity on retrieval rather than strategy, gaining little until fluency is supported [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **String problems**: a sequence of related problems (e.g., 2 × 15, 4 × 15, 8 × 15) that surfaces a pattern across talks
- **Which One Doesn't Belong? / dot talks**: subitizing variants for early grades using dot images instead of computation
- **Fraction and decimal talks**: same structure applied to rational number reasoning in upper grades
- **Written follow-up**: students record a peer's strategy in their own words, adding an [articulation](../elements/articulation.md) step

### Target Learners
- K–8 students developing number sense and computational fluency; strongest evidence base is elementary mathematics [+M]
- Students who rely on rote procedures without understanding place value or properties — the strategy comparison exposes why procedures work
- Less effective as-is for advanced students who see only one method; give them the role of evaluating efficiency or proving why a strategy works [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of arithmetic properties and place value
- Procedural flexibility: selecting among strategies based on the numbers involved
- Mathematical discourse practices: explaining, justifying, and critiquing reasoning

### Instructions
1. Select a problem with multiple accessible solution paths and anticipate 3–4 strategies students will use.
2. Pose the problem and give silent think time; students signal readiness with a quiet hand signal.
3. Collect answers first (all recorded, none confirmed), then invite students to explain strategies while the teacher annotates each on the board — a form of [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) performed by students rather than the expert.
4. Facilitate comparison: ask which strategies are related, which are efficient for this problem, and whether a strategy would always work.
5. Close by naming the mathematical property or relationship the strategies share, connecting to prior talks.

## Examples
- **Parrino's original routine** — Number Talks developed by Kathy Parrino (Parrino, 1991) as a daily warm-up in her California classroom; the model later popularized by Sherry Parrish's *Number Talks* (2010), which includes grade-banded problem strings and facilitation video.
- **[Which One Doesn't Belong?](https://wodb.ca)** — a free image-based variant where students justify which of four numbers or shapes doesn't belong; every answer is defensible, maximizing strategy discourse.
- **[Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org)** — K–5 curriculum embedding number-talk-style warm-up routines ("Section Level Launches") before problem-based lessons.
