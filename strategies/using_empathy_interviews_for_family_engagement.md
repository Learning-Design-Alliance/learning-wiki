# Using Empathy Interviews for Family Engagement

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Empathy interviews are targeted, one-on-one conversations with a small, purposive sample of family members to learn from their lived experiences of schooling. Borrowed from human-centered design practice (notably the [Stanford d.school Bootcamp Bootleg](https://dschool.stanford.edu/resources)), the method prioritizes open-ended "tell me about a time…" questions over surveys or checklists, letting interviewees define what matters. In school settings, the collected stories drive district and school decisions, build trust, and strengthen family–school relationships — the listening itself is an engagement act, not just a data-collection step.

## Design Implications

Empathy interviews work because they position families as experts on their own experience rather than as passive recipients of school decisions; this stance shift is what distinguishes them from conventional parent surveys or town halls. The quality of what is learned depends almost entirely on interviewer discipline: withholding judgment, following up on stories rather than steering them, and probing for specific episodes instead of opinions [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. Because the sample is small and purposive, findings should be treated as formative insight for design decisions, not as generalizable needs assessments.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear purpose: which decision or design problem will these stories inform?
- Interviewers trained in open-ended questioning and non-judgmental listening ([Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md))
- Purposive sampling that reaches families beyond the usual engaged voices — multilingual families, families of students who are struggling, families who have disengaged
- A translation and interpretation plan so language is not a barrier to participation
- A closed loop: participants and the wider community must see how their input shaped decisions, or trust erodes

#### Constraints
- Small, self-selected samples overrepresent accessible voices; findings from ten interviews cannot stand in for a representative needs assessment [-W]
- Interviews conducted by staff with prior adversarial contact with a family (e.g., truancy calls) can suppress candor [~M]
- Extractive use — collecting stories without visible follow-through — damages trust more than never asking [-M]
- Poorly framed questions ("Don't you agree that…?") elicit compliance rather than experience [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Design-cycle mode:** interviews feed a redesign team (e.g., a [Collaborative Inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md) cycle) that prototypes responses
- **Relationship-first mode:** interviews are conducted purely to build relationships, with no decision attached — common at the start of a school year
- **Student-inclusive mode:** parallel interviews with students triangulate family accounts
- **Peer-interviewer mode:** trained family members interview their peers, increasing candor and reach

### Target Learners
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- School and district leaders making decisions that affect family experience (enrollment, communication, discipline, family engagement programming)
- Teachers seeking to understand individual families at the start of a year or intervention
- Family and community liaisons building outreach capacity
- The families themselves benefit relationally: being heard by school staff supports a sense of belonging that predicts engagement [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Holistic understanding of community needs, values, and barriers from families' own frames of reference
- Empathic perspective-taking by educators [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Trust and relational capital between families and schools
- Evidence for equity-centered design decisions

### Instructions
1. **Define the design question.** Name the decision the interviews will inform (e.g., "Why do families of our multilingual learners rarely attend conferences?").
2. **Recruit purposively.** Identify 5–15 families across relevant segments; use trusted intermediaries (community organizations, liaisons) for introductions.
3. **Prepare open-ended questions.** Draft 5–8 story-eliciting prompts ("Tell me about a time school communication worked well for you") using careful [Question Formulation](../elements/question-formulation.md); pilot them with a colleague.
4. **Interview with discipline.** Meet in a comfortable setting (home, community space, or phone); open with a warm [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md); ask "why" and "tell me more" follow-ups; avoid defending the school or problem-solving mid-interview.
5. **Capture stories, not verdicts.** Record (with consent) or take verbatim notes; preserve quotes and specific episodes.
6. **Synthesize collectively.** Debrief as a team, cluster themes, and identify surprises and tensions rather than averaging responses.
7. **Close the loop.** Report back to participants and the community on what was heard and what will change; repeat interviews to check whether changes landed.

- **The Learning Accelerator** documents districts using empathy interviews to redesign family communication, interviewing 8–12 families per design cycle and reporting themes back through community meetings ([learningaccelerator.org](https://learningaccelerator.org)).
- **Stanford d.school's K12 Lab** trains educators in interview techniques as part of design challenges, with school teams interviewing students and families before prototyping solutions ([dschool.stanford.edu](https://dschool.stanford.edu)).
- **Home-visit programs** such as the Parent Teacher Home Visits model ([pthvp.org](https://pthvp.org)) use relational, story-first conversation structures closely aligned with empathy interviewing.
