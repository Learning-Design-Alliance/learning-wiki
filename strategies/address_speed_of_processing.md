# Address Speed of Processing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Processing speed is the pace at which a learner takes in information, makes sense of it, and begins to respond. Addressing speed of processing means diagnosing where pace — not knowledge or reasoning — is the bottleneck, then adjusting instruction: slowing and structuring input (guided notes, advance organizers, chunked content), extending think-time and response windows, and offering alternative formats for demonstrating knowledge. The goal is to decouple *speed of processing* from *depth of learning*, so that assessment reflects understanding rather than rate.

## Design Implications

Processing speed is a distinct cognitive capacity that develops over time and correlates with, but is not identical to, intelligence and working memory [Kail & Salthouse, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-6918(94)90003-5) [+M]. Students with slow processing speed are not less capable; they are at risk of being underestimated when timed formats dominate instruction and assessment. Reducing extraneous processing demands — through [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and clear structure — frees limited capacity for the actual learning task [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Awareness of which students face processing-speed challenges (from assessment data, IEPs/504 plans, or observation of unfinished work despite strong reasoning)
- Instructional materials pre-structured for reduced processing load: guided notes, [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), chunked text
- Deliberate wait-time routines (e.g., 5+ seconds after questions, extended time on tasks)
- Multiple response formats so speed of writing or speaking does not gate expression

#### Constraints
- Extended time and reduced pacing can reduce challenge for students who process quickly, producing disengagement [~M] — pair supports with extension tasks rather than uniform slowdown
- Over-scaffolding (e.g., always providing guided notes) can prevent students from developing their own note-taking and self-regulation skills [~M]; supports should fade as fluency grows
- Timed testing that is not aligned to the construct being measured systematically underestimates the knowledge of slow processors [-S]
- Identifying processing-speed challenges is time-consuming and easily confused with motivation or attention issues, leading to misaligned supports [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Input side: pre-recorded lectures students can pause and re-watch; repeated key points in multiple modalities; manipulatives that make abstract concepts concrete
- Output side: oral responses, scribes, speech-to-text, or extended deadlines in place of timed written responses
- Environmental: reduced-distraction settings for independent work; previewing directions before activities begin

### Target Learners
- Students with identified processing-speed weaknesses (often co-occurring with ADHD or specific learning disabilities) [+M]
- Students whose strong reasoning is masked by slow output — these learners benefit most from untimed, format-flexible assessment
- English language learners processing in two languages, who face similar pace bottlenecks [~W]
- Fast processors, who need acceleration or enrichment rather than pacing supports [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- All content-area objectives where speed is not the construct being measured
- Fluency-building goals (math facts, decoding), where timed practice is itself the intervention and should be kept [~M]
- Self-regulation: students learn to recognize their own pace profile and request appropriate supports

### Instructions
1. Identify the bottleneck: observe whether a student struggles with input (keeping up with lectures/reading), processing (formulating responses), or output (writing/speaking under time pressure).
2. Structure the input: provide [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) and guided notes before lectures; break content into segments using [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).
3. Build in think-time: extend wait-time after questions; allow re-reading; use think-pair-share so processing happens socially before public response.
4. Flex the output: offer alternative response formats and extended time on tasks that do not measure speed itself.
5. Fade supports: as fluency develops, gradually remove scaffolds so students build independent pacing strategies ([Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md)).
6. Monitor and adjust: review work samples and student reflections on which supports actually help ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback).

## Examples
- A middle-school science teacher posts a graphic organizer before a lecture; students with slow processing speed capture key points without falling behind, then use the organizer to structure a lab report.
- Khan Academy's self-paced video model lets students pause, rewind, and re-watch explanations — effectively removing the pace constraint of live instruction.
- A math class separates fluency drills (timed, by design) from problem-solving assessments (untimed), so processing speed is measured only where it is the goal.
