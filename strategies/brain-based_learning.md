# Brain-Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Brain-based learning (BBL) proposes that instruction should be organized around how "the brain learns": presenting content in small chunks, engaging multiple senses, embedding movement and social activity, and building in processing time. It originated with Caine and Caine's (1991) principles for "brain-compatible" teaching. In practice it overlaps heavily with well-established instructional techniques — chunking, multisensory presentation, active learning — but its distinctive claims about neuroscience are frequently unsupported. Critics argue BBL as a distinct framework is largely a bridge from brain science to classrooms that has not yet been built [Bruer's "bridge too far" argument](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X026008004) [-M].

## Design Implications

The defensible core of BBL is not neuroscience but cognitive psychology: managing working memory load, spacing practice, and requiring active processing. Techniques marketed as "brain-based" (movement breaks, music, multisensory activities) work only when they serve a specific cognitive function — reducing extraneous load or supporting encoding — rather than as generic engagement devices. Teachers should treat specific BBL claims skeptically: popular ideas such as learning styles, left/right brain learners, and the "10% usage" myth persist among educators despite being contradicted by evidence [Dekker et al. found high neuromyth prevalence among teachers](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00429) [X].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content broken into segments that respect working memory limits [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Activities that require learners to actively process content, not merely be stimulated by it [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- A cognitive rationale for each multisensory or movement element — what load it reduces or what encoding it supports

#### Constraints
- Multisensory embellishment (background music, decorative images, movement unrelated to content) adds extraneous load and can depress learning [Clark & Mayer's coherence principle findings](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119239086) [-S]
- "Brain-based" framing does not by itself improve outcomes; identical activities labeled differently show no differential effect [Howard-Jones, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3820) [-M]
- Rest and "processing time" without a retrieval or elaboration task produce shallow encoding; unstructured downtime is not consolidation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding more than re-exposure.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Neuromyth-based differentiation (e.g., teaching to "visual" vs. "auditory" learners) wastes instructional time and can lower expectations [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Chunked lesson timing paired with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and spaced review is the most evidence-aligned variant [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Movement is most defensible when it is content-embedded (gesturing, enacting concepts) rather than as a break from content
- Multisensory design matters most for dual-channel presentation of *the same content* (narration + relevant diagram), not for adding sensory variety for its own sake

### Target Learners
- Novices benefit from the chunking and load-management components [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Young learners benefit from embodied, content-linked movement; benefits shrink with age and expertise
- No evidence supports tailoring instruction to sensory "learning styles"; differentiation should follow prior knowledge instead [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention of factual and procedural content (via chunking, spacing, retrieval)
- Attention management within lessons (segmenting, pacing)
- Not a distinct goal category: BBL serves the same objectives as any well-designed instruction

### Instructions
1. Segment content into short, coherent chunks aligned to working memory limits ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
2. Present each chunk with dual-channel, content-relevant media — narration plus supporting visual, avoiding decorative extras ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
3. Follow each chunk with an active processing task: [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), self-explanation, or [Application](../elements/application.md)
4. Embed movement only where it represents the content (gestures, physical models, enacting processes)
5. Schedule spaced retrieval of earlier chunks rather than relying on in-class "rest" for consolidation [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]

## Examples
- **Segmented video lessons with embedded questions** (e.g., [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)) — chunking plus active processing, the defensible core of BBL without the neuromyth framing
- **[Responsive Classroom](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)** — structured movement and social routines in K-8; effective where routines support attention and community, not because of brain claims
- **Gesture-enriched math instruction** (e.g., research by Goldin-Meadow and colleagues) — movement that *represents* mathematical structure improves learning, unlike unrelated physical activity
