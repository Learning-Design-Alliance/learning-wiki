# Attribution Retraining

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Attribution retraining is a strategy grounded in Weiner's attribution theory: learners who attribute failure to stable, uncontrollable causes ("I'm just not a math person") disengage, while those who attribute it to unstable, controllable causes ("I didn't use the right strategy") persist. The strategy uses structured feedback, guided discussion, and reflection to shift attributions toward effort, strategy use, and task conditions. It is typically delivered after failure or difficulty experiences, when attributions are most salient and malleable.

## Design Implications

Attribution retraining targets the motivational antecedents of persistence rather than the learning task itself. Its effects are strongest when reattribution is tied to a concrete, teachable strategy — telling learners "try harder" without showing *what to do differently* produces little benefit [~M]. Because attributions are beliefs, not skills, the strategy works best as repeated, embedded messaging over time rather than a one-off session.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A credible failure or difficulty experience to anchor the reattribution (real or structured)
- A specific alternative strategy the learner can actually deploy next attempt — reattribution must be paired with actionable instruction ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- Consistent instructor messaging: feedback that names effort and strategy as causes, never fixed ability
- Low-stakes opportunities to re-attempt the task and experience the effect of the new attribution ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Effort-only attributions backfire when effort fails repeatedly; learners conclude the cause is ability after all [-M] — reattribution must include *strategy* change, not just "work harder"
- One-shot interventions show effects that fade within weeks unless reinforced by ongoing feedback practices [-W]
- Instructors' own implicit beliefs leak into feedback; praise for "being smart" undermines retraining by reinforcing ability attributions [-S]
- Less effective for learners whose failures stem from genuine skill deficits that strategy change alone cannot close — the attribution must be accurate to be credible [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written attributional feedback**: comments on returned work that name controllable causes ("this draft shows you rushed the planning stage — try outlining first")
- **Group workshops**: structured discussion of failure causes using real or hypothetical scenarios
- **Embedded narrative**: course materials that normalize struggle and model productive attributions (instructor [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of their own revision process)
- **Combined designs**: attribution retraining paired with explicit strategy instruction or self-regulation training, which produce larger and more durable effects than either alone

### Target Learners
- Students with learned helplessness patterns — those who give up quickly after failure and attribute it to low ability [~M]
- Struggling learners in early failure-prone stages (first-year courses, gatekeeper subjects) before attributions harden
- Less effective for high-achieving students who already hold mastery-oriented attributions; ceiling effects are common [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivational: persistence, resilience after failure, willingness to attempt challenging tasks
- Metacognitive: accurate self-assessment of what caused a performance outcome
- Self-regulation: linking cause analysis to strategy selection and monitoring

### Instructions
1. **Diagnose current attributions.** Use reflection prompts or surveys ("When you got this wrong, what was the main reason?") to surface whether learners cite ability, effort, strategy, task difficulty, or luck.
2. **Create a safe failure experience.** Use low-stakes tasks where errors are expected and treated as information, consistent with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md).
3. **Deliver reattributional feedback.** In written or verbal [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md), explicitly name controllable causes and pair each with a concrete alternative strategy — never generic effort praise.
4. **Model productive attributions.** Narrate your own response to difficulty ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)): "I got this wrong because I assumed X; now I'll check that assumption first."
5. **Provide immediate re-attempt.** Let learners apply the new strategy right away so the controllable-cause attribution is confirmed by experience.
6. **Reinforce over time.** Repeat attributional framing in every feedback cycle; a single session does not durably shift attributions.

## Examples
- **Project for Education Research That Scales (PERTS)**, at Stanford — offers classroom resources and mindset-supportive teaching practices that operationalize attributional messaging at scale ([perts.net](https://www.perts.net)).
- **Writing conference feedback**: instead of "you're a strong writer," an instructor comments "your argument stalled in paragraph three because the evidence wasn't sequenced — reorder it and the logic will carry," naming a controllable cause and a fix.
- **Mathematics recovery programs**: after a failed quiz, students complete a structured reflection identifying which *strategy* was missing, then immediately retry a parallel problem set using it.
