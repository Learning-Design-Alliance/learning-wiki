# Standards-Based Grading

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Standards-based grading (SBG) replaces single-letter averages of heterogeneous evidence (behavior, effort, homework completion, early failures) with scores on clearly defined learning standards, typically reported on a mastery scale (e.g., 1–4). Grades communicate *what a student knows and can do* rather than accumulating points; reassessment is permitted so a grade reflects eventual mastery, not the speed of first learning.

## Design Implications

SBG aligns grading with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): when scores are tied to specific standards, feedback becomes actionable at the task and process level rather than a global judgment [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Separating academic achievement from behavior and effort increases grade validity and, by removing penalties for early failure, supports persistence and self-efficacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Allowing reassessment reframes errors as recoverable, which supports mastery orientation over performance orientation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clearly defined, learner-facing standards or learning targets, ideally unpacked into [Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) with observable performance descriptors
- Assessments that map to standards individually, so each score has a single meaning
- A reporting system (gradebook) that tracks scores by standard, not by assignment category
- A reassessment policy that specifies when and how students may demonstrate improved mastery
- Teacher training in standard unpacking and rubric design — the largest implementation cost

#### Constraints
- Averaging scores across a term contradicts the mastery premise; averaging early low scores with later evidence of mastery misrepresents final learning [-S] — most SBG implementations use most-recent-evidence or highest-evidence rules instead
- Without teacher calibration, scores on the same standard vary widely across classrooms, undermining the comparability that motivates SBG [~M]
- Converting mastery scales back to traditional A–F transcripts for GPA can reintroduce the distortions SBG was meant to remove [~W]
- Reducing the number of graded assignments can lower completion of ungraded practice for students motivated primarily by points [~W]
- Implementation without concurrent changes to instruction and reassessment time produces grade inflation or workload spikes rather than learning gains [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Scale:** 4-point mastery scales, binary "meets/not yet," or narrative standards-based report cards
- **Scope:** single classroom adoption, whole-school, or district-wide with common assessments
- **Hybrid models:** standards-based academic grade plus a separate citizenship/work-habits report
- **Reassessment rules:** unlimited with required remediation, capped attempts, or student-initiated only

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students, where traditional point-accumulation grading is most entrenched
- Students who learn at uneven rates — reassessment prevents early low scores from permanently capping their grade [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Struggling students benefit most from the removal of zero/averaging penalties; high achievers accustomed to point accumulation may initially resist reduced grade-granularity [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Mastery of well-defined knowledge and skill standards (procedural and conceptual objectives)
- Accurate self-monitoring: standard-referenced scores help students locate their own gaps [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Not well suited to grading participation, discussion quality, or highly individualized creative goals without supplementary reporting

### Instructions
1. Define and unpack standards into student-friendly learning targets; state them with objectives before instruction ([State Objectives](../elements/state-objectives.md))
2. Design or adapt assessments so each item maps to one standard ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
3. Score student work against rubric descriptors per standard, not against point totals ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
4. Deliver standard-referenced feedback identifying what is met and what is not yet ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md))
5. Offer structured reassessment after remediation; replace, don't average, prior scores
6. Report by standard; convert to transcript grades only at the final reporting boundary

## Examples
- **Marzano Academies / Marzano scales** (https://www.marzanoacademies.org) — 4-point proficiency scales per standard as the basis for both instruction and reporting.
- **ActiveGrade / JumpRope / Empower** — standards-based gradebooks that track scores by learning target and support most-recent-evidence reporting.
- **High school physics SBG implementations** (e.g., work popularized by Frank Noschese, https://fnoschese.wordpress.com) — each quiz targets 2–3 standards; students reassess individual standards on demand.
