Jigsaw Reading¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Jigsaw Reading is a cooperative structure in which a text is divided into segments and each member of a "home" group is assigned a different segment. Learners first meet in temporary "expert" groups with others who read the same segment to deepen comprehension, then return to their home groups and teach their segment to teammates, so that no learner can complete the task without contributions from every member. The interdependence is structural: each learner holds a unique piece, making listening and explaining mandatory rather than optional.

Design Implications¶

Jigsaw converts reading comprehension into a social obligation: because each member possesses unique information, the structure enforces both individual accountability and positive interdependence — the two conditions most consistently associated with cooperative learning gains Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement. [+S]. Explaining one's segment to peers is a form of self-explanation, which supports deeper conceptual processing than silent reading alone Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. The design also distributes reading load across the group, allowing longer or denser texts to be covered in less individual reading time.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A text that can be meaningfully partitioned into interdependent segments (not strictly sequential content where later parts are unintelligible without earlier ones)
  • Text segments of comparable difficulty, or deliberate assignment of segments to match reader proficiency
  • Sufficient time for both the expert-group and home-group phases; rushing the teaching phase collapses the design
  • Clear comprehension aids for each segment (guiding questions, glossaries) so experts can accurately represent their piece
  • A text that decomposes into segments of roughly equal difficulty and importance
  • Sufficient time for two full group phases (expert and home); rushing the expert phase degrades the teaching phase
  • Comprehension support for each segment (Advance Organizers, guiding questions, or Annotating prompts), since learners must reach mastery of their segment independently
  • Explicit norms for the teaching phase — what a good explanation includes, how listeners question and check understanding

Constraints¶

  • If one member fails to prepare or teach their segment, teammates have a permanent gap in the content — there is no redundancy to absorb the failure [-M]
  • Weaker readers assigned difficult segments may transmit incomplete or inaccurate understandings to their group [-M]
  • Segments that cannot be understood in isolation (e.g., dense argumentative texts where each paragraph depends on the last) produce fragmented comprehension [-M]
  • The teaching phase is often rushed or reduced to reading aloud, eliminating the self-explanation benefit Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [-M]
  • A weak expert becomes a bottleneck: their home group loses an entire segment of the content, and there is no built-in remedy [-M] — the teacher must monitor expert groups and provide corrective support before the teaching phase
  • Segments read in isolation lose coherence; learners may assemble facts without grasping how the whole argument fits together [~M] — a synthesis task or whole-text debrief mitigates this
  • Unequal participation persists if status differences are unaddressed; lower-status students' contributions can be dismissed by peers [-M] — assigning competence publicly and rotating roles helps
  • Less suitable for texts where segments are heavily interdependent at the sentence level (e.g., dense mathematical derivations) than for texts with naturally modular sections

Implementation Variability¶

  • Jigsaw II (Slavin): all students read a common base text first, then expert groups specialize in one topic — reduces the risk of gaps from a missing member
  • Reverse jigsaw: experts teach in expert groups first, then report to home groups — lowers the stakes of individual teaching
  • Listening jigsaw: segments delivered as audio or video, useful for differentiating by modality rather than difficulty
  • Jigsaw II (Slavin): all learners read a common base text first, then expert groups take complementary subtopics — reduces the risk of missing foundational context
  • Reverse jigsaw: experts circulate to other groups rather than reconvening home groups
  • Listening jigsaw: segments delivered as audio or video for multimodal or pre-reader populations
  • Jigsaw writing: each expert drafts a section of a collaborative document, then groups integrate and revise

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescent and adult readers with sufficient decoding fluency to independently comprehend an assigned segment
  • Mixed-proficiency groups, provided segment difficulty is matched to readers Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement. [+M]
  • Less suitable for beginning readers who cannot yet extract and re-explain a segment's gist independently [-M]
  • Adolescent and adult readers with sufficient decoding fluency to process a segment with support; for younger or struggling readers, use Jigsaw II with a shared base text and shorter segments
  • Mixed-ability groups: the expert role gives every learner genuine responsibility, which benefits lower-achieving students' engagement and self-efficacy [+M]
  • Learners who benefit from structured interdependence over unstructured group work, where free-riding is common [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension and summarization of informational text
  • Oral explanation and academic communication skills
  • Content coverage of long or multi-topic texts within limited instructional time
  • Interdependent collaboration skills
  • Comprehension and retention of multi-part informational content
  • Oral summarization and explanation skills
  • Perspective-taking and intergroup cooperation — the original design goal of Aronson's jigsaw classroom was reducing racial tension through interdependence [+M]
  • Accountability in Collaborative Learning settings

Instructions¶

  1. Select and segment a text so each part is self-contained enough for independent comprehension; prepare guiding questions per segment.
  2. Assign segments to members of home groups (Assigned Readings), matching difficulty to reader proficiency.
  3. Learners read their segment individually, using Annotating to prepare for teaching.
  4. Convene expert groups: same-segment readers compare understanding, check each other's summaries, and rehearse how they will teach the segment.
  5. Return to home groups: each expert teaches their segment while teammates take notes and ask questions (Class Discussion norms help here).
  6. Close with an individual accountability task — a quiz, synthesis writing, or concept map — that requires knowledge of all segments, so no member can opt out of teaching or listening.

Related Strategies¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching — shares the structure of learners taking turns leading comprehension of a text, but rotates roles rather than partitioning content
  • Think-Pair-Share — a lighter-weight discussion structure often used within the expert-group phase
  • Text-Based Collaborative Learning — broader family of discussion-centered reading activities
  • Text-Based Discussion Protocols — norms that improve the quality of the teaching phase

Examples¶

  • The Jigsaw Classroom (jigsaw.org) — Elliot Aronson's original implementation, developed for desegregated classrooms in Austin, TX, using expository text segments on historical figures.
  • Jigsaw II in cooperative learning programs — Slavin's adaptation used in reading and social studies curricula, where all students read a common narrative before expert specialization.
  • University seminar preparation — instructors divide a long empirical article (e.g., methods, results, discussion sections) across a study group so each member teaches one section before whole-class discussion.
  • The Jigsaw Classroom (jigsaw.org) — Elliot Aronson's original implementation for desegregated Texas classrooms in the 1970s; the method's canonical source and ongoing resource site.
  • Facing History and Ourselves (facinghistory.org) — uses jigsaw reading regularly in its published units, with each expert group examining a different primary source or testimony before home groups synthesize across perspectives.
  • AP and IB science courses — commonly jigsaw case readings (e.g., different enzyme disorders in biology) so each home group assembles a complete diagnostic picture from complementary expert reports.
