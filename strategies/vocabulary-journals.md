# Vocabulary Journals

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A vocabulary journal is a personal, cumulative record in which learners collect target words and, for each, record information such as a student-friendly definition, an example sentence, a personal connection or image, and notes on how the word was encountered. The journal functions as both an encoding activity at entry and a review artifact for spaced revisiting.

## Design Implications

Vocabulary journals work because they force active, generative processing of word meaning rather than passive exposure to definitions [Deep, generative processing of word meanings outperforms rote definition copying.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on what learners *do* with each entry: copying dictionary definitions produces weak learning, while self-generated definitions, example sentences, and personal associations produce durable word knowledge [Elaborative interrogation and self-generated examples support durable learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The journal also externalizes word knowledge so teachers can assess growth and select words for re-teaching.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A deliberate word-selection policy — a small set of high-utility words (Tier 2 academic vocabulary or discipline-critical terms), not every unfamiliar word encountered
- An entry structure that prompts elaboration: definition in the learner's own words, original sentence, context where the word was found, and a visual or personal connection ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Scheduled opportunities to revisit and use entries — retrieval, peer discussion, or writing tasks that require the words ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Teacher modeling of a high-quality entry before independent use

#### Constraints
- Journals become busywork when entries are copied verbatim from dictionaries or glossaries [-M] — copying without transformation yields shallow encoding
- Unstructured "collect every new word" policies overload learners and dilute attention from high-utility terms [~M]
- Without spaced revisiting, entries are encoded once and forgotten; the journal must be a review tool, not a one-time worksheet [-M]
- Overly elaborate entry templates consume working memory on format compliance rather than word meaning [Reducing extraneous load matters more than adding features.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Frayer model entries**: four quadrants (definition, characteristics, examples, non-examples) — strong for concept-like words
- **Personal thesaurus format**: word, synonyms/antonyms, collocations — suited to language learners
- **Digital journals** (e.g., a shared slide deck or Padlet wall) enable peer visibility and teacher comment
- **Interactive notebooks**: vocabulary pages interleaved with content notes so words stay tied to the concepts they serve

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through adult learners; particularly strong for English learners building academic vocabulary [~M]
- Learners with some writing fluency — entry demands exceed the capacity of beginning readers/writers, who need oral or pictorial variants
- Struggling readers benefit when word selection is tightly limited and entries are co-constructed

### Target Learning Goals
- Breadth and depth of word knowledge: meaning, use, and relationships among words
- Academic language for disciplinary reading and writing
- Word-consciousness: metalinguistic awareness of how words work and how one learns them

### Instructions
1. Select 5–10 high-utility words from current texts or units; prioritize words appearing across contexts ([Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior encounters with the word).
2. Model one entry aloud, thinking through the definition, an original sentence, and a personal connection.
3. Learners complete entries in their own words — never copied definitions — adding a picture or memory hook where useful ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. Build in spaced revisits: quick retrieval warm-ups ("use three journal words in a sentence about today's topic"), partner quizzing, or journal-based review before assessments.
5. Require authentic use: writing tasks and discussions must call on journal words so entries move from recognition to production ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
6. Periodically review journals with learners to prune, refine definitions, and celebrate growth.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Bringing Words to Life (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan)** — robust vocabulary instruction in which students maintain records of Tier 2 words and interact with them across multiple contexts; the journal is a common classroom companion to this approach.
- **Frayer Model journals** — widely used in disciplinary literacy programs; each entry's non-example quadrant sharpens word boundaries.
- **English learner personal dictionaries** — word, first-language cognate, English definition, original sentence, and image; revisited in small-group oral review.
