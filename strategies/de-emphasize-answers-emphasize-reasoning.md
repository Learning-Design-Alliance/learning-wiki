# De-emphasize Answers, Emphasize Reasoning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy shifts classroom attention from *what* the answer is to *why* it works and *how* it was reached. Teachers present worked examples — including deliberately flawed ones — and ask learners to find and explain the error, or restructure participation routines so that learners must articulate their thinking process before, or instead of, stating a final answer. The answer becomes evidence in an argument about reasoning, not the endpoint of the activity.

## Design Implications

Requiring learners to explain and justify their steps triggers self-explanation, which improves conceptual understanding beyond what answer-checking alone produces [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Studying erroneous examples — worked solutions containing a plausible mistake — builds conceptual knowledge and error-detection skill better than studying only correct solutions [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. The strategy also reframes evaluation around process rather than outcome, which benefits novices who lack the knowledge to reason from outcome goals [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom culture in which reasoning is publicly valued and errors are treated as informative, not shameful — this must be established before learners will expose partial thinking
- Teachers skilled at facilitating discussion of strategies, including responding to wrong reasoning without simply supplying the answer
- Tasks with reasoning worth examining: multi-step problems, contrasting solution methods, or cases where plausible errors occur
- Time: reasoning-focused discussion is slower than answer-checking; fewer problems must be covered

#### Constraints
- Difficult to implement in answer-focused settings where high-stakes assessment rewards speed and correctness; learners may perceive reasoning talk as off-task [-M]
- For learners with very low prior knowledge, open reasoning discussion can exceed working memory capacity and produce unproductive search; some structure (worked examples, [Fading](../elements/fading.md)) is needed first [~M]
- If facilitation is weak, discussion can drift to a single confident voice or degenerate into unfocused talk without consolidation of the target concept [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Error-finding tasks**: present a worked solution with a planted error; learners locate, explain, and repair it
- **Answer-last routines**: learners explain their approach before revealing the answer ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- **Multiple methods**: compare two or more valid solution paths and discuss why each works
- **Think-pair-share**: pairs negotiate reasoning before whole-class discussion, lowering the risk of public error

### Target Learners
- Novices and learners who struggle with mathematics or lack confidence in problem-solving; de-emphasizing the single correct answer reduces anxiety and legitimizes partial thinking [~M]
- Learners with some prior knowledge, who benefit most from explaining and comparing methods [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Less effective for complete beginners without worked examples to anchor the discussion [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: why procedures work, not just how to execute them
- Error detection and self-monitoring: recognizing when a solution path has gone wrong
- Mathematical discourse and argumentation: justifying, critiquing, and comparing reasoning

### Instructions
1. Select a problem with rich reasoning and plausible errors; solve it (or present a prepared solution) containing a deliberate mistake
2. Ask learners to work through the solution and identify where and why it fails, using [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) to sharpen discrimination
3. Have learners [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) their own reasoning aloud or in writing *before* stating an answer; the teacher probes with "why?" and "how do you know?" rather than confirming correctness
4. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) comparing different solution strategies, treating each as a candidate argument to evaluate
5. Consolidate: name the key idea the reasoning revealed, and follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) that requires the same reasoning on new problems

## Examples
- **Error-finding in algebra**: present a worked equation-solving sequence with a sign error; learners locate the step, explain why it is wrong, and correct it — a form of erroneous-example learning shown to build conceptual knowledge [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **Number talks (K–6 mathematics)**: a short daily routine in which learners solve a computation mentally and the teacher records and compares multiple strategies, with the class discussing why each works; the answer is noted but never the focus
- **[Kapur's Productive Failure](https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831214533065)**: students attempt novel problems in groups, generating reasoning that is often wrong, before receiving canonical instruction — outperforming direct instruction followed by practice on conceptual and transfer measures [+S]
- **[Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org)** curriculum: tasks are structured around comparing student solution strategies and explaining reasoning, with answers treated as claims to justify
