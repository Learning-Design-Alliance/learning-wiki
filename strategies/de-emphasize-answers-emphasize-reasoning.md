# De-emphasize Answers, Emphasize Reasoning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (5 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
This strategy shifts classroom attention from *what* the answer is to *why* it works and *how* it was reached. Teachers present worked examples — including deliberately flawed ones — and ask learners to find and explain the error, or restructure participation routines so that learners must articulate their thinking process before, or instead of, stating a final answer. The answer becomes evidence in an argument about reasoning, not the endpoint of the activity.

## Design Implications

Requiring learners to explain and justify their steps triggers self-explanation, which improves conceptual understanding beyond what answer-checking alone produces [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Studying erroneous examples — worked solutions containing a plausible mistake — builds conceptual knowledge and error-detection skill better than studying only correct solutions [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. The strategy also reframes evaluation around process rather than outcome, which benefits novices who lack the knowledge to reason from outcome goals [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom culture in which reasoning is publicly valued and errors are treated as informative, not shameful — this must be established before learners will expose partial thinking
- Teachers skilled at facilitating discussion of strategies, including responding to wrong reasoning without simply supplying the answer
- Tasks with reasoning worth examining: multi-step problems, contrasting solution methods, or cases where plausible errors occur
- Time: reasoning-focused discussion is slower than answer-checking; fewer problems must be covered
- Tasks with multiple defensible solution paths or interpretable errors, not single-step recall items
- A classroom culture where errors are treated as information; teachers must respond to wrong answers by probing the reasoning rather than immediately correcting
- Assessment and grading practices that reward explanation and justification, or the strategy will be undermined by what students perceive as counting
- Teacher facility with facilitating discussion and with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) moves that press on thinking ("Why did you choose that operation?")

#### Constraints
- Difficult to implement in answer-focused settings where high-stakes assessment rewards speed and correctness; learners may perceive reasoning talk as off-task [-M]
- For learners with very low prior knowledge, open reasoning discussion can exceed working memory capacity and produce unproductive search; some structure (worked examples, [Fading](../elements/fading.md)) is needed first [~M]
- If facilitation is weak, discussion can drift to a single confident voice or degenerate into unfocused talk without consolidation of the target concept [-M]
- Undermined when high-stakes assessments reward speed and correct answers only — students rationally optimize for what is graded [-M]
- Time-intensive: examining several reasoning paths takes longer than confirming one answer, creating pressure to revert to answer-checking under pacing constraints [-M]
- Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who may lack the schema needed to evaluate reasoning at all and benefit more from direct modeling first [~M]
- Can confuse novices if multiple student strategies are compared without teacher synthesis; unstructured sharing of flawed reasoning can propagate misconceptions [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Error-finding tasks**: present a worked solution with a planted error; learners locate, explain, and repair it
- **Answer-last routines**: learners explain their approach before revealing the answer ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- **Multiple methods**: compare two or more valid solution paths and discuss why each works
- **Think-pair-share**: pairs negotiate reasoning before whole-class discussion, lowering the risk of public error
- **Erroneous examples**: present a fully worked but incorrect solution; students find and fix the error, then explain why it was wrong
- **Answer-delayed discussion**: collect answers to a conceptual question, have students defend or revise them in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) before the teacher confirms
- **Reasoning-first norms**: teacher opens with "Tell me the first thing you thought about," explicitly banning immediate answer-giving
- **Two approaches compared**: display two student solutions side by side and ask which is more efficient or generalizable — a [Comparing Cases](../elements/case-studies.md) move that supports abstraction [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novices and learners who struggle with mathematics or lack confidence in problem-solving; de-emphasizing the single correct answer reduces anxiety and legitimizes partial thinking [~M]
- Learners with some prior knowledge, who benefit most from explaining and comparing methods [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Less effective for complete beginners without worked examples to anchor the discussion [~M]
- Students who experience mathematics as answer-production and have developed unproductive beliefs about speed and correctness; the strategy broadens participation beyond fast answerers
- Novices with minimal prior knowledge need worked modeling before error analysis becomes productive [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: why procedures work, not just how to execute them
- Error detection and self-monitoring: recognizing when a solution path has gone wrong
- Mathematical discourse and argumentation: justifying, critiquing, and comparing reasoning
- Metacognitive monitoring: evaluating one's own solution path for validity
- Mathematical argumentation and justification

### Instructions
1. Select a problem with rich reasoning and plausible errors; solve it (or present a prepared solution) containing a deliberate mistake
2. Ask learners to work through the solution and identify where and why it fails, using [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) to sharpen discrimination
3. Have learners [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) their own reasoning aloud or in writing *before* stating an answer; the teacher probes with "why?" and "how do you know?" rather than confirming correctness
4. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) comparing different solution strategies, treating each as a candidate argument to evaluate
5. Consolidate: name the key idea the reasoning revealed, and follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) that requires the same reasoning on new problems

## Examples
- **Error-finding in algebra**: present a worked equation-solving sequence with a sign error; learners locate the step, explain why it is wrong, and correct it — a form of erroneous-example learning shown to build conceptual knowledge [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **[Number talks](math-talks-number-talks.md) (K–6 mathematics)**: a short daily routine in which learners solve a computation mentally and the teacher records and compares multiple strategies, with the class discussing why each works; the answer is noted but never the focus
- **Kapur's Productive Failure**: students attempt novel problems in groups, generating reasoning that is often wrong, before receiving canonical instruction — outperforming direct instruction followed by practice on conceptual and transfer measures [+S]
- **[Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org)** curriculum: tasks are structured around comparing student solution strategies and explaining reasoning, with answers treated as claims to justify
- **Erroneous-example routine**: a teacher projects a solved equation with a sign error and asks, "Find the step where this went wrong, and convince your neighbor." Students must locate and justify the error before any correct solution appears.
- **"My favorite no"**: a widely used middle-school routine (popularized by Levi & Teaching Channel) in which the teacher collects anonymous wrong answers, displays one, and has the class identify what is *right* about the reasoning before fixing the error.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../principles/responsive-teaching.md)** — teachers elicit and build instruction around students' own solution strategies rather than demonstrating the canonical algorithm first.
- **[Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md)** — in professional education, discussion of reasoning and diagnosis is the point; the "answer" is often deliberately withheld or contested.

## Description
De-emphasizing correct answers and spending more time on reasoning shifts the instructional focus from the product of problem solving to the process. Teachers elicit and examine students' thinking — initial ideas, solution paths, and errors — before or instead of revealing the correct answer. Common enactments include presenting worked examples containing deliberate errors for students to diagnose, asking students to share approaches before confirming answers, and grading or giving feedback on the quality of justification rather than only on correctness.

## Design Implications

Attending to reasoning converts answer-checking into [Self-Explanation](../elements/articulation.md), which reliably improves conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Diagnosing flawed worked examples (erroneous examples) builds conceptual knowledge by forcing learners to discriminate correct from incorrect reasoning [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M], and errors held with high confidence are especially productive learning moments when surfaced and corrected [High-confidence errors improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]. Feedback should therefore target the task and process levels — the strategy chosen and why — rather than the person or the final answer [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Instructions
1. Select or design a task with a rich, diagnosable solution space; prepare one or more erroneous worked examples.
2. Pose the problem and elicit initial thinking before confirming any answer — "I want to hear your approach, not the answer yet."
3. Have students articulate, compare, and critique solution paths in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or small groups, using [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) norms ("agree/disagree, and say why").
4. Guide error analysis: locate the faulty step, explain why it fails, and repair it.
5. Synthesize: name the key reasoning moves across approaches so novices leave with a coherent model, not just a collection of strategies.
6. Give feedback on the reasoning — strategy choice, justification quality, error detection — not just correctness [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
7. Align grading rubrics so explanation and justification carry explicit weight.
