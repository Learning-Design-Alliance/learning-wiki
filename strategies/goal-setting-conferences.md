# Goal Setting Conferences

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A goal setting conference is a structured one-on-one (or small-group) conversation in which a teacher and learner jointly identify a specific, challenging-but-attainable learning goal, agree on success criteria and strategies, and schedule a follow-up to review progress. The conference converts abstract expectations ("do better in reading") into concrete commitments ("read two chapter books this month and track summaries in my journal"), and positions the learner — not the teacher — as the owner of the goal.

## Design Implications

Goal setting conferences operationalize two well-supported findings: specific and challenging goals produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals [Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705) [+S], and proximal subgoals raise self-efficacy and persistence, especially for young or struggling learners [Bandura & Schunk, 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586) [+S]. The conference format matters because goals set *with* learners, rather than *for* them, support autonomy and increase commitment [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. The conference is only the visible tip of the cycle — its value depends on the monitoring and feedback that follow [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Evidence about the learner's current performance (work samples, data, [Self-Assessment](../elements/self-assessment.md)) so goals are grounded in a realistic baseline
- A predictable conference routine with a short protocol: review progress → set/revise goal → name strategies → record the goal
- A visible record of the goal (goal card, tracker, portfolio) that the learner can refer to between conferences
- Scheduled follow-up — a conference without a check-in cycle is a conversation, not an intervention ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))

#### Constraints
- Goals imposed by the teacher without learner input reduce ownership and can undermine intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]
- Goals that are outcome-only ("get an A") rather than process-oriented ("use the revision checklist") can encourage performance-avoidance and surface strategies [~M]
- Conference time is expensive; without a tight protocol, conferences consume instructional time disproportionate to their effect [-W]
- Public goal displays can backfire for learners motivated to avoid looking incompetent; keep goals private or learner-controlled [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reading/writing workshop conferences** (e.g., Lucy Calkins' Units of Study): 3–5 minute conferring sessions during independent work, research-decide-teach structure
- **Data meetings**: longer conferences built around progress data, common in [Competency-Based Learning](../patterns/competency-based-learning.md) schools
- **Advisory/ILP conferences**: periodic goal reviews against a personal learning plan, as in Summit Public Schools' personalized learning model
- **Peer conferences**: learners run the same protocol with a partner once the routine is established

### Target Learners
- Developing self-regulators in upper elementary through adulthood; goal setting is a trainable component of self-regulated learning [Zimmerman, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2) [+M]
- Struggling or disengaged learners, for whom proximal subgoals build self-efficacy [Bandura & Schunk, 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586) [+S]
- Very young learners need heavy scaffolding — teacher-narrowed goal menus rather than open-ended goal choice [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning
- Metacognition: articulating what "good work" looks like and where one stands relative to it
- Motivation and ownership: building commitment through learner-set, proximal goals
- Skill growth in any domain where progress can be made visible (reading level, writing traits, math fluency)

### Instructions
1. Gather baseline evidence — recent work samples, assessment data, or [Self-Assessment](../elements/self-assessment.md) — before the conference so the conversation starts from shared facts.
2. Open with the learner's self-report: "How do you think it's going? What's working?" ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
3. Co-construct one specific, proximal goal with visible success criteria; offer a constrained menu of options rather than open choice when learners are novice goal-setters ([Achievable Micro-Goals](achievable_micro-goals.md))
4. Name 1–2 concrete strategies the learner will use, and record the goal and strategy in a tracker the learner owns ([Action Planning](action_planning.md))
5. Schedule brief follow-up [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) to monitor progress and deliver [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) tied to the goal criteria.
6. At the next conference, review progress against the goal, celebrate or revise, and set the next goal — closing the cycle.

## Examples
- **Reading workshop conferring** (Teachers College Reading & Writing Project, [https://readingandwritingproject.org](https://readingandwritingproject.org)): teachers hold 3–5 minute goal conferences during independent reading, each ending with a recorded goal the student pursues until the next conference.
- **Summit Public Schools** ([https://summitps.org](https://summitps.org)): students set weekly goals in personalized learning plans and review them in mentor conferences, pairing goal setting with progress data.
- **Responsive Classroom** ([https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)): teacher-student goal setting conversations embedded in advisory and academic choice routines.
