# Project-Based Transformation of Society

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students work in small groups to identify and propose solutions to crucial problems in their society. In the classic formulation, groups generate 15 crucial problems and draft 15 laws to address them, then decide how each law would be enforced — fines, rewards, prison sentences, persuasion campaigns, or economic stimuli. The strategy combines [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) with structured [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md): identifying issues, developing solutions, and confronting the practical and ethical trade-offs of governance.

## Design Implications

The strategy's power comes from forcing learners past simplistic solutions into genuine trade-off reasoning. Structured group problem-solving of this kind supports engagement and ownership [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], and open-ended collaborative inquiry can improve conceptual learning when it is scaffolded rather than left unguided [Productive failure improves conceptual learning.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]. The enforcement step is the pedagogical hinge: it converts opinion into consequence, requiring students to weigh competing values and constituencies.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sufficient time for extended group work and access to resources for researching societal issues
- Clear norms for ethical, respectful discussion of controversial topics, since the task surfaces political and moral disagreement
- A facilitator who can probe proposals for feasibility, unintended consequences, and hidden assumptions rather than judging them politically
- A structure for [Decision-Making](../elements/decision-making.md) within groups, since drafting and enforcing laws requires reaching consensus

#### Constraints
- Without careful facilitation, groups may propose offensive or stereotype-reinforcing "solutions," or default to punitive enforcement for out-groups [-W]
- Controversial-issue discussion can backfire when students lack background knowledge or discussion norms; unstructured political talk tends to entrench prior positions rather than develop reasoning [~M]
- Groups with unequal participation let a few students carry the intellectual work; roles and individual accountability are needed [-M]
- The task rewards opinion fluency over evidence unless research and justification requirements are explicit [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Scope can range from the full 15-problems/15-laws format to a single issue examined in depth
- Local variants: school, community, or city problems instead of national ones, connecting to [Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md)
- Add an [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) layer by presenting proposals to a panel, mock legislature, or community members
- [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) can be used to force perspective-taking before consensus drafting

### Target Learners
- High school students in English or social studies, particularly those ready to engage with controversial issues; adaptable to college and adult civic education
- Students who have not previously been permitted to discuss controversial subjects at school benefit from the structured exposure to disagreement and perspective diversity [+W]
- Less suitable for learners without enough background knowledge to evaluate proposals; a research or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) phase should precede drafting [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Civic reasoning: understanding how policy, enforcement, and trade-offs interconnect
- Critical thinking and problem-solving: moving from complaint to feasible, justified solution
- Collaboration and compromise: experiencing why satisfying all constituencies is difficult
- Perspective-taking and empathy: encountering classmates' differing values [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)

### Instructions
1. **Frame the task.** Present the challenge (e.g., "transform the society by identifying its 15 most crucial problems") and establish discussion norms; consider an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) showing the problem → law → enforcement pipeline.
2. **Form groups and assign roles.** Use [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) structures with defined roles to ensure individual accountability.
3. **Identify problems.** Groups brainstorm, research, and prioritize crucial problems, using [Challenge Identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md) and evidence gathering; a [Case Study](../elements/case-study.md) of an existing policy can anchor the analysis.
4. **Draft laws.** For each problem, groups draft a law, using [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) to justify it against objections.
5. **Design enforcement.** Groups specify how each law would be enforced — fines, rewards, imprisonment, persuasion, economic stimuli — and analyze costs, ethical implications, and likely resistance.
6. **Deliberate across groups.** Groups present and critique one another's proposals; [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) can surface conflicts between proposals.
7. **Reflect.** Close with structured [Reflection](../elements/reflection.md) on what made compromise hard and how classmates' reasoning differed from their own.

## Related Strategies

- Mock trial and simulation strategies — similarly place students inside decision-making roles with real consequences to weigh

## Assessment Evidence
- Evaluate the thoroughness, feasibility, and evidence base of proposed solutions
- Assess whether students considered diverse perspectives and trade-offs, not just whether they produced laws
- Track individual participation in discussion and the quality of reflective writing; feedback should target reasoning and group process rather than the political correctness of conclusions [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

## Impact
Experience shows that students come to appreciate how hard it is to compromise and solve national problems to everyone's satisfaction. A few students begin to question simplistic solutions. Some are shocked to discover how many classmates think differently from them — unsurprising where controversial subjects have rarely been discussed at school. This productive disequilibrium is a designed outcome, not a side effect.

## Examples
- The classic classroom version: small groups transform the United States via 15 crucial problems and 15 laws, each with a specified enforcement mechanism, culminating in cross-group deliberation and reflection
- Civic-action capstones such as [iCivics](https://www.icivics.org) curriculum units, where students draft and simulate policy, provide a digital analog
- Model United Nations and Youth Parliament programs enact the same structure at international and parliamentary scales
