Written Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Written feedback delivers comments on learner work — identifying what was done well, where performance falls short of the goal, and what to do next. It is carried out through margin comments, end comments, rubric-anchored notes, or structured feedback sheets, ideally framed as actionable guidance rather than verdicts.

Design Implications¶

Written feedback is one of the most powerful — and most variable — influences on achievement: its average effect is large, but roughly a third of feedback studies show negative effects because poorly designed feedback directs attention to the self rather than the task [~S]. Feedback works when it answers three questions — Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next? — and fails when it merely grades or praises. Because written comments arrive after performance, they must be paired with opportunities to revise or re-attempt, or they are simply documentation of failure.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear success criteria shared with learners in advance, so comments reference a known target (Assessment for Learning)
  • Comments that are specific, task-focused, and include a next action — not just error location
  • Time and structure for learners to act on the feedback (Revision or resubmission)

Constraints¶

  • Feedback accompanied by a grade or score is often ignored; learners attend to the mark and skip the comments [-S]
  • Praise and personal comments ("you're so smart") redirect attention to ego and can depress subsequent performance, especially after failure [-S]
  • Overly detailed feedback on weak work can overwhelm working memory and produce worse outcomes than no feedback for novices [~S]
  • Feedback without an opportunity to apply it produces negligible learning gains [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Comment codes (e.g., "AWK" for awkward syntax) reduce marking load and force learners to self-correct
  • Whole-class written feedback (e.g., "next-step" sheets addressing common errors) trades individualization for speed and reduced threat
  • Audio/screen-recorded feedback conveys tone and nuance that text strips out, at similar production cost
  • Peer written feedback using structured protocols builds evaluative judgment, though quality must be trained

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling learners benefit most from specific, actionable, task-level comments; ego-directed feedback harms them disproportionately [~S]
  • High-achievers benefit more from hints and prompts than from full corrections, which can do the thinking for them
  • Novices need more directive feedback; experts benefit more from facilitative comments that prompt self-evaluation [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving quality of complex products (writing, code, designs) through iterative revision
  • Developing evaluative judgment — the capacity to judge one's own work against criteria
  • Closing identified gaps between current and target performance (Assessment as learning, not just measurement)

Instructions¶

  1. Establish success criteria or a rubric before the task so feedback has a shared reference point (Clear Structure)
  2. Collect work and diagnose against the criteria, identifying one or two high-leverage issues per learner rather than marking everything (Cognitive Load Management)
  3. Write comments in the form of a next action ("Add a topic sentence that states your claim") rather than a judgment ("weak paragraph")
  4. Withhold the grade until the learner has read and acted on the comments
  5. Build in class time for revision and resubmission, so feedback is a midpoint, not an endpoint (Action-Oriented Feedback)

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the framing principle: every comment should specify what to do next
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — the oral counterpart; written and conferenced feedback are often alternated in writing instruction
  • 3-2-1 Reflection — a lightweight structure for learners to process and respond to feedback received

Examples¶

  • Writing instruction with revision cycles — Draft–comment–revise sequences in which the teacher's margin comments drive a mandatory second draft; the revision, not the comment, produces the gain.
  • Code review on GitHub — Inline line-level comments plus summary review comments on pull requests; the merge-blocking review is a structural guarantee that feedback is acted on.
  • No-grades marking policies — Several UK schools (e.g., approaches documented by Evidence Based Education) replace scores with comment-only marking after evidence that grades mask comments.
