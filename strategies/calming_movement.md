# Calming Movement

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Calming movement uses slow, repetitive, rhythmic movement — rocking, swaying, gentle swinging, rhythmic songs with motion ("Row, Row, Row Your Boat"), or slow back-and-forth movement on a scooter board or therapy ball — to deliver predictable vestibular input that helps a learner down-regulate arousal. It is typically used as a reset when a child is overstimulated, overwhelmed, or escalating toward dysregulation, and is drawn from sensory integration practice rather than from cognitive instructional design.

## Design Implications

Calming movement is best understood as an arousal-regulation support that prepares a learner for instruction, not as an instructional strategy itself; its classroom value lies in reclaiming attention and reducing time lost to dysregulation [Classroom physical activity improves attention.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. Evidence specifically for rhythmic vestibular input as a calming mechanism is weaker and largely observational, drawn from occupational therapy literature rather than controlled experiments [~W]. Because arousal state gates learning, a brief calming routine can be a precondition for [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and other demanding activities, but it should not replace them.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Safe equipment or space: rocking chair, therapy ball, scooter board, or simply a defined swaying/rocking routine
- Adult knowledge of the individual learner's sensory profile — the same movement can calm one child and arouse another
- A consistent cue or routine so the learner recognizes the movement as a regulation opportunity, not a reward
- Supervision appropriate to the child's age and motor abilities

#### Constraints
- Sensory-based interventions as a class show weak and inconsistent evidence for improving functional outcomes; effects are often indistinguishable from attention alone [Sensory integration therapy shows limited, inconsistent effects.](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2014-0836) [-W]
- Fast, unpredictable, or spinning movement is typically alerting rather than calming and can escalate dysregulation [~W]
- If used as an escape from demands or delivered on a fixed schedule regardless of state, the routine can function as reinforcement for escape behavior rather than self-regulation [~M]
- Movement breaks do not substitute for instruction; time spent regulating must be balanced against time on task [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Proactive vs. reactive:** embedded in a daily schedule as a regulation break, or deployed in response to early signs of escalation
- **Embedded vs. equipment-based:** rhythmic whole-class routines (stretching, rhythmic chanting with motion) require no equipment and reduce stigma
- **Learner-led vs. adult-led:** older learners can be taught to select and initiate their own calming movement, building self-regulation rather than dependence on adult prompting

### Target Learners
- Young children and learners with sensory sensitivities, autism, or ADHD who become easily overwhelmed [~W]
- Learners who show observable arousal escalation (fidgeting, covering ears, agitation) before dysregulation
- Less appropriate as a whole-class default; most learners do not need vestibular input to regulate, and unnecessary breaks cost instructional time [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and managing arousal states
- Behavioral readiness: returning to [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or instruction after dysregulation
- Interoceptive and body awareness: noticing how movement changes internal state

### Instructions
1. **Establish a baseline routine.** Choose one or two slow, rhythmic movements (rocking, swaying, therapy-ball rocking) and practice them when the learner is already calm, so the movement is familiar before it is needed.
2. **Teach the cue.** Pair the movement with a consistent signal or [Check-in](../elements/check-in.md) ("How is your body feeling? Let's rock for a minute") so the learner connects state awareness with the strategy.
3. **Keep it slow and predictable.** Use gentle, rhythmic, linear movement; avoid spinning or bouncing, which are alerting.
4. **Return promptly to the task.** Treat the movement as a bridge back to [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or instruction, not a destination; fade adult prompting as the learner initiates independently ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. **Document and individualize.** Track behavior and engagement before, during, and after; adjust movement type, duration, and timing to the individual ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)).

## Examples
- **Early-childhood classrooms:** a quiet corner with a rocking chair a child can visit during free play or after a transition, paired with a teacher check-in about feelings.
- **Occupational therapy "sensory diets":** individualized schedules of rhythmic movement (therapy-ball rocking, scooter-board pulls) embedded in the school day for students with sensory processing needs.
- **Whole-class reset:** 60 seconds of slow swaying to a rhythmic chant after a loud assembly, requiring no equipment and carrying no stigma.
