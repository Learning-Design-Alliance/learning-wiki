# Rubric Co Construction

## Description
Rubric co construction engages learners in developing the scoring criteria and quality definitions for an assessment task, typically by analyzing exemplars, proposing criteria, negotiating language, and refining performance-level descriptors. The instructor facilitates rather than dictates, ensuring the final rubric is both rigorous and owned by learners. The process converts assessment criteria from an external mystery into shared, internalized standards.

## Design Implications

Co constructing rubrics makes evaluative criteria explicit and gives learners practice in quality judgment, which supports the development of self-regulated evaluation skills [~M]. Because learners help define what "good" looks like, the rubric functions as a formative tool rather than a post-hoc grading instrument, aligning with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [+M]. Studies of rubric use generally show benefits for learning and self-efficacy, with transparency of criteria as a key mechanism [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- High-quality exemplars of the target performance at varying quality levels, so learners have something concrete to judge ([Worked Examples](../elements/worked-examples.md) or [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) serve this role)
- Time and structure for negotiation — criteria discussion is the learning event, not overhead
- Instructor expertise to ensure proposed criteria are valid and complete, filling gaps learners cannot see
- A follow-on use: the co-constructed rubric must actually be applied in [Self-Assessment](../elements/self-assessment.md), peer review, or revision cycles

#### Constraints
- Learners without prior exposure to the task domain tend to propose surface features (length, neatness) rather than quality criteria [-W] — exemplar analysis and instructor seeding are needed to move past this
- Co construction adds substantial class time; if the rubric is then used only once for a single graded product, the investment rarely pays off [-M]
- Negotiated criteria can drift toward what learners already know how to do, lowering standards unless the instructor holds the quality bar [~M]
- With very young learners or complete novices, full co construction may exceed their evaluative skill; partial co construction (instructor provides criteria, learners define performance levels) is often more workable [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full co construction**: learners generate criteria from exemplar comparison; strongest ownership, highest time cost
- **Partial co construction**: instructor supplies criteria, learners draft level descriptors or "student-friendly" translations
- **Rubric revision**: learners critique and improve an existing rubric after using it on exemplars — lower cost, retains much of the judgment practice
- **Peer rubric development**: groups draft competing rubrics, then merge them, forcing explicit justification of criteria

### Target Learners
- Learners developing self-regulatory skill, since judging quality against criteria is the same act as self-monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Intermediate learners with enough domain exposure to distinguish strong from weak work; complete novices need exemplar scaffolding first [~W]
- Less beneficial for learners who already have well-calibrated criteria for the task, for whom the negotiation is redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive: internalizing quality standards and evaluative judgment
- Self-regulated learning: using criteria to plan, monitor, and revise work
- Deep understanding of task demands: what distinguishes competent from excellent performance
- Not well suited to simple factual recall objectives, where criteria are trivial [~W]

### Instructions
1. Present exemplars at different quality levels and have learners evaluate them informally ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. Elicit criteria: ask learners what distinguished the strong exemplars, recording proposed criteria
3. Facilitate negotiation and consolidation, adding missing criteria the learners could not identify
4. Draft performance-level descriptors collaboratively, using language learners understand
5. Apply the rubric immediately in [Self-Assessment](../elements/self-assessment.md) or peer feedback on a draft, then revise work against it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
6. Revisit and refine the rubric after use — treat it as a living document

## Related Strategies
- Exemplar analysis — the typical entry activity that grounds criteria discussion in concrete cases
- Peer assessment — the co-constructed rubric supplies the shared criteria peer feedback requires
- Self-assessment — co construction builds the evaluative criteria that self-assessment applies

## Examples
- **Writing instruction**: Andrade's work with middle-school English learners had students co construct rubrics for essays, then self-assess drafts against them before revision, improving writing quality and student understanding of "good writing"
- **Portfolio programs**: many university capstone and e-portfolio programs (e.g., those following the [AAC&U VALUE rubrics](https://www.aacu.org/initiatives/value) tradition) involve students in interpreting and adapting institutional rubrics to their own projects
- **Design studio courses**: architecture and design studios commonly negotiate project evaluation criteria with students at project launch, then apply them in group critique
