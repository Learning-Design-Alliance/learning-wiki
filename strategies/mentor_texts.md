# Mentor Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Mentor texts are examples of high-quality writing — professional or student-authored — that learners study and imitate to improve their own writing. The strategy involves deconstructing a text to identify effective "writer's moves" (sentence structure, organization, voice, use of detail), then emulating those moves in students' own drafts. Mentor texts can be multimodal: essays, poems, newspaper articles, song lyrics, comic strips, or manuals.

## Design Implications

Mentor texts are an example-based learning strategy for writing: they substitute a studied model for unguided invention, reducing the search burden on novice writers [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on active analysis — students must articulate *why* an author's move works, not just observe it — which converts reading into self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Comparing several mentor texts that address the same goal supports abstraction of the underlying technique rather than surface imitation [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to high-quality writing samples (professional or exemplary student work), selected to match the target genre and technique
- Structured analysis activities — annotation, discussion, naming of "writer's moves" — rather than passive reading ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Immediate imitation opportunities: students apply an identified move in their own writing shortly after studying it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Time for reading, analysis, and discussion, and a classroom culture where students share and compare drafts

#### Constraints
- Students may copy surface features of a mentor text without understanding the underlying technique, producing pastiche rather than transfer [-M]
- Exemplary professional models can intimidate struggling writers or feel unattainable; pairing them with flawed or developing examples lowers the comparison bar [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- A single mentor text anchors students to one authorial style; multiple contrasting texts reduce this risk [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Techniques studied in isolation often fail to transfer to independent drafting without explicit prompts to apply them [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class study**: one shared text analyzed together, with the teacher making the deconstruction visible ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
- **Small-group or independent study**: students select texts matched to their current writing goal, supporting personalization and autonomy
- **Student-authored mentor texts**: peers' work as models, which signals attainability and builds community
- **Multimodal texts**: song lyrics, comics, or infographics for genre and craft analysis beyond prose

### Target Learners
- Novice writers in K–12 and higher education who lack a repertoire of structural and stylistic moves [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]
- English language learners, who benefit from concrete models of disciplinary and genre conventions [+M]
- Advanced writers benefit less from single models and more from contrasting texts that support stylistic experimentation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing craft: sentence variety, organization, voice, use of evidence and detail
- Genre knowledge: understanding the conventions and purposes of forms (narrative, argument, report)
- Critical reading: analyzing how authorial choices affect a reader
- Writing confidence and risk-taking: seeing that published techniques are learnable, not innate

### Instructions
1. Select a mentor text that exemplifies the target technique or genre ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))
2. Read aloud or display the text; the teacher models noticing a writer's move and naming why it works ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
3. Students annotate the text to identify additional moves, individually or in pairs ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
4. Discuss findings as a class or in small groups to build a shared vocabulary of techniques ([Discussion Sections](../elements/discussion-sections.md))
5. Students imitate one identified move in a short piece of their own writing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
6. Provide feedback focused on the imitated technique ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
7. Students reflect on which moves they adopted and how they might adapt them to their own voice ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md))

## Examples
- Using a [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com) feature article as a mentor text for narrative writing, focusing on how the author "shows" rather than "tells."
- The [WritingFix](http://writingfix.com) project and Dorfman & Cappelli's *Mentor Texts* (Stenhouse, 2007) provide published lesson sequences built around picture books as craft models.
- Examining successful college application essays to study voice and risk-taking in personal narrative.
- Using student writing from prior cohorts as mentor texts, signaling that exemplary craft is attainable by peers.
