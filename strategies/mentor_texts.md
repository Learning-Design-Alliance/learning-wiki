# Mentor Texts

## Description
Mentor texts are examples of high-quality writing used to teach students how to improve their own writing skills. Teachers and students return to these texts for various purposes, studying and imitating the techniques employed by the authors. Mentor texts can be multimodal (books, stories, poems, essays) and encompass various forms such as newspaper articles, song lyrics, comic strips, or manuals. They help students take risks, explore new strategies and formats, and develop their unique voice and confidence. The use of mentor texts involves deconstructing the writing to identify effective 'writer's moves' and then emulating those moves in student work.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to high-quality writing samples (student or professional). Time for reading, analysis, and discussion in class. Structured activities for imitation and practice. A classroom environment where students can work together and share.
#### Constraints
- Students may mimic mentor texts without developing their unique voice, may feel overwhelmed by exemplary models, or may struggle to transfer learned techniques to their own writing. Requires careful selection of appropriate mentor texts and thoughtful scaffolding of activities.

### Target Learners
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- K-12 and higher education students learning writing and literacy skills

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims -->
- Develop students' writing abilities, enhance disciplinary literacy, foster confidence in writing, expose students to various writing techniques and genres, improve critical thinking and analytical skills

### Affordances
- Provides concrete examples of effective writing, allows students to see real-world applications of writing techniques, promotes active learning and critical analysis, enhances understanding of different writing styles and structures, allows students to see different authorial voices and potentially emulate/find their own voice through the process.

### Personalization
- Mentor texts can be selected based on student interests and skill levels. Questions and annotations can be adapted to meet individual student needs. Exercises can be scaffolded for struggling learners, and advanced students can be challenged to explore more complex techniques.

## Assessment Evidence
- Student writing samples demonstrating the application of techniques learned from mentor texts. Student participation in discussions and analyses of mentor texts. Student reflections on their writing process and the impact of mentor texts.

## Impact
- Research indicates that using mentor texts can enhance writing skills, promote critical thinking, and increase student engagement in writing. It supports the development of disciplinary literacy by exposing students to diverse writing styles and techniques. Improved ability to notice and implement specific writing techniques, increased confidence in writing, better understanding of how different authorial choices impact the reader.

## Examples
- Using a New York Times article as a mentor text to teach narrative writing, focusing on elements like crafting a narrative arc or using details to 'show' rather than 'tell'. Analyzing published essays or short stories to understand sentence structure, diction, and overall organization. Examining successful college application essays to inspire creativity and bravery in student writing.
