# Choice Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Choice based learning structures decision points into instruction so learners select among meaningful options — what topic to study, which process to use, how to demonstrate learning, or where and with whom to work. The choices must be consequential and aligned to learning goals; offering trivial or overwhelming option sets does not produce the same effects.

## Design Implications

Choice supports motivation primarily by satisfying the basic psychological need for autonomy [Choice enhances intrinsic motivation and perceived competence.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows that providing choice reliably improves intrinsic motivation, effort, and task performance, with effects strongest when options are educationally meaningful rather than superficial [Patall et al. (2008) meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.3.511) [+M]. However, choice imposes decision-making demands: too many options, or options that differ in difficulty, can burden working memory or lead learners to avoid challenge [Katz & Assor (2007)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543073001037) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of 2–5 genuinely distinct, goal-aligned options — not cosmetic variation
- Clear success criteria that apply across all options, so choice does not change what is assessed ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) must remain comparable)
- Enough learner readiness to make a reasonable selection; [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md) or teacher conferencing for learners who cannot yet choose well
- A structure for committing to and completing the chosen path (e.g., a planning step as in [Academic Choice (planning, working, reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md))

#### Constraints
- Choice of *task difficulty* often backfires: learners choose options that are too easy or too hard, and low-prior-knowledge learners especially make maladaptive selections [~M] — constrain difficulty choices or use [Adaptive Difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md) instead
- Large option sets increase decision cost and can reduce satisfaction and performance relative to small sets [~M]
- When options differ in cognitive demand, choice can widen achievement gaps rather than close them [~M]
- Choice is less effective when learners lack the knowledge to evaluate options; novices benefit more from expert-selected sequences [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices hinders experts.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Topic choice** — learners pick the subject of inquiry within a shared goal (e.g., choosing a case in [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md))
- **Process choice** — learners pick their method or route (read, watch, simulate) while the objective is fixed
- **Product choice** — learners pick the demonstration format (essay, podcast, model) against a common rubric
- **Environment/social choice** — learners pick where, with whom, or at what pace to work
- [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) — a concrete artifact that presents the option set in a grid, often mixing product and process choices

### Target Learners
- Learners with sufficient prior knowledge to evaluate options meaningfully; novices need constrained or scaffolded choice [Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices hinders experts.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Disengaged or low-motivation learners, for whom autonomy is the most direct lever [Choice enhances intrinsic motivation and perceived competence.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Learners building self-regulation, since choosing and committing exercises self-monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement goals: ownership, persistence, intrinsic interest
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own path
- Content goals are served indirectly — choice is a motivational wrapper, not a substitute for well-structured instruction

### Instructions
1. Define the non-negotiable learning goal and the success criteria all options share.
2. Design 2–5 options that are equivalent in demand but distinct in topic, process, or product; present them in a [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md) or similar artifact.
3. Have learners commit to a choice with a brief plan (what, why, first step) — the planning step converts preference into ownership.
4. Provide [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and feedback on the chosen path, holding criteria constant across options.
5. Debrief the choice itself: what learners selected, why, and whether they would choose differently next time — this builds transferable decision-making.

## Related Strategies
- [Academic Choice (planning, working, reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md) — a structured planning–working–reflecting cycle that operationalizes choice for younger learners
- [Project-Based Learning](../strategies/project-based-learning.md) — typically embeds topic and product choice within an extended authentic task

## Examples
- **Montessori classrooms** — children select work from a prepared environment; the option set is curated so every choice is productive, an early and influential model of structured choice.
- **[Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) in differentiated units** — a 3×3 grid of product options (write, record, build) all mapped to the same rubric, common in UDL-aligned curricula ([CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org)).
- **Flipped courses with process choice** — learners choose among video, reading, or interactive-simulation paths to reach the same prerequisite understanding before class ([Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md)).
