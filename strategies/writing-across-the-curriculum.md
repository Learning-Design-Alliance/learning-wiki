# Writing Across The Curriculum

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Writing Across The Curriculum (WAC) is a whole-program strategy in which writing is treated as a learning tool in every discipline, not solely as an assessed product in English/language arts. It typically combines *writing-to-learn* tasks (journals, low-stakes in-class writing, informal explanations) with *writing-in-the-disciplines* tasks (lab reports, case analyses, argumentative essays) that teach disciplinary genres and norms. Faculty across departments assign, scaffold, and respond to writing rather than delegating it to composition courses.

## Design Implications

Writing forces learners to transform material into their own words, which promotes elaboration and integration rather than passive review [Writing-to-learn tasks produce modest but consistent gains in content learning.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The effect is strongest when tasks require genuine reasoning — explaining, arguing, connecting — rather than transcription or summary of what was already said [Writing-to-learn effects depend on task design; unstructured expressive writing shows weak effects.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [~M]. Low-stakes, frequent writing with feedback functions as [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md), surfacing misconceptions instructors can act on [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Institutional or departmental commitment: writing tasks in multiple courses, not one-off adoption
- Tasks aligned to disciplinary thinking (a physics explanation differs from a history argument)
- Feedback structures that are sustainable — peer review, rubrics, or brief comments — since instructor grading load is the usual failure point
- Scaffolding for genre conventions unfamiliar to students ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md), models of disciplinary writing)

#### Constraints
- High-stakes graded writing in every course overloads both students and instructors; without low-stakes variants, WAC collapses into more grading [-M]
- Writing about material students have not yet understood produces fluent-sounding but empty prose; writing-to-learn presupposes some prior exposure or [Activation](../principles/activation.md) of prior knowledge [~M]
- Adding writing to a content-heavy course without reducing other load can exceed working memory capacity for novice writers, whose transcription and composition consume resources that content learning needs [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Faculty without writing-instruction background may assign writing without teaching it, yielding poor results and resistance

#### Implementation Variability
- **Writing-to-learn (WTL):** informal, ungraded or lightly graded — journals, minute papers, entry/exit slips, in-class "explain to a novice" prompts
- **Writing-in-the-disciplines (WID):** formal, genre-based — lab reports, case briefs, design memos, literature reviews
- **Scaffolded variants:** sentence starters, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) source texts before writing, structured outlines, or [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) peer-review protocols [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- **Threshold variants:** 5-minute writing conferences for individualized feedback (see [5-minute writing conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md))

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary students across all disciplines; effects are broadest in content-heavy courses where discussion time is scarce
- Struggling or disengaged students benefit from low-stakes expressive writing that lowers risk [~M]
- Advanced students benefit more from WID genre instruction than from generic expressive prompts [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and integration of course content
- Disciplinary argumentation and genre fluency ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md))
- Metacognition and self-monitoring through reflective writing
- Formative diagnosis of misconceptions

### Instructions
1. Identify the disciplinary thinking you want students to do (explain, argue, hypothesize, evaluate) and design a writing task that requires it — not a summary task.
2. Sequence low-stakes before high-stakes: use short in-class or journal writing to activate and surface prior understanding ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
3. Model the target genre with an annotated example, making disciplinary reasoning visible as in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md).
4. Provide feedback focused on ideas first, mechanics second; use peer review or brief conferences to keep load sustainable ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
5. Close the loop: have students revise or apply feedback, and use what their writing revealed to adjust instruction.

## Examples
- **Michigan State University's WAC program** (https://writing.msu.edu) — departmental writing consultants and discipline-specific assignment design across the curriculum.
- **The WAC Clearinghouse** (https://wac.colostate.edu) — curated assignment repositories and disciplinary writing guides used by programs worldwide.
- **Physics "explain-your-reasoning" prompts** — introductory courses requiring short written justifications alongside problem solutions, converting problem sets into writing-to-learn opportunities.
