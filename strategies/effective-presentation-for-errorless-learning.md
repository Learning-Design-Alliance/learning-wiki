# Effective Presentation for Errorless Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Behavioral scientists identify three levers a teacher controls when presenting instructional material to minimize errors and bring correct responses under reliable stimulus control (Cooper, Heron, & Heward, 1987; Englemann, 1991):

**Specific directions.** Directions should focus only on the response the teacher wants the learner to make, discarding extraneous information that can distract from the target response. For example, teaching a sight word with "This is the word 'rabbit.' Say 'rabbit' and point to the word" is more effective than adding unrelated facts about rabbits, which risks becoming what the learner actually attends to.

**Active over passive responding.** Research on "opportunity to respond" (Delguardi, Greenwood, & Hall, 1979; Hall, Delguardi, Greenwood, & Thurston, 1982; Lindsley, 1992) distinguishes **active responding** (writing, calculating, focusing a microscope, recording observations — the learner does something) from **passive responding** (listening to lectures, watching, waiting for assistance). Greenwood, Delguardi, and Hall (1984) found that nearly half of a typical learner's school day involves passive responding, despite research showing a strong relationship between active responding and achievement; behavioral scientists recommend planning lessons so learners spend at least 75% of class time in active responding. Practice materials (worksheets, seatwork, homework) should also be designed to elicit correct responses 70-90% of the time (Borich, 1996; Stephens, 1976) — deliberately making practice materials difficult enough to produce frequent errors slows acquisition of basic facts and skills rather than accelerating it.

**Rapid pacing.** Counterintuitively, faster-paced presentation of basic academic material tends to produce fewer errors, not more, for fact and simple-sequence learning. Carnine's studies on reading-instruction pace found that rapid teacher presentations produced greater achievement, fewer errors, and more sustained learner attention during letter and word identification than slower presentations (Carnine, 1976; Carnine & Fink, 1978).

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clearly defined target response, so directions can be pared down to only what supports that response
- Instructional material and practice sets calibrated to a 70-90% success rate rather than deliberately made "challenging" to the point of frequent error
#### Constraints
- This cluster of techniques targets fact and basic-skill acquisition specifically; the research base does not claim rapid pacing benefits complex reasoning or open-ended problem solving in the same way
- Designing practice for 70-90% success requires knowing where a learner currently stands — material calibrated for one learner's success rate may be miscalibrated for another

### Target Learners
- Learners acquiring basic facts, letter/word identification, or other well-defined procedural skills where errors slow the learning process and provoke frustration or avoidance

### Target Learning Goals
- Fast, reliable acquisition of foundational facts and skills, minimizing the frustration and avoidance behavior that high error rates tend to produce

### Instructions
1. Write directions that name only the target response; remove explanatory or contextual material that does not directly support making that response.
2. Plan lesson activities so learners are actively responding (writing, calculating, manipulating, recording) for the large majority of instructional time, rather than passively listening or watching.
3. Design practice materials so learners produce correct answers roughly 70-90% of the time; avoid deliberately difficult practice sets intended to "stretch" learners into frequent error.
4. Where the content is basic facts or simple sequences, favor a brisk response pace over a slower, more deliberative one.
