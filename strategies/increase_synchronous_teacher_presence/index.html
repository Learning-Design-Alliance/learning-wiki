Increase Synchronous Teacher Presence¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 3 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Teacher presence in synchronous (live) online sessions is the deliberate use of instructor visibility, immediacy, and interaction — greeting learners by name, narrating decisions, responding in real time, and structuring the session — so that learners experience the course as personally guided rather than self-serve. It is carried out through live video/audio, chat, polls, and shared workspaces, combined with tight session structure to limit distraction and cognitive overload.

Design Implications¶

Teacher presence is one of three legs of the Community of Inquiry framework (teaching, social, and cognitive presence), and perceived instructor presence and social presence are consistently associated with higher student satisfaction and, more moderately, with perceived learning in online courses Richardson et al. meta-analysis [+M]. Presence works partly by restoring the relational cues (voice, facial expression, immediate feedback) that asynchronous formats strip away, which supports learner belonging and persistence [+M]. However, live delivery also adds extraneous load and distraction risk, so presence must be paired with Cognitive Load Management — clear agendas, chunked segments, and purposeful media use Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Reliable synchronous technology (video conference, chat, shared whiteboard) and a fallback for connectivity failures
  • A visible session structure: agenda, segment timing, and interaction points (Clear Structure)
  • Instructor willingness to show face and voice; camera-on instruction supports immediacy [+W]
  • Active participation structures — polls, cold-calling with warmth, breakout tasks — rather than lecture-only delivery Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Lecture-heavy live sessions with no interaction produce no presence benefit and add scheduling cost Active learning improves exam performance. [-S]
  • Large enrollments make individual recognition impractical; presence techniques must shift to group-level (naming cohorts, public chat acknowledgment)
  • Poor audio, screen-share sprawl, and multitasking invitations (long unbroken slides) increase extraneous load Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]
  • Mandatory camera-on policies can disadvantage learners with bandwidth limits, caregiving duties, or social anxiety; presence should be instructor-led, not universally enforced [-W]
  • Time-zone spread can make "synchronous" a burden for some learners; record sessions and pair with asynchronous equivalents [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • High-presence openings: a brief Check-In or personal greeting at session start builds social presence cheaply
  • Modeled sessions: instructor uses Demonstration and Modeling with live narration, enacting Cognitive Apprenticeship in real time
  • Interactive segments: Class Discussion, chat-storms, and polls interleaved every 10–15 minutes
  • Coaching formats: live Coaching or office-hours blocks where the instructor works problems with individuals or small groups
  • Flipped pairing: content delivery moved to pre-work so synchronous time is spent on interaction (Flipped Classroom)

Target Learners¶

  • Fully online learners at risk of isolation — first-year students, part-time and adult learners, and students new to online study [+M]
  • Learners in discussion-heavy or skills-based courses where immediate feedback changes performance
  • Less critical for self-regulated, experienced online learners who prefer asynchronous pacing [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Belonging and persistence: reducing attrition in online programs [+M]
  • Procedural and skills instruction where live modeling and immediate corrective feedback matter
  • Discussion-based and case-based objectives requiring real-time dialogue

Instructions¶

  1. Open with a structured Check-In — one-question pulse or roll-call greeting — to establish social presence in the first five minutes.
  2. Present a visible agenda and chunk the session into 10–15 minute segments alternating instructor input and learner activity (Clear Structure, Chunking).
  3. Model the target skill live with narration, thinking aloud through decisions rather than presenting finished results (Demonstration).
  4. Interleave interaction — polls, chat responses, breakout tasks, Class Discussion — and name learners when responding to their contributions.
  5. Close with a live Coaching moment or Q&A that addresses the session's sticking points, and post a brief follow-up message referencing learner contributions to extend presence beyond the live hour.

Related Strategies¶

  • Establish instructor presence in asynchronous channels (announcements, video feedback) so synchronous presence lands on an existing relationship
  • Pair synchronous sessions with structured pre-work so live time is spent interacting, not transmitting
  • Invite students to co-facilitate tasks by typing content into the shared whiteboard or chat
  • Require regular online instructor participation in asynchronous discussions, which may mitigate the lack of face-to-face interaction

Examples¶

  • Zoom-based seminar courses — instructor opens camera-on, runs a two-minute check-in, alternates mini-lectures with breakout discussions, and closes by naming student contributions.
  • Flipped Classroom online delivery — recorded content before class; synchronous time devoted to problem-solving with the instructor circulating among breakout rooms.
  • Live office hours / coaching blocks — scheduled small-group sessions where the instructor works through student-submitted problems in real time.
