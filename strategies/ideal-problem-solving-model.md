# IDEAL Problem-Solving Model

## Description
IDEAL (Bransford & Steen, 1984) is a general problem-solving system usable across curriculum areas and problem types, named for its five stages:

1. **Identify the problem** — Learners confirm they know what the problem actually is and whether they have stated it clearly, before attempting a solution.
2. **Define terms** — Learners check that they understand what each word in the problem statement actually means.
3. **Explore strategies** — Learners compile relevant information and try out candidate strategies — drawing diagrams, working backward, or breaking a complex problem into manageable units.
4. **Act on the strategy** — Learners select one of the explored strategies and actually apply it.
5. **Look at the effects** — Learners ask themselves whether the result is an acceptable solution, closing the loop back to Identify if it is not.

Many other systems for teaching problem-solving exist for specific domains — general problem solving (Burkell, Schneider, & Pressley, 1990; Mayer, 1987; Sternberg, 1988), scientific thinking (Kuhn, 1989), mathematical problem solving (Schoenfeld, 1989), and writing at both the elementary (Harris & Graham, 1992) and adolescent (Applebee, 1984; Langer & Applebee, 1987) levels — but IDEAL's value is its explicit generality: unlike a domain-specific heuristic, it can be taught once and applied across curriculum areas (Pressley, 1995).

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A problem complex enough that jumping directly to a solution attempt is likely to fail or waste effort
- Willingness to have learners pause at each explicit stage rather than moving straight from reading a problem to attempting an answer
#### Constraints
- The model's five stages are a general scaffold, not a domain-specific technique — for a specific problem type, a more specialized system (e.g., a mathematical problem-solving heuristic) may add value IDEAL alone does not

### Target Learners
- Learners across a wide range of curriculum areas who need an explicit, transferable structure for approaching complex problems, rather than a domain-specific technique

### Target Learning Goals
- Applying a general, teachable problem-solving process to well- or ill-structured problems, per [Well-Structured vs. Ill-Structured Problems](../principles/well-structured-vs-ill-structured-problems.md)

### Instructions
1. **Identify** — Confirm the problem is correctly and clearly understood before proceeding.
2. **Define** — Check the meaning of each term in the problem statement.
3. **Explore** — Gather relevant information and generate candidate strategies (diagrams, working backward, decomposition into subproblems).
4. **Act** — Choose one strategy from those explored and apply it.
5. **Look** — Evaluate whether the outcome is an acceptable solution; if not, cycle back to Identify with the new information gained.
