# Graphic Organizers and Visual Aids (Attention)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (6 for, 1 against) · 16 studies (7 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 review), `q2`–`q4` · 5 of 16 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
Graphic organizers and visual aids structure content spatially — through mind maps, advance organizers, comparison matrices, flowcharts, and diagrams — so that learners can see which information matters and how it relates. As an attention strategy, the organizer functions as a pre-selection device: it directs the learner's limited attentional resources toward essential relationships rather than surface details.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers work by externalizing structure, reducing the working-memory burden of holding relationships in mind while processing new content [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their attentional value comes from emphasis: visually highlighting what is central directs selective attention and improves later recall of emphasized content [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. Combining verbal and visual representations supports dual coding, producing better recall than either channel alone [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows knowledge maps and similar graphic organizers outperform text-only or list formats for retention, with the largest gains when learners actively construct the organizer rather than only study a provided one [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear analysis of the content's structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, cause–effect) so the organizer type matches the relationships being taught
- Explicit instruction in how to read and use the organizer — learners unfamiliar with a format need modeling ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- For maximal benefit, a step where learners complete or construct the organizer themselves rather than passively receiving it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- A clear analysis of the content's relational structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, cause–effect) so the organizer type matches the content
- Brief instruction in how to read and use the organizer — learners unfamiliar with a format need modeling before it helps

#### Constraints
- Poorly designed organizers with excessive boxes, colors, or cross-links add extraneous load and depress learning [~M] — visual complexity must be pruned, not accumulated
- Provided (completed) organizers can encourage shallow copying; learners who fill them in without generating connections show smaller gains [~M]
- Mismatched structure harms comprehension: a hierarchical map applied to non-hierarchical content distorts the relationships [~M]
- Benefits shrink for learners with high prior knowledge, who can impose structure on text themselves and may find the organizer redundant [~M]
- Pre-filled organizers viewed passively yield smaller gains than learner-completed ones; the attention benefit collapses when learners do not process the structure [~M]
- Poorly matched organizer types (e.g., a linear flowchart for comparative content) add extraneous processing rather than reducing it [-M]
- Overly dense or cluttered visuals increase cognitive load and split attention [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Learners with strong prior knowledge may find organizers redundant, and rigid templates can constrain learners who would benefit from generating their own structure [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Advance organizers** presented before text to prime relevant schema ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- **Partially completed organizers** that learners finish during instruction, fading support over time
- **Learner-constructed maps** (concept mapping) as an active-learning and assessment activity
- **Signal-enhanced materials** — headings, color-coding, and callouts embedded in existing texts or slides rather than separate artifacts
- **Provided vs. learner-generated:** give a completed organizer as an advance organizer before instruction, or a partial one to complete during it; learner-constructed maps (e.g., concept mapping) trade guidance for generative processing
- **Static vs. animated:** animated diagrams help for dynamic processes; static diagrams suffice for structural relationships
- **Color-coding and highlighting:** effective when the color scheme is consistent and semantically meaningful (e.g., one color per claim type), ineffective when arbitrary

### Target Learners
- Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral information in a domain [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners with limited working-memory capacity who benefit from externalized structure [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for advanced learners, who may experience redundancy effects [~M]
- Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral information in a domain [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for experts, who already impose structure and may find provided organizers redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Selective attention: identifying the key ideas and relationships in complex material
- Relational knowledge: comparing, sequencing, and classifying content
- Retention of structured information [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Comprehension of relational content: hierarchies, processes, comparisons, cause–effect chains
- Selective attention: identifying key ideas in dense text or multimedia
- Retention of structured knowledge [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Analyze the content to determine its dominant structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison, cycle).
2. Select an organizer type that matches that structure; keep the design minimal ([Cognitive Load Management](../elements/cognitive-load-management.md)).
3. Present the organizer before or during instruction as an advance organizer, modeling how to read it ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Have learners complete or construct the organizer themselves, then check it against a reference version ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Fade the organizer as learners internalize the structure, eventually asking them to generate their own from raw material.

## Examples
- **Inspiration / Kidspiration (https://www.inspiration.com)** — concept-mapping software widely used in K–12 for learner-constructed graphic organizers across content areas.
- **Comparison matrices in history instruction** — a grid with civilizations as rows and institutions (religion, economy, government) as columns directs attention to comparable features across cases; multiple contrasting cases presented in such a matrix support abstraction of shared principles [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- **Khan Academy unit maps (https://www.khanacademy.org)** — visual course maps showing lesson hierarchy, orienting learners to where the current lesson sits in the unit's structure.
- **Inspiration / Kidspiration (https://www.inspiration.com)** — concept-mapping software used in K–12 classrooms for learner-constructed graphic organizers across content areas.
- **Story maps in reading instruction** — narrative element charts (setting, characters, problem, resolution) that direct attention to story structure; widely used in explicit comprehension curricula.
- **Comparison matrices in science instruction** — a grid organizing variables, conditions, and outcomes across experiments, used to focus attention on what differs between conditions rather than prose re-reading.

## Description
Graphic organizers (concept maps, flowcharts, comparison matrices, story maps) and visual aids (diagrams, color-coded notes, highlighted headings) structure content spatially so learners can identify the most important information and the relationships among ideas. As an attention strategy, their function is *signaling*: they cue which material deserves focal processing rather than incidental exposure. They differ from decorative visuals in that they encode substantive relationships, not merely attract the eye.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers work by spatially representing relational structure, which supports both attention allocation and encoding: combining verbal and visual representations improves recall [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M], and organizing material into coherent visual chunks reduces extraneous working-memory load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Provided organizers are most effective when they require learners to actively complete or apply them; simply viewing a pre-filled organizer produces weaker learning than constructing or filling in the structure [~M]. Visuals must be relevant to the content — decorative illustrations that do not carry information do not improve learning and can distract [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+S].

#### Requirements

- Integration with the lesson: the organizer should be referenced, completed, or revised during instruction, not handed out as an afterthought ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))

#### Constraints
- Pre-filled organizers viewed passively yield smaller gains than learner-completed ones; the attention benefit collapses when learners do not process the structure [~M]

- Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral information in a domain [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Analyze the content to determine its dominant structure (sequence, hierarchy, comparison, cycle) and select a matching organizer type.
2. Introduce the organizer before instruction as a framing device ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)), or present a partial version for learners to complete.
3. Model how to use it — think aloud while filling in the first entries so learners see how the structure maps onto the content.
4. Have learners complete, extend, or revise the organizer during instruction ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)), rather than only viewing a finished version.
5. Use the completed organizer as a retrieval or summary tool — learners reconstruct it from memory or use it to generate explanations.
