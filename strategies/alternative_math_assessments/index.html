Alternative Math Assessments¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal), q2–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Alternative math assessments are formats for measuring number-fact fluency and mathematical understanding that do not rely on speeded, high-stakes recall tests. They include interview-based assessment, games, number talks, portfolio tasks, and untimed or low-stakes fluency checks. The goal is to measure what learners actually know — not how their knowledge holds up under time pressure and anxiety.

Design Implications¶

Timed tests impose pressure that consumes working memory resources needed for retrieval, particularly for learners with math anxiety, and can produce scores that underestimate knowledge Math anxiety interferes with working memory during computation. [+S]. Because anxiety about mathematics emerges early and correlates with avoidance, assessment format is not a neutral choice — it shapes learners' relationship with the subject Math anxiety predicts lower achievement even in early elementary students. [+M]. Alternative formats align with formative assessment principles: the purpose is to inform instruction, not merely to rank Assessment functions formatively when it reveals learning gaps to teacher and learner. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear fluency criteria defined in terms of accuracy, flexibility, and efficiency — not speed alone
  • Multiple assessment occasions, since single snapshots misrepresent developing fluency
  • A way to record observations (interview notes, game-based data, work samples) so assessment informs Assessment decisions
  • Follow-up Feedback tied to strategies, not just correctness Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Timed formats depress performance for anxious and working-memory-limited learners even when they know the facts [-S] — speed pressure converts a retrieval task into a stress task
  • Games and open formats can mask whether fluency is automatic or scaffolded by counting; the assessor must probe strategy use, not just answers [~M]
  • Alternative formats take more administration time than a one-page timed sheet; scaling requires routines (rotating interview schedules, self-recorded game data)
  • Removing time pressure entirely can delay development of efficient retrieval if learners are never asked to consolidate strategies [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Interviews ("How did you solve 8 + 7?") reveal strategy maturity and are the gold standard for diagnosing fluency quality
  • Games (e.g., "How Close to 100," math card games) embed retrieval in purposeful contexts with repeated, spaced exposure Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S]
  • Number talks make strategies public and let teachers assess the whole class's flexibility at once
  • Untimed fluency inventories preserve the individual-recall format while removing the speed stressor
  • Portfolios and work samples document growth over time rather than single-moment performance

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with math anxiety or test anxiety, whose timed-test scores systematically underestimate their knowledge Math anxiety interferes with working memory during computation. [+S]
  • Early-elementary learners, in whom timed testing can seed durable negative attitudes toward mathematics Math anxiety predicts lower achievement even in early elementary students. [+M]
  • Learners building fact fluency through strategy-based instruction, where assessment should track strategy efficiency, not just speed
  • Less necessary for learners with high fluency and low anxiety, for whom timed checks add little distortion [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Fact fluency: accuracy, flexibility, and efficiency with basic number combinations
  • Strategy awareness: whether learners use derived facts and decomposition rather than counting
  • Mathematical self-efficacy: assessment experiences that support rather than erode confidence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Define fluency criteria (accurate, flexible, efficient) and share them with learners so assessment targets are transparent.
  2. Replace the weekly timed test with a rotating cycle: individual interviews, strategy-focused Number Talks, and fluency games.
  3. During interviews, ask learners to explain their strategy before confirming the answer, using Assess Performance routines to record strategy level.
  4. Give feedback on strategy choice and efficiency, not just correctness Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person. [+S].
  5. Use results to plan targeted Practice with spaced retrieval, rather than repeating whole-class timed drills Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Number Talks — a discussion routine that doubles as whole-class fluency assessment
  • Formative Assessment — the broader pattern these formats instantiate
  • Spaced Retrieval Practice — the practice mechanism that builds the fluency being assessed

Examples¶

  • "How Close to 100" (youcubed, Stanford) — a dice-and-grid game in which learners repeatedly compute products in a purposeful context; the teacher observes fluency without any timed component. https://www.youcubed.org
  • Number Talks (Parrish, 2010, Number Talks: Helping Children Build Mental Math and Computation Strategies) — daily mental-math discussions revealing the strategies every learner uses.
  • Math Recovery / Add+Vantage MR — one-on-one interview-based assessment instruments that profile a child's number strategies and inform targeted teaching. https://www.mathrecovery.org
  • DreamBox Learning — continuous, embedded game-based assessment that infers fluency and strategy from gameplay rather than discrete tests. https://www.dreambox.com
