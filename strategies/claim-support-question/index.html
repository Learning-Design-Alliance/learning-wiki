Claim Support Question¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 11 studies (5 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 4 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Claim Support Question (CSQ) is a thinking routine developed by Project Zero at Harvard Graduate School of Education in which learners (1) make a claim about a topic or text, (2) identify support — evidence, reasoning, or examples — for that claim, and (3) ask a related question that the claim does not resolve. The routine structures argumentation into a compact, repeatable format that makes reasoning visible and keeps inquiry open rather than closing on a verdict.

Design Implications¶

CSQ operationalizes argumentation by requiring learners to commit to a position and then justify it, which research on argumentation shows improves reasoning quality and conceptual understanding Argumentation improves reasoning and conceptual learning. [+M]. The final question step is what distinguishes it from simple claim-evidence tasks: it pushes learners toward Cognitive Disequilibrium and sustained inquiry rather than premature closure Encountering discrepant or unresolved questions motivates conceptual change. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A topic, text, artifact, or phenomenon genuinely open to interpretation — not a single settled fact
  • Explicit modeling of what counts as "support" (evidence vs. opinion vs. restatement), ideally via Think-Aloud or a worked example
  • Time and norms for learners to share claims publicly and respond to one another's questions

Constraints¶

  • With weak prior knowledge, learners generate claims they cannot support, producing fluency illusions rather than learning Learners with low prior knowledge benefit less from minimally guided inquiry. [-S] — pair with Advance Organizers or source material to anchor claims
  • If the question step is skipped or treated as an afterthought, the routine collapses into assertion-and-justification and loses its inquiry function
  • In large classes without discussion structures, claims go unchallenged; combine with Think-Pair-Share or Discussion Groups

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written (exit ticket, discussion board post) or oral (whole-class or small-group discussion)
  • Repeated over time on the same topic: learners revisit and revise earlier claims as evidence accumulates
  • Reversed order ("Question → Claim → Support") to open a unit rather than consolidate one

Target Learners¶

  • Middle school through adult learners; the routine scales in sophistication with the material
  • Learners developing disciplinary habits of evidence-based reasoning in humanities, science, and civic education
  • Novices need scaffolded source material or modeled claims; more expert learners can apply the routine to primary sources and data Minimally guided approaches favor learners with sufficient prior knowledge. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Argumentation: constructing and justifying claims with evidence Argumentation improves reasoning and conceptual learning. [+M]
  • Epistemic cognition: understanding what makes evidence adequate and what remains uncertain
  • Conceptual change: surfacing initial claims so they can be tested and revised

Instructions¶

  1. Present the prompt — a text, image, dataset, or event with genuine interpretive room; see Case Studies or Anchored Instruction for rich prompt design.
  2. Claim — each learner states a clear position in one sentence. Require commitment before discussion to activate prior knowledge Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+M].
  3. Support — learners identify evidence, examples, or reasoning from the material; the instructor models the difference between evidence and assertion.
  4. Question — each learner poses one question the claim leaves open; collect questions to drive the next activity or Discussion-Based Learning session.
  5. Revisit — return to claims after further instruction and ask learners to revise them, making conceptual change visible.

Related Strategies¶

  • Claim-Evidence-Reasoning (CER) — the science-classroom variant; CSQ adds the open question step
  • See-Think-Wonder — a Project Zero routine better suited to initial observation before claims are possible
  • Socratic Seminar — extended discussion format that CSQ outputs can feed
  • 3-2-1 Reflection — a lighter consolidation routine usable at the end of a CSQ cycle

Examples¶

  • Project Zero Thinking Routines Toolbox — the canonical source, with CSQ documented among core routines for understanding: pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines
  • History classrooms — after reading two conflicting primary-source accounts of the same event, students claim which account is more reliable, cite textual support, and ask what additional evidence would settle the question.
  • Science discussion — students claim an explanation for a discrepant event (e.g., why ice floats), support it with prior observations, and pose the question that launches the next investigation.
