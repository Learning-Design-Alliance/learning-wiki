# Design instructional sequences whose later questions cannot be answered without considering mechanism, forcing an epistemological frame shift

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (2 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q2` · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The lecture's classroom example shows that when later worksheet questions "can't be answered without considering how the cart is moving and the mechanism creating the tapes, so a frame shift was needed", students returned and corrected their earlier answers. The strategy is to sequence tasks so that early quick-answer framings fail on later questions, prompting students to reconsider the mechanism and revise. This treats misconceptions as sometimes containing "the structures that can provide their own cure" rather than as irreducible defects to excise.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task sequence in which later questions depend on mechanism the earlier framing ignored
- Group work facilitated by a teaching assistant trained to encourage students to explore their own ideas
#### Constraints
- The article's example comes from a modified algebra-based recitation run as University of Washington-model tutorials; the author does not claim the strategy generalizes beyond such settings

### Target Learners
- Students in algebra-based introductory physics recitations

### Target Learning Goals
- Activating the epistemological resource of mechanism
- Correcting quick-answer errors through re-framing
