# Word Sort

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (5 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 9 report an effect size

## Description
In a Word Sort, small groups receive an envelope of key terms on separate slips of paper. Before reading or lecture, students discuss what the terms might mean and organize them into categories based on perceived relationships. After exposure to the terms through text or lecture, groups reconvene to revise their sorts, now grounded in formal definitions.

## Design Implications

Word Sorts work by forcing retrieval and elaboration of prior knowledge before authoritative definitions are supplied, creating a framework that gives new vocabulary context and improves retention [Activating prior knowledge before instruction improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The pre-instruction sort also creates productive contrast: when students compare their intuitive categorizations with the expert structure presented later, the discrepancies become memorable learning events [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Grouping terms into categories additionally reduces the memory burden of learning many terms at once [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Instructions
1. Select 10–20 key terms from the upcoming unit and print each on a separate slip; ensure terms admit multiple defensible groupings.
2. Form small groups and distribute envelopes. Instruct groups to discuss what each term might mean and sort the slips into categories, recording a brief rationale per category.
3. Debrief initial sorts briefly, emphasizing that disagreement and uncertainty are expected — this is diagnosis, not assessment.
4. Deliver instruction (reading, lecture) that defines the terms and presents the expert structure.
5. Reconvene groups to revise their sorts, explicitly comparing their original categories to the formal ones.
6. Close with a whole-class discussion of which categories changed and why, connecting the revised sort to what comes next in the course.

# Word Sorts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Word sorts are a hands-on word study activity in which learners categorize a set of words according to shared features — sound patterns (e.g., short vs. long vowel), spelling patterns (e.g., -ck vs. -k endings), or morphological elements (e.g., prefixes, roots, inflections). Sorts may be *closed* (the instructor supplies the categories) or *open* (learners determine their own categories and justify them). Physically cutting, moving, and rearranging word cards adds a multisensory, manipulable dimension, and sorts can be completed independently or collaboratively with discussion of sorting rationale.

## Design Implications

Word sorts operationalize inductive concept learning: rather than being told a rule, learners compare and contrast exemplars and derive the pattern themselves, which supports deeper encoding of orthographic and morphological regularities [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because learners must attend to the critical features that distinguish categories, sorts direct attention to word structure rather than meaning-free memorization, supporting automatic word recognition that frees resources for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. The activity functions as structured [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with built-in self-checking, and instructor feedback on sorting decisions is central to its effectiveness.

## Description
A word sort presents learners with a set of words (typically on cards) to be grouped under category headers according to a shared feature — vowel pattern, initial sound, inflectional ending, or derivational morpheme. Learners compare and contrast words, articulate why each belongs where it does, and check their sorts against known words. The activity is inductive: the pattern is discovered through classification rather than stated as a rule first.

## Design Implications

Word sorts turn spelling and vocabulary instruction into guided [active learning](../principles/active-learning.md): learners generate the generalization themselves through repeated comparison, which supports deeper encoding than copying or rule recitation [~M]. The sorting structure functions like a [graphic organizer](../principles/clear-structure.md) for word knowledge, reducing the working-memory demand of holding multiple words in mind at once [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Reading each word aloud during sorting builds automatic word recognition, freeing attention for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A carefully chosen word set that contrasts the target feature with confusable features (e.g., *short a* vs. *long a* words)

- Repeated sorts across days with speed and blind sorts to build fluency

#### Constraints
- If learners sort correctly by surface features without articulating the underlying pattern, no generalization occurs [-M] — the "sort and say" discussion is essential, not optional
- Word sets that mix too many features at once overload novices and produce guessing rather than analysis [~M]

- Learners who already know the pattern gain little from re-sorting; sorts should target the learner's developmental spelling stage [~M]

# Word Sorting

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
Word sorting asks learners to classify a set of words into categories based on shared features — spelling patterns (e.g., *-ake* vs. *-ick*), initial or final sounds, vowel teams, or morphological elements (prefixes, suffixes, roots). Learners compare and contrast words, articulate why each belongs where it does, and induce the underlying pattern themselves rather than being told a rule first. The canonical implementation is developmental word study as practiced in [Words Their Way](https://www.wordstudy.wayne.edu/) (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston), where sorts are matched to a learner's spelling-development stage.

## Design Implications

Word sorting is a form of guided induction: by contrasting exemplars, learners discover orthographic regularities, which supports decoding and automatic word recognition [Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. The comparison structure is the active ingredient — sorting works because it forces attention to similarities and differences among cases, not because words are handled physically [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Sorts should be followed by reading and writing the sorted words in context so patterns transfer to authentic text.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A word set carefully selected to contrast the target pattern with known or confusable patterns (typically 15–25 words)
- Category headers or anchor words that make the sorting dimensions explicit
- A follow-up step in which learners verbalize the pattern ("these all say /ā/ with *a_e*") — sorting without articulation becomes mechanical
- Periodic re-sorting of previous sets for spaced review

#### Constraints
- Sorting becomes rote shuffling if learners can complete it by visual matching without attending to the pattern [-M] — require learners to read each word aloud and justify placements
- Ineffective when the sort dimension is opaque to the learner; novices cannot induce patterns they lack the phonological awareness to perceive [~S]
- Over-reliance on single-pattern sorts can produce overgeneralization (applying a pattern where it does not apply); include oddball/exception words in the sort set [-M]
- Time-intensive relative to explicit teaching of high-frequency irregular words, which are not reliably sorted by pattern [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Closed sorts** — teacher-defined categories; appropriate for introducing a new pattern
- **Open sorts** — learners devise their own categories; appropriate for consolidating and extending knowledge
- **Speed sorts and buddy sorts** — timed or partner-based re-sorts that build fluency and force verbal justification
- **Writing sorts** — learners record sorted words under headers, connecting orthographic categorization to spelling production
- **Concept sorts** — semantic rather than orthographic categories (e.g., by topic), useful for vocabulary before learners can sort by spelling

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers in the alphabetic and within-word-pattern stages, who are consolidating sound–spellings [Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Struggling readers and spellers who need many contrasting exemplars to discriminate confusable patterns
- English learners, for whom sorting makes the orthographic system of English explicit and comparable to their first language's system [~M]
- Less appropriate for learners already reading fluently, for whom pattern study adds little to automaticity

### Target Learning Goals
- Decoding: inducing vowel and consonant patterns that support unfamiliar-word attack
- Spelling: internalizing orthographic conventions through categorization rather than rote lists
- Phonological and morphological awareness: attending to sounds, syllables, and meaningful word parts
- Vocabulary: concept sorts linking word meanings to categories

### Instructions
1. Select words contrasting the target pattern with two or more known or confusable patterns; include 2–3 exception words.
2. Model sorting 3–4 words under labeled headers, thinking aloud about the feature that determines placement ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Have learners sort the remaining words individually or in pairs, reading each word aloud ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Debrief: learners state the pattern for each category in their own words and explain where the exception words go ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)).
5. Follow with writing the words under headers, then reading connected text containing the pattern so the sort connects to authentic reading.
6. Re-sort previous weeks' sets at intervals for spaced review ([Fading](../elements/fading.md) — reduce support as patterns are mastered).
