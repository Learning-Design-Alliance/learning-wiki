# Word Sort

## Description
In a Word Sort, small groups receive an envelope of key terms on separate slips of paper. Before reading or lecture, students discuss what the terms might mean and organize them into categories based on perceived relationships. After exposure to the terms through text or lecture, groups reconvene to revise their sorts, now grounded in formal definitions.

## Design Implications

Word Sorts work by forcing retrieval and elaboration of prior knowledge before authoritative definitions are supplied, creating a framework that gives new vocabulary context and improves retention [Activating prior knowledge before instruction improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The pre-instruction sort also creates productive contrast: when students compare their intuitive categorizations with the expert structure presented later, the discrepancies become memorable learning events [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Grouping terms into categories additionally reduces the memory burden of learning many terms at once [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Key terms printed on separate, manipulable slips of paper (or digital cards)
- Terms chosen so multiple plausible categorizations exist — ambiguity is the point
- A follow-up instructional exposure (reading, lecture) that supplies authoritative definitions
- A scheduled second pass where groups revise and justify their revised sorts

#### Constraints
- Students may object to "figuring out definitions themselves" or perceive the activity as the instructor shirking their job — framing the sort as a diagnostic of prior knowledge, not a test, mitigates this
- If terms are too obscure or too transparent, the initial sort produces either random guessing or trivial agreement, and the contrast effect disappears
- Without the mandatory revision pass after instruction, the activity becomes a one-shot guess with no corrective feedback [~M]
- Large term sets (more than ~15–20) can overload groups and devolve into mechanical sorting [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Open sort vs. closed sort:** students generate their own categories (more prior-knowledge activation) or sort into instructor-specified categories (more structure, less discovery)
- **Pre/post timing:** the revision pass can occur mid-unit, after reading, or as a final review — a final review sort doubles as retrieval practice
- **Individual then group:** students sort alone first, then negotiate a group sort, surfacing individual misconceptions for discussion
- **Digital adaptation:** shared slide decks or whiteboard tools with draggable term cards for remote courses

### Target Learners
- College students encountering discipline-specific vocabulary for the first time [Activating prior knowledge before instruction improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Students who underestimate their own partial knowledge of a domain — the sort reveals that they already hold relevant intuitions
- Less effective for true novices with no experiential foothold in the terminology, whose sorts are essentially random

### Target Learning Goals
- Vocabulary acquisition and retention in a conceptual framework
- Relational knowledge: understanding how terms relate to and differ from one another
- Metacognitive awareness of prior knowledge and its gaps

### Instructions
1. Select 10–20 key terms from the upcoming unit and print each on a separate slip; ensure terms admit multiple defensible groupings.
2. Form small groups and distribute envelopes. Instruct groups to discuss what each term might mean and sort the slips into categories, recording a brief rationale per category.
3. Debrief initial sorts briefly, emphasizing that disagreement and uncertainty are expected — this is diagnosis, not assessment.
4. Deliver instruction (reading, lecture) that defines the terms and presents the expert structure.
5. Reconvene groups to revise their sorts, explicitly comparing their original categories to the formal ones.
6. Close with a whole-class discussion of which categories changed and why, connecting the revised sort to what comes next in the course.

## Examples
- An introductory psychology course sorts terms from a cognition unit (*encoding, retrieval, chunking, interference, primacy effect…*) before the memory lecture; groups revise sorts afterward and the revised sort becomes their study sheet for the exam.
- A nursing pharmacology course has students sort drug classes by guessed mechanism before instruction, then re-sort by actual mechanism, making the misclassifications themselves the teaching content.
- Origin: the activity is documented in Barkley's *Student Engagement Techniques* as a classroom engagement technique for college settings.
