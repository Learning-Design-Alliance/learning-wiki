# Fidget Tools

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Fidget tools are small, quiet, hand-held or foot-operated objects that learners manipulate while listening, reading, or working. The rationale is that some learners — particularly those with ADHD or high trait fidgeting — experience improved attention when excess motor energy is discharged through a low-cognitive-demand outlet, keeping the primary task from competing with the need to move.

## Design Implications

The evidence base is thin and mixed. Some studies find modest attention benefits for learners with elevated hyperactivity [~W], while others find that novel or visually interesting fidgets act as attentional competitors and reduce task performance [-W]. The critical design variable is the tool itself: a fidget must be *less* interesting than the lesson. Novelty (e.g., a new fidget spinner) reliably draws attention away from instruction [-W], whereas familiar, low-stimulation items (putty, a soft ball) are less intrusive [~W]. Fidget tools should be treated as one accommodation within a broader set of movement and attention supports, not a standalone intervention.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Quiet, non-disruptive manipulation (no clicking, spinning visible to others, or throwing)
- Low novelty and low visual salience relative to the instructional content
- Clear norms for use, ideally co-developed with learners ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) conversations about whether the tool helps or distracts)
- Framing as an [Accommodation](../elements/accommodations.md) for learners who need it, not a universal requirement

#### Constraints
- Novel or visually engaging fidgets reduce attention to instruction, especially for learners *without* hyperactivity [-W]
- Benefits do not generalize: effects found for learners with ADHD do not transfer to whole-class distribution [~W]
- Whole-class mandates ("everyone gets a fidget") can add distraction and off-task behavior for the majority [-W]
- No consistent evidence that fidget tools improve learning outcomes (retention, test performance) rather than proximal on-task behavior [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual accommodation (IEP/504-style) vs. classroom-wide availability
- Hand-held tools vs. whole-body alternatives: foot bands, standing desks, [Active Recess](../strategies/active-recess.md) breaks, or scheduled movement
- Learner-selected tools ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) vs. teacher-assigned tools — selection supports autonomy, consistent with [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) [+M], but only if choices are pre-screened for low distraction

### Target Learners
- Learners with ADHD or high hyperactivity/impulsivity, for whom fidgeting may serve a self-regulatory function [~W]
- Learners with sensory regulation needs, as one option within a broader [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) plan
- Not recommended as a default for learners without attention difficulties, where evidence suggests neutral-to-negative effects [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning-goal strategy per se; serves self-regulation and classroom participation as prerequisites for goals in attention-dependent tasks (listening, sustained reading, independent work)

### Instructions
1. Identify learners whose attention or participation is limited by the need to move; consult the learner directly ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
2. Select or offer low-novelty, quiet tools; avoid spinners, lights, or anything visually salient.
3. Establish explicit use norms: silent, in one hand, below the desk, tool stays with the learner.
4. Pair with movement alternatives — scheduled breaks, [Active Recess](../strategies/active-recess.md), standing options — rather than relying on the tool alone.
5. Review after 1–2 weeks: compare on-task behavior and work completion with and without the tool; discontinue if no benefit.

## Examples
- A fourth-grade teacher keeps a basket of putty, soft balls, and textured strips; learners with an agreed accommodation take one during read-alouds, with a two-week review of on-task data.
- Foot bands on chair legs in a middle-school inclusion classroom, allowing leg movement without visible or audible distraction to peers.
- Stress-ball use during standardized testing for a student with a documented ADHD accommodation, per their 504 plan.
