# Good Behavior Game

## Description
The Good Behavior Game (GBG) is a group-contingency classroom management strategy: the class is divided into teams, rules for disruptive behavior are posted, and teams earn (or avoid losing) privileges by staying under a preset criterion of behavior marks during a defined activity period. Because consequences depend on the whole team's conduct, peer attention shifts toward on-task behavior rather than disruption. It is carried out by the teacher during ordinary instruction, typically in short sessions that are gradually lengthened.

## Design Implications

GBG applies group contingency and reinforcement principles to reduce disruption and increase academic engaged time, with effects documented across dozens of classroom studies [Behaviorism](../principles/behaviorism.md) [+S]. Its power comes from restructuring peer dynamics: students who might otherwise reinforce disruption instead cue teammates toward compliance, making the intervention largely self-sustaining once routines are established [+M]. Longitudinal research links early GBG exposure to reduced aggression and later risk of substance-use and antisocial outcomes, suggesting effects beyond immediate classroom order [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicitly stated, observable behavior rules (e.g., no out-of-seat, no talking out of turn)
- A visible tallying method and a criterion announced in advance
- A menu of team rewards deliverable immediately after the game period
- Balanced teams and a teacher routine for marking infractions neutrally, without shaming individuals

#### Constraints
- Rewards based solely on team outcomes can pressure or stigmatize students whose self-regulation is weakest; teams should be structured so no single student is scapegoated [~M]
- Overly strict criteria or long game periods early on produce frustration and can increase problem behavior [-M]
- Effects depend on consistent, neutral implementation; punitive or inconsistent marking erodes the peer-support mechanism [-M]
- Rewards that are too delayed or too weak fail to sustain behavior change [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **PAX Good Behavior Game** — adds "tootles" (positive notes) and self-regulation language; widely disseminated version
- **Inclusive/individualized GBG** — teams earn points for prosocial acts, or individual goals are embedded for high-need students
- **Fading** — sessions lengthen from minutes to full periods, and tangible rewards shift to natural contingencies (praise, activity preferences)
- **Teacher-vs-student scoring** — older students can take over tallying, building self-monitoring

### Target Learners
- Elementary students, especially early grades where disruptive behavior is being shaped [+S]
- Classrooms with high rates of off-task or disruptive behavior [+M]
- Students with externalizing behavior problems benefit, but only when team structures prevent them from becoming the team's designated liability [~M]
- Less suited to settings where disruption stems from unmet instructional needs rather than social contingencies [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Increased academic engaged time and on-task behavior as a precondition for academic learning [+S]
- Self-regulation and inhibition — students practice tolerating delay and monitoring their own behavior [+M]
- Prosocial peer norms and cooperative classroom climate [+M]

### Instructions
1. Define 3–4 observable rules with the class and post them; model examples and non-examples of each rule.
2. Divide the class into balanced teams; consider [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) to give every student a role and reduce scapegoating.
3. Announce the criterion (e.g., fewer than 5 team marks) and the reward, keeping initial game periods short (5–10 minutes).
4. During the activity, mark infractions neutrally and visibly; avoid lecturing or singling out individuals.
5. At the end, deliver team rewards immediately and briefly; teams under criterion win.
6. Gradually lengthen game periods, raise criteria, and fade tangible rewards toward natural contingencies, consistent with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) feedback on teacher implementation fidelity.
7. Use brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines to review team functioning and repair team climate when needed.

## Examples
- **PAX Good Behavior Game** (Paxis Institute) — disseminated in thousands of US schools; teacher training plus kernel-approach tools. https://www.paxis.org
- **Barrish, Saunders & Wolf (1969)** — the original demonstration in a fourth-grade classroom, reducing out-of-seat and talking-out behavior during math using a team tally on the blackboard.
- **Baltimore Prevention Program / Kellam trials** — GBG delivered in first-grade classrooms as part of a randomized preventive trial tracking students into adolescence and adulthood.
