# Correcting Students In A Constructive Way

## Description
Correcting students constructively means responding to academic errors or behavioral missteps with feedback that is specific, behavior-focused, and forward-looking, rather than punitive or person-focused. The correction names what went wrong, why it matters, and what to do next, while explicitly protecting the student's sense of competence and belonging. It treats errors as information for learning rather than evidence of failure.

## Design Implications

Constructive correction works because feedback directed at the task and process — not the self — improves learning, while person-level praise or criticism produces inconsistent and often null effects [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. A large meta-analysis found that roughly a third of feedback interventions actually *reduced* performance, with the most damaging being feedback that directed attention to the self rather than the task [Kluger & DeNisi's meta-analysis of feedback interventions](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) [-S]. Corrections that preserve self-efficacy matter doubly because students' belief in their capability predicts persistence through difficulty [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear behavioral or academic standard the correction can reference ("the rule is X," "the goal was Y") — corrections land as fair when anchored to expectations, not teacher mood
- Privacy or low-visibility delivery for most corrections; public correction invites face-threat and power struggle
- A next step the student can actually take, converting the correction into actionable guidance
- Teacher language that separates behavior from identity ("that choice" vs. "you are")

#### Constraints
- Vague or global criticism ("careless work," "you never listen") directs attention to the self and can depress performance below no-feedback baselines [Kluger & DeNisi, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) [-S]
- Harsh public correction can suppress the behavior temporarily while undermining relationship and intrinsic motivation, especially when it thwarts autonomy and competence needs [~M]
- Over-correction of every error removes productive struggle; some errors are better surfaced and analyzed than prevented [High-confidence errors, when corrected, improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M]
- Correction without follow-up fades quickly; behavioral gains require consistent, low-drama enforcement over time rather than intensity

#### Implementation Variability
- **Academic correction:** use erroneous examples — display a flawed solution and have students find and fix the error — turning correction into analysis [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **Behavioral correction:** private, brief, specific restatement of expectation plus a reset opportunity; escalate minimally
- **Self-correction prompts:** ask "what would you check first?" to shift monitoring responsibility to the student [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- **Deferred correction:** note the error and address it later in whole-class form (anonymized) when the error is common and public discussion would help more than the individual

### Target Learners
- Novices, who need error information at the task level to avoid entrenching misconceptions [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Students with fragile self-efficacy, for whom person-focused criticism triggers disengagement rather than effort [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less effective as pure external correction for advanced learners, who benefit more from prompts to self-evaluate than from direct correction [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural and conceptual accuracy: eliminating misconceptions before they consolidate
- Self-regulation: internalizing standards so students monitor and correct their own work [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Classroom community norms: maintaining behavioral expectations without damaging relationships

### Instructions
1. Establish the standard first — students should know the expectation before it is enforced, so correction reads as predictable rather than arbitrary ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Catch the error early and deliver the correction privately or discreetly, describing the gap between the work/behavior and the standard.
3. Name the behavior, not the person: "The paragraph is missing a claim" rather than "You're not trying."
4. Attach a process-level next step the student can execute immediately ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Where the error is instructive, convert it into analysis — an [erroneous example](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) the class examines together.
6. Follow up with a brief [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) to confirm the student recovered, signaling the relationship is intact.

## Examples
- **Doug Lemov's *Teach Like a Champion* "Positive Framing"** — narrating what students should do rather than what they did wrong, keeping corrections brief and anonymous ("Check your punctuation in sentence two" rather than naming the offender).
- **Math error-analysis routines** — publishing an anonymized common error from a problem set and having students diagnose it, converting individual correction into collective learning.
- **Responsive Classroom "Teacher Language"** — reinforcing, reminding, and redirecting language tiers that keep behavioral corrections brief, specific, and relationship-preserving (https://www.responsiveclassroom.org).
