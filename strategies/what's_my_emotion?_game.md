# What's My Emotion? Game

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Using an EQ Feeling Chart, students first identify how they are feeling individually. Then a volunteer demonstrates that feeling through body language and facial expressions only — no words — and the whole class tries to guess what the student is feeling. The activity combines private emotion labeling with public non-verbal expression and group interpretation.

## Design Implications

The game builds two complementary skills: emotional granularity (accurately naming one's own state) and non-verbal decoding (reading facial expression and body language in others). Emotion labeling itself supports self-regulation — putting feelings into words reduces amygdala reactivity and improves behavioral control [~M] — while accurate emotion recognition in peers underpins empathy and prosocial behavior [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. The demonstration-and-guess format also enacts observational learning: students watch peers encode and decode emotional signals, acquiring the skill vicariously as well as directly.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An EQ Feeling Chart (e.g., RULER Mood Meter or a simpler happy/sad/angry/scared grid) visible to all students
- Time for individual reflection before the demonstration phase
- A volunteer willing to demonstrate; a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine so reflection feels habitual rather than exposed
- Norms for respectful guessing — descriptions of what was observed ("your shoulders went up"), not judgments of the person

#### Constraints
- Students who are uncomfortable being observed may experience the public demonstration as exposure rather than play; forcing participation can undermine the sense of safety that makes disclosure possible [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [-M] — participation should be voluntary with an opt-out (e.g., pointing to a chart face instead of acting)
- Students with limited emotional vocabulary or alexithymia-like difficulties may not accurately identify or demonstrate their feelings; the game can frustrate rather than develop them without scaffolding (chart labels, sentence stems)
- Cultural display rules vary: the same emotion may be expressed differently across cultures, so "wrong" guesses may reflect decoder assumptions rather than encoder error — worth naming explicitly
- As a repeated routine, novelty wears off; effectiveness depends on varying the emotion set and deepening the debrief

#### Implementation Variability
- **Silent version:** all students point to a face on the chart simultaneously; no individual demonstration
- **Small-group version:** demonstrations happen in groups of 4–6, lowering the social stakes
- **Reverse round:** the teacher demonstrates and students guess, modeling the skill and taking pressure off students
- **Literature link:** students act out how a character feels at a story moment, connecting emotion identification to [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) comprehension work
- **Older students:** add a debrief on display rules — when is it appropriate to show what you feel?

### Target Learners
- K–12; especially effective in upper elementary and middle school where emotional vocabulary is still consolidating
- Students who struggle to articulate emotions verbally — the non-verbal channel gives them a route in [~M]
- Groups with intergroup tension or low cohesion, since accurately reading peers builds empathy [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for students with acute social anxiety around peer observation

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional self-awareness: identifying and labeling one's current state
- Non-verbal communication: expressing and decoding facial expressions and body language
- Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' internal states from observable cues
- Classroom climate: normalizing talk about feelings as a routine part of learning

### Instructions
1. Display the EQ Feeling Chart and give students 1–2 minutes of private reflection to identify their current feeling ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
2. Invite a volunteer to choose a feeling and demonstrate it using only facial expression and body language ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)).
3. The class guesses the emotion; ask guessers to cite the observable cue that informed their guess ("your eyebrows went down") rather than just naming a word.
4. The volunteer confirms or corrects, optionally adding nuance ("close — I was more frustrated than angry").
5. Rotate volunteers across sessions; vary the emotion set, including low-intensity states (curious, tired, proud), not only high-arousal ones.

## Examples
- **[RULER Mood Meter](https://www.rulerapproach.org)** (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — a feeling chart on which this activity can be based; RULER classrooms use daily mood check-ins followed by discussion of emotional cues.
- **Second Step (Committee for Children)** — K–8 SEL curriculum that includes emotion-card guessing games in which students identify feelings from facial and body cues.
- **Morning Meeting (Responsive Classroom)** — the greeting and sharing phases often incorporate emotion identification activities structurally similar to this game.

## Assessment Evidence
- Observe growth in the specificity of emotion labels students use over time (from "good/bad" toward granular terms)
- Track accuracy of peer guesses as a proxy for class-wide decoding skill
- Collect student feedback on comfort and perceived safety, especially from volunteers
