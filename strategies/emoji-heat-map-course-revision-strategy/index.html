Use an emoji heat map to revise course content and plan classes around student reactions¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (2 for, 3 mixed) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 design), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that instructional teams use the emoji heat map to decide where to intervene in discussions and which reading sections to revise or expand. Participants stated they were willing to use it "for revising course content and for planning classes while a course is in session", and one noted it can show aggregated reaction information otherwise achievable only by reading a large number of comments. The strategy suits large-enrollment courses where comment volume overwhelms simpler tools.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A collaborative annotation platform with emoji self-reporting and the heat map visualization
  • Students must be prompted to use emoji in their posts (in FYBIO, emoji use earned additional credit)

Constraints¶

  • The heat map was less helpful for identifying difficult sections when students rarely use the #lost emoji; the authors suggest pairing it with emoji filtering
  • It scored low (2.4) as a tool for finding struggling individual students

Target Learners¶

  • instructors and teaching teams of large-enrollment courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • identifying reading sections needing revision or clarification
  • planning lectures and interventions around student reactions

Claims¶

  • Emoji use aligns with the cognitive engagement category of the posts carrying them [~W]
  • In a five-participant user study, instructors found the emoji heat map saved time and helped identify interesting and difficult reading sections [+W]
  • Most student posts in the emoji-annotated biology course contained at least one emoji, with #question and #i-think used most often [~W]
  • Instructors found the heat map better aligned with sections they believed students found interesting (4.4) than difficult (3.6) [~W]
  • Special-issue evidence: a knowledge-graph-enriched legal-writing textbook led to better learning outcomes than baselines, and aggregated emoji annotations helped teachers identify challenging text [+M]
