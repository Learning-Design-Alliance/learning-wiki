# Notice And Wonder

## Description
Notice and Wonder presents learners with a stimulus — a graph, image, data display, or problem stem — stripped of its question, and asks two open prompts: "What do you notice?" and "What do you wonder?" Learners generate observations and questions before the target task is revealed. The routine originated in mathematics education (popularized by Annie Fetter and NCTM) and is now used across disciplines to slow down sense-making and surface prior knowledge before formal problem solving.

## Design Implications

The routine works because it forces activation of prior knowledge and attention to problem structure *before* answer-seeking begins, countering the well-documented novice tendency to grab numbers and compute without modeling the situation. By removing the question, it prevents premature solution attempts and gives every learner a low-floor entry point into discourse [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning of new material.](../claims/activation.md) [+M]. Collecting notices and wonders publicly also lets the instructor orchestrate which ideas get taken up in discussion, sequencing student contributions toward the target concept.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, question-free stimulus with enough ambiguity to generate genuine wondering
- Public recording of responses (board, shared doc) so contributions are visible and buildable
- A planned follow-up that connects selected student ideas to the target task
- Sufficient [wait time](wait-time.md) after each prompt — several seconds minimum — for idea generation

#### Constraints
- Without a deliberate follow-up linking student ideas to the target task, the routine becomes a warm-up ritual with no learning payoff [~M]
- Open prompts can drift into low-value observations ("I notice it's blue") if the instructor does not model substantive noticing early on
- Time cost is real; overuse on trivial stimuli erodes its value and student investment
- Less effective when the stimulus is so constrained that the "hidden" question is obvious — wondering collapses into guessing the teacher's intent

#### Implementation Variability
- **Silent individual first, then share:** private noticing before public sharing increases participation breadth
- **What do you notice? only:** for dense stimuli (e.g., a complex graph), a single prompt reduces load
- **Wonder-to-problem handoff:** the instructor selects a student "wonder" and reframes it as the day's problem, giving learners ownership of the question
- **Digital variants:** annotation tools (e.g., Desmos activity builder screens) collect notices asynchronously

### Target Learners
- Novices and math-anxious learners, who benefit from an entry point with no wrong answers and from attending to structure before computation [~M]
- Multilingual learners — the routine values observational language before technical vocabulary
- Whole-class settings where the instructor needs a window into heterogeneous prior knowledge [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning of new material.](../claims/activation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Problem comprehension: modeling a situation before solving it
- Conceptual sense-making: attending to structure, relationships, and anomalies in a representation
- Discourse norms: building a classroom culture where student ideas drive instruction

### Instructions
1. Select or design a stimulus and strip away the question or task demand.
2. Display the stimulus and ask "What do you notice?" Allow silent thinking time, then collect and publicly record responses without evaluation.
3. Ask "What do you wonder?" and record questions, resisting the urge to filter.
4. Reveal or select the target question — ideally one connected to a student-generated wonder — and transition into [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or problem solving.
5. Debrief by returning to the recorded notices/wonders to connect student ideas to the solution.

## Examples
- **NCTM / Illustrative Mathematics routines** — Notice and Wonder is a named instructional routine in the [Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org) K–12 curriculum, typically opening lessons with a graph or context before the task.
- **The Math Forum "Problems of the Week"** — the original home of the routine; scenarios presented without questions so students formulate what is being asked.
- **Data science warm-ups** — presenting a scatterplot of real data and collecting notices before teaching trend or correlation concepts.
