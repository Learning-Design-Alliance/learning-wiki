# Stations: Interacting with Multiple Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Small groups rotate through a sequence of stations, each presenting a different resource — informational text, poetry, art, photography, maps, video, or audio — related to a single event, theme, or anchor question. At each station, learners engage the material and complete a short task before rotating. A whole-group debrief follows, where learners synthesize across stations. The strategy works well to launch a new unit or to deepen study of an already-introduced topic.

## Design Implications

Stations exploit the benefits of multiple, contrasting representations: encountering the same idea through different texts and modalities supports abstraction of the underlying concept and guards against the distortions of any single source [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying principles.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The rotation structure also embeds movement and varied activity, which sustains attention relative to extended single-format seatwork [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture-only formats.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Because each station is short and self-contained, the design must manage load carefully: one focused task per station, with clear instructions, prevents the variety itself from becoming distracting [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A coherent anchor question or theme that every station addresses, so the rotation builds one understanding rather than a collection of disconnected impressions
- Diverse, curated resources per station (texts, images, maps, audio, video) with a short, specific task at each — not just "read and discuss"
- An [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or framing question before rotation begins, so learners know what to look for across stations
- A recording sheet or response format that prompts learners to connect each station back to the anchor question
- A structured debrief ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)) where cross-station synthesis happens

#### Constraints
- Preparation is heavy: gathering, copying, and organizing materials for 4–6 stations takes significant teacher time, and poorly curated stations produce shallow engagement [-M]
- If station tasks are generic ("summarize this"), learners extract surface details rather than building cross-station connections; tasks must require comparison to the anchor question [~M]
- Time pressure during rotation can truncate processing — learners who have not finished a text before rotating encode it incompletely, and the variety of media can increase extraneous load for novices if tasks are not tightly scoped [-M]
- Media variety alone does not improve learning; combinations of media affect recall and retention only when each format is used for what it does best [Media combinations affect recall and retention depending on how they are paired.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Launch vs. deepen:** as a unit launch, stations function like contrasting cases that create readiness for instruction ([A Time for Telling](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4)); as a deepening activity, stations extend and complicate an existing schema
- **Jigsaw variant:** each group becomes expert at one station, then regroups to teach others — trading breadth-first exposure for depth-first expertise
- **Leveled stations:** identical anchor question with tiered text complexity at each station for heterogeneous groups
- **Digital stations:** QR codes or a learning-management-system module deliver audio/video stations; enables asynchronous completion but loses the physical rotation's energy

### Target Learners
- Middle and secondary learners (grades 6–12) with enough reading independence to process a short text in 5–10 minutes
- Heterogeneous groups benefit from built-in [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) at each station, where stronger readers can support others [~M]
- Novices to a topic benefit most from the contrasting-representations structure; learners with strong prior knowledge may find short, varied excerpts fragmentary unless tasks demand integration [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Multi-perspective understanding: seeing one event or question through multiple genres, voices, and media [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying principles.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Source evaluation and synthesis across texts
- Conceptual understanding deepened by prompted reflection at each station [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Define the anchor question and select 4–6 resources that each offer a *different* perspective or modality on it.
2. Open with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md): present the anchor question and the recording sheet so learners rotate with a purpose.
3. At each station, learners engage the resource, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or responding to a station-specific task tied to the anchor question.
4. Groups discuss briefly at each station ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)) before rotating on a timed signal.
5. After rotation, run a whole-group [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) in which groups synthesize across stations: What did the photograph show that the poem could not? Where did sources agree or conflict?
6. Close with an individual synthesis task (short write, claim-evidence paragraph) to consolidate learning.

## Examples
- **Facing History and Ourselves** uses this stations structure extensively in its published teaching strategies (e.g., "Analyzing Visual Images" stations for studying the Weimar Republic), pairing primary-source photos, speeches, and newspaper excerpts with reflection prompts: [facinghistory.org](https://www.facinghistory.org/resource-library/teaching-strategies)
- **Reading Like a Historian (Stanford History Education Group)** lessons adapt naturally to stations: each station presents one document about a historical question (e.g., the Ludlow Massacre), and groups source, contextualize, and corroborate across stations before a corroborating debrief: [sheg.stanford.edu](https://sheg.stanford.edu)
- A middle-school ELA unit on *refugee experiences* might run five stations — a news article, a first-person memoir excerpt, a data map of displacement, a recorded interview, and a poem — all answering "What does it mean to be forced to leave home?" with a synthesis paragraph as the closing task.
