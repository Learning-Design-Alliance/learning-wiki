# Learner-Centric Approach

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A learner-centric approach positions the learner, rather than the instructor or a fixed curriculum sequence, as the primary decision-maker in learning. Problems are presented as open-ended — there are several viable solution paths, each leading to different outcomes — and learners must analyze the problem, select and justify an approach, and live with the consequences. The instructor's role shifts from transmitter to facilitator, providing resources, [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), and feedback as learners construct understanding through [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md).

## Design Implications

Learner-centric designs draw on constructivist and self-determination research: autonomy and relevance increase intrinsic motivation and engagement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], and active participation improves exam performance relative to lecture alone [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. However, the approach works only when learners have sufficient prior knowledge to navigate open-endedness; for novices, unguided exploration imposes extraneous load and can leave misconceptions uncorrected [Minimal-guidance instruction is less effective for novices than explicit instruction.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S]. The design problem is therefore not *whether* to center the learner but *how much structure* to provide at each point.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An open-ended problem with multiple defensible solution paths and meaningful consequences for each
- Access to resources learners can consult independently (reference materials, data, experts)
- A facilitator who monitors progress and intervenes strategically rather than lecturing continuously
- A supportive environment that permits productive failure and treats wrong turns as data

#### Constraints
- Novices without domain schemas flounder in fully open-ended tasks; guidance must be scaffolded in [Minimal-guidance instruction is less effective for novices than explicit instruction.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [-S]
- Open-ended problems take more instructional time and smaller facilitator-to-learner ratios than direct instruction
- Without structured debriefs, learners may complete activities without extracting transferable principles
- Assessment is harder: divergent solutions resist rubric standardization and can mask gaps in foundational knowledge

#### Implementation Variability
- **Problem-based learning**: open problem precedes instruction; learners identify what they need to learn
- **Case-based formats**: a bounded real case constrains the space while preserving choice ([Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md))
- **Choice within structure**: learners select topics, pathways, or deliverables inside a fixed framework — a middle path that preserves autonomy without abandoning sequencing
- **Self-directed study**: learners set their own goals and pace, appropriate mainly for experienced learners

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate-to-strong prior knowledge who can evaluate options without drowning in search [Minimal-guidance instruction is less effective for novices than explicit instruction.](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Adult and professional learners whose experience supplies the context open problems require
- Learners with low self-efficacy benefit from early, achievable choices that build confidence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Pure novices need more structure initially; learner-centricity should be faded in rather than started with

### Target Learning Goals
- Problem-solving and adaptive expertise: choosing among approaches, not just executing one
- Transfer: applying knowledge to ill-structured, real-world situations
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own approach
- Motivation and engagement: tasks perceived as relevant and autonomous [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Present an authentic open-ended problem with visible stakes and multiple viable outcomes ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. Activate relevant prior knowledge so learners enter the problem with something to build on ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
3. Have learners analyze the problem and commit to a solution path, articulating their reasoning ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
4. Support exploration with facilitator coaching and peer input rather than answers ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
5. Debrief outcomes — both successes and failures — to consolidate principles learners can transfer ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
6. Assess both the solution quality and the reasoning process ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))

## Related Strategies
- Contrast with instructor-centered sequencing: [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — the structured alternative that better serves novices; the two are complements across a learning sequence, not rivals
- [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) — frees contact time for learner-centric application by moving exposition outside class
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — a structured way to make learner exploration productive through modeling and fading

## Examples
- **Problem-based learning in medical education** (e.g., the Maastricht and McMaster curricula): small groups work through authentic patient cases, deciding what to investigate and which diagnoses to pursue, with a tutor facilitating rather than lecturing.
- **Harvard Business School case method** ([Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)): students must take a position on an open business problem and defend it against peers, with each defensible decision leading to different consequences.
- **Project-based courses** such as Buck Institute's PBLWorks model (https://www.pblworks.org): learners choose their approach to a real-world driving question with milestone checkpoints and rubrics.
