# Debriefing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), `q4` · 2 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Debriefing is a facilitated, structured reflection conducted after a learning experience — most prominently simulation, but also case discussions, role-plays, games, and complex tasks. Learners and facilitator jointly examine what happened, why it happened, and what should change next time, converting raw experience into articulated principles. It is typically conversational and learner-led, with the facilitator probing rather than lecturing.

## Design Implications

Debriefing operationalizes the reflection stage of experiential learning: experience alone produces weak and sometimes erroneous learning, while structured reflection consolidates correct insights and corrects misconceptions [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on psychological safety and on facilitator skill in eliciting learner reasoning rather than delivering verdicts. Feedback delivered during debriefing is most effective when it addresses the task and the learner's self-regulation rather than the person [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared, concrete experience to reflect on (simulation, case, performance, project)
- A facilitator skilled in open questioning and withholding premature judgment
- Psychological safety — learners must believe candor about errors will not be punished
- Adequate time; rushed debriefs degrade into recitation of events
- A structure (e.g., phases: reaction → analysis → application) so reflection does not remain at the level of feelings

#### Constraints
- Unstructured "how did that feel?" debriefs produce emotional catharsis but little transfer [-M]
- Facilitator-led lecturing during debriefing suppresses the self-explanation that drives learning [~M]
- In low-safety environments, learners conceal errors, so the debrief analyzes a sanitized version of events [-M]
- Debriefing after very short or trivial tasks adds overhead without proportional benefit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Plus-Delta / After-Action Review**: rapid structured formats (what was planned, what happened, why, what next) suited to workplace and military settings
- **Video-assisted debriefing**: replaying recorded performance; helpful for making behavior concrete, but not consistently superior to verbal debriefing [~W]
- **Peer debriefing**: learners facilitate for one another, trading facilitator expertise for scalability
- **In-situ vs. deferred**: immediate debriefing leverages vivid memory; deferred debriefing allows emotional distance for high-stakes events

### Target Learners
- Professionals in high-stakes domains (healthcare, aviation, emergency response) where errors must be examined without blame
- Learners with enough experience to have something to analyze; complete novices may lack the schema to interpret their own performance [~M]
- Groups, since hearing peers' divergent readings of the same event exposes hidden assumptions

### Target Learning Goals
- Integration of experience into principles (experiential learning cycle completion)
- Error analysis and clinical/professional judgment
- Team coordination and communication behaviors
- Metacognitive habits: self-monitoring and self-correction after action

### Instructions
1. Stage the experience — a [Case Study](../elements/case-study.md), simulation, role-play, or complex task with genuine decision points.
2. Open with reactions, briefly: let learners vent and surface first impressions before analysis, establishing safety.
3. Elicit description before judgment: "What happened? What did you notice?" — keep the facilitator in [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) mode, asking rather than telling.
4. Analyze: probe the reasoning behind actions and the gap between intended and actual outcomes; introduce frames, evidence, or expert comparison only after learner analysis stalls.
5. Consolidate: name the principles that emerged and connect them to prior instruction or [Case-Based Learning](../elements/case-based-learning.md) materials.
6. Apply forward: have each learner commit to one specific change for the next attempt, then close the loop in a subsequent session.

## Examples
- **Simulation-based healthcare education** — Post-scenario debriefing is considered the pedagogical heart of simulation; programs following Fanning & Gaba's framework treat the scenario as merely the stimulus for the debrief.
- **[After-Action Review](after-action-review.md) (AAR)** — The U.S. Army's structured post-mission review (what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, what to sustain/improve), widely adopted in corporate and safety training.
- **Harvard Business School case method** — The post-discussion wrap-up in which the instructor consolidates the board plan and takeaways functions as a debrief of the case discussion ([Case-Based Learning — Harvard Method](../patterns/case-based-learning.md)).
