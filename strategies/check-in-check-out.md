# Check In Check Out

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Check In Check Out (CICO) is a targeted, small-group behavioral intervention for students at risk of persistent problem behavior. At the start of each day the student meets briefly (2–3 minutes) with a coordinator — often a non-classroom adult — to review goals and collect a point card; at the end of the day they return to review points, celebrate success, and take the card home for a parent signature. Throughout the day, each teacher rates the student's behavior against 2–3 explicit goals and delivers immediate, positive feedback.

## Design Implications

CICO works because it compresses the feedback loop: behavior that would otherwise be evaluated at report time is rated and reinforced within minutes, and the adult relationship adds a motivational layer to the contingency system [Feedback is most effective when delivered at the task and process levels with immediacy.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The daily check-in also functions as a low-cost relational intervention — a consistent, caring adult contact that can improve students' sense of belonging and connectedness [Brief interventions that strengthen students' sense of being seen reduce disciplinary outcomes.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A designated CICO coordinator with 5–10 minutes available at the start and end of each day
- A simple Daily Progress Report (point card) with 2–3 positively stated, observable behavioral goals (e.g., "worked with teacher's directions," "kept hands to self")
- Teacher buy-in: classroom staff must rate the card consistently and deliver feedback non-punitively
- A data system for daily point totals so the team can judge response within 4–6 weeks
- A home component: card goes home daily so caregivers can reinforce

#### Constraints
- Ineffective for students whose behavior is maintained primarily by escape from academic demands rather than adult attention — CICO's reinforcement structure assumes attention-seeking motivation [~S]; function-based matching matters more than intervention fidelity alone
- Fails when teachers rate inconsistently or use the card as a threat ("you'll lose points"), converting a positive feedback system into a punishment cue [-M]
- Not sufficient for students with intensive, multi-setting needs; non-response should trigger escalation to Tier 3 supports rather than repeating CICO [-S]
- Gains often fade after withdrawal unless supports are systematically faded and classroom contingencies take over [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **CICO with individualized goals** — point card items tailored to the student's specific function of behavior
- **CICO with self-monitoring** — students rate themselves before the teacher rates, comparing scores to build self-regulation [Self-regulated-learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- **Check and Connect-style mentoring** — adds a persistent mentor relationship across years for disengaged students
- **Digital variants** — platforms such as [Schoolrunner](https://www.schoolrunner.org) or [Hero by SchoolMint](https://www.schoolmint.com/hero) automate point tracking and family communication

### Target Learners
- Students in grades K–8 with mild, attention-maintained problem behavior who respond to adult praise and structure [+S]
- Students who are not dangerous or severely disruptive but are at risk of escalation without support
- Students who benefit from frequent adult contact and clear expectations [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for students at risk of disconnection.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: meeting explicit expectations across settings
- Goal-setting and self-monitoring habits
- School connectedness and positive adult relationships

### Instructions
1. Identify students through office discipline referral data and teacher nomination; confirm the behavior is likely attention-maintained.
2. Design the Daily Progress Report with 2–3 positively stated goals aligned to school-wide expectations ([Check-in](../elements/check-in.md) as the structural element).
3. Hold a brief orientation meeting with the student, explaining the card, goals, and daily routine; frame it as support, not surveillance.
4. Run the morning check-in: greet, review yesterday's points, set a realistic point goal for today, hand over the card.
5. Have each teacher rate the card at a consistent time and pair the rating with specific verbal feedback ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)).
6. Run the afternoon check-out: total points, compare to goal, deliver praise or brief problem-solving, send the card home.
7. Review point data weekly with the intervention team; after 4–6 weeks, decide to maintain, modify, fade, or escalate ([Fading](../elements/fading.md) — shift from daily to weekly check-ins as points stabilize).

## Examples
- A middle school runs CICO from its PBIS framework: 40 students check in with the front-office counselor each morning; students earning 80% of points for a week earn a brief preferred activity, and point data drives the student-support team's Tier 3 referrals.
- **Check & Connect** ([University of Minnesota, ICI](https://checkandconnect.umn.edu)) — an extended mentoring variant with a "monitor" who maintains contact with disengaged students over multiple years.
