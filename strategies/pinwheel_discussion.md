# Pinwheel Discussion

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners are divided into four groups. Three groups are assigned to represent specific points of view; members of the fourth group act as "provocateurs," tasked with keeping the discussion going and challenging claims. One speaker from each group sits facing the other speakers, forming a square in the center of the room; remaining group members sit in a wedge behind their speaker, so the arrangement resembles a pinwheel from above. Speakers discuss questions their groups prepared in advance, teammates may pass notes or quietly coach, and after a set interval new learners rotate into the center seats to continue the conversation.

## Design Implications

The format combines two evidence-backed mechanisms: structured peer discussion and cooperative interdependence. Because each speaker is accountable to a prepared group position and can be replaced by a teammate at rotation, individual accountability and group reward are both present — conditions that distinguish effective cooperative learning from unstructured group work [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Structured discussion formats also outperform recitation and lecture for higher-order outcomes when talk norms (reasoning aloud, building on others' ideas) are explicitly taught [Structured classroom discussion improves reasoning and achievement.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Arguing an assigned perspective — including one the learner may not hold — supports perspective-taking and can improve intergroup attitudes when positions are treated with equal legitimacy [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text, issue, or controversy rich enough to sustain multiple defensible positions
- Preparation time for groups to research their perspective and draft questions
- A physical space that can be arranged in the pinwheel formation; clear norms for coaching from behind (quiet, brief, supportive)
- Explicit talk norms — e.g., accountable talk moves such as "What's your evidence?" — taught before the first discussion

#### Constraints
- Unstructured or unprepared discussion degrades into opinion exchange with little reasoning; the preparation phase is load-bearing, not optional [-M]
- The assigned-positions structure can flatten nuance if groups are told to *win* rather than to *understand*; framing as collaborative sense-making about competing views, not debate-to-victory, preserves quality [~M]
- Difficult in small or crowded classrooms and with classes larger than ~32; rotation logistics consume time
- Dominant speakers can monopolize center seats unless rotation is enforced on a timer [-M]
- Less suitable for content with a single correct answer or for novices who lack enough background knowledge to construct any defensible position — preparation without prior knowledge produces shallow arguments [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Fishbowl variant:** two speakers per group instead of a full square, with the class as outer-circle observers
- **Digital variant:** breakout rooms with rotating "speaker" roles in video conferencing; provocateurs post challenge questions in chat
- **Silent coaching:** teammates pass written notes instead of whispering, which doubles as a writing-for-audience exercise
- **Provocateur rotation:** the fourth group can instead be an "evidence team" that fact-checks claims against assigned readings

### Target Learners
- Secondary and higher-education learners in literature, history, social sciences, and ethics, where multiple defensible interpretations exist
- Learners who benefit from rehearsal before speaking: the behind-the-speaker wedge lets quieter students observe, coach, and rotate in with prepared support
- Less effective for learners with minimal prior knowledge of the topic unless substantial reading or research precedes the discussion [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Argumentation: constructing claims supported by evidence and responding to counterarguments
- Perspective-taking: understanding how position shapes interpretation [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Collaborative reasoning and academic talk: building on others' ideas, asking for and giving reasons
- Deep comprehension of complex, contested texts and issues

### Instructions
1. Select a rich text or issue and define three distinct perspectives plus the provocateur role.
2. Assign groups and give preparation time: each group researches its perspective and drafts 2–3 discussion questions ([Expert Groups](../elements/expert-groups.md)).
3. Arrange the room: four speakers in a central square, teammates in wedges behind each speaker ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)).
4. Review talk norms and the provocateur's role in sustaining challenge ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Run the discussion in timed rounds (5–10 minutes); teammates coach quietly or via notes ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
6. Rotate new speakers into the center and continue; close with a whole-class debrief on the strongest arguments heard ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).

- **Facing History and Ourselves** ([facinghistory.org](https://www.facinghistory.org)) — uses structured multi-perspective discussion formats, including pinwheel-style "identity" and "choice" discussions, in history and civics classrooms.
- **AP Seminar / AP English Literature classrooms** — teachers use pinwheel discussions to prepare students for evidence-based argument tasks, with groups assigned to interpretive stances on a shared text.
