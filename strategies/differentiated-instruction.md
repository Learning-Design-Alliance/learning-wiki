# Differentiated Instruction

## Description
Differentiated instruction is a framework in which teachers proactively plan varied approaches to content (what students learn), process (how they work), and product (how they demonstrate learning), based on ongoing assessment of readiness, interest, and learner profile. The goal is not individualized instruction for every student, but a small number of purposeful pathways converging on the same essential learning goals.

## Design Implications

Differentiation is fundamentally an assessment-driven practice: it works only when the teacher has valid, current information about what each learner knows and can do [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Differentiation that responds to readiness manages working-memory demands by matching task difficulty to current competence, avoiding both overload and disengagement [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Giving learners structured choices among pathways can support motivation through autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], but only when the options are pedagogically equivalent.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clearly defined, non-negotiable learning goal shared by all students — differentiation varies the path, not the standard
- Ongoing diagnostic and formative [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) to establish readiness groups and detect when they shift
- Tasks at multiple levels of challenge that address the same concept ([tiered assignments](../elements/choice-boards.md), [adaptive difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md))
- Classroom routines that let the teacher work with small groups while others work independently

#### Constraints
- Differentiation without accurate readiness data becomes arbitrary grouping; misassigned tasks either bore or overwhelm learners [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Fixed ability groups produce negative effects, especially for low-achieving students; grouping must be fluid and task-specific [~S]
- Differentiating many dimensions at once multiplies teacher planning load and fragments lessons; differentiating one dimension (usually readiness) at a time is more feasible and more effective [~M]
- Whole-class explicit teaching remains necessary — differentiation supplements rather than replaces shared instruction [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Tiered assignments**: same essential question, tasks at different complexity or abstraction levels
- **[Choice boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)**: students select among equivalent pathways or products, often by interest
- **Flexible small groups**: temporary, data-driven regrouping for targeted teaching
- **Technology-mediated differentiation**: [adaptive learning](../principles/adaptive-learning.md) systems adjust item difficulty per learner ([adaptive learning improves outcomes](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M])
- **[Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)**: adjustments for learners with identified needs, a narrower and legally grounded subset of differentiation

### Target Learners
- Mixed-readiness classrooms where a single task would be too hard for some and trivial for others
- Struggling learners benefit most from increased scaffolding and reduced task complexity [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- Advanced learners benefit from extension into abstraction and transfer, not merely more of the same work
- Effects are weaker when differentiation is implemented as static tracking rather than responsive regrouping [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding at varied depths of the same essential content
- Skill fluency, where practice must be matched to current proficiency
- Transfer and application, via varied products and authentic contexts

### Instructions
1. Define the essential learning goal all students must reach; identify non-negotiable knowledge and skills.
2. Administer a short diagnostic [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) to map current readiness.
3. Design 2–4 task versions that address the same goal at different levels of scaffolding or complexity, using [cognitive load management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) to calibrate challenge.
4. Offer structured choice where interest or product can legitimately vary ([choice boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)).
5. Teach a targeted mini-lesson to one readiness group while others work independently; rotate.
6. Re-assess and regroup frequently — groups are task-specific and temporary, not fixed labels.
7. Use a common closing synthesis so all learners consolidate the shared goal.

## Related Strategies
- [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) — the primary mechanism by which readiness-based differentiation raises struggling learners' ceiling
- [Formative Assessment](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — supplies the readiness data differentiation depends on
- [Adaptive Learning](../principles/adaptive-learning.md) — automates readiness-based adjustment at scale
- [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — the whole-class core that differentiation supplements

## Examples
- **Tiered math tasks (Elementary)**: after a whole-class minilesson, students solve the same problem context at three levels — concrete manipulatives, symbolic, and multi-step generalization — grouped by a quick exit-ticket diagnostic.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-based pathways let each student work at their current level while the teacher uses dashboards to pull small groups, a technology-mediated version of readiness differentiation.
- **William & Mary language arts units (Center for Gifted Education)** — published curriculum with built-in tiered tasks and open-ended products for mixed-readiness humanities classrooms.
