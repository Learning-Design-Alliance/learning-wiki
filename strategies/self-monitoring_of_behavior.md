# Self-Monitoring of Behavior

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self-monitoring is a strategy in which learners observe, evaluate, and record their own behavior — typically on-task attention (self-monitoring of attention, SMA) or academic output (self-monitoring of performance, SMP). The learner is trained to recognize the target behavior, then cued at intervals (e.g., a timer or audio tone) to ask "Was I on task?" or "Did I meet my goal?" and tally the result. Because the act of observation itself functions as a prompt, the strategy frequently changes the behavior it measures, reducing the need for continuous instructor supervision.

## Design Implications

Self-monitoring works because it converts an external contingency into a self-regulated one: the learner becomes both observer and agent, which builds the self-observation component of self-regulated learning [Self-monitoring is a foundational self-regulation skill in SRL models.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. It draws on behaviorist principles — the recording act serves as a conditioned cue and the record as immediate feedback [Behavior is shaped by its antecedents and consequences.](../theories/behaviorism.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows self-monitoring reliably improves on-task behavior and reduces disruption, with effects comparable to teacher-managed interventions [Self-monitoring interventions improve classroom behavior.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.94.3.562) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- An operational definition of the target behavior, stated in observable terms
- A recording form or tally sheet matched to the learner's age and skill level
- A cueing method for signaling monitoring intervals (timer, audio tones, vibrating watch)
- Initial training: modeling and [practice](../elements/practice.md) of accurate self-rating, with accuracy checked against instructor ratings until agreement is high
- A goal or criterion so the learner can evaluate the record, not just collect it

#### Constraints
- Learners often rate their behavior more favorably than observers do; inflated ratings reduce the intervention's effect until accuracy training is added [Self-monitoring accuracy training improves outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.1177/001440290507200105) [~M]
- Effectiveness drops when the target behavior is vaguely defined or when intervals are too long for the learner's attention span
- Requires sustained instructor support during the training phase; premature withdrawal of cues and feedback leads to regression
- Reactive effects can be temporary — behavior may revert once monitoring stops unless paired with self-reinforcement or goal-setting [Self-monitoring effects fade without maintenance supports.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.94.3.562) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **SMA vs. SMP**: monitoring attention/on-task behavior vs. monitoring academic output (problems completed, words written, accuracy)
- **Cueing**: teacher-signaled, audio-signaled, or self-initiated (learner decides when to check in) — self-initiated monitoring transfers better but requires more training
- **Matching vs. counting**: learner marks whether the behavior occurred at the cue vs. tallies every occurrence
- **Self-evaluation and self-reinforcement**: adding a comparison of the record against a goal, and learner-administered rewards, strengthens maintenance

### Target Learners
- Students with attention difficulties or ADHD, where self-monitoring is among the best-supported self-regulation interventions [Self-regulation interventions improve academic performance for students with ADHD.](https://doi.org/10.1177/001440290507200105) [+S]
- K–12 learners needing support with task completion, compliance with routines, or social behavior
- Adult learners building study habits or professional self-regulation (e.g., tracking practice time, safety behaviors)
- Less appropriate as a standalone strategy for learners who cannot yet accurately discriminate the target behavior — discrimination training must come first

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: increasing on-task attention, task completion, and rule-following
- Metacognitive awareness: developing the habit of evaluating one's own performance against a standard
- Independence: shifting behavior management from instructor-controlled to learner-controlled

### Instructions
1. Define the target behavior in observable, countable terms and collect baseline data.
2. Train the learner to recognize the behavior — model examples and non-examples, then have the learner [practice](../elements/practice.md) rating while the instructor provides feedback until self-ratings match instructor ratings.
3. Introduce the recording form and cueing signal; begin with short intervals and frequent [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) moments.
4. Add a goal and self-evaluation step: the learner compares their tally to the criterion and records whether they met it.
5. Fade instructor involvement — lengthen intervals, shift to self-initiated cues, and transfer reinforcement to the learner ([assess performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) against baseline to confirm the behavior is maintained).

## Examples
- **SMA with audio cueing**: A teacher uses a tape of random tones; at each tone a student with attention difficulties marks "yes/no — was I attending?" on a card, then compares the total to a daily goal.
- **SMP for written work**: A student tallies correctly completed math problems per work period and graphs the count, targeting an improvement over their own baseline rather than peer comparison.
- **ClassDojo-style self-report**: Digital behavior-tracking tools let students self-log points against class expectations, with the teacher spot-checking agreement.
