# Summarization

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Summarization instruction explicitly teaches learners how to identify the most important information in a text, condense it, and restate it coherently in their own words. Learners then practice writing summaries across increasingly complex texts, with feedback on their selections and phrasing. Having learners write summaries of what they read is a key recommendation of the *Writing to Read* report, and summarization is an increasingly common expectation as students advance and are assigned more complex texts to read and comprehend.

## Design Implications

Summarization works because it forces generative processing: learners must select, delete, and reorganize ideas, which deepens comprehension beyond passive reading [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Explicit instruction in the rules of summarizing (delete trivial and redundant information, substitute superordinate terms, find or invent a topic sentence) produces larger gains than simply assigning summaries [Rinehart et al. found direct instruction in summarization rules improved both summary quality and study behavior [+S]. Feedback on early summaries is essential — without it, learners persist in copying verbatim or omitting main ideas [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit instruction in the elements and rules of a good summary ([Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md))
- Texts at appropriate complexity for practice, escalating over time
- Feedback on summaries, ideally identifying what was wrongly included or omitted ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
- Ongoing [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of summary quality

#### Constraints
- Assigning summaries without teaching how to summarize yields little benefit; untaught summarizers default to verbatim copying or topic-listing [-M]
- Effectiveness depends on text complexity: texts far above learners' reading level overload selection processes and produce poor summaries [-M]
- Requires significant instructional time; benefits accrue over repeated cycles, not single lessons [-W]
- As a study technique, summarization is less effective than practice testing for later recall when used without training [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Rule-based instruction (Brown & Day's five rules) versus modeling via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of expert summarizing
- Collaborative summary writing before independent practice
- Automated feedback tools such as [Summary Street](https://ls6.cs.cmu.edu/summary-street) score content coverage and paraphrasing, allowing practice without teacher turnaround time
- Oral summaries, graphic-organizer summaries, or one-sentence "gist" summaries as lower-load variants

### Target Learners
- All writers, especially those who must work with complex expository texts
- Adolescent and adult readers who have not received explicit comprehension strategy instruction
- Struggling readers benefit from the selection-and-condensation structure, which reduces the demand to hold whole texts in memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less effective for learners who already summarize fluently; instruction becomes redundant [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and text structure
- Writing skill: concise, organized restatement in one's own words
- Metacognitive monitoring: judging what is important forces evaluation of understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Model expert summarizing on a short text, verbalizing decisions about what to keep and cut ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md))
2. Teach the summarization rules explicitly: delete trivial/redundant information, substitute superordinate terms, select or invent a topic sentence
3. Have learners write summaries of progressively more complex texts ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
4. Give feedback targeting content selection and paraphrasing, not just mechanics ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
5. Assess summary quality against criteria (coverage, accuracy, concision) and connect to comprehension objectives ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

## Examples
- **[Summary Street](https://ls6.cs.cmu.edu/summary-street)** (Kintsch et al., CU Boulder) — intelligent tutoring system that evaluates student summaries against source text for content coverage and flags copying, enabling self-guided practice with automated feedback
- **[Reading Apprenticeship](https://readingapprenticeship.org)** (WestEd) — disciplinary literacy routines in which students write and compare summaries of complex subject-area texts
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown) — small-group routine in which students take turns leading summarization of text segments, with teacher modeling fading over time
