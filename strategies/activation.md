# Activation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Activation is a pre-instructional strategy that prompts learners to retrieve and articulate relevant prior knowledge before new material is presented. It can be carried out through recall questions, brainstorming, [Analogies](analogies.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), prediction tasks, or brief diagnostic quizzes. The goal is to bring existing schemas into working memory so incoming information can be connected to them rather than encoded in isolation.

## Design Implications

Activation works because new knowledge is stored in relation to existing knowledge; material that connects to an activated schema is comprehended and retained better than the same material presented cold [Prior knowledge activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. The activation must be *relevant* — surfacing knowledge that maps onto the new content — and learners must be prompted to elaborate on what they retrieve, not merely mention it. Activation also serves a diagnostic function: it exposes misconceptions and gaps the instructor can address before they interfere with new learning [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Knowledge of what learners already know that is relevant to the target content
- A prompt that requires retrieval and some elaboration (writing, discussing, predicting), not just passive exposure
- Time to process the activation activity before new material begins
- A plan for responding to what surfaces — misconceptions and gaps must be addressed, not ignored

#### Constraints
- Activating irrelevant or tangential prior knowledge can impair comprehension by loading working memory with unhelpful associations [~M]
- Activation of flawed or misconceived knowledge without correction can entrench errors; high-confidence errors are especially resistant to revision [High-confidence errors improve retention only when corrected.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~M]
- For learners with little relevant prior knowledge, activation prompts can frustrate rather than prepare; provide the missing background instead [~S]
- Overly long or elaborate activation activities consume time and attention better spent on the new material itself [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Recall prompts**: open questions ("What do you already know about…?") before a unit
- **Prediction tasks**: learners predict outcomes of a demonstration or experiment, then compare with results
- **Analogical activation**: mapping a familiar domain onto the new one ([Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md))
- **Advance organizers**: expository or comparative text presented before the material ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- **Diagnostic pre-questions**: brief quizzes that both retrieve and reveal gaps

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate prior knowledge benefit most — they have something to activate but have not yet organized it [Prior knowledge activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Learners with misconceptions, for whom activation makes faulty schemas visible and correctable [~M]
- Complete novices benefit less from retrieval-based activation and need background-building instead [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: integrating new concepts with existing knowledge structures
- Comprehension of complex text or expository material
- Misconception diagnosis and conceptual change

### Instructions
1. Identify the prior knowledge the new material depends on (concepts, experiences, or procedures).
2. Choose an activation format — recall question, prediction, analogy, or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — that targets that knowledge.
3. Prompt learners to retrieve *and elaborate*: write, discuss, or explain, not just recall ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. Surface and address misconceptions before presenting new content; correct errors explicitly.
5. Explicitly connect the activated knowledge to the new material as it is presented, naming the links.

## Examples
- **Predict-Observe-Explain (POE)** in physics teaching: students predict what a demonstration will show, commit to a prediction, observe, and reconcile discrepancies — activating (and exposing) intuitive misconceptions before formal explanation.
- **[KWL charts](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/comprehension/articles/kwl-chart)** — learners record what they *Know*, what they *Want* to know, and later what they *Learned*; the K and W columns are an activation routine.
- **Case opening in problem-based curricula** (e.g., [Maastricht University PBL](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl/education/problem-based-learning)): students brainstorm what they already know about a case before self-directed study, a structured activation phase built into the tutorial cycle.
