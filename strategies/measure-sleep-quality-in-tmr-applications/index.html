Measure and optimize sleep quality when applying targeted memory reactivation¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that sleep-quality variation, often overlooked in the TMR literature, be measured and controlled when applying TMR. Because benefits of reactivation were greater for individuals with longer slow-wave sleep and without signs of sleep disruption, "optimizing sleep quality during TMR may increase the overall size of TMR benefits on memory or other aspects of cognition," and "Measures of sleep disturbance should thus be examined closely in future studies seeking to determine if applications of targeted memory reactivation can produce clinical or other benefits."

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Objective measures such as N3 duration, sleep disruption after cues, and sleep-stage transitions per hour should be recorded alongside TMR delivery

Constraints¶

  • The article notes differences in sleep quality and disruption may explain only some instances in which TMR does not benefit memory and some variability in meta-analytic effect sizes

Target Learners¶

  • learners receiving sleep-based memory interventions, including clinical contexts where memory modification is desirable

Target Learning Goals¶

  • strengthening specific recently learned associations during sleep

Affordances¶

  • Targeted Memory Reactivation Sleep Quality Moderators

Claims¶

  • Sleep Disruption Index classifies TMR responders: participants with an index below 0.13 showed a positive recall cuing effect, those above did not show a significant one [+W]
  • N3 duration, cuing, and sleep-disruption measures are intercorrelated but represent separable facets of sleep quality in predicting the recall cuing effect [+M]
  • TMR benefits on name recall are negatively correlated with post-cue sleep disruption and post-cue beta-band EEG power, suggesting reactivation depends on undisturbed sleep [+M]
  • The TMR cuing effect on name recall is positively correlated with the duration of stage N3 (slow-wave) sleep during the nap [+M]
  • The TMR cuing effect on face recognition accuracy is associated with N3 sleep duration, though cueing did not significantly improve recognition overall [+M]
  • Closed-loop TMR timed to the slow-oscillation up-state improves memory consolidation relative to down-state cueing, though direct up-vs-down comparisons yield mixed results [+W]
