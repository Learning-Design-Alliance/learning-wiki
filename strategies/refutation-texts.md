# Refutation Texts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A refutation text explicitly names a misconception learners are likely to hold ("Many people believe heavier objects fall faster"), refutes it ("This is not the case…"), and presents the accepted explanation. The classic structure is *misconception → refutation cue → correct statement*. It is carried out by embedding this structure in expository text, lectures, or multimedia before or alongside the target content.

## Design Implications

Refutation texts work by surfacing and directly challenging misconceptions rather than ignoring them, creating the cognitive conflict that drives conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows refutation texts outperform standard expository texts for both comprehension and misconception repair, particularly when learners' misconceptions are actually activated and confronted [Guzzetti et al. (1993) meta-analysis] [+S]. Simply presenting correct information without addressing the misconception leaves the faulty model intact, and learners often assimilate the new text into their existing (wrong) framework.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Accurate knowledge of the target misconception(s) — from research literature, diagnostic questions, or [Misconception Probes](../elements/misconception-probes.md)
- Explicit refutation language ("it is a common mistake to think…", "this is not true because…") rather than merely stating the correct version
- The correct conception must be stated clearly and coherently after the refutation, not left implicit

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when learners do not actually hold the refuted misconception — the refutation is redundant and adds load [~M]
- Can backfire through the *continued influence effect*: repeating the misconception, even to refute it, can increase its familiarity and later recall if the correct explanation is weak or absent [-M]
- Less effective for learners with very low prior knowledge, who lack the schema to evaluate the refutation [~W]
- Learners with strong commitment to a belief may respond defensively rather than revising; acknowledging the plausibility of the misconception before refuting it moderates this [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-refutation**: diagnostic question or [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) activates the misconception before the text confronts it
- **Embedded**: refutation structure woven throughout a text wherever a misconception is relevant
- **Multimedia**: narration plus animation stating and refuting the misconception (e.g., physics simulations with refutational voiceover)
- **Dialogic**: [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) or [Predict-Observe-Explain](predict-observe-explain.md) activities where learners commit to the misconception before seeing it refuted

### Target Learners
- Learners who hold the target misconception, confirmed by pre-assessment [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]
- Middle school through adult learners; effects are documented across K–12 and undergraduate science populations [+S]
- Less useful for learners who already hold the correct conception, for whom standard expository text suffices [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual change: replacing intuitive but incorrect models with accepted ones
- Misconception repair in science, mathematics, and health education
- Epistemic awareness: recognizing that one's intuitions can be wrong

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the misconception with a pre-test or [Misconception Probes](../elements/misconception-probes.md) so the refutation targets a belief learners actually hold.
2. Activate the misconception explicitly — ask learners to predict or commit to an answer ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)) so the refutation lands on an engaged belief.
3. Present the refutation text: state the misconception verbatim, signal its rejection ("However, this is incorrect…"), and explain *why* it is wrong.
4. Immediately present the correct conception with a coherent causal explanation, not just a corrected fact.
5. Follow with [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or application tasks that require learners to use the corrected model, so it competes with the old one in use.

## Examples
- **Physics education**: Texts refuting "force is needed to keep an object moving" before Newtonian instruction; used widely in [Physics by Inquiry](https://www.phys.washington.edu/groups/pei/) and [Modeling Instruction](https://www.modelinginstruction.org) materials.
- **Biology**: Refutation passages addressing "trees get their mass from the soil" in photosynthesis units (e.g., materials from the [Understanding Evolution](https://evolution.berkeley.edu) project refuting "individuals evolve").
- **Climate and health misinformation**: Kowalski & Taylor's refutational texts on climate change misconceptions with undergraduates; CDC-style myth-busting formats ("Myth vs. Fact") apply the same structure.
