# Prep Activities for Shadowing

## Description
Prep activities for shadowing are short, structured tasks completed before a learner observes an expert (in a clinic, classroom, workplace, or simulation) to make the subsequent observation active rather than passive. Typical activities include activating relevant prior knowledge, previewing the task's structure and vocabulary, and setting specific observation goals or question prompts. They convert shadowing from unguided watching into a purposeful [Observation](../elements/observation.md) episode with a clear attentional agenda.

## Design Implications

Unprepared observers lack the schemas to know where to look, so much of an expert's performance is invisible or misinterpreted; prep activities supply the missing frame in advance [Advance organizers that bridge new material to existing knowledge improve retention and transfer.](../claims/activation.md) [+M]. They also reduce extraneous load during observation by pre-teaching vocabulary and task structure, freeing attention for the expert's decision-making [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Prep must be followed by debriefing and [Practice](../elements/practice.md); preparation alone does not produce skill.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear description of the task the expert will perform, including its phases and key decision points
- Observation prompts or a structured guide that directs attention to reasoning, not just actions
- Activation of relevant prior knowledge ([Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)) so the observation connects to existing schemas
- A post-observation debrief where learners compare what they saw against their expectations

#### Constraints
- Overly detailed prep scripts can consume the working memory resources the observation needs, or bias learners to see only what the guide names [~M]
- If the shadowed task is far above the learner's current competence, even good prep cannot close the gap; observation of far-too-complex performance yields little [~M]
- Without a debrief, learners retain an impression of fluency without extractable structure — watching alone produces illusory competence [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Question prompts**: learners bring 2–3 questions about *why* the expert makes particular choices
- **Structured observation guides**: checklists mapping task phases to look-fors, faded as learners gain experience
- **Pre-reading cases**: reviewing the actual patient, student, or client case beforehand so the observation is contextualized
- **Predict-then-observe**: learners predict what the expert will do at key junctures, then check against reality — a variation of [Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md)

### Target Learners
- Novices with limited prior knowledge of the domain, who otherwise cannot parse expert performance [Advance organizers that bridge new material to existing knowledge improve retention and transfer.](../claims/activation.md) [+M]
- Students in clinical, teaching, or workplace placements where observation precedes supervised practice
- Less necessary for advanced learners, who can self-direct their observation and may find structured guides constraining [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Schema formation: building a mental model of how a complex task unfolds over time
- Procedural knowledge: recognizing the phases and decision points of professional practice
- Observational learning: acquiring strategies and standards from a model [Social learning theory holds that observational learning requires attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation — prep activities target the attention and retention prerequisites.](../claims/activation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Brief learners on the task structure and key vocabulary using an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) before the observation.
2. Activate relevant prior knowledge with a short case, reading, or discussion ([Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)).
3. Distribute observation prompts directing attention to the expert's *decisions and reasoning*, ideally elicited via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) during the shadowing itself.
4. Keep prep short and focused — a few targeted prompts rather than exhaustive pre-teaching — consistent with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).
5. After shadowing, run a debrief in which learners compare observations to predictions and articulate what they would do differently; then schedule guided [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on the observed task.

## Examples
- **Clinical education**: medical and nursing students receive the patient chart and a "what would you do next?" prompt before shadowing rounds, then discuss decisions in post-round debriefs; simulation programs formalize this as prebriefing (e.g., standards from the International Nursing Association for Clinical Simulation and Learning).
- **Teacher education**: student teachers use an observation protocol (e.g., focused on questioning moves) before observing a mentor teacher's lesson, then compare notes in a seminar.
- **Workplace onboarding**: new hires at consulting firms review the engagement brief and a list of meeting objectives before shadowing a client call.
