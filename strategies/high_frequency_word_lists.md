# High Frequency Word Lists

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
High-frequency word lists (e.g., the Dolch list of 220 service words and the Fry list of the 1,000 most common English words) are collections of the words that account for the majority of running text — the first 100 Fry words alone cover roughly half of all written material. They are taught so learners can recognize these words instantly by sight, freeing attentional resources for decoding unfamiliar words and comprehending text. Many high-frequency words are phonetically irregular (e.g., *said*, *was*, *of*), so instruction combines orthographic mapping with repeated exposure rather than pure rote memorization.

## Design Implications

Automatic recognition of high-frequency words reduces the working-memory burden of reading, allowing comprehension processes to operate on meaning rather than word identification [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Words become sight words fastest when learners attend to letter–sound correspondences and spelling patterns, even for irregular words — mapping letters to sounds is more effective than visual memorization of word shapes [Ehri's phases of word reading](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532799xssr0902_5) [+S]. Practice should be brief, frequent, and spaced rather than massed [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A validated list (Dolch, Fry, or a curriculum-embedded list) matched to learner reading level
- Brief, repeated practice opportunities: flashcards, word walls, games, cloze passages, and sentence writing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Explicit teacher guidance connecting each word's spelling to its pronunciation ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md))
- Progress monitoring so instruction targets the specific words each learner has not yet automatized

#### Constraints
- Rote memorization of whole-word shapes without letter–sound analysis produces fragile learning and can interfere with decoding development [Whole-word shape memorization is less effective than orthographic mapping.](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532799xssr0902_5) [-M]
- Drill in isolation without reading connected text limits transfer; words must be encountered in meaningful context [~M]
- Overloading learners with too many new words at once exceeds working memory limits [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S] — introduce 3–5 new words per session
- Word-shape worksheets (memorizing outlines) have weak evidence compared with mapping activities [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Word walls and classroom displays for passive reinforcement
- Flashcard drills and digital apps (e.g., [Quizlet](https://quizlet.com), [Teach Your Monster to Read](https://www.teachyourmonster.org)) for spaced individual practice
- Cloze passages and sentence-writing tasks that embed words in context
- Decodable texts deliberately saturated with taught high-frequency words ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers (K–2) building a sight vocabulary [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Older struggling readers and learners with dyslexia who need targeted, structured practice on irregular words
- English language learners, who benefit from pairing words with oral language and sentence contexts [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Automatic word recognition and reading fluency
- Spelling accuracy for common words in writing
- Reduced cognitive load during reading, supporting comprehension [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Select 3–5 target words from the list that the learner cannot yet read instantly; prioritize words appearing in upcoming texts.
2. Explicitly teach each word: pronounce it, map its letters to sounds, and highlight the irregular part ("In *said*, the *ai* makes the /e/ sound") ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md)).
3. Run brief spaced practice — flashcard review, word games, or a word wall hunt — revisiting previously taught words ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Have learners read and write the words in connected text: cloze passages, dictated sentences, or decodable readers ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)).
5. Assess recognition speed (1–2 seconds per word) and retire mastered words, replacing them with new ones.

## Examples
- A first-grade teacher introduces five Fry words per week, posts them on a word wall, and runs two-minute flashcard warm-ups before guided reading, retiring words once students read them instantly in three consecutive checks.
- [Reading A–Z](https://www.readinga-z.com) decodable books and [Starfall](https://www.starfall.com) embed taught high-frequency words in short texts so learners meet them in context immediately after instruction.
- A dyslexia interventionist uses the Dolch list sorted by phonetic regularity, teaching irregular words with explicit "heart part" mapping (the part of the word that must be learned by heart).
