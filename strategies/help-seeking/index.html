Help Seeking¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Help seeking is an adaptive self-regulation strategy in which learners recognize they need assistance, formulate a request, and obtain help from a teacher, peer, or system. It is carried out through explicit requests (questions, office hours, forum posts), structured mechanisms (hint buttons, peer tutoring), and classroom norms that legitimize asking. Because it requires monitoring one's own understanding and acting on that judgment, it is classified as a core self-regulatory behavior rather than a passive receipt of support.

Design Implications¶

Help seeking depends on learners first accurately judging their own comprehension, then overcoming social and cognitive costs of admitting difficulty. Instructional designs that lower the cost of asking — anonymous channels, on-demand hints, normalized help routines — produce more frequent and more instrumental help use, while designs that make help available but effortful to interpret produce "help avoidance" or shallow "answer shopping" [~M]. The goal is not more help seeking but instrumental help seeking: requests for explanations and hints that leave the learner to do the remaining work, rather than requests for answers that bypass it.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Learners with enough metacognitive monitoring to detect when they do not understand
  • Accessible, low-threat channels for requesting help (peer structures, hint systems, office hours, forums)
  • Help content designed as graduated hints or explanations, not direct answers
  • Classroom norms that frame asking as competent behavior rather than failure

Constraints¶

  • Learners frequently avoid seeking help even when they need it, due to perceived social cost and threat to self-worth [-M] — simply providing a help channel does not ensure it is used
  • Overly accessible help invites "click-through" abuse: learners exhaust hint levels to obtain answers without engaging, degrading learning relative to no help [-S]
  • Executive-function limitations and young developmental stages make spontaneous help seeking rare; it must be explicitly taught and prompted [~M]
  • Competitive or high-stakes classroom climates suppress adaptive help seeking and push it toward avoidance or executive (answer-only) forms [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • System-mediated: on-demand hints in intelligent tutoring systems (e.g., the Geometry Tutor line of research), with hint levels from vague to specific
  • Peer-mediated: reciprocal tutoring, study groups, and structured peer review that make help exchange bidirectional
  • Teacher-mediated: routines such as "ask three before me," question stems, and scheduled check-ins that prompt help seeking before failure accumulates
  • Taught directly: scripts and role-plays that rehearse how to formulate a specific, instrumental question

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling learners and those with low prior knowledge, who benefit most — but who are paradoxically least likely to ask without structural support [~M]
  • Adolescents and adults, for whom social-cost concerns are strongest; younger children need prompts rather than persuasion
  • Online and distance learners, where help seeking must be deliberately designed in (forums, hint systems) because informal channels disappear

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulated learning: monitoring comprehension and acting on it (see Self-Regulated Learning)
  • Procedural skill acquisition: using graduated hints to get unstuck without abandoning productive struggle
  • Long-term academic persistence: adaptive help seeking predicts course completion and achievement over time

Instructions¶

  1. Normalize asking. Establish norms and language for help seeking early; frame questions as evidence of engagement, not deficiency.
  2. Teach the skill. Model and rehearse formulating specific, instrumental questions ("I understand step 2 but not why we divide here") rather than "I don't get it."
  3. Design graduated help. Provide hints in levels — general principle, then strategy, then worked step — so learners can stop as soon as they can proceed (Scaffolding).
  4. Lower the social cost. Offer anonymous question channels, small-group help routines, or peer structures (Collaborative Learning).
  5. Prompt at the right moment. Cue help seeking when learners show signs of unproductive struggle, not before they attempt the task themselves.
  6. Follow up. After help is received, require learners to re-attempt independently so the help transfers into performance.

Related Strategies¶

  • Reciprocal Peer Tutoring — structures help exchange as mutual, reducing the social cost of asking
  • Hint Design — the graduated-hint architecture that makes system-mediated help instrumental
  • Metacognitive Prompting — builds the monitoring that triggers help seeking in the first place
  • Use predicted hint-taking likelihood and hint effects to adaptively decide whether to withhold or provide hints
  • Normalize and de-stigmatize help-seeking behavior in the classroom so academic help-seeking habits carry over to health issues

Examples¶

  • Intelligent tutoring systems (e.g., Carnegie Learning's MATHia, ASSISTments) implement on-demand hint sequences; research on the Geometry Tutor showed that hint quality and learner persistence in using them, not mere availability, determine learning gains.
  • Piazza (https://piazza.com) — course Q&A platform with anonymous posting, designed explicitly to lower the social cost of help seeking in large STEM courses.
  • "Ask three before me" — a common K-12 routine that routes help seeking through peers first, building both help-seeking practice and help-giving skill.
