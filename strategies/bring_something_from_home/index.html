Bring Something From Home¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Students bring a personal object from home — a photograph, a stuffed animal, a musical instrument, a family artifact — to keep in or contribute to the classroom. The strategy serves two functions: it provides a source of comfort and familiarity for students adjusting to a new environment, and it makes students' families and cultures visible in the shared classroom space. It is typically launched with a sharing ritual in which each student tells the class about their item.

Design Implications¶

The strategy works primarily through belonging and personalization rather than direct content instruction. Environments that signal identity safety and personal connection improve engagement and outcomes, particularly for students at risk of feeling like outsiders Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]. The sharing component converts a private comfort object into a public act of self-disclosure, which builds relational knowledge among classmates and positions families as part of the classroom community.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Display or storage space where items are visible and safe (a "family shelf," cubbies, a classroom quilt of photos)
  • Clear guidelines communicated to families about what is appropriate, and a guarantee that items will be cared for and returned
  • A structured sharing format so every student can participate equitably (Class Discussion, circle sharing)
  • A no-cost alternative for students who cannot or do not wish to bring an object (a drawing, a dictated story about home)

Constraints¶

  • Students from unstable housing, foster care, or families with few material possessions may experience the request as exclusionary or shaming [-M] — the invitation must always include non-material options
  • Can surface sensitive family situations (loss, separation, immigration stress) that the teacher must be prepared to handle privately and respectfully
  • In classrooms with large status differences between students, sharing can become a competition over objects rather than connection [~W]
  • Comfort objects can become a distraction or an avoidance tool if norms for their use are not established

Implementation Variability¶

  • Photo-only variant: families contribute one photograph for a permanent classroom display, reducing logistical burden and status comparison
  • Rotating showcase: one student's item is featured each week ("All About Me"), spreading attention across the year
  • Early-childhood transition objects: comfort items stay in cubbies for the first weeks of school to ease separation, then gradually go home
  • Digital variant: older or remote learners share a photo or short recorded story, integrated into a class gallery

Target Learners¶

  • Early-grade and early-childhood students experiencing separation from caregivers Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]
  • Students new to a school (transfers, immigrants, refugees) for whom familiar objects signal identity safety
  • Less appropriate as a whole-class requirement in secondary settings, where it can feel infantilizing [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Social-emotional: sense of belonging, comfort during transition, self-regulation under stress
  • Community-building: classmates learn about one another's families and cultures
  • Oral language: descriptive and narrative talk during sharing (Show and Tell-style presentation practice)

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce the idea in a class meeting and model it by bringing and talking about something of your own (Class Discussion).
  2. Send home a family letter explaining the purpose, care of items, and explicitly offering non-material alternatives (a drawing, a story, a recipe).
  3. Schedule a low-pressure sharing circle where each student says what the item is and why it matters; keep contributions brief and celebrate all of them.
  4. Establish where items live during the day and norms for when they may be used (e.g., quiet reading time, not during instruction).
  5. Fold items into ongoing classroom life — a family photo wall, instruments used during music, artifacts anchoring a unit on family heritage (Activation of home knowledge in content lessons).
  6. Check in periodically with students who seem anxious or left out (Check-In) and adjust individually.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Background Knowledge — home objects are a concrete hook for connecting students' out-of-school knowledge to classroom learning
  • Academic Choice — like choice of a personal object, structured autonomy supports ownership of the classroom space

Examples¶

  • Responsive Classroom practices (Center for Responsive Schools, https://www.responsiveschools.org) — early-year community-building routines include family photo displays and sharing circles that normalize home-to-school connection.
  • Transitional kindergarten and kindergarten classrooms commonly maintain a "family wall" of contributed photos; separation-anxiety research supports transitional objects for young children entering group care.
  • Heritage/identity units — students bring an artifact representing family culture as the seed for an oral history or "where I'm from" writing project.
