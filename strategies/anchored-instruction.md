# Anchored Instruction

## Description
Anchored instruction situates learning in a rich, realistic problem context — the "anchor" — typically a detailed narrative, video case, or simulation that contains embedded data and multiple solvable problems. Learners explore the anchor, generate their own questions, and acquire knowledge as a tool for solving the problem rather than as an end in itself. The approach was developed by the Cognition and Technology Group at Vanderbilt, whose Jasper Woodbury series used extended video adventures as anchors for mathematical problem solving.

## Design Implications

Anchors give knowledge a context of use, supporting transfer and making abstract content meaningful [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Because the anchor presents a complex problem before instruction, it activates prior knowledge and generates a need to know, which improves subsequent learning [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The anchor must be genuinely problematic — rich enough to sustain exploration but structured so the target concepts are the natural tools for resolution.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A realistic, engaging scenario containing embedded problems and sufficient data for solution
- Problems that cannot be solved without the target knowledge and skills
- Tools and resources (reference materials, data sets, coaching) accessible during exploration
- Opportunities for learners to generate their own subproblems before instruction ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))

#### Constraints
- Novices can become overwhelmed by the complexity of a rich anchor and engage in unproductive search; without scaffolding, the cognitive load of navigating the scenario can exceed working memory capacity [~M]
- Effectiveness reverses for learners with high prior knowledge, who benefit more from direct presentation than from guided discovery in context [Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Anchors that are engaging but tangential to target concepts produce engagement without learning; the anchor must be designed around the concepts, not merely decorated with them
- Video-heavy anchors are resource-intensive to produce and difficult to update

#### Implementation Variability
- Video-based anchors (Jasper Woodbury series) vs. text cases vs. simulations vs. real community problems
- Whole-class anchor exploration followed by small-group problem solving, or individual navigation
- Single-anchor units vs. sequences of related anchors building a domain over time

### Target Learners
- Novices who lack the experience to see how abstract content applies to real situations [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]
- Learners who ask "when will we ever use this?" — the anchor supplies the answer experientially
- Less suitable for advanced learners, for whom the contextual scaffolding is redundant [Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied problem solving: using concepts to resolve realistic, ill-structured problems
- Knowledge integration: connecting concepts across topics as they arise within one scenario
- Transfer: recognizing when learned knowledge applies in new situations
- Problem identification: noticing what questions a complex situation raises

### Instructions
1. Select or design an anchor — a rich scenario whose embedded problems require the target knowledge ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. Have learners explore the anchor and generate the problems they notice ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
3. Pose the anchor's central problem and let learners identify what they need to know ([Challenge Identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md))
4. Deliver instruction as needed-to-know: teach concepts at the moment learners recognize their use ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md))
5. Have learners apply the instruction to solve the anchor's problem ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md))
6. Debrief: compare solutions across groups and abstract the generalizable principles ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — the closest relative; anchors are extended, media-rich cases with embedded data rather than completed narratives
- [Problem-Based Learning](problem-based-learning.md) — shares the problem-first sequence but anchors emphasize a shared, detailed scenario over a brief problem statement
- [Project-Based Learning](project-based-learning.md) — anchors can launch projects; the anchor supplies context while the project supplies production

## Examples
- **[Jasper Woodbury Problem Solving Series](https://peabody.vanderbilt.edu/departments/teachandlearning/research/jasperproject/)** — Vanderbilt's video adventures (e.g., "Journey to Cedar Creek") in which students plan boat trips requiring distance-rate-time reasoning; the canonical anchored instruction curriculum
- **[The Adventures of Jasper Woodbury](https://www.ascilite.org)**-style video anchors adapted in teacher education programs to anchor study of classroom management around recorded classroom episodes
- **Scientific Sense-Making units** using a discrepant real-world event (e.g., a local fish kill) as the anchor for an entire ecology unit
