Learner-to-Learner (Peer) Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 3 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Learner-to-learner (peer) feedback involves having learners provide constructive feedback on each other's work, typically against explicit criteria. Learners act alternately as reviewers and reviewees: givers must analyze a peer's work against quality standards, while receivers get specific, actionable suggestions for revision. Effective implementation requires modeling feedback techniques, providing guidelines or rubrics, and building a supportive classroom climate.

Design Implications¶

Peer feedback benefits learning on both sides of the exchange: giving feedback drives deeper engagement with criteria and self-assessment of one's own work, while receiving it supplies task-level information for revision Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and the process rather than the person. [+S]. The quality of the exchange depends on structure — rubrics, sentence stems, and worked models of good feedback — since unstructured peer comments tend toward vague praise ("good job") that carries little instructional value.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit criteria or a rubric so feedback is anchored to quality standards rather than personal taste
  • Modeling of appropriate feedback techniques before students begin (Class Discussion with exemplar comments)
  • A supportive climate with norms for constructive criticism; anonymity options can reduce social risk
  • Time built in for receivers to act on feedback through revision, closing the loop

Constraints¶

  • Untrained reviewers default to vague praise or personal comments; without criteria, feedback quality is low Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and the process rather than the person. [-M]
  • Novice reviewers may lack the domain knowledge to detect errors, so peer feedback should supplement — not replace — instructor feedback in early skill acquisition
  • Perceived low reviewer credibility can cause receivers to dismiss useful feedback; establishing reviewer training and reciprocity mitigates this
  • Time-intensive: requires class time for training, exchange, and revision cycles

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written vs. oral feedback; anonymous vs. identified; synchronous workshop vs. asynchronous online exchange
  • Structured protocols (e.g., "two stars and a wish," TAG: Tell something you liked, Ask a question, Give a suggestion) vs. open commentary
  • Calibrated peer review: students first grade exemplar samples and compare their judgments to expert grades, improving review accuracy before reviewing peers

Target Learners¶

  • Learners in upper elementary through higher education; benefits emerge once learners can evaluate work against criteria
  • Givers benefit even when their feedback is imperfect — the act of judging work against criteria builds evaluative knowledge Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Less effective for very young learners without substantial scaffolding, and for novices reviewing highly technical work beyond their current competence

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving work quality through revision cycles (writing, design, problem solutions)
  • Developing evaluative judgment: internalizing quality criteria by applying them to others' work
  • Metacognition and self-regulation: comparing one's own work to standards Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Communication and collaboration skills

Instructions¶

  1. Establish criteria: share the rubric or success criteria and discuss what quality looks like, using exemplars of different quality levels.
  2. Model the feedback: demonstrate giving feedback on a sample work, verbalizing how to be specific, kind, and useful.
  3. Pair or group students and exchange work; have reviewers use the rubric and a structured protocol to generate comments.
  4. Have receivers read the feedback, ask clarifying questions, and plan revisions.
  5. Close the loop: students revise and optionally reflect on which feedback they used and why.

Related Strategies¶

  • Instructor feedback — peer feedback supplements but does not replace expert feedback, especially for novices
  • Self-assessment — giving peer feedback builds the evaluative skills self-assessment requires
  • Revision workshops — peer feedback supplies the input that drives revision

Examples¶

  • Writing workshops — Students in a writing class exchange drafts and use a rubric-based protocol to comment on thesis clarity and evidence before revision.
  • Calibrated Peer Review (CPR) — An online tool (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) in which students first calibrate their judgments on expert-graded exemplars, then review peers' assignments.
  • Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms — Learning management integrations that distribute submissions, anonymize reviews, and aggregate feedback quality ratings.
  • Lab report review — Science students review each other's lab reports against a methods-and-reasoning checklist before submission.
