Expect Excellence¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 13 studies (5 review, 3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 13 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Expect Excellence means communicating — through goals, feedback, task design, and daily interactions — that all students are capable of high-quality work, then providing the scaffolding to make that work achievable. In project-based learning (PBL) contexts, it counters the misconception that open-ended projects are unsuitable for struggling or marginalized learners by pairing ambitious goals with structured support.

Design Implications¶

High expectations function through two mechanisms: they raise the goals students pursue, and they shape teacher behavior — more wait time, more substantive feedback, more re-engagement after failure [~M]. Goal-setting research shows that specific, difficult goals outperform easy or vague "do your best" goals, provided learners have the ability and commitment to pursue them Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals. [+S]. Expectations also operate through self-efficacy: when students see credible evidence that peers like them succeed at demanding work, their persistence improves Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]. Crucially, high expectations without adequate scaffolding produce failure and disengagement rather than growth — the expectation must be paired with Coaching and contingent support Contingent scaffolding improves learning outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Publicly communicated, specific quality criteria (rubrics, exemplars of excellent work) so "excellence" is observable rather than rhetorical
  • Scaffolding that adapts to learner need — modeling, coaching, and fading — so demanding tasks remain attainable Contingent scaffolding improves learning outcomes. [+M]
  • Feedback focused on task and process, not person, so students know how to close the gap to excellence Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Revision cycles built into projects, signaling that first drafts are not final judgments of ability

Constraints¶

  • High expectations without scaffolding widen achievement gaps: struggling learners experience repeated failure, lowering self-efficacy Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [-M]
  • Teacher expectations can become self-fulfilling in the negative direction — low expectations produce differential treatment and depressed performance (Pygmalion effects) [~M]
  • Praise that attributes success to innate ability ("you're so smart") undermines persistence after setbacks compared to process-focused framing [~M]
  • Unrealistic expectations relative to available time and support produce burnout and disengagement rather than growth [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-culture level: school-wide norms, public exhibitions of high-quality student work, portfolios with revision histories
  • Task level: tiered entry points into the same demanding project, so all students pursue the same standard through different supports
  • Feedback level: critique protocols (e.g., gallery walks with "kind, specific, helpful" norms) that make excellence a shared, discussable standard

Target Learners¶

  • Students from marginalized or historically low-expectation backgrounds, for whom teacher expectation effects are strongest [~M]
  • Learners with fragile self-efficacy who benefit from visible evidence that demanding work is achievable Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • All learners in open-ended PBL settings, where ambiguity makes quality criteria especially necessary

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Dispositional: persistence, ownership of quality, growth mindset
  • Performance goals: producing work that meets authentic, high standards
  • Self-regulation: internalizing quality criteria for self-assessment Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Define excellence concretely: publish rubrics and anchor exemplars so the standard is visible, not implied (Advance Organizers can frame what excellent work looks like before students begin)
  2. Set specific, challenging goals for each project rather than "do your best" framing Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals. [+S]
  3. Scaffold toward the standard: model expert work, then coach and progressively release responsibility as competence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]
  4. Give task- and process-level feedback on drafts, with structured opportunities to revise Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  5. Make excellence public: exhibit final work to authentic audiences (Authentic Audiences & Purposes) so the standard has real consequences

Related Strategies¶

  • Establish a Culture of Revision — normalizes drafts and critique, making high standards attainable rather than punitive
  • Critique Protocols (Kind, Specific, Helpful) — structures peer feedback that operationalizes quality criteria
  • Public Exhibitions of Learning — authentic audiences raise the stakes and credibility of excellence expectations

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — the ongoing support that makes demanding goals reachable
  • Assessment — rubrics and exemplars make the standard explicit and actionable
  • Scaffolding — temporary support structures that must fade as competence grows

Examples¶

  • Expeditionary Learning (EL Education) — schools built around "Deeper Instruction" and public exhibitions of high-quality student work; crew structures pair high expectations with individualized support (https://eleducation.org)
  • High Tech High (San Diego) — project-based school where all students exhibit polished work publicly, communicating that excellence is expected of everyone, not a select few (https://www.hightechhigh.org)
  • PBLWorks (Buck Institute) Gold Standard PBL — lists "challenge and success" as a design principle: projects must be demanding yet scaffolded so all students can produce high-quality work (https://www.pblworks.org)
