# Brainstorming (Pre-Reading)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners are given a limited time (e.g., five minutes) to generate as many ideas as possible related to the topic of an upcoming reading. They then organize their ideas — often into sentences or a graphic organizer — and circulate to share them with peers before the class begins the text. The activity functions as a pre-reading activation routine: it surfaces what learners already know, exposes them to peers' associations, and creates a purpose for reading.

## Design Implications

Pre-reading brainstorming works because retrieval and generation before reading create a knowledge scaffold that new text information can attach to — learners with activated prior knowledge integrate text content better than those reading "cold" [Activation of prior knowledge improves text comprehension.](../principles/activation.md) [+M]. The generation requirement matters: producing ideas oneself leads to deeper processing than passively hearing a topic announced, and the subsequent peer sharing adds the exposure benefits of group idea exchange [Group brainstorming with idea exchange can outperform individuals when ideas are shared and built upon.](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) [~M]. The activity also serves a motivational function — learners read to confirm, extend, or correct their own predictions.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A focused prompt tied to the reading's central concept, not just its surface topic
- A strict time limit to preserve fluency and defer evaluation (Osborn's classic "no criticism" rule)
- A structure for organizing ideas (graphic organizer, list, concept map) and a mechanism for sharing ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), gallery walk, or pair exchange)
- A follow-through: the teacher should explicitly connect brainstormed ideas to the text during and after reading, or the activation decays

#### Constraints
- Unstructured group brainstorming often underperforms nominal (individual) brainstorming: production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and social loafing reduce idea output [-S] — the individual-then-share sequence is what avoids this
- Brainstorming can activate *inaccurate* prior knowledge or misconceptions, which then interfere with comprehension unless the reading explicitly confronts them [~M]
- Learners with little topical knowledge may generate few ideas and feel exposed during sharing; prompts and pair sharing reduce this risk
- If the brainstorm becomes an end in itself, it consumes time without improving reading; the payoff depends on the subsequent reading task referencing the brainstorm

#### Implementation Variability
- **Brainstorm-List-Share**: individual list, then whole-class compilation
- **Brainstorm-Organize-Share**: add a categorization step (grouping ideas into themes) before sharing, which adds structure for weaker generators
- **Rapid round-robin**: each learner offers one idea in turn, useful for large classes
- **Digital boards** (Padlet, Jamboard): anonymous contribution lowers evaluation apprehension and preserves a record for later comparison with the text

### Target Learners
- Learners with some relevant background knowledge, whose associations can be retrieved and refined [Activation of prior knowledge improves text comprehension.](../principles/activation.md) [+M]
- Second-language and adolescent readers, for whom the organize-and-form-sentences phase provides low-stakes language rehearsal before engaging dense text
- Less useful for learners with essentially no prior knowledge of the topic — pair them with knowledgeable peers or supply a brief advance organizer first

### Target Learning Goals
- Activation and elaboration of prior knowledge relevant to a text
- Oral fluency and idea articulation in low-stakes conditions
- Predictive reading: forming hypotheses the text will confirm or contradict
- Metacognitive awareness of what one does and does not yet know

### Instructions
1. Present the topic prompt and set a short timer (3–5 minutes); instruct learners to generate ideas individually without evaluating them ([Individual Reflection](../strategies/individual_reflection.md) or a quick-write)
2. Ask learners to organize their ideas — group them, pick the two strongest, or form them into sentences
3. Have learners move around the room or turn to partners to share and collect new ideas ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
4. Compile a class list on the board; flag predictions and questions to revisit
5. Read the text, explicitly linking it back to brainstormed ideas; after reading, have learners compare what they generated with what the text said

## Examples
- Before reading a passage on the benefits of exercise, learners spend five minutes listing types of exercise, reasons people exercise, and health effects, then circulate to share; the teacher records ideas and returns to them after reading to confirm or correct.
- **Padlet** (https://padlet.com) — a shared digital board where each learner posts brainstormed ideas anonymously before reading; the board stays visible during reading as a comparison anchor.
- In an ESL reading course, learners brainstorm vocabulary they expect to encounter in a news article, then verify their predictions against the actual text.
