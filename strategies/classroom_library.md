# Classroom Library

## Description
A classroom library is a dedicated space within the classroom stocked with a diverse, well-organized collection of books spanning multiple reading levels, genres, languages, and cultural backgrounds. Unlike a school-wide media center, it puts books within arm's reach of daily instruction, enabling immediate self-selection, quick book exchanges, and teacher modeling of reading behaviors. Effective libraries pair the physical collection with routines — browsing, book talks, independent reading time — that convert access into actual reading.

## Design Implications

Access to books is one of the strongest environmental predictors of reading volume, and reading volume in turn predicts comprehension and vocabulary growth [Books make a difference: A study of access to literacy.](https://doi.org/10.1598/RRQ.34.3.3) [+M]. However, access alone is insufficient: the library must be actively integrated into instruction and reading routines, since merely stocking shelves produces little change in reading behavior without teacher mediation and time to read [+M]. Physical design matters — clearly labeled bins, comfortable seating, and visible displays increase voluntary use, particularly for young readers [Morrow & Weinstein, 1986](https://doi.org/10.1086/461478) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated collection (research suggests a minimum of several books per student, refreshed regularly) spanning levels, genres, and cultural backgrounds
- An organizational system (bins, labels, checkout routines) that lets students find and return books independently
- Scheduled time for students to actually read — browsing without reading time yields no benefit
- Teacher modeling of book selection, care, and reading response ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) book previews, book talks)

#### Constraints
- A large but uncurated or unappealing collection can depress engagement; students rarely select books they cannot access or that feel irrelevant [-M]
- Without protected reading time, the library becomes decorative — access studies show volume only rises when time and choice co-occur [-M]
- Under-resourced classrooms face real cost barriers to building a diverse collection; inequitable library quality widens rather than narrows achievement gaps [-M]
- Overly rigid leveling systems (restricting students to a single "just-right" level) can undermine motivation and self-selection [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Themed/rotating displays** tied to curriculum units or seasons to renew interest
- **Student-curated shelves** where learners recommend and shelve books, building ownership
- **Multilingual and audiobook corners** — pairing print with [Audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md) supports striving readers and multilingual learners
- **Choice boards** ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) structuring how students respond to self-selected books

### Target Learners
- Early childhood and elementary students establishing independent reading habits [+M]
- Students from print-poor home environments, for whom classroom access is the primary source of books [+M]
- Multilingual learners, when the collection includes heritage-language and high-interest titles [+W]
- Older students benefit too, but require age-appropriate, high-interest collections; a juvenile collection in a secondary classroom sees little use [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading engagement and motivation: building voluntary reading habits [+M]
- Vocabulary and background knowledge growth through volume of reading [+M]
- Reading fluency and automaticity — practice from wide reading supports [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Perspective-taking and empathy through diverse fiction [Fiction reading improves empathy.](../claims/fiction-reading-improves-empathy.md) [+W]

### Instructions
1. **Curate the collection** — audit for level range, genre diversity, mirrors-and-windows representation; remove worn or unengaging titles
2. **Organize for independence** — front-facing displays, labeled bins by genre/topic/author, a simple checkout system
3. **Model selection and response** — book-talk new titles, demonstrate how to preview and abandon books ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
4. **Schedule reading time** — protect regular independent reading blocks; pair with [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) only as a complement, not a replacement for choice
5. **Build social reading routines** — peer recommendations, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of self-selected books, and [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) in reading journals
6. **Maintain and refresh** — rotate displays, involve students in curation, track circulation to identify gaps

## Examples
- **Elementary themed bins and reading nooks** — teachers rotate genre bins (Morrow & Weinstein, 1986, showed physical redesign significantly increased children's voluntary literature use in kindergarten and first grade)
- **Reading workshop classrooms** (e.g., Teachers College Reading and Writing Project model) — mini-lessons, self-selected books from the classroom library, and conferring during independent reading
- **Secondary ELA** — a high school teacher curates young adult literature aligned to course themes, with student-written "shelf talker" reviews
- **First Book Marketplace** (https://www.firstbook.org) and **Reading Is Fundamental** (https://www.rif.org) — programs supplying low-cost, diverse books to under-resourced classrooms
