Train assessors in giving optimal feedback and administering assessments to improve feedback quality¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 7 claims (6 for, 1 against) · 7 studies (4 associational, 1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The review reports that several studies suggested maximising opportunities for training of assessors in giving optimal feedback and administering assessments would improve the quality of feedback. This strategy addresses identified barriers including a lack of training on how to provide feedback and trainers avoiding feedback to prevent offence or provoking defensiveness. Programs should consider both specific training in feedback and changes to assessment forms to facilitate interactive feedback.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Institutional commitment to assessor development time
  • Training content should address giving optimal feedback and administering assessments

Constraints¶

  • Trainers reportedly often avoid giving feedback, in order to prevent offence or provoking defensiveness, which training must overcome

Target Learners¶

  • clinical supervisors and assessors of postgraduate doctors

Target Learning Goals¶

  • improving quality and delivery of WBA feedback

Claims¶

  • Quality of written MSF feedback comments varies widely, with a substantial portion lacking actionable information and global judgements predominating [+W]
  • Feedback usefulness from multisource feedback is enhanced by increasing its specificity, and specific, patient-sourced feedback on communication skills is most consistently used [+W]
  • Giving feedback that identifies problems in peers' work is a significant predictor of assessors' own project performance [+W]
  • The quality of feedback students give peers predicts gains in their own work, but no recorded study isolates a causal benefit of the assessor role [+W]
  • In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems [+W]
  • Generating peer feedback prior to internal feedback does not improve the quality of assessors' subsequent internal feedback [-W]
  • Cognitive feedback benefits assessors rather than assessees, and weak students do not benefit from receiving more feedback [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Design peer assessment to encourage specific problem-identifying feedback, suggestions, and affective support rather than grades alone
  • Promote interaction between provider and receiver of peer feedback to stimulate elaboration on judgments
