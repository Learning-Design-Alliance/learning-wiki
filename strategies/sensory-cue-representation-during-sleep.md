# Pair learning material with a distinct sensory cue and re-present that cue during subsequent sleep at home to reactivate the material

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 1 study (1 review), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The review describes TMR as associating learning content with sensory cues "such as odors or sounds, that are presented during subsequent sleep to promote memory reactivation". Applied versions pair classroom content (vocabulary, history, microeconomics lectures) with odors or music during encoding and replay the cue overnight, increasingly with home devices that target sleep stages automatically.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Learning content must be paired with a distinct sensory cue (odor or sound) during encoding so the cue can later reactivate that material.
#### Constraints
- Unsupervised home sessions with loud sounds may cause sleep arousals that weaken memories; benefits of unsupervised cueing emerged only on the third night, suggesting habituation and sleep disturbance matter.

### Target Learners
- school students
- undergraduate students
- adult learners

### Target Learning Goals
- declarative memory for vocabulary and lesson content
- procedural skill memory

## Claims

- [TMR enhances declarative memory after one night of sleep across paradigms, though some recent studies did not replicate the effect](../claims/tmr-enhances-declarative-memory.md) [+W]
