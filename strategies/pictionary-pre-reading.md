# Pictionary (Pre-Reading)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Key words from an upcoming text are selected, and the class is divided into two or three teams. A learner from each team comes to the whiteboard and draws a word without using letters or numbers while teammates race to guess it. The activity primes vocabulary, activates prior knowledge, and builds visual-verbal associations before learners encounter the words in context.

## Design Implications

Pre-reading vocabulary games work because they combine retrieval of word meanings with dual coding — pairing verbal labels with nonverbal imagery strengthens memory for word–concept links [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. The competitive, game-like format also raises engagement and participation compared with passive vocabulary presentation [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Crucially, the guessing process forces learners to articulate what they already know about a concept, which serves as [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge before reading.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A whiteboard or shared drawing surface, markers, and a timer
- Pre-selected key words drawn from the target text — concrete, drawable concepts work best
- Team structures that give every learner a turn drawing and guessing ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
- A brief debrief connecting each guessed word to the upcoming reading ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Abstract or low-frequency words (e.g., "symbiosis," "inference") are hard to draw and can produce wrong guesses that entrench misconceptions [~M] — the debrief must correct these before reading
- Learners anxious about drawing in public may disengage; rotating roles and accepting stick figures lowers the stakes
- The game itself teaches word–image association, not deep word meaning; without follow-up reading and use, gains are shallow [~M]
- Time spent on the game competes with reading time; overlong rounds reduce instructional efficiency

#### Implementation Variability
- **Charades variant**: learners act out words instead of drawing, useful for verbs and processes ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md))
- **Learner-selected words**: teams choose which words to draw, adding a selection/reasoning step
- **Digital versions**: tools like [Kahoot!](https://kahoot.com) or a shared [Jamboard](https://support.google.com/jamboard) whiteboard support remote classes
- **Post-reading reversal**: the same game after reading checks retention rather than activating knowledge

### Target Learners
- Language learners and younger readers who benefit from visual scaffolds for new vocabulary [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Mixed-proficiency groups: guessing is lower-stakes than drawing, so reluctant learners can participate
- Less suitable when the text's key vocabulary is overwhelmingly abstract or technical

### Target Learning Goals
- Vocabulary pre-teaching and word recognition before reading
- Prior knowledge activation: surfacing what learners already know about text concepts
- Conceptual association: linking new terms to familiar images and experiences

### Instructions
1. Select 6–10 key words from the text, favoring concrete, drawable concepts; keep the list short to avoid overload [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
2. Divide the class into two or three teams and explain the rules (no letters, numbers, or gestures)
3. A drawer from each team sketches a word while teammates guess; first correct guess scores a point
4. After each round, briefly connect the word to the reading: "Where do you think we'll see *habitat* in this text?" — this step enacts [Activation](../elements/activation.md) and is where most of the learning value lies
5. Close with a quick review of all words before transitioning to the text ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) follows during and after reading)

## Examples
- Before reading about ecosystems, words like *habitat*, *species*, and *biodiversity* are drawn and guessed; the debrief asks teams to predict how each word will appear in the text.
- In an ESL class before a short story, teams draw emotion words (*anxious*, *relieved*) and then sort them by which characters might feel them.
