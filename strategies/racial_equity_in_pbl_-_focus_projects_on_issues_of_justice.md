# Racial Equity in PBL - Focus Projects on Issues of Justice

## Description
This strategy directs project-based learning toward authentic problems of racial, social, and environmental justice so that students investigate and act on issues with real stakes for their communities. Rather than treating PBL as a format applied to any content, the strategy makes the *purpose* of the project — confronting injustice — the engine of relevance, engagement, and agency. It draws on culturally relevant pedagogy and critical literacy traditions, in which academic learning is coupled with developing students' capacity to identify and challenge inequity [Ladson-Billings, 1995] [+M].

## Design Implications

Anchoring projects in justice issues raises the authenticity and personal stakes of [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md), which supports stronger engagement and performance than passive formats [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Because students exercise meaningful choice over which issues to pursue, the approach supports autonomy, a key driver of intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Doing consequential work for a real audience can also build students' sense of efficacy and persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Curriculum with room for extended exploration of justice issues; standards mapped to the project's academic targets so rigor is not sacrificed to relevance
- A facilitator prepared to discuss race and injustice productively, including with students whose lived experience differs from the issue at hand
- Authentic [Authentic Audiences and Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) — community partners, policymakers, or public forums — so student work has real consequence
- Structures for [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) and perspective-taking when the issue does not affect all students equally

#### Constraints
- Projects perceived as tokenistic or performative (e.g., one-off diversity posters) can breed cynicism rather than engagement, particularly among students from marginalized communities [~W]
- Poorly framed justice projects can produce anxiety, hopelessness, or polarization if students confront systemic problems without agency or a path to action [-W]
- Teachers unprepared for sensitive discussion may shut down or misframe conversations, doing harm as well as losing instructional time [-W]
- Community-embedded projects require relationship-building time that compressed schedules and standardized pacing do not accommodate [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Issue selection:** teacher-proposed anchor issues, student-selected issues, or co-constructed with community input
- **Scope of action:** research-and-recommend projects, advocacy campaigns (letters, testimony, exhibitions), or implemented community interventions
- **Disciplinary entry points:** data analysis of local inequities (math), documentary and narrative work (ELA), environmental justice fieldwork (science), civic policy analysis (social studies)
- **Depth of partnership:** from guest speakers to sustained [Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md) partnerships with local organizations

### Target Learners
- Students from marginalized communities, for whom seeing their communities' concerns treated as worthy academic content signals belonging and value [Ladson-Billings, 1995] [+M]
- Disengaged adolescents who respond to work with authentic stakes over simulated exercises [~M]
- All learners benefit, but teachers must scaffold perspective-taking for students whose identities or backgrounds differ from those most affected by the issue

### Target Learning Goals
- Engagement and motivation: making learning relevant and meaningful
- Civic agency: empowering students to act as change agents
- Critical literacy: analyzing how systems produce and sustain inequity
- Disciplinary skills: applied through authentic research, argumentation, and communication tasks

### Instructions
1. **Select or co-construct the issue.** Begin from a real, local manifestation of injustice (e.g., a neighborhood's environmental data, school funding disparities) and connect it to academic standards.
2. **Present the problem authentically** through community voices, primary sources, and local data — a [Problem Presentation](../elements/problem-presentation.md) grounded in evidence rather than abstraction.
3. **Build background and empathy.** Use [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) and community interviews so students understand the issue's history and human stakes before proposing solutions.
4. **Organize collaborative inquiry.** Structure investigation through [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) teams with defined roles, mirroring how community organizers and researchers work.
5. **Develop and test solutions.** Students draft proposals, campaigns, or prototypes, gathering feedback from community partners and revising iteratively.
6. **Act and reflect.** Deliver the work to an authentic audience, then debrief both the academic learning and the emotional experience of confronting injustice.

## Related Strategies
- Community-embedded and service-oriented project designs — share the commitment to consequential work beyond the classroom
- Culturally responsive teaching approaches — provide the pedagogical stance this strategy operationalizes within PBL

## Examples
- **Science/math:** Students analyze air-quality or lead-pipe data from their own zip code, then present remediation demands to the city council — a structure used in community science curricula such as those from the [Public Science Project](https://www.publicscienceproject.org) (participatory action research in New York schools).
- **ELA/social studies:** Students research local housing discrimination history and produce a public exhibition or podcast for a community audience.
- **Youth participatory action research (YPAR):** Programs documented by Duncan-Andrade & Morrell (2008) have high school students design and conduct research on issues affecting their communities, presenting findings to school boards.
