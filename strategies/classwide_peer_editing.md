# Classwide Peer Editing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Classwide peer editing involves having students review and critique examples of student writing, which can be real or mock examples displayed or distributed to the entire class. Students compliment the piece (word choice, sentence structure, tone, organization), make specific suggestions for improvement, and point out objective errors in grammar, spelling, and continuity. Because the whole class works on the same piece under teacher facilitation, the strategy doubles as training in evaluative judgment before students apply the same routine to peers' or their own drafts.

## Design Implications

Peer review of writing improves both writing quality and evaluative skill when feedback is structured and criterion-referenced rather than unguided [Peer assessment improves learning when structured with clear criteria.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. Critiquing flawed or anonymous samples lets students practice detecting problems without the social risk of criticizing classmates, and analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual understanding of what makes writing effective [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. The classwide format is essentially a [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) phase: the teacher models evaluative moves on shared text before students edit independently.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared piece of writing (real, anonymized, or teacher-constructed) visible to all students
- An explicit feedback protocol — e.g., Compliment–Suggest–Correct — with sentence starters and a rubric
- Teacher training of students in giving constructive, specific feedback before independent peer editing
- Teacher facilitation and monitoring to keep critique specific and on-task

#### Constraints
- Unstructured or purely social feedback ("I liked it") produces little learning; without criteria, peer comments are unreliable [Peer assessment reliability depends on training and clear criteria.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [~M]
- Students may struggle to give or receive critique; anonymity of samples and norms for respectful feedback must be established first
- Novice editors can reinforce incorrect "corrections" (e.g., misapplied grammar rules); teacher verification of the Correct phase is needed
- Whole-class critique of one piece reduces writing time; overuse can crowd out actual composing

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mock flawed drafts** — teacher-written pieces with planted errors, functioning as [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) for error-hunting practice
- **Anonymous real drafts** — authentic student work with names removed, increasing authenticity while lowering social risk
- **Gradual release** — whole-class critique of one piece → small-group critique → paired editing → independent self-editing, consistent with fading responsibility [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- **Cross-curricular adaptation** — the same compliment-suggest-correct routine applied to lab reports, arguments, or code reviews

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through secondary students developing writing and revision skills [+M]
- Students who benefit from explicit criteria before evaluating their own work; the routine builds the evaluative vocabulary self-editing requires
- Less effective for very young writers who lack the grammatical metalanguage to identify errors [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision and self-regulated writing: internalizing the criteria used to critique others [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Procedural knowledge of writing conventions (grammar, organization, cohesion)
- Collaborative and communication skills: giving specific, respectful, actionable feedback

### Instructions
1. **Select and display a sample** — a mock draft with planted issues or an anonymized student draft; mock drafts work well as [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md).
2. **Model the routine** — the teacher thinks aloud through one Compliment–Suggest–Correct pass, making evaluative reasoning visible ([Cognitive Apprenticeship](../elements/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)).
3. **Guided class critique** — students identify compliments, suggestions, and corrections on the shared piece; the teacher verifies corrections and sharpens vague suggestions.
4. **Structured practice** — students apply the routine to a second sample in pairs, then to peers' drafts ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
5. **Transfer to self-editing** — students apply the same checklist to their own drafts, converting peer-editing criteria into revision moves.

## Tools
- Document cameras or shared displays for whole-class markup
- Collaborative annotation platforms (e.g., Google Docs suggesting mode, Perusall) for digital versions
- Printed editing checklists aligned to the Compliment–Suggest–Correct protocol

## Examples
- **Writing workshop routines** — many elementary writing workshop curricula (e.g., Units of Study in Writing, Heinemann) open revision mini-lessons with whole-class critique of a shared or teacher-written draft before students revise independently.
- **Secondary English** — an anonymized persuasive essay is projected; students first post compliments on organization and tone, then suggestions, then corrections, using a rubric before editing partners' drafts.
