# Assume Support

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Assume Support is a stance toward family and community engagement: practitioners treat all families as caring about their children's education and doing the best they can with the resources, time, and knowledge they have. Rather than interpreting low visible engagement as low commitment, the practitioner designs flexible communication channels and support structures that accommodate widely varying home circumstances — work schedules, language, digital access, and differing beliefs about the school's role.

## Design Implications

Family involvement predicts achievement, but the relationship runs through families' sense of efficacy and invitation from the school: parents engage when they believe they can help and when the school actively welcomes them [Hoover-Dempsey & Sandler's model of parental involvement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543065003391) [+S]. Assuming support reframes "hard-to-reach" families as "under-invited," shifting the design burden from the family to the school.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Multiple, low-barrier communication channels (translated messages, phone as well as email, asynchronous options)
- Flexible expectations for participation — deadlines, meeting times, and formats that vary with family circumstances
- Awareness of logistical variability: consistent internet, reliable devices, and quiet study space cannot be assumed, especially in online or hybrid settings
- Referral pathways to school and community support services when families need more than the classroom can provide

#### Constraints
- Deficit framing by educators — attributing low engagement to indifference — suppresses trust and reduces subsequent family contact [~S]; the assumption must be genuine, not rhetorical
- Assuming support without adjusting logistics (e.g., requiring synchronous video meetings) still excludes families with inflexible work schedules or poor connectivity
- Educators' implicit biases about family structure, income, and culture can override the stated stance; one-off training rarely changes this without ongoing [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and accountability

#### Implementation Variability
- In K-12 settings, this typically means proactive family outreach and flexible homework design; in higher education, it translates to assuming students juggle work and caregiving and designing asynchronous, deadline-flexible courses
- In online learning contexts, the strategy expands to include device and connectivity audits rather than assuming home infrastructure

### Target Learners
- K-12 educators and administrators designing family engagement
- Teachers in online or hybrid settings who cannot observe home learning conditions directly
- Students from families with limited school engagement benefit indirectly: teacher outreach grounded in an assume-support stance raises families' sense of efficacy for supporting learning [Hoover-Dempsey & Sandler's model of parental involvement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543065003391) [+M], which supports student persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Equity-oriented professional dispositions: reducing judgment and deficit thinking about families
- Communication and relationship-building practices
- Designing flexible, accessible learning logistics

### Instructions
1. Audit your assumptions: list what you currently require of families (attendance times, internet access, paperwork) and mark which are essential versus habitual.
2. Open with strengths-based outreach — the first contact with each family should communicate care and an invitation, not a deficit report.
3. Offer at least two participation pathways for every family-facing expectation (e.g., in-person or phone conference; synchronous or asynchronous updates).
4. Use regular [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to surface logistical barriers early, and route families to support services when needs exceed classroom scope.
5. Treat non-response as a signal to change channel or format, not as disengagement; follow up through a different medium.

## Examples
- **Epstein's Six Types of Involvement framework (Johns Hopkins NNPS)** — a widely used structure for school-family partnership that treats communication and parenting as shared responsibilities rather than family deficits (https://nnps.jhucsos.com).
- **Dual-capacity building framework (Mapp & Bergman, 2013)** — positions family engagement as building relational capacity between staff and families, explicitly rejecting deficit models of "hard-to-reach" parents.
- A teacher offering recorded video lessons plus downloadable low-bandwidth versions, so families without reliable internet can support homework offline.
