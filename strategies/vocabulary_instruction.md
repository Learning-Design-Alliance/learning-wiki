# Vocabulary Instruction

## Description
Explicitly teaching vocabulary words and their definitions, along with providing context, to help students understand and use new words effectively. Vocabulary instruction can include pre-teaching challenging words before reading a text, so students don't become discouraged. Activities can be incorporated into any content area. Effective programs go beyond definitional teaching: they provide multiple encounters with words in varied contexts, require learners to actively process word meanings, and connect new words to known concepts.

## Design Implications

Explicit vocabulary instruction reliably improves word learning, and word learning shows a moderate, well-replicated relationship to reading comprehension [Vocabulary instruction improves word learning and, more modestly, comprehension.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017269) [+S]. However, the comprehension benefit is smaller than word-learning gains alone would suggest — knowing more words does not automatically produce better understanding of connected text [~M]. Instruction is most effective when it moves beyond dictionary definitions toward rich, contextualized encounters: multiple exposures across varied contexts, semantic mapping, and activities requiring learners to generate their own examples and non-examples. Pre-teaching a small number of high-utility words before reading reduces the chance that unfamiliar vocabulary derails comprehension of the whole text.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identification of key vocabulary words — prioritizing high-utility, widely applicable words (Beck et al.'s "Tier Two") over rare, text-specific terms
- Clear, learner-friendly definitions paired with examples in context, not just dictionary paraphrases
- Multiple spaced encounters with each word across reading, writing, and discussion
- Time for instruction and active practice in class

#### Constraints
- May be time-consuming; the number of words that can be taught deeply is far smaller than the number students need, so instruction cannot be the sole route to vocabulary growth [-M]
- Students may memorize definitions without truly understanding the words in context, especially when assessment rewards verbatim recall [-M]
- Definitional-only instruction produces weaker, less durable word knowledge than instruction with contextual and generative activities [~S]
- Does not address underlying issues with decoding or reading fluency; for struggling readers, word-recognition problems may consume the capacity that vocabulary instruction is meant to free [Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-teaching before reading**: selecting 5–10 critical words to teach before a text to protect comprehension
- **Rich/multi-context instruction**: teaching words through multiple meanings, contexts, and semantic connections over several days
- **Generative activities**: word maps, semantic gradients, "would you rather" questions, and having students create examples and non-examples
- **Incidental/contextual approaches**: wide reading and [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to known words, less efficient per word but scalable to large volumes of vocabulary

### Target Learners
- All grade levels; particularly beneficial for struggling learners and those with limited vocabulary or English learners, who cannot rely on incidental acquisition from text [-M]
- Learners with weak prior knowledge of a topic, for whom pre-taught terms provide entry points into new content
- Less necessary for strong readers with large vocabularies, who acquire most new words incidentally from context [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Increase students' vocabulary breadth and depth of word knowledge
- Improve reading comprehension of challenging texts
- Build academic and domain-specific language for use in writing and discussion
- Reduce frustration when encountering unfamiliar words

### Instructions
1. Select a small set of high-utility words from the target text or topic; prioritize words learners will encounter repeatedly across contexts.
2. Provide [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md): a learner-friendly definition, an example in context, and a non-example.
3. Activate prior knowledge by connecting new words to known concepts or synonyms using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and semantic maps.
4. Assign [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) containing the words in authentic context, with multiple encounters planned across the unit.
5. Require generative practice: students write original sentences, sort examples from non-examples, and discuss nuanced usage.
6. Space review across days and assess usage in writing and speaking, not just recognition.

## Examples
- **Beck, McKeown & Kucan's Text Talk / robust vocabulary instruction** — teachers read a story aloud, then engage students in extended discussion of Tier Two words with multiple contexts and questions requiring judgment about usage.
- **Word Generation** (SERP Institute, https://www.serpinstitute.org/word-generation) — a middle-school program teaching five high-utility academic words per week through discussion, debate, and writing across content areas.
- Pre-teaching definitions for difficult words before reading a challenging text, then using flashcards or games to practice, and requiring new words in writing assignments.
