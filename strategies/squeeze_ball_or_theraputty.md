# Squeeze Ball or Theraputty

## Description
A squeeze ball or Theraputty is a handheld tactile object provided to a learner as a self-regulation tool. The learner squeezes, stretches, or manipulates the object during instruction or independent work, with the intended function of discharging agitation, sustaining arousal at an optimal level, or providing a permissible movement outlet that reduces more disruptive fidgeting.

## Design Implications

Fidget tools are best understood as arousal-regulation supports rather than attention or learning interventions. The underlying rationale draws on optimal-arousal accounts of self-regulation: some learners, particularly those with ADHD or autism spectrum profiles, may perform better when allowed a low-demand motor outlet that helps them maintain an alert but not overstimulated state [~W]. Evidence that fidget objects improve attention or achievement is weak and inconsistent; several studies find no benefit for typically developing learners, and some find that the object itself becomes a distraction [-W]. Effectiveness depends on the tool being uninteresting relative to the task — a plain squeeze ball is preferable to a novel or multi-feature toy, which competes for the same attentional resources as the lesson [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A simple, quiet, repetitive tactile object (not a toy with visual or interactive features)
- Explicit norms for use: when, where, and what it is for (a tool, not a plaything)
- A brief introduction or modeling of use, ideally co-developed with the learner
- A replacement plan if the object becomes a distraction or is thrown

#### Constraints
- For learners without a demonstrated regulation need, fidget objects can reduce attention and task performance [-W] — the object functions as a competing stimulus rather than a regulator
- Novel, colorful, or multi-feature fidgets reliably draw gaze and working memory resources away from instruction [-M]; the same attentional competition documented for irrelevant multimedia stimuli applies
- As a universal (whole-class) intervention, evidence does not support improved outcomes; benefits, where found, are confined to identified individual learners [~W]
- If offered as a substitute for explicit regulation instruction, it can displace teaching of strategies the learner will need across settings [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual protocol: tool assigned to one learner as part of an accommodation or behavior support plan, often paired with a [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) routine
- Classroom bin: tools available to all during specific activities (independent reading, long lectures), with rules for use
- Resistance-graded Theraputty: used in occupational therapy contexts with prescribed resistance and exercises, doubling as fine-motor work
- Scheduled versus on-demand access: some learners benefit from a set "movement break" with the tool rather than continuous availability

### Target Learners
- Learners with ADHD or high arousal/impulsivity profiles, for whom a permissible motor outlet may reduce disruptive fidgeting [~W]
- Learners with sensory-processing needs identified through occupational therapy assessment
- Anxious learners who benefit from a discrete self-soothing action during assessments [~W]
- Not recommended as a default for whole classes without individual need [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and managing agitation or frustration before it disrupts work
- Behavioral support: reducing off-task or disruptive motor behavior during seat-based tasks
- Not a tool for content learning itself; any academic gains are indirect, via improved time-on-task

### Instructions
1. Identify the need: confirm the learner shows agitation, off-task movement, or frustration during specific task types, ideally with input from the learner and support staff.
2. Select a low-stimulus tool: plain squeeze ball or Theraputty; avoid lights, scents, or novelty features that compete for attention.
3. Establish norms: name it as a tool, define when it is available, and agree on a signal or routine for putting it away.
4. Model and practice brief use: show how to use it quietly and briefly, then return to the task.
5. Monitor and review: track whether time-on-task or disruption improves over 2–3 weeks; withdraw or swap the tool if it becomes a distraction or shows no benefit.

## Related Strategies
- Movement breaks — a gross-motor alternative serving the same arousal-regulation function at higher intensity
- Sensory accommodations — the broader category of environmental and tool-based supports of which fidget tools are one instance
- Check-in routines — pairing the tool with brief emotional state monitoring makes it part of a regulation strategy rather than a standalone object

## Examples
- An occupational therapist prescribes medium-resistance Theraputty for a student with autism who squeezes it during morning meeting, with use faded as tolerance for seated group time grows.
- A middle-school teacher keeps a basket of plain stress balls available during independent reading only, with a class norm that tools stay on the desk and out of sight during discussion.
- A student with ADHD keeps a squeeze ball in their desk for use during tests, agreed in a 504 plan, paired with a brief self-talk script ("squeeze three times, then back to the page").
