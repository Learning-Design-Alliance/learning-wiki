Front Loading Vocabulary¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 12 studies (4 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 4 of 12 report an effect size

Description¶

Front loading vocabulary means explicitly pre-teaching the meanings of high-value words before learners read a text, watch a demonstration, or begin a task. It typically involves selecting a small set of words critical to understanding, providing student-friendly definitions and examples, and engaging learners in brief active processing of those meanings before the main activity.

Design Implications¶

Pre-teaching vocabulary reduces the extraneous cognitive load of decoding unfamiliar terms during the main task, freeing working memory for comprehension and reasoning Unfamiliar vocabulary consumes working memory resources that would otherwise support comprehension. [+M]. Effects are strongest when the pre-taught words are selected for their centrality to the passage or task — "tier two" and "tier three" words in Beck et al.'s framework — rather than taught as isolated lists Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension when it targets words important to the text and includes multiple encounters in meaningful contexts. [+S]. Brief activation of related prior knowledge during pre-teaching further supports integration of new word meanings Activating prior knowledge improves learning of new material. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Selection of a small number (typically 5–10) of words that are essential to the target text or task, not exhaustive lists
  • Student-friendly definitions, examples, or Analogies rather than dictionary recitation
  • Active processing — learners say, sort, use, or contrast the words, not just hear them
  • Follow-up encounters with the words during and after the main activity

Constraints¶

  • Pre-teaching too many words dilutes attention and consumes instructional time better spent on the task itself [~M]
  • Isolated, definitional drill without contextual encounters produces fragile word knowledge that does not transfer to reading [~S]
  • Front loading can remove productive struggle: for some learners, inferring word meaning from context is itself a valuable skill, and pre-teaching everything eliminates that practice [~M]
  • Less effective when the pre-taught words are peripheral to the passage's main ideas — time cost without comprehension benefit [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pre-teaching critical words only — the classic form; teach only words without which the text cannot be understood
  • Preview-in-context — show the word in the actual sentence it will appear in, then discuss meaning
  • Frayer model / four-square — learners complete definition, characteristics, examples, and non-examples for each word
  • Morphological front loading — teach a root or affix (e.g., photo-, -ology) that unlocks a family of words in the upcoming unit
  • Multimedia pre-teaching — images, video, or Act It Out gestures paired with definitions, leveraging dual channels Pairing verbal definitions with visual representations supports vocabulary learning. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Beginning readers and English language learners, for whom unfamiliar vocabulary is the dominant barrier to comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension; unfamiliar vocabulary has the opposite effect. [+M]
  • Learners with limited background knowledge of the topic domain, who lack contextual cues for inferring meanings
  • Less necessary for proficient readers with strong domain knowledge, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension of informational and disciplinary texts
  • Academic language development (discipline-specific terminology)
  • Conceptual understanding where vocabulary and concept are inseparable (e.g., photosynthesis, tariff, variable)

Instructions¶

  1. Select 5–10 words that are essential to understanding the upcoming text or task; prioritize words that appear frequently in the discipline over rare, single-passage words.
  2. Define simply — write student-friendly definitions and prepare an example, image, or Analogy for each.
  3. Activate — briefly connect each word to learners' prior experience Activating prior knowledge improves learning of new material. [+M].
  4. Process actively — have learners sort, match, use in sentences, or contrast words with Non-Examples; keep this under 10 minutes.
  5. Revisit in context — flag the words when they appear in the main text or task, and schedule at least one later encounter (discussion, writing, Assessment) to consolidate.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — front loading vocabulary is often paired with activation of related concepts; both prepare the knowledge base the main task will draw on
  • Pre-Questioning — a parallel front-loading move that directs attention before a text

Examples¶

  • Bringing Words to Life (Beck, McKeown & Kucan) — the tiered vocabulary framework underlying most robust-vocabulary instruction programs; recommends teaching a small set of high-utility words with multiple meaningful encounters.
  • Word Generation (SERP Institute) — a middle-school academic vocabulary program that front loads weekly target words before discussion and writing tasks across subject areas.
  • Science notebooks in FOSS kits — teachers pre-teach terms like mixture and solution with hands-on examples before students read the investigation text.
