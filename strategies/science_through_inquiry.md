# Science Through Inquiry

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Science Through Inquiry positions learners as investigators: they pose questions, design and conduct investigations, gather and interpret evidence, and revise explanations or models in light of data. Rather than first receiving canonical explanations, learners construct understanding through structured exploration that mirrors authentic scientific practice. The strategy spans a continuum from fully guided (confirmation, structured inquiry) to open inquiry, with the degree of learner autonomy over question, method, and solution varying by design.

## Design Implications

Inquiry can produce strong conceptual gains, but only when it is scaffolded: unguided or minimally guided discovery consistently underperforms explicit guidance, especially for novices [Minimal guidance during instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices.](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [-S]. The most effective forms provide a clear question, a workable investigation structure, and instructor moves that probe and redirect learner reasoning rather than simply supplying answers [Teacher-led, scaffolded inquiry outperforms student-led inquiry on conceptual outcomes.](../claims/teacher-guided-inquiry-outperforms-student-led.md) [+S]. Because learners must hold a question, a procedure, and emerging evidence in mind simultaneously, managing working memory load is central to the design [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Encountering data that contradicts expectations creates productive cognitive conflict that motivates conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A driving question that is genuinely investigable with available materials and time
- Materials, lab space, or simulations that allow real data collection and iteration
- Instructor expertise in both the science content and facilitative questioning — inquiry fails when the teacher cannot recognize and build on partial understanding
- Structured scaffolds: question frames, data tables, argument templates that are progressively faded
- Time for cycles of prediction, investigation, and [Model Revision](../elements/model-revision.md), not single-shot experiments

#### Constraints
- Unguided discovery with novices produces weak or negative outcomes compared with guided instruction [Minimal guidance during instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices.](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [-S] — learners without domain knowledge cannot yet recognize which patterns in data matter
- Time- and resource-intensive; poorly planned inquiry consumes class time without converging on target concepts
- Difficult to assess with conventional tests; requires rubrics for investigation design, evidence use, and reasoning
- Learners with high prior knowledge may benefit less from heavy scaffolding and more from open inquiry [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M] — the expertise-reversal pattern applies to inquiry scaffolds as well
- Hands-on activity alone does not guarantee learning; "activity without conceptual focus" yields engagement but shallow understanding [Hands-on activity without conceptual integration does not improve science learning.](../claims/hands-on-without-conceptual-integration-insufficient.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured inquiry**: instructor provides question and procedure; learners generate explanations — appropriate for novices
- **Guided inquiry**: learners design methods within an instructor-framed question — the modal classroom form
- **Open inquiry**: learners pose their own questions end-to-end — appropriate once investigation skills are established
- **Model-based inquiry**: learners commit to an initial model, test it, and revise — strong for addressing misconceptions
- **Citizen-science variants**: learners contribute authentic data to real research programs (e.g., Zooniverse projects)

### Target Learners
- Elementary through undergraduate learners building first exposure to scientific practices [Active learning improves exam performance in science courses.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Novices benefit most from the structured end of the continuum; open inquiry suits learners with substantial domain knowledge [Minimal guidance during instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices.](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [-S]
- Learners holding common science misconceptions, for whom confronting discrepant evidence is productive [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of scientific mechanisms and models
- Epistemic practices: forming questions, controlling variables, evaluating evidence, [Evidence-Based Reasoning](../elements/evidence-based-reasoning.md)
- Nature of science: understanding how claims are justified and revised
- Transferable reasoning and argumentation skills

### Instructions
1. Launch with an anchoring phenomenon or discrepant event and elicit initial explanations ([Activation](../elements/activation.md); [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md))
2. Support learners to generate investigable questions ([Question Formulation](../elements/question-formulation.md))
3. Frame the investigation: provide procedures or design constraints appropriate to learner expertise, keeping cognitive load within working memory limits ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md))
4. Facilitate data collection and [Inquiry and Research](../elements/inquiry-and-research.md), circulating to probe reasoning with questions rather than answers
5. Require learners to construct claims supported by evidence ([Evidence-Based Reasoning](../elements/evidence-based-reasoning.md)) and argue from data in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
6. Close the cycle with [Model Revision](../elements/model-revision.md) — learners explicitly compare their revised explanation to their initial one, and the instructor consolidates the canonical science

## Examples
- **[FOSS (Full Option Science System)](https://www.foss.science)** — A research-based K–8 curriculum in which structured, hands-on investigations precede formal explanation of concepts, with embedded scaffolds for data recording and science-notebook argumentation.
- **[Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org)** — Citizen-science platform where learners classify real research data (galaxies, wildlife camera traps), experiencing authentic evidence-based reasoning at scale.
- **Physics classroom sequence**: Students predict whether a heavy or light object falls faster, test it, confront the discrepant result, and revise their model of free fall — a classic guided-inquiry cycle targeting a robust misconception.
