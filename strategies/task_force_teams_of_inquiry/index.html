Task Force Teams of Inquiry¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 9 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 causal, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Task force teams of inquiry organize learners into small, mission-driven teams, each of which selects a topic from a class-generated brainstorm list. The team's mission is to explore, research, and present on that topic, typically culminating in a public product such as a report, presentation, or recommendation. The teacher frames the inquiry, connects it to relevant standards, and facilitates rather than directs the investigation.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines two well-supported mechanisms: cooperative goal structures, which raise achievement and motivation relative to individualistic or competitive structures Johnson & Johnson's meta-analytic review of cooperative learning. [+S], and inquiry-oriented tasks, which support deeper conceptual understanding when scaffolded Inquiry-based approaches improve conceptual understanding relative to traditional instruction. [+M]. Topic choice from a brainstorm list leverages learner autonomy, which supports intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. However, the inquiry component requires substantial guidance: minimally guided team inquiry produces weaker outcomes than scaffolded inquiry, particularly for novices Minimally guided instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices. [-S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A brainstorm list of genuinely researchable topics, generated with learner input
  • Clear team guidelines: roles, timelines, deliverables, and quality criteria shared in advance
  • Structured collaboration supports — assigned roles, check-ins, and milestone deadlines — so that responsibility is distributed rather than absorbed by one or two members
  • Access to research resources and instruction in how to evaluate and synthesize them
  • A culminating product with an authentic audience where possible Authentic audiences improve student work. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Unguided team inquiry degrades into unfocused searching for novices; teams need embedded scaffolds, checkpoints, and expert feedback Minimally guided instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices. [-S]
  • Free-riding and status hierarchies undermine learning unless roles and individual accountability are designed in; group work without individual accountability can reduce achievement for capable members Cooperative learning requires both positive interdependence and individual accountability. [~S]
  • Teams formed solely by friendship tend to replicate existing social hierarchies and exclude marginalized learners [~W]
  • Large teams (more than 4–5 members) dilute participation and increase coordination costs [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Team formation: self-selected, teacher-assigned, or randomly formed — teacher assignment with attention to heterogeneity is often safest for equity
  • Duration: single-week mini-inquiries to semester-long projects
  • Structure: fully open inquiry, guided inquiry with teacher-provided questions, or structured case-based-learning variants
  • Jigsaw adaptation: each member researches a subtopic and teaches it to teammates, enforcing interdependence

Target Learners¶

  • Upper elementary through adult learners; younger learners need heavier scaffolding of both research skills and teamwork
  • Learners with basic research and self-regulation skills benefit most; novices need guided inquiry structures rather than open exploration Minimally guided instruction is less effective than guided instruction for novices. [~S]
  • Learners who struggle socially may need explicit collaboration instruction and role support rather than exemption from team work

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Inquiry and research skills: question formulation, source evaluation, synthesis
  • Collaborative competence: shared planning, negotiation, and accountability Cooperative goal structures raise achievement and motivation. [+S]
  • Communication: presenting findings to authentic audiences
  • Deep conceptual understanding of the investigated topic Inquiry-based approaches improve conceptual understanding relative to traditional instruction. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Facilitate a class brainstorm of researchable topics; curate the list against standards and feasibility.
  2. Form teams of 3–5, balancing self-selection with teacher judgment about composition and heterogeneity.
  3. Have each team select a topic and draft a focused inquiry question; require teacher approval of the question before research begins.
  4. Assign rotating roles (coordinator, researcher, recorder, presenter) and set milestone check-ins with deliverables.
  5. Teach or review research and source-evaluation skills before teams begin; provide curated starting resources for novices.
  6. Structure interim peer feedback using assessment criteria shared in advance Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].
  7. Schedule culminating presentations to an audience beyond the team; include individual reflections and peer evaluations to secure individual accountability.

Related Strategies¶

  • Jigsaw — a structured interdependence variant that guarantees each member's contribution is essential
  • Project-Based Learning — the broader family this strategy belongs to, with a driving question and sustained investigation
  • Gallery Walk — a presentation format for sharing team findings
  • Use named cooperative learning structures adapted to adult classrooms, including Student Team Learning, Think-Pair-Share, Jigsaw, Teaming Together, and Group Investigation

Examples¶

  • Project Citizen (Center for Civic Education, https://www.civic-ed.org) — class teams identify a community problem, research policy alternatives, and present a portfolio and hearing before civic panels.
  • eCYBERMISSION (Army Educational Outreach Program, https://www.ecybermission.com) — grades 6–9 teams select a community problem, investigate it with STEM methods, and submit findings to a national competition.
  • A middle-school science unit where teams each select one human-impact topic from a brainstormed list (water quality, invasive species, urban heat), research it over three weeks with weekly teacher check-ins, and present recommendations to a local council member.
