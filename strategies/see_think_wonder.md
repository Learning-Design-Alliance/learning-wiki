# See_Think_Wonder

## Description
See_Think_Wonder is a thinking routine developed by Harvard Project Zero's Visible Thinking initiative. Learners examine an artifact — an image, object, text, data display, or demonstration — and respond to three prompts in sequence: *What do you see?* (careful observation), *What do you think about it?* (interpretation and reasoning), and *What do you wonder?* (curiosity-driven questioning). The routine is typically carried out individually, in pairs, or as a whole-group discussion before formal instruction on the topic.

## Design Implications

The routine activates prior knowledge and directs attention before content delivery, converting passive viewing into active observation and hypothesis generation [Activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Its three-step structure deliberately separates observation from interpretation, which trains learners to distinguish evidence from inference — a core disciplinary habit. The "wonder" step generates authentic questions that can anchor subsequent instruction and gives the instructor a real-time map of the class's prior conceptions and misconceptions.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, ambiguous-enough artifact that rewards close looking (a photograph, artwork, graph, primary source, specimen, or code snippet)
- Time for unhurried observation before interpretation — the "see" step must not be rushed
- A mechanism for capturing responses (board, shared document, sticky notes) so the group's thinking is visible and reusable
- A follow-on lesson that actually addresses the questions generated, or learners learn that wondering is decorative

#### Constraints
- With sparse or overly familiar artifacts, "see" collapses into "think" and the observation discipline is lost [-W]
- Whole-group versions can be dominated by a few confident speakers; think-pair-share or written variants mitigate this [-W]
- If the instructor does not connect the routine to subsequent content, it functions as an isolated activity with little durable learning benefit [~M]
- Less effective when the learning goal is procedural fluency rather than observation, interpretation, or inquiry

#### Implementation Variability
- **Zoom In** — reveal an image in successive enlargements, running the routine at each stage to model how evidence changes interpretation
- **Written/silent variant** — learners record all three columns independently before discussion, increasing participation equity
- **Chalk Talk variant** — responses posted on paper and built on silently by peers, then discussed
- **Discipline-specific framing** — in science, "see" becomes observations vs. inferences; in history, evidence vs. speculation; in mathematics, what the representation shows vs. what it implies

### Target Learners
- Novices in a domain, who benefit from structured observation before being asked to interpret [Activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners and younger students — the routine's simple, repeated sentence stems lower the language barrier to participation
- Learners prone to jumping to conclusions; the enforced see-before-think sequence slows and disciplines inference

### Target Learning Goals
- Observation skills: attending carefully to evidence before interpreting it
- Inferential reasoning: justifying interpretations with reference to what was observed
- Curiosity and question-posing: generating genuine inquiry questions that drive subsequent learning
- Metacognition: making one's own interpretive moves visible and discussable

### Instructions
1. Select and display an artifact connected to the upcoming content; give silent looking time.
2. Ask "What do you see?" — collect observations only, enforcing the rule that interpretations wait ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) the artifact with labeled observations supports this step).
3. Ask "What do you think is going on?" — require learners to connect interpretations back to specific observations ("I think… because I see…").
4. Ask "What does it make you wonder?" — record questions visibly; use them to launch the lesson or unit.
5. Debrief by contrasting the group's interpretations, surfacing how the same evidence supported different inferences [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
