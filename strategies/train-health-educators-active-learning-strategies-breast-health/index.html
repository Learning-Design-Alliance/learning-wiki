Train health educators to teach breast health using active learning strategies including simulation, cooperative learning, peer teaching, practice, and discussion groups¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 against) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 review), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that because most health education curricula lack breast-health-specific content, educators be trained to incorporate active learning/teaching strategies "such as: simulation, using cooperative learning , peer teaching, practice, and discussion groups." It argues incorporating active learning is more likely to increase knowledge retention and addresses different learning styles, and suggests professional development days, empowerment workshops, and instructional coaches as delivery mechanisms.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Health educators need training to incorporate breast health into selected content areas and to feel comfortable with the subject

Constraints¶

  • The recommendation rests on a single quasi-experimental study in two high schools with voluntary participation

Target Learners¶

  • adolescent females ages 15-18 in high school health education classes

Target Learning Goals¶

  • breast health knowledge
  • breast health beliefs
  • breast self-examination skills

Claims¶

  • Interactive learning produced a larger immediate gain in perceived benefits of breast self-awareness, but the comparison group sustained its gain over the treatment group by follow-up [+W]
  • Adolescent females taught breast health with interactive learning using simulated breast models gained more knowledge and sustained the gain longer than the comparison group [+W]
  • Interactive learning lowered adolescent females' perceived barriers to breast self-examination immediately after the program [+W]
  • Knowledge and belief gains decayed from immediate post-test to 4-week follow-up regardless of teaching condition [-W]
  • Hybrid simulation of breast examination produces better lesion reporting and malignant-feature identification than traditional teaching [+W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Schedule breast health instruction early enough in the semester that it is not perceived as an unimportant topic
  • Implement formal peer tutor training programs under faculty supervision within simulation curricula
  • Enrich secondary school Business Studies curricula with an extended curriculum using active learning in a business-simulated, cooperative set-up
