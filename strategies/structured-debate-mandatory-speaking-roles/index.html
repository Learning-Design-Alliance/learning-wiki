Use structured classroom Debate with mandatory speaking roles to build ESL learners' willingness to communicate¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's Debate treatment introduced learners to Debate structure, speaker roles, argument and rebuttal, then had them debate course-related topics after research and half-hour brainstorming sessions. Because "it was mandatory for each speaker to deliver an argument", every student gained speaking practice, which the authors link to improved communicative skills, confidence and WTC. This contrasts with the PI condition, where participation in discussion was not compulsory.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • every student is assigned a speaker role and must deliver an argument
  • topics are given in advance so students can research them
  • instructor evaluates delivery and argument after a mock debate

Constraints¶

  • the article reports Debate also triggered more communicative apprehension than PI discussion

Target Learners¶

  • ESL undergraduates in oral/aural language courses

Target Learning Goals¶

  • willingness to communicate
  • self-perceived communicative competence
  • persuasive public speaking

Claims¶

  • Both Debate instruction and Philosophy Inquiry discussion significantly increased ESL learners' Willingness to Communicate over a semester [+M]
  • Debate was more effective than Philosophy Inquiry discussion in enhancing ESL learners' WTC at post-test [+M]
  • The Debate group showed a larger descriptive gain in Self-Perceived Communicative Competence than the PI group [+W]
  • Communicative Apprehension increased slightly more in the Debate group than in the PI group [-W]
  • The Debate and PI groups did not differ significantly in WTC at pre-test [+W]
