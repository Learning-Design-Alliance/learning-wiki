# Numbers To 100

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Numbers to 100 instruction develops children's ability to count, read, write, compare, and decompose whole numbers up to 100. It is carried out through structured counting routines, work with concrete and visual models (hundred charts, base-ten blocks, number paths/lines), and activities that emphasize the base-ten structure of two-digit numbers rather than rote recitation alone.

## Design Implications
The central design problem is that the count sequence to 100 far exceeds what young children can hold in working memory, so instruction must organize number into decadal and base-ten structures rather than treating 100 items as an undifferentiated sequence [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Instruction that emphasizes numerical magnitude — where numbers fall relative to one another — produces broader gains in arithmetic than counting-skill drill alone [Playing linear number board games improves children's numerical magnitude knowledge and arithmetic.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2008.01144.x) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Concrete and visual models that expose base-ten structure: ten-frames, bundles of ten, base-ten blocks, hundred charts, and number paths
- Counting routines that cross decade boundaries (e.g., 28→29→30→31), where most counting errors concentrate
- Activities connecting numerals, number words, and quantities — not numeral naming alone
- Ongoing [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of individual counting ranges, since children's reliable count ranges vary widely at entry

#### Constraints
- Rote recitation of the count sequence without one-to-one correspondence or cardinality understanding does not produce usable number sense — children can count to 100 verbally while failing to give the number "just after" 29 [-M]
- Hundred charts can encourage row-by-row rote patterns; children may read 34 as "three-ten four" without decomposing it into 30 + 4 unless tasks require decomposition [~M]
- Purely symbolic drill before concrete quantity experience is less effective for children with weak magnitude representations [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Counting collections**: children count real object sets of 50–100 items, self-organizing into groups of ten — builds grouping and skip-counting simultaneously
- **Linear board games**: games on a 1–100 number path with numbered squares improve magnitude comparison and estimation, especially for children from low-income households [+S]
- **Hundred-chart puzzles and missing-number tasks**: shift attention from recitation to the structure of the sequence
- **Decade-crossing routines**: daily oral counting with deliberate pauses at 29→30, 59→60, 99→100

### Target Learners
- Children ages 5–7 (Kindergarten–Grade 2) building initial two-digit number concepts
- Children with weak magnitude representation benefit disproportionately from linear, spatially structured number activities [Linear number board games produce durable gains in numerical estimation.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2008.01144.x) [+S]
- Children who already count fluently to 100 need decomposition and comparison challenges, not more recitation [Guidance that is redundant for a learner's expertise level adds load rather than support.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Counting sequence knowledge: oral count, numeral recognition, and writing to 100
- Base-ten structure: understanding two-digit numbers as tens and ones
- Magnitude and comparison: ordering, comparing, and estimating quantities to 100
- Skip-counting by tens (and later fives) as a bridge to multiplicative thinking

### Instructions
1. **Establish cardinality first.** Verify each child can count a small set with one-to-one correspondence and state "how many" before extending the range; use [practice](../elements/practice.md) with counting collections.
2. **Introduce decades as units.** Use bundles of ten and ten-frames so that 40 is experienced as "four tens," not just a word in a sequence — a form of [chunking](../principles/chunking.md).
3. **Use a hundred chart and number path together.** The chart shows decadal structure; the linear path shows magnitude and interval size. Have children locate, compare, and find missing numbers on both.
4. **Play linear number games.** Board games on a 1–100 path (e.g., *Chutes and Ladders*-style tracks) with verbal number naming on each turn [+S].
5. **Cross decade boundaries deliberately.** Orally count with pauses and errors at 29→30, 49→50, 99→100; ask "what comes just after?" rather than restarting from 1 each time.
6. **Decompose two-digit numbers.** Have children build 47 as 4 tens and 7 ones in multiple materials, then record as 40 + 7; use [analogies](../elements/analogies.md) across materials (bundles ↔ blocks ↔ chart columns).
7. **Fade supports.** Move from full material support to partial (pictures of bundles) to symbolic-only as children demonstrate fluency [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

## Related Strategies
- Counting collections routines — the object-counting counterpart that grounds grouping concepts
- Number talks — oral decomposition discourse that extends this work to mental computation
- Skip-counting introduction — the natural successor once tens-structure is secure

## Examples
- **Counting Collections (Teresa Lindblom / Cognitively Guided Instruction classrooms)** — children count jars of 60–120 objects, record how they grouped them, and share strategies; see [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)
- **Siegler & Ramani's linear number board game** — a 1–100 path game shown to raise magnitude comparison and estimation accuracy in preschoolers [+S]
- **DreamBox Learning** — adaptive digital lessons that use ten-frames and number-line models to build numbers to 100 with [adaptive difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md)
- **Every Day Counts calendar math** — daily whole-class routines building the count sequence and hundred-chart patterns across the school year
