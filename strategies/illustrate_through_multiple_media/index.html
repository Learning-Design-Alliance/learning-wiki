Illustrate Through Multiple Media¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Illustrating through multiple media means presenting the same core content through complementary channels — diagrams and animations alongside narration or text, simulations alongside static graphics, video alongside worked demonstrations. The strategy is grounded in dual coding: combining verbal and visual representations gives learners two coordinated memory codes rather than one Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. It is not about adding more media, but about selecting media whose affordances match the content — animation for dynamic processes, static diagrams for structure, simulation for manipulation.

Design Implications¶

Multimedia works when the combination reduces extraneous load and enables referential connections between words and pictures; it fails when channels compete or when media are decorative Pairing words and pictures in ways that manage working memory load improves learning. [+M]. Mayer's coherence, signaling, and redundancy principles are the operative design rules: exclude seductive but irrelevant detail, cue attention to essential content, and avoid narrating text that is displayed verbatim Clark, R. C., & Mayer, R. E. (2016) [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Content analysis identifying which concepts are structural (best as diagrams), dynamic (best as animation or video), or procedural (best as Demonstration)
  • Narration or captions that integrate verbal and visual streams rather than duplicating them
  • Segmented, learner-paced presentation so learners can process each medium before the next Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Redundancy harms learning: simultaneous identical narration and on-screen text overloads visual working memory Clark, R. C., & Mayer, R. E. (2016) [-S]
  • Decorative graphics and seductive details divert attention from essential content and depress retention [-M]
  • High element interactivity content can exceed working memory when split across media that require integration (split-attention effect) [-S]
  • The strategy does not work by matching a learner's preferred "learning style" — the learning-styles hypothesis is empirically unsupported Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008) [X]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Static diagrams often outperform animation for complex systems because learners control pacing and can revisit [~M]
  • Simulations shift the medium from presentation to manipulation; pair with prompts or they become play [~M]
  • Video demonstrations can substitute for live modeling when narration makes reasoning explicit (Think-Aloud)

Target Learners¶

  • Novices, who benefit most from coordinated verbal–visual codes that reduce unguided search Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]
  • Learners with low prior knowledge in the domain, for whom well-designed multimedia reduces cognitive load [~M]
  • Less beneficial for experts, for whom redundant graphics can impose unnecessary integration costs [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding of structures, systems, and spatial relationships
  • Procedural knowledge via narrated video or animated Demonstration
  • Abstraction from varied representations — presenting contrasting cases across media supports generalization Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the core concept and choose the medium that matches its structure (diagram, animation, simulation, video) rather than defaulting to text-plus-slides
  2. Open with an Advance Organizer or brief orientation so learners know what to attend to in each medium
  3. Present the concept through the primary medium with integrated narration, using Analogies to bridge to prior knowledge
  4. Follow with Application or Practice in a different medium than the presentation, forcing learners to translate between representations
  5. Use Assessment items that require learners to interpret or produce the visual representation, not just restate the narration

Related Strategies¶

  • Worked examples — the multimedia version is a narrated video demonstration; same example–practice logic
  • Case-based teaching — cases are frequently delivered through video and documents across media
  • Flipped classroom — pre-class video is the most common multimedia illustration vehicle

Examples¶

  • PhET Interactive Simulations (https://phet.colorado.edu) — research-validated science simulations paired with guided inquiry activities; studies show simulation-plus-guided-activity outperforms simulation alone [~M]
  • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) — narrated visual problem-solving videos followed by practice, enacting the presentation–practice sequence
  • Anatomy & physiology courses using Netter's atlases with narrated labeling tasks — static visuals with learner-paced annotation rather than passive animation
