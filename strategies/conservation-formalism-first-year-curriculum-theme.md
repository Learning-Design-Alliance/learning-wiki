# Introduce the conserved-quantity formalism in the first semester and return to it across the first-year curriculum as a unifying theme

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · no claims cited

## Description
The paper recommends using the suite of five examples as a theme running through a first-year physics sequence: the formalism is introduced early with momentum and angular momentum, then revisited when students encounter fluids and capacitors later in the curriculum. Because the examples are ordered as students would meet them in a standard first-year sequence, each return reinforces the identification of state variables M and V and the role of conserved quantities. A final extension example (two springs in a fluid bath) then demonstrates how to apply the formalism to a new problem.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Examples placed in the order students encounter them in a standard first-year physics sequence
- Discussion of the extension example after students have worked through the earlier four examples
#### Constraints
- Deriving dissipation directly from forces for the collision case requires differential equations beyond most introductory courses; the fluid and capacitor derivations assume quasi-static processes

### Target Learners
- high school physics students
- introductory college physics students

### Target Learning Goals
- recognizing universal features in diverse physical systems
- understanding energy transformations and conserved quantities
