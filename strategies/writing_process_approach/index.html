Writing Process Approach¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q3–q4 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

The writing process approach treats composing as a recursive cycle of planning, drafting, revising, and editing rather than a single-pass transcription task, grounded in the cognitive model of writing as goal-directed problem solving Flower & Hayes, 1981 [+S]. Instruction is typically organized as a writers' workshop: extended time for writing, mini-lessons, teacher–student conferences, and peer response, with writing framed around authentic audiences and purposes. The teacher acts as coach and fellow writer rather than sole evaluator.

Design Implications¶

The approach works because it externalizes and distributes the cognitive demands of composing across time and social support, reducing the working-memory burden of attending to ideas, syntax, and mechanics simultaneously Flower & Hayes, 1981 [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows the process approach produces reliable gains in writing quality, roughly 0.34 SD in adolescents Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S], with effects strongest when it includes explicit strategy instruction rather than immersion alone Graham & Sandmel, 2011 [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Sustained, protected writing time with regular opportunities to draft and redraft
  • Explicit instruction in specific processes — planning, revising strategies, sentence combining — not just workshop structures Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S]
  • Feedback directed at ideas and text structure rather than surface errors Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  • Authentic purposes and audiences so revision has a real motivation (Authentic Audiences & Purposes)
  • A classroom community where peer response and collaborative learning are taught and normed, not merely assigned

Constraints¶

  • Weak implementations — workshop routines without explicit strategy instruction or substantive teacher feedback — show markedly smaller effects Graham & Sandmel, 2011 [~M]
  • Unstructured peer response often devolves into praise ("I liked it") unless response protocols and coaching are explicitly taught [-M]
  • Overemphasis on process can shortchange transcription fluency; struggling writers whose handwriting and spelling are effortful have little working memory left for revision, so mechanics instruction must not be omitted [-M]
  • Full process cycles for every piece are time-expensive; forcing the full cycle on low-stakes writing dilutes both coverage and quality [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Writers' workshop (Calkins/Units of Study model): mini-lesson → independent writing → conferring → sharing
  • Strategy-infused process (e.g., Self-Regulated Strategy Development): process stages combined with explicit strategies, self-talk, and goal setting — the highest-effect variant Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S]
  • Digital process writing: versioned drafting in platforms like Google Docs makes revision history visible and enables asynchronous peer and teacher comment
  • Scaffolded fading: teacher support moves from heavy modeling and shared writing toward independent management of the full cycle Support fades as responsibility transfers to the learner. [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Adolescents and older students with enough transcription fluency to benefit from revising Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S]
  • Struggling writers, when the process structure is paired with explicit strategies and scaffolds rather than open-ended immersion Graham & Sandmel, 2011 [~M]
  • Young or novice writers need more direct modeling and shared writing before managing the cycle independently

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: coherent, well-developed, audience-appropriate compositions
  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own composing Flower & Hayes, 1981 [+M]
  • Metacognitive knowledge of writing as a craft, including revision as re-seeing rather than proofreading

Instructions¶

  1. Set an authentic purpose. Establish a real audience and genre for the piece (Authentic Audiences & Purposes).
  2. Model the process. Demonstrate planning and drafting think-aloud style, making writer decisions visible.
  3. Teach a focused mini-lesson. Target one strategy (e.g., planning with a graphic organizer, sentence combining) drawn from evidence-based practices Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S].
  4. Support drafting with conferences. Hold brief individual conferences; give process-level feedback on ideas and structure [+S].
  5. Structure peer response. Use taught protocols (e.g., "praise–question–suggest") so feedback is specific and usable.
  6. Require revision and editing as separate passes. Revision addresses meaning; editing addresses conventions — keep them distinct.
  7. Publish. Bring the piece to its authentic audience to close the cycle and motivate future effort.

Related Strategies¶

  • 5-minute writing conferences — the conferring mechanism at the heart of workshop implementations
  • Activating prior knowledge — planning in the process model is fundamentally retrieval and organization of prior knowledge
  • Action-oriented feedback — conference and peer feedback should specify what to do next, not just evaluate
  • Use GenAI across the writing process stages of pre-writing, drafting language support, and revision feedback, and for discussion questions, differentiated explanations, and scaffolded practice

Examples¶

  • Units of Study (Heinemann, Lucy Calkins) — a widely adopted K–8 writers' workshop curriculum built on the process approach.
  • National Writing Project (nwp.org) — teacher professional network whose summer institutes model the process approach for teachers as writers.
  • Google Docs revision history — makes recursive drafting visible, enabling teachers to assess process, not just product.
