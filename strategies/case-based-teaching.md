# Case Based Teaching

## Description
Case based teaching organizes instruction around detailed narratives of real or realistic situations — a business decision, a patient presentation, an engineering failure — that learners must analyze, discuss, and resolve. Rather than presenting principles first and examples second, the case comes first: learners confront a messy, ill-structured situation and extract the relevant concepts through guided analysis. It is typically carried out through preparation of the case before class, followed by facilitated whole-class or small-group discussion.

## Design Implications

Cases situate abstract principles in concrete, memorable contexts, which supports transfer by giving learners a rich retrieval structure [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on active analysis — cases read passively produce little learning, while structured discussion and decision-making drive the benefit [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Because real situations are ill-structured, multiple cases across varied contexts are needed to build flexible knowledge rather than case-specific routines [Multiple cases across varied contexts build flexible knowledge.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M]. Comparing two or more contrasting cases is more effective than studying a single case, because contrast makes the deep structure visible [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-written case with genuine ambiguity and decision points — not an illustration of an already-taught principle
- Learner preparation time; discussion fails if participants have not engaged the case beforehand
- A facilitator skilled at questioning, probing reasoning, and withholding the "answer" until learners have committed to positions
- Clear discussion norms, since case teaching depends on learners defending and revising positions publicly

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when learners lack the prior knowledge needed to analyze the case; novices can be overwhelmed by ill-structured complexity and extract surface features instead of principles [~M]
- Discussion quality collapses with unprepared participants — the method front-loads learner responsibility in ways that disadvantage students unfamiliar with the format
- A single case can anchor learners to its surface details; without contrasting cases, transfer to new contexts is weak [Multiple cases across varied contexts build flexible knowledge.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [~M]
- Requires small-to-medium class sizes or heavy small-group structure; large lectures dilute the discussion mechanism

#### Implementation Variability
- **Discussion-led (Harvard method):** cold-calling and board-based analysis of a single case in a facilitated whole-class session
- **Small-group analysis:** teams work the case, then present and defend recommendations
- **Progressive disclosure:** the case is revealed in stages, with learners making decisions before seeing consequences — common in simulation-adjacent formats
- **Case + lecture hybrid:** the case opens the unit and the debrief consolidates principles afterward

### Target Learners
- Advanced novices and intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to analyze a case but need practice applying it [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]
- Professional and pre-professional students (business, law, medicine, teaching) preparing for judgment-intensive practice
- Less suitable for absolute novices, who need more structured guidance before confronting ill-structured material [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Application and transfer: using principles to diagnose and decide in novel situations
- Ill-structured problem solving: tolerating ambiguity and reasoning with incomplete information
- Professional judgment and argumentation: defending a position and evaluating alternatives

### Instructions
1. Select or write a case with a genuine decision point and enough ambiguity to sustain multiple defensible positions.
2. Assign the case in advance with guiding questions; require a written position or analysis to ensure preparation.
3. Open class by activating relevant prior knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)) before diving into analysis.
4. Facilitate discussion using probing questions; make learners commit to positions before evaluating them ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)).
5. Where possible, pair the focal case with a contrasting case so learners compare and contrast rather than study one instance [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S].
6. Close with an instructor debrief that consolidates the principles the case illustrates — the synthesis learners cannot yet make alone.

## Related Strategies
- Problem Based Learning — similar case-first structure but organized around an unsolved problem rather than a resolved narrative
- Peer Instruction — a complementary in-class discussion mechanism usable within case sessions
- Jigsaw — a way to distribute case complexity across group members before synthesis

## Examples
- **[Harvard Business School case method](https://www.hbs.edu/mba/academic-experience/Pages/the-hbs-case-method.aspx)** — every MBA course is built on ~500 prepared cases discussed via cold-calling; the canonical discussion-led implementation ([Case-Based Learning: Harvard Method](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)).
- **[National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science](https://www.nccsts.org)** — University at Buffalo repository of peer-reviewed science cases with teaching notes, used across undergraduate STEM.
- **Problem-based medical curricula** — patient cases drive tutorial learning in programs modeled on McMaster and Maastricht ([Anchored Instruction](../patterns/anchored-instruction.md) shares the case-as-context structure).
