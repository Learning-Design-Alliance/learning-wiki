# Tactile Toys for Sensory Integration

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Tactile toys — fidgets, stress balls, textured objects, putty, tangle toys — are small manipulable objects given to learners to occupy the hands and provide sensory input during instruction. The rationale draws on sensory integration theory: some learners, particularly those with ADHD or autism spectrum conditions, may regulate arousal and attention through self-directed tactile stimulation, and an appropriate toy may substitute for more disruptive fidgeting. Unlike most learning strategies, the evidence here is thin and conditional; this page treats tactile toys as a low-cost [accommodation](../elements/accommodations.md) to be trialed and monitored, not an established intervention.

## Design Implications

Tactile toys are best understood as an arousal-regulation accommodation rather than a cognitive or instructional strategy. The plausible mechanism is that structured, repetitive tactile input occupies spare attentional capacity or provides calming proprioceptive feedback, reducing off-task motor behavior [~W]. Evidence is genuinely mixed: a small classroom study found stress balls improved on-task behavior for some sixth graders, particularly those with ADHD [~W], while a systematic review of school-based "fidget" and sensory-diet interventions found little rigorous support for generalized attention gains [-W]. Effects appear highly individual — the same toy that calms one learner becomes a distraction for another [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Individualized selection: the learner (with teacher support) chooses a toy that is quiet, visually unobtrusive, and does not compete with the instructional task for attention
- Clear ground rules for when and how the toy is used, so it functions as a tool rather than a toy in the play sense
- Ongoing monitoring of on-task behavior and work completion to evaluate whether the accommodation is helping ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines work well here)

#### Constraints
- Novel or visually interesting toys divert attention from instruction itself; the toy must be low-stimulation relative to the lesson [-W]
- Effects do not generalize: a toy that helps in one setting or with one learner often shows no benefit with another [~W]
- Whole-class distribution tends to fail — benefits appear only for learners who specifically seek sensory input [-W]
- For learners without sensory-seeking behavior, toys add a distraction with no compensating regulation benefit [-W]
- Sensory integration therapy as a broader framework (of which tactile toys are a fragment) has weak empirical support for its theoretical claims [~W]; toys should be justified by observed behavioral effects, not by the theory

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual accommodation (IEP/504-style) vs. a classroom "fidget basket" learners may opt into
- Resistance/putty and weighted objects add proprioceptive input, not just tactile
- Alternatives serving the same function: standing desks, movement breaks, chewable pencil toppers, seat cushions
- Scheduled use (during listening tasks only) vs. always-available use

### Target Learners
- Learners with ADHD who show excess motor activity during seated instruction [~W]
- Autistic learners with documented sensory-seeking behavior, as one component of a broader sensory profile [~W]
- Learners with anxiety who use the object as a self-soothing tool [~W]
- Not indicated for learners without sensory-seeking behavior, where toys function as plain distractions [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a learning-goal strategy: tactile toys target behavioral prerequisites for learning (sustained attention, reduced disruptive movement) rather than any curricular objective
- Indirect support for goals requiring extended seated attention (listening, independent work)

### Instructions
1. Identify learners whose fidgeting or sensory-seeking interferes with participation; consult existing [accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) plans.
2. Offer a small set of quiet, low-visual-stimulation options and let the learner select — autonomy in selection supports buy-in and matches self-determination principles ([Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md)).
3. Establish explicit use rules: hands only, silent, during instruction or independent work.
4. Collect baseline on-task data, introduce the toy, and compare after 1–2 weeks; discontinue if no improvement or if the toy becomes a distraction.
5. Pair with movement-based alternatives ([Active Recess](../strategies/active-recess.md), scheduled breaks) rather than relying on the toy alone.

## Related Strategies
- Movement breaks — serve the same arousal-regulation function through gross motor activity
- Flexible seating — an environmental alternative targeting the same attentional mechanism
- Check-ins — the monitoring routine that makes individualized trialing of toys evidence-based

## Examples
- **Stress balls during whole-class instruction** — Stalvey & Brasell (2006) provided stress balls to sixth graders during language arts and found improved on-task writing behavior for several students with ADHD, with no benefit for classmates.
- **"Fidget kits" in autism support classrooms** — individualized kits (putty, tangle, textured strip on the desk) selected with an occupational therapist and reviewed at each progress-report cycle.
- **Opt-in classroom fidget basket** — a common elementary practice; works only when paired with clear rules and removal of items that prove distracting.
