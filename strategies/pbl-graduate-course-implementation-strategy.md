# Implementation recipe for a graduate PBL course: administration support, facilitator buy-in, group continuity, curricular coordination, and recognized effort

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

## Description
From their experience scaling the course to 25–30 students in six groups, the authors list what implementers must secure: "support of the administration for developing the curriculum, facilitator buy-in to the approach, continuity (not uniformity) between PBL groups", leveraging other curriculum components, and recognizing the development effort. The curriculum is described as readily scalable by adding students per group or more groups. Standardization mechanisms included regular coordinator–facilitator meetings, shared rubrics, and a rotating facilitator observing other groups.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Strong faculty commitment and skilled facilitators, with time reflected in teaching assignments and evaluation
- Administrative support for course creation, facilitator assignment, and logistics
#### Constraints
- Comprehensive facilitator training requires an extensive time commitment; real-time peer-evaluation of facilitators was challenging within a three-week format

### Target Learners
- matriculating doctoral students in biomedical programs of any size

### Target Learning Goals
- critical thinking and experimental design training at program level
