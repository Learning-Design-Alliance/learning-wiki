Laboratory Reports¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 4 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

A laboratory report is a written artifact produced after hands-on experimentation, following the canonical structure of scientific writing: introduction with hypothesis, methods, results, and discussion. Learners must translate a concrete experience into formal scientific prose, which forces them to articulate procedures precisely, represent data accurately, and connect findings to theory. The report functions simultaneously as a learning activity (structuring and consolidating the experience) and as an assessment of both conceptual understanding and scientific communication.

Design Implications¶

Writing a lab report requires learners to transform raw experience into organized, theory-linked knowledge — a generative process that strengthens retention and reveals misconceptions that hands-on activity alone leaves hidden [~M]. The discussion section carries most of the learning load: interpreting results against predictions engages Argumentation and Articulation, converting procedural experience into transferable conceptual understanding. Reports also enact Assessment for Learning when paired with rubrics and feedback, since the draft-report-feedback-revision cycle makes scientific norms visible and improvable Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An actual experiment or data set the learner generated or can interrogate — reports written about fabricated data undermine authenticity and motivation
  • An explicit structure or template (IMRaD or equivalent) so novices know the genre conventions
  • A rubric distinguishing procedural accuracy, data representation, and quality of interpretation
  • Feedback that addresses reasoning in the discussion, not just formatting and mechanics Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • When learners follow cookbook procedures without understanding, the report becomes a transcription exercise with minimal learning value [-M] — the write-up cannot rescue an experiment the learner did not understand
  • Heavy emphasis on formatting conventions can crowd out scientific reasoning; novices often spend their effort on surface structure rather than interpretation [-M]
  • Grading reports purely on matching "expected results" punishes honest data and teaches that science is about confirming answers, not interpreting evidence [-S]
  • Writing load can impose extraneous cognitive load for learners still mastering the content, degrading both the writing and the learning Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Scaffolded templates: sentence frames and section prompts for novices, faded toward open reports as expertise grows
  • Partial reports: assigning only the discussion or only the results analysis when time is limited, targeting the highest-value section
  • Peer review: structured peer feedback on drafts before submission builds evaluation skills and exposes learners to alternative interpretations
  • Post-lab data comparison: pooling class data and having learners compare their results to others' supports Comparing Cases and normalizes variability in empirical data Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students in science courses encountering experimental method
  • Novices benefit from explicit templates and rubrics; advanced students should write open-ended reports on self-designed investigations [~M]
  • Students learning discipline-specific communication norms (the report is the genre of professional science)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: connecting experimental evidence to theory in the discussion section
  • Procedural and epistemic knowledge: understanding how methods, data, and claims relate
  • Scientific communication: writing in the conventions of the discipline
  • Data literacy: representing and interpreting quantitative results

Instructions¶

  1. Before the lab, activate relevant theory and have learners state a testable hypothesis (Advance Organizers, Activation)
  2. During the experiment, require structured recording of procedures and raw data (Annotating) so the report has authentic material to draw on
  3. After data collection, support analysis with Chunking of data into tables and graphs before any prose is written
  4. Have learners draft the report using a template, with the discussion requiring explicit comparison of results to the original hypothesis and to theory
  5. Provide rubric-based feedback prioritizing interpretation, and allow revision (Assess Performance)
  6. Optionally, run structured peer review between drafting and submission

Related Strategies¶

  • Case-Based Learning — reports on real datasets or published studies share the same evidence-to-claim reasoning structure
  • Peer Review — a natural complement that adds an authentic audience and evaluation practice

Examples¶

  • 5E Learning Cycle — the lab report serves the "Explain" and "Evaluate" phases, where learners formalize what the "Explore" phase surfaced
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — instructor annotation of a model report makes expert scientific writing visible before students draft their own
  • Constructive Alignment — report rubrics aligned to stated learning outcomes ensure the writing task assesses the intended reasoning, not formatting compliance
  • CUREs (Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences), e.g., the Tiny Earth network — students write reports on genuine original research, raising authenticity and ownership
