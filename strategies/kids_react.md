# Kids React

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Perform an online image search for a charged everyday term (the original uses "school lunch"), project the results or distribute them as handouts, and have students attend to one image at a time. Students signal an immediate emotional reaction — a face, or thumbs up, down, or sideways — and the teacher charts the class's responses. The class then discusses what assumptions each reaction rested on, and students write individually about the assumptions a person might make from a simple image search.

## Design Implications

The strategy converts an everyday search result into a [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)-style object of analysis: affective first responses are treated as data, then examined. Making one's initial judgment explicit before analyzing it supports metacognitive awareness of bias, and structured discussion of contrasting reactions to the same image helps students abstract the general point that images frame interpretation [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. Verbalizing and writing about one's reasoning deepens conceptual understanding beyond passive viewing [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated image set from an online search (projected or as handouts), a chart for recording reactions, and notebooks or journals
- A facilitator prepared to normalize disagreement and model non-defensive examination of one's own assumptions
- A follow-on writing task that requires students to articulate assumptions, not just report feelings

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends heavily on facilitation quality; without skilled prompting, discussion stays at the level of "I like this one" and never reaches assumptions [-W]
- Some students need additional support to identify their assumptions and biases; direct questions ("What did you expect to see?") work better than abstract prompts about "bias" [-W]
- If the image set is homogeneous, contrasting reactions collapse and the abstraction opportunity is lost [~M]
- Emotionally charged topics can produce defensiveness that shuts down reflection; the low-stakes "school lunch" framing is part of the design, and substituting more charged topics requires deliberate safety norms

#### Implementation Variability
- Silent chalk-talk or sticky-note charting instead of public thumbs signals, for students reluctant to reveal reactions publicly
- Repeat the routine with different search terms across the year to build a reusable media-analysis habit
- Extend to a media-production follow-up: students search, curate, and critique their own image sets

### Target Learners
- Elementary students encountering media literacy concepts for the first time; the concrete image-first, analysis-second sequence matches their developmental need for observable referents [~M]
- Reluctant writers — the affective entry point gives everyone something to say before writing
- Less suitable without adaptation for learners who already have explicit media-literacy vocabulary, for whom the reaction step may feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive awareness: recognizing one's own emotional reactions and the assumptions beneath them
- Critical media literacy: understanding that search results and images frame interpretation rather than neutrally record reality
- Discussion and perspective-taking: comparing one's reaction with peers' and reasoning about the difference

### Instructions
1. Run the image search in advance and curate the set for variety; project or distribute the images.
2. Display one image at a time; students signal their reaction (face, thumbs up/down/sideways) and the teacher charts responses.
3. Facilitate [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) on the charted pattern: What did you expect? What surprised you? What were you assuming?
4. Have students write in journals about assumptions a person might make from a simple image search, using sentence starters if needed.
5. Close by naming the general lesson: images and search results carry framing, and reactions reveal assumptions worth examining.

## Examples
- Typical assumptions surfaced by the "school lunch" search: all school lunches are disgusting; all school lunches are nutritious; kids are usually smiling when they eat school lunch; kids only like junk food; schools are always racially diverse; American kids are less healthy than kids in other countries.
- A teacher repeats the routine with the search term "scientist" to surface gender and race stereotypes in image results.
