Growth Mindset Interventions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 unmarked) · 2 studies (2 causal), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Growth mindset interventions teach learners that intellectual abilities are not fixed but can be developed through effort, effective strategies, and help from others. They are typically delivered as brief (30–60 minute), structured sessions using neuroscience evidence, stories of people who improved, and writing exercises in which learners advocate the malleability view to a struggling peer. Critically, effective versions pair the belief message with concrete strategy guidance and reduce environmental cues (e.g., harsh public ranking) that signal ability is fixed.

Design Implications¶

Mindset interventions work by changing how learners interpret difficulty: setbacks become signals to change strategy rather than evidence of low ability Yeager & Dweck's synthesis of experimental studies. [+M]. Effects are real but modest and highly conditional — the National Study of Learning Mindsets found meaningful grade improvements only for lower-achieving students in supportive school contexts Sisk et al. meta-analysis found weak average effects on achievement. [~S]. Interventions that only exhort "try harder" without teaching what to do differently show little benefit; the mechanism is effort-strategy attribution, not effort alone.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Credible, non-preachy delivery — ideally by someone other than the regular evaluator of the learner, framed as science rather than encouragement
  • Integration with instructional design that actually rewards strategy use and revision (e.g., Assessment for Learning practices, actionable Feedback)
  • A writing or self-persuasion component, which produces more durable change than passive listening Self-persuasion outperforms direct persuasion in mindset studies. [+M]
  • School or course structures that do not contradict the message (e.g., no public ability ranking)

Constraints¶

  • Effects on grades for already-high-achieving students are near zero Sisk et al. meta-analysis found weak average effects on achievement. [~S]
  • Interventions fail in environments where effort is punished or where success is attributed purely to innate talent; a mindset message cannot override a fixed-mindset system [-M]
  • Single-session "booster poster" versions without the structured curriculum or follow-up show no measurable effects [-W]
  • Overemphasis on effort praise ("you worked so hard") without strategy content can reduce performance compared to strategy-focused praise [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Core curriculum: the ~2-session online intervention used in the National Study of Learning Mindsets, with reading, brain science, and peer-advocacy writing
  • Embedded framing: instructors weaving malleability language into syllabi, error-handling routines, and Check-Ins rather than a standalone session
  • Combined belonging + mindset: pairing with belonging uncertainty interventions, which share the same "adversity is normal and temporary" attribution logic [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Lower-achieving or at-risk students, who show the largest grade gains Lower-achieving students benefited most in the national experiment. [+S]
  • Students transitioning into more challenging environments (middle school, first-year college) where setback interpretation matters most
  • Students experiencing stereotype threat or belonging uncertainty, for whom attributing difficulty to normal learning processes is protective [+M]
  • Little benefit for students who already hold growth beliefs or perform well [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Academic resilience: persistence after failure and willingness to revise work
  • Metacognitive strategy selection: treating difficulty as a cue to change approach
  • Long-term course trajectories (e.g., continued enrollment in advanced math) rather than single-test performance

Instructions¶

  1. Present scientific evidence that the brain changes with learning (neuroplasticity), establishing the malleability belief.
  2. Have learners read or hear about a person who improved through changed strategy, not just effort.
  3. Run a self-persuasion writing exercise: learners write advice to a hypothetical struggling student, articulating the growth view in their own words.
  4. Connect explicitly to strategy: teach what to do when stuck — seek help, change approach, use Practice with feedback — so effort has a direction.
  5. Align the surrounding environment: grade for revision, normalize errors, and use Assessment for Learning so the message is reinforced by experience, not words alone.

Related Strategies¶

  • Belonging interventions — same brief, attribution-based logic targeting social rather than ability uncertainty
  • Attribution retraining — the broader family of interventions changing causal explanations for success and failure
  • Formative feedback strategies — supplies the "what to do next" content that makes mindset messages actionable
  • Deliver growth mindset messages once, near the start of college or around first exams, in roughly 30-minute sessions

Examples¶

  • PERTS / National Study of Learning Mindsets — the scalable online two-session intervention used in Yeager et al. (2019), which raised grades for lower-achieving U.S. high school students across 65 schools.
  • Brainology — Mindset Works' multimedia curriculum teaching neuroplasticity to middle school students.
  • Khan Academy — embeds growth-mindset framing ("mistakes are how learning happens") into its hint and mastery systems, pairing the message with immediate retry opportunities.
