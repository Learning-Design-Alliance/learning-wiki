Turn Around Time¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 quant-synthesis), q4 · 2 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Turn around time is the interval between a learner submitting or performing work and receiving feedback on it. The strategy treats that interval as a design variable: instructors and course systems deliberately shorten it — through rapid grading cycles, automated checks, in-class conferencing, or live commentary — so learners can act on feedback while their reasoning about the task is still retrievable.

Design Implications¶

Feedback loses instructional value as the mental context of the performance decays; immediate or near-immediate feedback supports error correction and prevents errors from consolidating, particularly for procedural skills [~M]. Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and the process rather than the person, and timeliness is what makes process-level feedback actionable at all Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [+S]. Fast turn around also sustains formative assessment cycles: feedback only functions formatively if learners have time and opportunity to use it before the next performance (Assessment for Learning).

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A feedback mechanism sized to the volume of work: rubrics, comment banks, automated checking, or peer review structures
  • A scheduled opportunity for learners to apply the feedback — a revision, retry, or subsequent task — otherwise speed is wasted
  • Clear priorities in the feedback itself; rapid feedback on everything becomes noise (Feedback)

Constraints¶

  • Immediate feedback during complex problem solving can promote guessing and trial-and-error rather than deliberation, and can increase cognitive load for novices [~M] — delayed feedback sometimes benefits retention of complex material
  • Speed achieved by cutting feedback quality (bare scores, generic comments) negates the benefit; task- and process-level information matters more than raw latency Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [-S]
  • Automated instant feedback works well for well-defined answers but misleads on open-ended work where judgment is required
  • Very fast turn around is unsustainable for instructors and can produce burnout or superficial grading late in a term

Implementation Variability¶

  • Automated: auto-graded quizzes and coding checkers (e.g., Codecademy, WebAssign) give second-level turn around on well-structured tasks
  • In-class: live circulation, Check-Ins, and 5-Minute Writing Conferences deliver feedback during production rather than after
  • Staged: rapid low-stakes feedback on drafts, slower holistic feedback on final work
  • Peer-mediated: structured peer review shortens the loop while building evaluative judgment

Target Learners¶

  • Novices acquiring procedural skills, where early error correction prevents consolidation of wrong methods [~M]
  • Learners in iterative production contexts (writing, coding, design) who can revise
  • Less suitable when learners lack the background to interpret fast feedback without Coaching support

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural accuracy and skill refinement
  • Formative improvement of drafts and performances
  • Self-regulation: learners calibrate their own judgment against rapid external feedback (Self-Regulated Learning)

Instructions¶

  1. Identify which tasks most need speed — typically those where errors compound or where a revision will follow.
  2. Build the fastest adequate mechanism: rubrics, comment banks, or automated checks (Assessment).
  3. Focus rapid feedback on task and process, not scores alone Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [+S]; see Action-Oriented Feedback.
  4. Schedule the follow-up act — revision, retry, or next task — so feedback is used, not just received.
  5. For complex or open-ended work, deliberately delay feedback until learners have attempted self-assessment.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the content side of the same coin: what the feedback tells learners to do
  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — in-production feedback with near-zero turn around

Examples¶

  • Codecademy (codecademy.com) — code runs and returns errors within seconds, the extreme case of automated turn around on well-structured tasks.
  • Writing process pedagogy — instructor comments on drafts returned before the revision deadline, so feedback feeds the next version rather than terminating the assignment.
  • WebAssign / auto-graded homework systems — immediate right/wrong feedback with worked hints, trading depth for latency.
