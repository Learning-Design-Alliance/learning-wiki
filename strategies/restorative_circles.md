# Restorative Circles

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Restorative Circles bring together the people affected by a conflict or harm — typically students, plus teachers or community members — in a facilitated, round-robin dialogue. A talking piece and structured sequential questions ("What happened? Who was affected? What is needed to make things right?") ensure every voice is heard before the group jointly agrees on repair actions. The format derives from restorative justice practice (Braithwaite, 1989) and is used both reactively (responding to incidents) and proactively (community-building circles with no incident at hand).

## Design Implications

Restorative Circles convert discipline and community maintenance into a learning activity: students practice perspective-taking, accountable speech, and collective problem-solving rather than receiving a verdict from an authority. Evidence from school implementations is promising but conditional — a large randomized study of the Pittsburgh Public Schools' restorative practices program found reductions in suspensions and improved teacher-student relationships, but no improvement in academic outcomes and, for some students, worsened perceptions of school climate [~M] (Acosta et al., 2019). Empathic, relationship-focused discipline approaches show measurable reductions in exclusionary discipline [Brief empathic discipline intervention reduced suspension rates.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M], and belonging is a plausible mediating mechanism [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained facilitator who can hold neutrality and manage strong emotion
- Voluntary participation by the harmed party; circles fail when attendance is coerced
- Sufficient time — a responsive circle typically needs 30–60 minutes, not a passing-period conversation
- Follow-through structures: the group's repair agreement must be revisited and monitored
- A prior culture of [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and community-building so participants have practiced the format before using it under stress

#### Constraints
- Poorly facilitated circles can re-traum victims by forcing confrontation with the person who harmed them [~M] — victims report lower satisfaction when participation is pressured (Strang et al., 2006)
- Implemented as a substitute for adequate behavioral support rather than alongside it, restorative practices can leave teachers feeling unsupported and can worsen climate [~M] (Acosta et al., 2019)
- Requires significant adult time and training; under-resourced rollouts degrade into unstructured conversations with no repair outcome
- Power imbalances (e.g., a popular student vs. an isolated one, or staff vs. student) can silence less powerful participants unless the facilitator actively manages turn-taking

#### Implementation Variability
- **Proactive/community-building circles**: low-stakes weekly rounds on prompts, building the relational capital that responsive circles draw on
- **Responsive circles**: convened after a specific harm, following the classic three-phase question sequence
- **Talking-piece vs. open discussion**: the physical talking piece enforces sequential speaking and slows the exchange; open formats are faster but allow dominant voices to take over
- **Whole-class vs. small conference**: classroom circles address diffuse climate issues; small restorative conferences address specific incidents

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and older students, who have the perspective-taking capacity the format demands; simplified versions work in upper elementary [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Students at risk of exclusionary discipline, for whom relationship-based accountability is an alternative to suspension [Brief empathic discipline intervention reduced suspension rates.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a first response to serious violence or when the harmed party declines participation

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, emotional regulation, accountable speech
- Community and belonging: building shared norms and relational trust [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Civic and moral reasoning: practicing collective decision-making and repair of harm

### Instructions
1. **Prepare separately.** Facilitator meets briefly with each party to confirm voluntary participation and surface what each wants from the circle.
2. **Open with a check-in round.** Use a [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) question and a talking piece so every participant speaks once before any discussion of the incident.
3. **Name what happened.** Facilitator asks each participant for their account; the goal is shared facts, not adjudication of blame.
4. **Surface impacts.** Participants describe who was affected and how — this is the perspective-taking core of the circle.
5. **Agree on repair.** The group generates and commits to concrete repair actions, recorded and assigned.
6. **Close and follow up.** A closing round; the facilitator schedules a follow-up to review whether the agreement held.

## Examples
- **Pittsburgh Public Schools** — a 5-year randomized evaluation of district-wide restorative practices (RAND, 2018–2019) found reduced suspensions and improved teacher-student relationships, with mixed effects on climate: [https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2840.html](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2840.html)
- **Oakland Unified School District** — a pioneer of whole-school restorative justice; its published implementation manual is widely adopted: [https://www.ousd.org/restorativejustice](https://www.ousd.org/restorativejustice)
- **Kay Pranis's peacemaking circles** — the talking-piece circle protocol adapted from Indigenous practice, used in classrooms and community settings (Pranis, Stuart, & Wedge, 2003)
