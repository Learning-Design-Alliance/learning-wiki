Include Physical Activity¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q3 · 2 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy embeds brief bouts of physical activity — stretching, jumping jacks, active games, or movement breaks — into the learning environment, or schedules active play immediately before demanding cognitive tasks. It is carried out through classroom-based activity breaks (typically 4–20 minutes), pre-task exercise, or active seating and movement options, rather than relying solely on scheduled recess or PE.

Design Implications¶

Single bouts of moderate physical activity acutely improve attention and executive function in children, with effects strongest for tasks requiring inhibitory control and sustained focus Classroom physical activity improves attention. [+M]. Timing matters: activity placed immediately before or between demanding segments of instruction yields measurable on-task gains, whereas activity unrelated to lesson structure tends to dissipate Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes. [~M]. The strategy works as a cognitive reset — restoring attentional resources depleted by sustained seatwork — rather than as direct instruction.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A predictable routine: brief (4–20 minute), moderate-intensity activity anchored to lesson transitions
  • Space and simple equipment (open floor area, exercise balls, stretch cards) that do not require lengthy setup
  • Clear behavioral norms so the break re-enters instruction quickly
  • Optional: activity integrated with content (e.g., acting out vocabulary, movement-based math games) to combine the break with Practice

Constraints¶

  • Poorly managed breaks can increase off-task behavior and cost instructional time if re-entry routines are weak [-M]
  • High-intensity or over-arousing activity immediately before tasks requiring calm focus can impair performance Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes. [~M]
  • Learners with motor impairments, self-consciousness, or certain medical conditions may be excluded unless alternatives are offered [-M]
  • Effects on learning outcomes (not just attention) are smaller and less consistent than effects on on-task behavior [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pre-task activity: 15–20 minutes of active play before homework or a challenging lesson to prime attention
  • In-class breaks: 4–5 minute movement breaks every 20–30 minutes during long instructional blocks
  • Integrated movement: content-embedded activity (act out a story, gesture-based recall) that doubles as encoding support
  • Active seating/allowances: exercise balls, standing desks, or permission to move for individual learners who need an outlet without whole-class breaks

Target Learners¶

  • Children who struggle with sustained attention, including learners with ADHD Classroom physical activity improves attention. [+M]
  • Whole classrooms during long blocks of seatwork, where attentional fatigue is universal
  • Less necessary for short, varied lessons with frequent active-learning segments already built in

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Attention and self-regulation: restoring focus for subsequent instruction
  • Behavioral management: reducing restlessness and disruptive behavior
  • Indirect support of academic objectives by protecting time-on-task

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the points in a lesson or homework session where attention typically declines (e.g., after 20 minutes of seatwork).
  2. Schedule a brief, moderate-intensity activity break (4–10 minutes) at those points; keep intensity moderate rather than exhausting Acute exercise timing affects memory outcomes. [~M].
  3. Establish a fast re-entry routine (a signal, a breathing count, or a check-in) so the break transitions cleanly back to instruction.
  4. For learners with persistent attention difficulties, offer pre-task active play (e.g., 15 minutes outdoors before homework) or active seating as an individual accommodation.
  5. Where possible, integrate movement with content — acting out processes, gesture-based recall — so the break also serves Practice and encoding.

Related Strategies¶

  • Chunking — activity breaks are most effective when instruction is already segmented into manageable chunks
  • Brain Breaks — a closely related family of short reset activities, of which physical activity is one type
  • Active Recess — structured recess as a scheduled complement to in-class breaks

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — movement can be the vehicle for retrieval practice, not just a reset
  • Check-in — a quick re-entry routine that consolidates the break's attentional benefit
  • Accommodations — individualized movement options (active seating, pre-task play) for learners with attention difficulties

Examples¶

  • GoNoodle — Free classroom movement-break videos (2–5 minutes) used in thousands of elementary classrooms; teachers play a segment between instructional blocks.
  • TAKE 10!® (https://www.take10.net) — A published curriculum that integrates 10-minute physical activity breaks with academic content in language arts, math, and science.
  • A teacher schedules a 5-minute stretching and jumping-jacks break between two segments of a writing lesson, using a consistent signal to return to desks.
  • A parent allows 15 minutes of outdoor play before homework to increase concentration on the first, most demanding task.
