# Action Oriented Feedback

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 1 mixed, 1 unmarked) · 10 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
Action oriented feedback is feedback phrased as a concrete next step the learner can take — "add a topic sentence to this paragraph," "re-check the sign when you moved the term across the equals sign" — rather than as evaluation ("good work," "unclear") or person-level judgment ("you're careless"). It answers Hattie and Timperley's (2007) question "Where to next?" by converting diagnosis into a specific, executable move. It is carried out by identifying the gap between current and target performance, then specifying the smallest action that closes it.

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable, and much of that variance comes from what the feedback directs attention toward [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Action oriented feedback works because it targets the process level — the strategies and steps a learner can change — rather than the self level, which tends to wash out or even depress performance [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]. For the action to be usable, the learner must have a chance to act on it: feedback delivered after the final attempt, or on work that will not be revised, rarely changes behavior [Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear performance target so the "next step" points somewhere definite
- A revision or retry opportunity built into the task cycle ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), drafts, resubmission)
- Feedback tied to the task's success criteria, not to generic effort or ability
- Small enough scope that the learner can act on one or two steps at a time
- The teacher (or trained peer) must diagnose *why* a piece of writing falls short, not just *where* — action suggestions require a causal model of the problem
- Time for [conferencing](../elements/coaching.md) or structured peer-review protocols; actionable feedback cannot be delivered efficiently through margin marks alone
- Follow-up to assess whether the suggested action was taken and whether it worked ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

#### Constraints
- Feedback on work learners cannot revise is inert — the action has no target
- Too many action items at once overloads working memory and produces no action at all [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less effective for learners with strong expertise, who need less directive feedback and may find step-level instruction redundant [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]
- If the "action" is vague ("try harder," "add more detail"), it functions as person- or effort-level feedback and loses its benefit [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]
- Substantially more time-intensive than error marking; whole-class written feedback at this level of specificity is often unsustainable, which pushes designs toward conferencing, peer feedback, or whole-class common-feedback sessions
- Overly long feedback lists overwhelm learners; more than two or three actionable items per draft tends to reduce follow-through
- Learners with low self-efficacy may interpret action lists as evidence of inadequacy rather than guidance [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [~M] — framing matters ("here is your next move" vs. "here is what you failed to do")

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written margin comments** on drafts, each ending with an imperative next step
- **Automated hints** in adaptive platforms, where each hint names the next move rather than revealing the answer
- **Peer feedback protocols** that require commenters to suggest one improvement (e.g., TAG or "two stars and a wish" structured as a specific revision)
- **Conferencing** — brief one-to-one exchanges that end with an agreed action the learner records
- **Teacher–student conferences** (writing workshop model): 3–5 minute individual conversations yielding one or two named actions
- **Structured peer editing**: protocols that require reviewers to phrase comments as suggestions ("try…" rather than "this is wrong")
- **Whole-class action feedback**: the teacher identifies 2–3 common patterns across the class and teaches a corrective action to everyone, then students apply it to their own drafts
- **Audio or video feedback**: recorded commentary that walks through the draft and names actions in context

### Target Learners
- Novices, who benefit from directive, step-level guidance about what to do differently [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Struggling learners, for whom evaluative-only feedback offers no path to improvement
- Advanced learners, who should receive progressively less directive feedback as expertise grows [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]
- Novice writers benefit most from process-level direction; expert writers typically self-generate next steps and need less prescriptive feedback [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill refinement: improving drafts, procedures, or performances through iteration
- Strategy use: shifting learners toward more effective process-level approaches
- Self-regulation: internalizing the "where to next?" question so learners generate their own next steps
- Revision skill: learning to diagnose and improve drafts rather than merely transcribe corrections
- Transferable writing strategy: building a repertoire of moves (sentence variation, elaboration, organization) applicable to future writing
- Growth mindset and self-regulation in writing: seeing ability as built through specific, repeatable actions

### Instructions
1. Establish success criteria learners can see before the task ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) for learning).
2. Collect work at a point where revision is still possible.
3. Diagnose the single highest-leverage gap between the work and the criteria.
4. Phrase feedback as one or two imperative next steps ("Rewrite the opening so it states your claim").
5. Provide time and structure for learners to act on the feedback ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), revision time, or a [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) check-in).
6. Fade directiveness over successive cycles, asking learners to propose their own next steps.

## Examples
- **Draft–revise cycles in writing workshop** — teacher comments on a draft each end with one concrete revision ("split this run-on into two sentences; make the second one your evidence"), and class time is reserved for acting on them.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — hint sequences in practice exercises reveal the next step of a solution rather than the full answer, letting the learner act on each hint.
- **[Turnitin Feedback Studio](https://www.turnitin.com)** — inline comments paired with rubric criteria; instructors using QuickMarks attach a suggested fix to each flagged issue.

## Description
Action-oriented feedback tells learners what to *do next*, not just what went wrong. Instead of marking grammar errors or assigning a grade, the teacher or peer suggests specific actions — "vary your sentence openings in paragraph two," "add a topic sentence that states your claim" — that the learner can carry out on the current draft or transfer to future writing. One-on-one [conferencing](../elements/coaching.md) and structured peer editing with concrete suggestions are the primary delivery mechanisms.

## Design Implications

Feedback improves learning when it addresses the task and the process rather than the self, and when it gives learners usable next steps [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Action-oriented feedback operationalizes this by converting an evaluation into an instruction the learner can execute immediately, which supports a view of writing ability as improvable through effort [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].

### Context

- Learners must have an immediate opportunity to act on the feedback — a revision cycle or next draft ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Follow-up to assess whether the suggested action was taken and whether it worked ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

#### Constraints
- Substantially more time-intensive than error marking; whole-class written feedback at this level of specificity is often unsustainable, which pushes designs toward conferencing, peer feedback, or whole-class common-feedback sessions
- Feedback that specifies actions without explaining the underlying principle can produce mechanical compliance — the learner fixes this draft but cannot transfer the move to new writing [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [~S]

### Target Learners
- Developing writers in K–12 and higher education who treat revision as cosmetic; concrete actions give them an entry point into substantive revision [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Novice writers benefit most from process-level direction; expert writers typically self-generate next steps and need less prescriptive feedback [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- Students with low writing self-efficacy, for whom small successful actions build a sense of controllable progress [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision skill: learning to diagnose and improve drafts rather than merely transcribe corrections
- Transferable writing strategy: building a repertoire of moves (sentence variation, elaboration, organization) applicable to future writing
- Growth mindset and self-regulation in writing: seeing ability as built through specific, repeatable actions

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the draft: identify one or two high-leverage issues, prioritizing higher-order concerns (argument, organization) before surface errors
2. Confer with the writer ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)) and name each issue as a concrete action ("combine these two short sentences using a subordinating conjunction"), briefly stating why the action helps
3. Have the learner act immediately — revise on the spot or in a scheduled revision cycle ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
4. Assess the revision against the named actions and confirm or extend ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
5. Gradually shift diagnosis to the student: ask "what is your next move and why?" so learners internalize the feedback process

## Examples
**Writing workshop conferences** (Atwell/Calkins model) — the teacher confers briefly with each student during writing time, naming one specific next action per conference; students keep an "editing checklist" of accumulated actions.

**[NoRedInk](https://www.noredink.com)** — adaptive writing practice that responds to errors with targeted lessons and retry opportunities, converting error feedback into a specific practice action.
