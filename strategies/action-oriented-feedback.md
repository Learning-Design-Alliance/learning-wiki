# Action Oriented Feedback

## Description
Action oriented feedback is feedback phrased as a concrete next step the learner can take — "add a topic sentence to this paragraph," "re-check the sign when you moved the term across the equals sign" — rather than as evaluation ("good work," "unclear") or person-level judgment ("you're careless"). It answers Hattie and Timperley's (2007) question "Where to next?" by converting diagnosis into a specific, executable move. It is carried out by identifying the gap between current and target performance, then specifying the smallest action that closes it.

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable, and much of that variance comes from what the feedback directs attention toward [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Action oriented feedback works because it targets the process level — the strategies and steps a learner can change — rather than the self level, which tends to wash out or even depress performance [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]. For the action to be usable, the learner must have a chance to act on it: feedback delivered after the final attempt, or on work that will not be revised, rarely changes behavior [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear performance target so the "next step" points somewhere definite
- A revision or retry opportunity built into the task cycle ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), drafts, resubmission)
- Feedback tied to the task's success criteria, not to generic effort or ability
- Small enough scope that the learner can act on one or two steps at a time

#### Constraints
- Feedback on work learners cannot revise is inert — the action has no target
- Too many action items at once overloads working memory and produces no action at all [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less effective for learners with strong expertise, who need less directive feedback and may find step-level instruction redundant [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]
- If the "action" is vague ("try harder," "add more detail"), it functions as person- or effort-level feedback and loses its benefit [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written margin comments** on drafts, each ending with an imperative next step
- **Automated hints** in adaptive platforms, where each hint names the next move rather than revealing the answer
- **Peer feedback protocols** that require commenters to suggest one improvement (e.g., TAG or "two stars and a wish" structured as a specific revision)
- **Conferencing** — brief one-to-one exchanges that end with an agreed action the learner records

### Target Learners
- Novices, who benefit from directive, step-level guidance about what to do differently [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Struggling learners, for whom evaluative-only feedback offers no path to improvement
- Advanced learners, who should receive progressively less directive feedback as expertise grows [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill refinement: improving drafts, procedures, or performances through iteration
- Strategy use: shifting learners toward more effective process-level approaches
- Self-regulation: internalizing the "where to next?" question so learners generate their own next steps

### Instructions
1. Establish success criteria learners can see before the task ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) for learning).
2. Collect work at a point where revision is still possible.
3. Diagnose the single highest-leverage gap between the work and the criteria.
4. Phrase feedback as one or two imperative next steps ("Rewrite the opening so it states your claim").
5. Provide time and structure for learners to act on the feedback ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), revision time, or a [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) check-in).
6. Fade directiveness over successive cycles, asking learners to propose their own next steps.

## Examples
- **Draft–revise cycles in writing workshop** — teacher comments on a draft each end with one concrete revision ("split this run-on into two sentences; make the second one your evidence"), and class time is reserved for acting on them.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — hint sequences in practice exercises reveal the next step of a solution rather than the full answer, letting the learner act on each hint.
- **[Turnitin Feedback Studio](https://www.turnitin.com)** — inline comments paired with rubric criteria; instructors using QuickMarks attach a suggested fix to each flagged issue.
