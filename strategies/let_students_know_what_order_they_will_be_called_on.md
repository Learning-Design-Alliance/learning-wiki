# Let Students Know What Order They Will Be Called On

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (3 review, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
The instructor announces — at the start of a discussion or before each round — the sequence in which students will be called on (e.g., alphabetical, by seating order, by a posted roster, or by a shuffled deck shown to the class). This converts unpredictable cold-calling into a predictable turn-taking structure. Students who need more processing time, who experience discussion anxiety, or who struggle with inhibitory control can rehearse or draft a response and hold it until their turn, rather than either blurting out or disengaging.

## Design Implications

Predictability reduces the cognitive and emotional load of participation: when students are not vigilantly monitoring *whether* they will be called on, attention can be directed to *what* is being discussed [~M]. Advance notice of one's turn functions as a brief planning window, which supports more elaborated spoken responses and lowers the arousal associated with public performance [~W]. The strategy trades the accountability benefits of surprise cold-calling — which keeps all students mentally engaged because anyone might be next [~M] — for the accessibility benefits of predictability; instructors should choose deliberately between these trade-offs rather than assuming one is superior.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible, fair, and stable ordering mechanism (posted roster, numbered cards, seating chart, shuffled index cards shown face-up)
- Enough wait time between naming a student and expecting an answer (typically 3+ seconds) for the planning benefit to materialize
- A norm that the announced order is honored — breaking it destroys the predictability that makes the strategy work
- An alternative participation channel for students for whom speaking in turn remains a barrier ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md))
- A visible, consistently followed sequence (seating order, name list, rotating roster, or digital randomizer announced in advance)
- Questions substantial enough to warrant preparation time, paired with adequate [Wait Time](wait-time.md) before each response
- Consistency: the announced order must be honored, or trust in the structure collapses
- A classroom climate where imperfect answers are treated as normal ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) norms)

#### Constraints
- Once students know their position in the order, those late in the sequence may stop attending after their turn passes [-W] — mitigate by cycling through the order multiple times or re-shuffling visibly between rounds
- Removes the "everyone must be ready" pressure that makes cold-calling effective for sustaining whole-class engagement [~M]; in large seminars relying on cold-calling for accountability, announcing the order can reduce preparation rates
- Predictability does not remove speaking anxiety for all students; some students with social anxiety report that a known turn produces anticipatory dread for the entire class period [-W]
- If the order tracks a visible attribute (e.g., seating), students may perceive bias or labeling
- If the sequence is not followed reliably, the predictability benefit disappears and students may experience the practice as arbitrary [-W]
- Some students fixate on the sequence itself — rehearsing only "their" question and tuning out other turns, reducing attention to peer contributions [~W]
- Announced order can enable avoidance: students may disengage after their turn has passed, or experience heightened anticipatory anxiety in the minutes before their turn [~M]
- Less appropriate for genuinely open, emergent discussions where the flow of ideas should determine who speaks next

#### Implementation Variability
- **Announced random order**: shuffle cards publicly each round — predictable within a round, unpredictable across rounds
- **Opt-in advance notice**: students may request knowing their turn in advance (a private accommodation) while the default remains random
- **Order within small groups**: apply the strategy at table level rather than whole-class level, lowering the audience size
- **Written-first variants**: students draft responses during the round before their turn ([Think-Pair-Share](../elements/class-discussion.md) hybrid)
- **Announced rotation** — a fixed order (alphabetical, seating) posted before discussion begins
- **Pre-assigned questions** — students receive their specific question in advance, not just their turn
- **Randomizer with preview** — a digital tool (e.g., a shuffled name wheel) displays the upcoming order so it is random but known
- **Opt-out clause** — students may pass once per session, preserving structure while lowering threat

### Target Learners
- Students with inhibitory control or impulsivity challenges, who benefit from an external structure that makes waiting purposeful rather than effortful [~W]
- Students with discussion anxiety or slower processing speed, who gain planning time before public performance
- English language learners, who can rehearse phrasing before speaking
- Less beneficial for students already confident speaking extemporaneously, for whom the notice adds little [~W]
- Students with inhibition, impulsivity, or self-regulation challenges (e.g., ADHD), for whom waiting unpredictably is disproportionately difficult [~M]
- Anxious students and English language learners, who benefit from preparation time before speaking publicly [+W]
- Whole-class use: predictable turn-taking raises overall participation relative to volunteer-only formats, where a small fraction of students dominate [Dallimore's cold-calling research](https://doi.org/10.1177/1052562912446067) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Participation and engagement in discussion-based objectives ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Self-regulation: practicing response planning and turn-taking [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Oral elaboration quality: longer, more considered contributions when planning time precedes speaking [~W]
- Participation equity: distributing speaking opportunities across all students
- Oral reasoning: improving response quality through advance rehearsal

### Instructions
1. Choose and publicize the ordering mechanism (roster, cards, seating) and explain *why* — that predictability is an access measure, not a surveillance tool.
2. Open the discussion round by announcing the order or displaying it ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can serve as the visible structure).
3. Pose the question to the whole group first, so all students prepare even though only one will answer.
4. When calling on a student, allow extended wait time before prompting; resist rescuing too quickly ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) cues rather than answers).
5. After each student's turn, briefly affirm the contribution to sustain [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M] and keep late-order students engaged.
6. Re-shuffle or rotate visibly between rounds so no student is permanently "last."

## Related Strategies
- Cold-calling with random selection — the accountability-oriented counterpart; announcing the order is a deliberate softening of it
- Wait time extension — the planning window only pays off if the instructor actually pauses after naming the student
- Talk tokens or equity sticks — alternative turn-management structures serving the same access goal
- Cold calling with advance warning — announced order is cold calling made predictable; the warning is what changes its affective profile
- Think-pair-share — pair rehearsal before the called-on turn improves response quality under the same structure
- Volunteer-only discussion — the contrast case; equitable but often dominated by a few confident students

## Examples
- **Harvard Business School-style cold-calling, modified**: some case-method instructors post the day's calling sequence on the board before class so students can prepare their case analysis for their slot — retaining accountability while adding preparation time.
- **Equity sticks with public draw**: an elementary teacher draws name sticks in view of the class and lays each drawn stick face-up in a row, so students can see exactly who is next.
- **CAST UDL Guidelines (Representation & Action/Expression)**: advance notice of turn order exemplifies UDL's guidance on making expectations and timing predictable to reduce threat and distraction ([https://udlguidelines.cast.org](https://udlguidelines.cast.org)).
- A middle-school teacher posts a rotating name wheel at the start of a [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md); a student with impulse-control challenges can see he is fifth, rehearses his answer, and waits without calling out.
- A law school seminar using modified cold calling (as studied at Harvard Business School) tells students the day's discussion order in advance, so all students prepare all cases rather than gambling on not being called.
- An ESL discussion course distributes numbered question cards before a seminar; each student knows both their turn and their question, allowing them to draft language in advance.

# Letting students know what order they will be called on

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 review, 1 theoretical), `q3` · 0 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
The instructor announces — or posts — the sequence in which students will be called on to respond during discussion, rather than selecting speakers unpredictably. This gives each student advance notice of when their turn will come, allowing them to rehearse a response, manage anxiety, and inhibit the impulse to call out or disengage. It converts an unpredictable social demand into a predictable, plannable one.

## Design Implications

Predictability reduces the executive-function load of classroom questioning: students who know when they will speak can allocate working memory to composing an answer rather than monitoring "will I be next?" [~M]. Advance notice also functions as a form of [Wait Time](wait-time.md) distributed across the room — every student gets thinking time before their turn, not just the one who is called on. Structured, predictable turn-taking is a core feature of well-managed classrooms and is associated with higher participation rates than purely voluntary or random questioning [Dallimore's cold-calling research](https://doi.org/10.1177/1052562912446067) [+M].

1. Decide the sequence method (rotation, seating order, or previewed randomizer) and make it visible before discussion begins.
2. Pose the question to the whole class and provide adequate [Wait Time](wait-time.md) — several seconds of silent thinking, or brief pair rehearsal — before the first call.
3. Follow the announced order exactly; if a student passes, move on without penalty and return later if desired.
4. Keep the whole class accountable between turns with follow-up prompts ("Who can build on that?") so attention does not end when a student's turn passes.
5. Debrief periodically: ask students whether the structure helps them participate, and adjust the sequence method accordingly ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md)).
