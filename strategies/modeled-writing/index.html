Modeled Writing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (3 for, 3 mixed) · 12 studies (7 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q3–q4 · 2 of 12 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Modeled writing is a strategy in which the teacher or expert writer composes text while learners watch, narrating each decision — word choice, sentence construction, planning, and revision — as it is made. Unlike shared or guided writing, the learners do not contribute to the text; their role is to observe the entire generative process, including hesitation, self-correction, and rereading. It is the writing analogue of a live Demonstration with Think-Aloud narration.

Design Implications¶

Modeled writing makes the normally invisible process of composition visible, converting tacit expert knowledge into observable moves van Gog & Rummel, 2010 [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the narration explaining why choices are made, not just what is written — silent modeling of finished text is far less effective than reasoning-aloud modeling Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S]. Because writing is a high-cognitive load task combining planning, transcription, and revision simultaneously Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+S], observing a model lets novices study one layer of the process at a time before coordinating all of them.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuine composition task performed live (or on screen), not a pre-written text merely displayed — the value lies in watching decisions unfold
  • Verbalized reasoning: the modeler articulates goals, alternatives considered, and self-monitoring ("This sentence sounds flat — let me reread the last one…")
  • Text at or slightly above learners' independent level, so the modeling stretches rather than loses them
  • A prompt follow-on: Guided Writing, Practice, or independent drafting of a similar text

Constraints¶

  • Observation without subsequent writing produces illusory competence; learners rate modeled text easier to produce than it actually is Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [-S]
  • Overly polished, error-free modeling denies learners the chance to see diagnosis and repair — deliberately composing through problems (and even modeling brief error-and-correction) improves discrimination Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [~M]
  • Less effective for learners already fluent in the genre, for whom modeling is redundant Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • Long modeling sessions exceed attention limits; keep segments short (5–10 minutes) and focused on one aspect of craft Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Interactive modeling: pause to poll learners on word choice before committing — a bridge toward Shared Writing
  • Revision-focused modeling: start from a rough draft and revise publicly, foregrounding the editing process rather than first-draft generation
  • Video or screencast modeling: recorded think-aloud writing (e.g., in Writing Next-style process instruction) allows replay and self-pacing
  • Faded modeling: move from full modeling → shared writing → guided writing → independent writing as the Scaffolding sequence

Target Learners¶

  • Novice writers and emergent literacy learners who have not internalized how writers plan and revise Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • English learners, who benefit from hearing the reasoning behind syntactic and vocabulary choices in accessible language
  • Struggling writers who tend to treat writing as pure transcription; modeling surfaces planning and self-regulation strategies [+M]
  • Less valuable for proficient writers in a familiar genre Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural knowledge of the writing process: planning, drafting, revising, editing
  • Genre conventions: how a particular text type is constructed sentence by sentence
  • Self-regulated writing: internalizing monitoring and revision moves observed in the model
  • Craft skills: sentence combining, word choice, paragraph organization

Instructions¶

  1. Choose a short, authentic writing task matched to the unit's genre and display the writing surface for all learners.
  2. Plan aloud first — model generating and rejecting ideas before any text appears (Think-Aloud).
  3. Compose slowly, verbalizing each decision: audience, purpose, sentence structure, word choice, and punctuation.
  4. Deliberately encounter and resolve a problem (an awkward sentence, a missing transition) so learners see revision as normal, not failure.
  5. Reread the completed text aloud, modeling evaluation against the intended purpose.
  6. Transition immediately to Guided Writing or independent drafting of a parallel prompt, with the model text available as a reference.

Related Strategies¶

  • Shared Writing — the next scaffold step, where learners contribute text while the teacher scribes
  • Guided Writing — practice phase following modeling, with teacher support during independent composition
  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the general narration technique applied across domains
  • Interactive Writing — a variant distributing the pen to learners during composition

Examples¶

  • Regie Routman's Writing Essentials (2005) — a widely used K–8 professional framework built around the "demonstration → shared demonstration → guided practice" writing sequence.
  • The Writing Revolution — Hochman & Wexler's method models sentence-level construction (e.g., because/but/so sentences) live before students attempt their own.
  • Early years "Morning Message" — the teacher composes a daily class message on chart paper, verbalizing letter formation, spacing, and word choice while children observe.
