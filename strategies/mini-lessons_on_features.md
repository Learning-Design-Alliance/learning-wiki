# Mini-Lessons on Features

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A mini-lesson on features is a brief (5–15 minute), tightly focused instructional episode targeting a single, nameable craft feature of writing — e.g., how a strong title works, how sentence variety controls rhythm, how evidence is introduced and cited. Each lesson typically follows a consistent arc: name the feature, show it in a mentor text or [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), then have students try it immediately in their own drafts. Mini-lessons are sequenced across a unit so that features accumulate into a repertoire rather than arriving as a one-time lecture.

## Design Implications

Mini-lessons work because they isolate one skill at a time, keeping working-memory demands low and making the target feature salient against the complexity of whole-text writing [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on the immediate application step: instruction on a feature followed by same-day drafting practice outperforms instruction alone, consistent with meta-analytic findings that explicit, strategy-focused writing instruction improves writing quality [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of writing instruction](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.276) [+S]. Asking students to find or explain the feature in mentor texts adds a [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) step that deepens conceptual understanding of *why* the feature works, not just *what* it is [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of mentor texts (published or student-written) that display the target feature clearly
- A predictable lesson arc: name → show → try, with time reserved for immediate drafting practice ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Ongoing student writing in progress, so each mini-lesson has a live place to land
- A planned sequence of features aligned to the unit's genre and [assessment criteria](../elements/assessment.md)

#### Constraints
- Fragmenting writing into isolated features can crowd out attention to ideas and meaning; students may produce technically polished but hollow prose if features are taught as ornaments rather than as serving purpose [~M]
- Whole-class mini-lessons mismatch learners who have already mastered the feature or lack the prerequisite skill; the [expertise-reversal effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) means redundant instruction for proficient writers wastes time or even interferes [~M]
- Time-consuming to prepare well: each lesson needs a genuine mentor example, not a contrived one
- If mini-lessons are not revisited, one-shot exposure to a feature rarely sticks; features need spaced re-teaching and conferring follow-up [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class launch:** teacher delivers the mini-lesson to all students before writing time (classic workshop model)
- **Needs-based small group:** re-teach a feature only to students whose drafts show the gap, using draft-based data
- **Student-selected:** students choose from a menu of feature mini-lessons (recorded videos or stations) based on their own revision goals
- **Mentor-text inquiry:** instead of direct telling, students examine contrasting mentor examples and name the feature themselves — a [comparing-cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) variant that supports abstraction [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Developing writers in upper elementary through college who have drafts in progress and can apply a feature the same day
- Particularly valuable for students who write at length but with limited craft range — the feature focus gives them a concrete next move
- Less beneficial as whole-class instruction for advanced writers, who need individualized goals rather than uniform feature coverage [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Craft and style: sentence variety, imagery, word choice, titles, leads
- Organization and structure: introductions, transitions, paragraphing
- Genre conventions: citing evidence, integrating quotations, audience-appropriate tone
- Revision behavior: giving students named features to hunt for in their own drafts builds revision strategies

### Instructions
1. **Name the feature** in student-friendly language and connect it to the unit's writing purpose ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md)).
2. **Show it working:** display the feature in one or two mentor texts, annotating what the writer did and why ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)); contrasting a strong and weak version sharpens discrimination.
3. **Debrief the why:** ask students to explain the effect of the feature on the reader, prompting [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
4. **Try it now:** students apply the feature to their own draft for 3–5 minutes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)), while the teacher confers with individuals.
5. **Share and revisit:** one or two students read their attempt aloud; the feature reappears in revision checklists and future conferences so instruction is spaced, not one-shot [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **Units of Study in Opinion, Information, and Narrative Writing (Calkins et al., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project)** — each session opens with a 10-minute mini-lesson on a named craft feature, followed by independent writing with conferring ([Heinemann](https://www.heinemann.com)).
- **A mini-lesson on introductions:** the teacher shows two published essay openings, annotates how each establishes stakes, students explain the difference, then revise their own opening paragraphs in the last five minutes of the lesson.
- **A mini-lesson on evidence integration:** students compare a dropped quotation with one introduced and analyzed in a mentor paragraph, then revise one quotation in their own draft using the "quote sandwich" move.
