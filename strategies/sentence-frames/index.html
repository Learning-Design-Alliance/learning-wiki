Sentence Frames¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (4 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q3 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Sentence frames are partially completed sentence templates ("I agree with __ because ____.") that learners complete with their own content when speaking or writing. They scaffold the language of a task so learners can devote working memory to the ideas, and they model the discourse conventions of a discipline or community — including how to agree, disagree, and ask for elaboration respectfully.

Design Implications¶

Sentence frames reduce the linguistic and cognitive burden of response production, letting learners focus on reasoning rather than formulating syntax from scratch Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. They function as a form of Cognitive Load Management: by externalizing the sentence structure, frames prevent learners from abandoning substantive participation because they lack the words. Frames are most effective when they model authentic disciplinary discourse (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning in science) rather than generic fill-in-the-blank forms, and when they are faded as learners internalize the structures [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Frames matched to the actual discourse function of the task (agreeing, disagreeing, citing evidence, hedging)
  • A language model worth imitating — frames should reflect how experts in the discipline actually talk and write
  • Opportunities for Peer Discussion where frames are used in authentic exchanges, not just written worksheets
  • A plan for fading: moving from full frames to word banks to independent production
  • Frames matched to the actual discourse moves of the task (agree, counter, elaborate, cite evidence), not generic sentence starters
  • Teacher modeling of frame use before independent or peer use (Think-Aloud or live demonstration)
  • A structured exchange context where frames are genuinely needed — e.g., Peer Discussion, Class Discussion, or structured academic controversy
  • A plan for fading: shifting from full frames to word banks to independent production as fluency grows

Constraints¶

  • Frames can feel artificial or stilted, especially for learners with stronger language proficiency, and may suppress authentic voice if mandated indefinitely [-M]
  • Over-reliance without fading can produce formulaic responses that mimic structure without understanding [~M]
  • Effectiveness depends on community norms: in discussion settings, frames only reduce conflict if peers actually use and reinforce them; without moderation or modeling, learners abandon them [-W]
  • Frames that supply too much content (not just structure) do the intellectual work for the learner and reduce the task to cloze completion [-M]
  • Overuse without fading produces formulaic, mechanical responses and can suppress authentic voice [-M] — learners may fill frames without genuine engagement when the frame no longer matches their thinking
  • Frames that exceed learners' current syntactic competence shift the load back to language production and defeat the purpose [~M]
  • In online or asynchronous settings, frames can feel artificial and invite perfunctory compliance unless community norms and modeling establish their purpose
  • Less useful for learners already fluent in the target register, for whom frames add redundancy rather than support [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Oral discussion frames — posted sentence starters for accountable talk in seminars and debates ("Can you say more about ______?")
  • Writing frames — paragraph or argument templates with discourse connectives ("One counterargument is…; however,…")
  • Digital citizenship frames — templates for respectful online disagreement ("I see it differently because __. Have you considered ____?")
  • Differentiated frames — multiple frame tiers offered simultaneously so learners self-select support level
  • Speaking frames for structured discussion protocols (e.g., Socratic seminars, debate prep) versus writing frames for constructed responses
  • Word banks + frames for multilingual learners at earlier proficiency levels; open frames ("One thing that complicates this is…") for advanced learners
  • Digital adaptations: discussion-board templates, chat starters in collaborative platforms, or comment stems embedded in peer-review tools
  • Frames can be co-constructed with students from an anchor text, which builds awareness of the discourse genre itself

Target Learners¶

  • Multilingual learners and students developing academic language, who benefit most from explicit models of disciplinary syntax [~S]
  • Novices to a discussion format (e.g., first-time debaters, online forum participants) who lack scripts for respectful disagreement
  • Less beneficial — and potentially irritating — for proficient speakers, for whom frames can be redundant or constraining [~M]
  • Multilingual learners and students acquiring academic language, where the gap between ideas and expression is largest [+M]
  • Novices to a discipline's discourse conventions (e.g., students learning to argue from evidence in science) Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension. [+M]
  • Reluctant participants in discussion, for whom the frame lowers the social risk of entering conversation
  • Less beneficial for learners already fluent in the register, who may find frames constraining [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Academic language development: producing discipline-appropriate syntax and discourse moves
  • Argumentation quality: structuring claims, evidence, and rebuttals
  • Social-emotional goals: respectful dialogue, perspective-taking, and productive disagreement in online and face-to-face settings
  • Academic language development: producing discipline-appropriate syntax and vocabulary Accessible vocabulary and syntax supports comprehension. [+M]
  • Dialogic skill: disagreeing respectfully, building on others' ideas, citing evidence
  • Argumentation quality: structuring claims with reasons and evidence Collaborative writing improves text quality. [+M]
  • Participation equity: distributing talk beyond confident volunteers

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the discourse function of the task (agree, disagree, elaborate, cite evidence) and draft 2–4 frames per function using authentic expert language.
  2. Model the frames in use before requiring them — think aloud while completing a frame with real content.
  3. Post frames visibly (slide, poster, chat template) during the Peer Discussion activity.
  4. Require initial use, then invite learners to adapt or abandon frames as fluency grows — fade deliberately.
  5. Assess both the content of responses and the quality of interaction (clarity, respectfulness), using observation and participant feedback.

Related Strategies¶

  • Think-Pair-Share — a discussion structure in which sentence frames are commonly embedded to structure the pair exchange
  • Accountable Talk — a discourse routine whose norms sentence frames operationalize
  • Sentence Stems and Word Banks — lighter-weight variants that provide vocabulary rather than full syntax
  • Provide templates and publicly posted talk moves to scaffold discussion and lower cognitive load
  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the modeling method that shows learners when a frame fits, not just what it says
  • Acting-Role-Play — rehearsal context where frames can be practiced before authentic use
  • Active-Listening — frames often encode listening moves ("So what you're saying is…") that support this strategy
  • Scaffold mathematical discussion with paraphrasing, restating, formulaic expressions, elaborating questions, and key vocabulary in context
  • Offer generative formulaic expressions instead of rigid sentence starters

Examples¶

  • Science argumentation: "The evidence shows __, which supports my claim that ____" used in claim–evidence–reasoning (CER) writing frames common in NGSS-aligned curricula.
  • Online discussion boards: An instructor pins a "respectful disagreement" template ("I understand your point about __, but I see it differently because ____") at the top of a course forum thread.
  • Structured academic controversy: In debate formats, each side is given frames for presenting positions ("Our position is __ because _") and responding ("We hear your claim that ; however, ___").
  • Academic conversations (Zwiers & Crawford, 2011) — content-area teachers use prompt, respond, and follow-up frames ("What do you mean by ?" "I agree/disagree with ___ because ") to build sustained peer dialogue across subjects.
  • Structured academic controversy in online forums — digital citizenship lessons supply disagreement frames ("I see it differently because ," "Can you say more about ?") so that online debate stays evidence-based and respectful rather than adversarial.
  • Science explanation frames — claim-evidence-reasoning stems ("The data show , which supports my claim that ___ because ") scaffold scientific writing in curricula such as those from the Claim-Evidence-Reasoning framework.
