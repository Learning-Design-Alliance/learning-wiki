# Graphic Organizers and Guided Notes (Processing Speed)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (5 for, 3 mixed, 2 against) · 28 studies (12 causal, 8 quant-synthesis, 7 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 7 of 28 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
This strategy provides students with pre-structured supports — graphic organizers (visual maps of relationships among ideas) or guided notes (lecture outlines with deliberate blanks, cues, and headings) — during direct instruction. The structure externalizes the organizational demands of note-taking so students can devote working memory to comprehending content rather than deciding what to record and how to format it.

## Design Implications

Guided notes and organizers reduce the real-time processing burden of lectures by pre-identifying main ideas and relationships, which is especially valuable for students whose processing speed lags behind the pace of spoken instruction [Guided notes improve note accuracy and quiz performance relative to self-generated notes.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Graphic organizers leverage dual coding — pairing verbal content with spatial-visual structure — to support encoding and retrieval [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because blanks and prompts direct attention to designated content, they also function as advance organizers that frame what is coming [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Organizer or note templates aligned section-by-section with the actual lecture sequence
- Cues that signal importance (headings, blanks, bolding) rather than decorative graphics [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [-M]
- Brief instruction in how to use the template, and a follow-up activity that requires students to work with their completed notes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- A prepared organizer or guided-notes handout aligned to the lecture's actual structure (sequence, hierarchy, compare/contrast)
- Blanks or prompts at the key points, not scattered trivially — the gaps should mark what matters
- Instructor cues ("this goes in your chart") signaling when to write, so learners with slow processing are not caught mid-sentence
- Writing utensils or digital input tools; allow drawing or typing as alternatives
- A clear analysis of the content's structure (hierarchy, sequence, cause–effect, compare–contrast) so the organizer matches it
- Blanks or prompts in guided notes that target the most important ideas, not trivial details
- Time during instruction for students to complete the organizer or notes ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure.md))
- An accessible format: text alternatives for visually impaired learners, and vocabulary at an appropriate level ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- A structure that mirrors the actual logical organization of the lecture content, not a generic template
- Deliberate blanks or incomplete cells that require active completion — fully pre-filled handouts invite passive receipt [-M]
- Alignment between the organizer's categories and later assessment or application tasks
- Brief instruction in how to use the organizer, especially for learners unfamiliar with the format

#### Constraints
- Poorly aligned templates force students to hunt for where the lecture "is" in the organizer, adding load rather than reducing it [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Over-scaffolding can reduce generative processing: students who only fill blanks may engage in shallower encoding than students who summarize in their own words [~M]
- Some students prefer and benefit from self-structured notes; mandating a single format can suppress useful personal note-taking strategies [~W]
- Less suitable for open-ended discussion, exploratory content, or lectures whose structure emerges only at the end
- Requires substantial preparation time for the instructor; poorly aligned organizers add load rather than reducing it
- Some learners find the structure too rigid if not tailored; over-specified notes can suppress note-taking skill development [~M]
- If blanks are too frequent, learners fall behind the lecture and lose the thread — the tool then *creates* processing-speed problems
- Benefits shrink for learners with strong prior knowledge, who may find the scaffolding redundant [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Pre-filled, complete organizers viewed passively produce weaker learning than ones students complete themselves [Concept and knowledge maps improve learning.](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- Overly detailed guided notes can reduce engagement — students copy mechanically without processing [Guided notes improve note accuracy and quiz performance.](../claims/guided-notes-improve-note-accuracy.md) [~M]
- A single organizer format will not fit all content types; forcing a timeline onto non-sequential content distorts it
- Visual formats may be inaccessible without accommodation ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md))
- Fully completed notes or organizers reduce engagement and produce shallower learning than partial versions requiring completion [-M]
- A poorly matched structure (e.g., a hierarchy imposed on content that is actually comparative) can mislead encoding and impair later recall [-M]
- Overly dense organizers can themselves overload working memory, reproducing the problem they were meant to solve [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Partial blanks (key terms deleted) vs. skeletal outlines (only headings provided) — fade the amount of pre-filled content as note-taking skill grows
- Visual formats: concept maps, flowcharts, comparison matrices, timelines, cause–effect frames
- Color-coding and icons for students who benefit from visual categorization
- Post-lecture completion: students fill remaining blanks from a recording or peer notes, adding retrieval practice
- Partial outlines (headings + key terms) vs. heavily scaffolded diagrams (flowcharts, matrices, concept maps)
- Pre-lecture distribution for preview vs. in-lecture completion; preview supports learners who need extra processing time
- Digital versions with typed or drag-and-drop input for learners with writing difficulties
- Fading the scaffold over a term — progressively more open notes as note-taking skill develops

- **Advance organizers**: a brief structural overview presented *before* new material ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- **Student-constructed maps**: learners build their own concept map after instruction, converting the organizer into a generative activity ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- **Scaffolded fading**: gradually removing blanks and prompts as note-taking skill develops ([Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md))
- **Partial guided notes**: headings and key terms provided; definitions, examples, and relationships left blank for completion

- **Student-completed organizers**: learners fill in an empty framework as a generative activity, shifting from scaffold to task
- **Post-lecture organizers**: provided after note-taking for learners who need to practice independent structuring first

- Less necessary for experts, who already organize content effectively and may find imposed structures constraining
- Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content in a lecture [Emphasis relevance directs attention during learning.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners with processing-speed or working-memory challenges, who lose lecture content while transcribing

### Target Learning Goals
- Identifying and recording main ideas and their relationships during live instruction
- Building structured schemas of lecture content (hierarchies, sequences, comparisons)
- Developing independent note-taking skills through gradually faded scaffolds
- Comprehension of key concepts during expository presentation
- Note-taking skill development (the organizer models what good notes look like)
- Comprehension and retention of structured expository content
- Integration: seeing relationships among concepts, not just isolated facts
- Note-taking as a study skill — guided notes model what good notes look like, supporting eventual independent structuring

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content and select an organizer format that matches its structure (sequence → flowchart; comparison → matrix; hierarchy → concept map).
2. Create guided notes with headings matching the lecture outline, key terms intact, and blanks for high-value details — roughly 20–30% of content left for students to complete.
3. Briefly orient students to the template before instruction begins, connecting it to prior knowledge ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Deliver the lecture with explicit verbal cues ("this goes in the first blank") so students can locate content without speed-dependent search.
5. Follow with an application activity that requires using the completed notes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)), then progressively reduce pre-structuring across the unit.

## Description
Graphic organizers are visual frameworks — concept maps, timelines, comparison matrices, flowcharts — that structure the relationships among ideas in a lesson. Guided notes are instructor-prepared lecture notes with key information removed (blanks, prompts, incomplete diagrams) that students complete during instruction. Both externalize the structure of the content so that learners spend limited working memory on comprehension rather than on deciding what to record.

## Design Implications

Both tools reduce extraneous load during lecture by shifting the burden of selection and organization from the learner to the instructor [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Graphic organizers show stronger effects when learners actively construct or complete them rather than passively view a finished organizer [Concept and knowledge maps improve learning.](../claims/concept-mapping-improves-learning.md) [+M]. ### Instructions
1. Analyze the lesson content to identify its core structure (e.g., cause–effect, classification, sequence).
2. Select or design an organizer that mirrors that structure — a timeline for historical processes, a matrix for comparisons, a concept map for hierarchical topics ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. Convert it into guided notes by removing key terms, examples, or link labels, leaving cues about what belongs in each blank ([Note-Taking](../elements/note-taking.md)).
4. Present the organizer at the start of the lesson so students know where the lecture is heading ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
5. Pause at each blank during instruction and prompt students to complete it; briefly discuss what they wrote ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md)).
6. End with a synthesis step — students summarize the completed organizer in their own words or use it to answer an application question ([Elaborative Interrogation](elaborative-interrogation.md)).

# Providing Graphic Organizers or Guided Notes

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q3` · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
During lectures, the instructor supplies a partially completed visual structure — a graphic organizer (matrix, hierarchy, concept map) or guided notes (skeleton outline with blanks, headings, and cues) — that students complete during the session. The materials externalize the lecture's structure, signaling which points matter and how they relate, so learners spend limited working memory on comprehension rather than on deciding what to record.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it offloads the selection and organization stages of note-taking, which compete with listening for working-memory resources [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Graphic organizers are most effective when they make relationships (compare/contrast, hierarchy, sequence) visible rather than merely listing topics; matrices that force learners to map facts across categories outperform outlines for retention and integration [Graphic organizers that structure relationships improve learning from lectures.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.87.3.455) [+M]. Guided notes also raise the quality of students' own notes and subsequent test performance compared with unstructured note-taking [+M], and provided organizers can outperform learner-constructed notes when learners lack the skill to organize on the fly [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A structure that mirrors the actual logical organization of the lecture content, not a generic template
- Deliberate blanks or incomplete cells that require active completion — fully pre-filled handouts invite passive receipt [-M]
- Alignment between the organizer's categories and later assessment or application tasks
- Brief instruction in how to use the organizer, especially for learners unfamiliar with the format

#### Constraints
- Fully completed notes or organizers reduce engagement and produce shallower learning than partial versions requiring completion [-M]
- A poorly matched structure (e.g., a hierarchy imposed on content that is actually comparative) can mislead encoding and impair later recall [-M]
- Overly dense organizers can themselves overload working memory, reproducing the problem they were meant to solve [~M]
- For learners with strong prior knowledge or fluent note-taking skills, provided structure adds little and may reduce generative processing [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional support.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Partial guided notes**: headings and key terms provided; definitions, examples, and relationships left blank for completion
- **Matrix organizers**: rows and columns for comparing cases, theories, or events — especially strong for integration across items
- **Advance organizer + guided notes**: a global preview before the lecture, then a completion scaffold during it
- **Student-completed organizers**: learners fill in an empty framework as a generative activity, shifting from scaffold to task
- **Post-lecture organizers**: provided after note-taking for learners who need to practice independent structuring first

### Target Learners
- Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content in a lecture [Emphasis relevance directs attention during learning.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners with processing-speed or working-memory challenges, who lose lecture content while transcribing
- Students with weak or untaught note-taking skills; benefit diminishes as note-taking fluency grows [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and retention of structured expository content
- Integration: seeing relationships among concepts, not just isolated facts
- Note-taking as a study skill — guided notes model what good notes look like, supporting eventual independent structuring

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content to identify its core structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison) and the handful of ideas that matter most.
2. Design a partial organizer or guided-note skeleton that makes that structure visible, leaving blanks at the points you most want attended and encoded — blank placement is the attentional signal.
3. Present a brief orientation to the organizer before the lecture begins, connecting it to prior knowledge where possible ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. During the lecture, pause at completion points so students can write; prompt brief elaboration of what they record, since generative completion beats verbatim transcription ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
5. Keep segments digestible so the organizer's chunks align with the lecture's chunks ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
6. Use the completed organizer as a retrieval or application tool afterward — a review prompt, a basis for discussion, or a pre-writing structure ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) — if unavailable, use application activities directly).
