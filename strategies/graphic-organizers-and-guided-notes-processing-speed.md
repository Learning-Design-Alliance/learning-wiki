# Graphic Organizers and Guided Notes (Processing Speed)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (4 for, 1 mixed, 2 against) · 19 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 5 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 6 of 19 report an effect size

## Description
This strategy provides students with pre-structured supports — graphic organizers (visual maps of relationships among ideas) or guided notes (lecture outlines with deliberate blanks, cues, and headings) — during direct instruction. The structure externalizes the organizational demands of note-taking so students can devote working memory to comprehending content rather than deciding what to record and how to format it.

## Design Implications

Guided notes and organizers reduce the real-time processing burden of lectures by pre-identifying main ideas and relationships, which is especially valuable for students whose processing speed lags behind the pace of spoken instruction [Guided notes improve note accuracy and quiz performance relative to self-generated notes.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Graphic organizers leverage dual coding — pairing verbal content with spatial-visual structure — to support encoding and retrieval [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because blanks and prompts direct attention to designated content, they also function as advance organizers that frame what is coming [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Organizer or note templates aligned section-by-section with the actual lecture sequence
- Cues that signal importance (headings, blanks, bolding) rather than decorative graphics [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-M]
- Brief instruction in how to use the template, and a follow-up activity that requires students to work with their completed notes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- A prepared organizer or guided-notes handout aligned to the lecture's actual structure (sequence, hierarchy, compare/contrast)
- Blanks or prompts at the key points, not scattered trivially — the gaps should mark what matters
- Instructor cues ("this goes in your chart") signaling when to write, so learners with slow processing are not caught mid-sentence
- Writing utensils or digital input tools; allow drawing or typing as alternatives

#### Constraints
- Poorly aligned templates force students to hunt for where the lecture "is" in the organizer, adding load rather than reducing it [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Over-scaffolding can reduce generative processing: students who only fill blanks may engage in shallower encoding than students who summarize in their own words [~M]
- Some students prefer and benefit from self-structured notes; mandating a single format can suppress useful personal note-taking strategies [~W]
- Less suitable for open-ended discussion, exploratory content, or lectures whose structure emerges only at the end
- Requires substantial preparation time for the instructor; poorly aligned organizers add load rather than reducing it
- Some learners find the structure too rigid if not tailored; over-specified notes can suppress note-taking skill development [~M]
- If blanks are too frequent, learners fall behind the lecture and lose the thread — the tool then *creates* processing-speed problems
- Benefits shrink for learners with strong prior knowledge, who may find the scaffolding redundant [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Partial blanks (key terms deleted) vs. skeletal outlines (only headings provided) — fade the amount of pre-filled content as note-taking skill grows
- Visual formats: concept maps, flowcharts, comparison matrices, timelines, cause–effect frames
- Color-coding and icons for students who benefit from visual categorization
- Post-lecture completion: students fill remaining blanks from a recording or peer notes, adding retrieval practice
- Partial outlines (headings + key terms) vs. heavily scaffolded diagrams (flowcharts, matrices, concept maps)
- Pre-lecture distribution for preview vs. in-lecture completion; preview supports learners who need extra processing time
- Digital versions with typed or drag-and-drop input for learners with writing difficulties
- Fading the scaffold over a term — progressively more open notes as note-taking skill develops

### Target Learners
- Students with processing-speed challenges or slow handwriting who cannot transcribe at lecture pace
- Students with limited prior knowledge who cannot yet judge which information is important [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]
- Students with working-memory constraints, for whom chunked visual structure reduces load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less necessary for students with strong domain knowledge, who already organize content effectively and may find templates redundant [~W]
- Students with learning disabilities or ADHD; graphic organizers show consistent positive effects for students with LD [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Identifying and recording main ideas and their relationships during live instruction
- Building structured schemas of lecture content (hierarchies, sequences, comparisons)
- Developing independent note-taking skills through gradually faded scaffolds
- Comprehension of key concepts during expository presentation
- Note-taking skill development (the organizer models what good notes look like)

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content and select an organizer format that matches its structure (sequence → flowchart; comparison → matrix; hierarchy → concept map).
2. Create guided notes with headings matching the lecture outline, key terms intact, and blanks for high-value details — roughly 20–30% of content left for students to complete.
3. Briefly orient students to the template before instruction begins, connecting it to prior knowledge ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Deliver the lecture with explicit verbal cues ("this goes in the first blank") so students can locate content without speed-dependent search.
5. Follow with an application activity that requires using the completed notes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)), then progressively reduce pre-structuring across the unit.

## Examples
- **Cornell-style guided notes in secondary science**: a teacher provides a two-column template (cues left, notes right) with blanks for definitions during a direct-instruction lesson, then students write summary questions at the bottom.
- **IRIS Center modules** ([https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu)) — model guided notes and graphic organizers as evidence-based accommodations for students with high-incidence disabilities.
- **Comparison matrices in history lectures**: students record causes, events, and outcomes for multiple conflicts in a pre-built table, supporting later essay planning.
- **Guided notes in inclusive classrooms** — Teachers provide lecture outlines with key-term blanks and cue words ("remember this!"), a format validated across studies of students with disabilities ([Konrad et al. meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0741932509336240)).
- **Cornell-notes hybrids** — A guided-notes left column with instructor-provided questions, right column with learner-completed blanks, and a summary section completed after lecture.
- **Science lecture concept maps** — Instructor projects a partially completed concept map; learners fill in linking words and missing nodes during the explanation.

# Graphic Organizers / Guided Notes (for Processing Speed)

## Description
During lecture, learners are provided with graphic organizers or guided notes to help pull out the important points. These tools structure the information being presented, making it easier to process and retain. Guided notes prompt learners to fill in key information — blanks, prompts, or diagram nodes — promoting active engagement during the lecture rather than verbatim transcription.

## Design Implications

By externalizing the lecture's structure, these tools reduce the working-memory burden of simultaneously listening, deciding what matters, and writing — a triple load that disproportionately penalizes learners with slow processing speed [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Instructor-provided organizers are especially effective for novices, who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content on their own [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]. The fill-in format converts passive listening into generative processing, which improves retention over instructor-complete notes [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — the organizer offloads structure-holding and importance-judging to the artifact, freeing capacity for comprehension
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — organizers present information in pre-chunked units rather than a continuous stream
- [Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure.md) — the organizer makes the lecture's skeleton visible before and during delivery
- [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — distributed before the lecture, the same artifact functions as an advance organizer priming relevant schema

### Personalization
- Customize organizers to content type (timeline for history, matrix for comparisons, cycle diagram for processes) and to learner needs
- Offer multiple response modes: writing, drawing, typing, or selecting from word banks

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content and choose an organizer structure that matches its logic (hierarchy, sequence, comparison).
2. Create the guided-notes version: full headings and key terms, with blanks at the most important points.
3. [Provide guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md) — distribute the organizer before class, briefly model how to use it, and cue fill-in moments during the lecture.
4. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) — have learners use the completed organizer to summarize, answer questions, or study, so the notes become a retrieval tool rather than a product.
5. Review completed organizers to check comprehension and adjust blank density for the next session.
