# SIFT Method

## Description
The SIFT method, developed by Mike Caulfield, is a streamlined approach to evaluating online information: **S**top and check your emotional reaction, **I**nvestigate the source, **F**ind better coverage of the claim, and **T**race claims, quotes, and media to their original context. Unlike traditional checklist-based source evaluation (e.g., CRAAP), SIFT treats the open web itself as the fact-checking tool — learners "read laterally," leaving an unfamiliar page to see what independent sources say about it and about the claim.

## Design Implications

SIFT operationalizes the professional fact-checking practices documented by Wineburg and McGrew, whose studies found that professional fact-checkers outperformed historians and Stanford students precisely because they left the page immediately to read laterally [Lateral reading outperforms vertical reading for evaluating online sources.](../claims/lateral-reading-improves-source-evaluation.md) [+S]. Checklist approaches that keep learners on the page ("vertical reading") are exploited by sophisticated misinformation, which often looks professional and cites plausible-sounding evidence [Checklist-based source evaluation is less effective than lateral reading.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]. SIFT's four moves are deliberately cheap — each should take seconds to a few minutes — so that evaluation becomes a habitual reflex rather than a burdensome task [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Internet access during instruction so learners can practice leaving the page and searching for independent coverage
- Authentic web content for practice, including realistic but flawed sources — sanitized examples do not transfer
- Brief direct instruction in each move, followed by immediate [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on live pages
- A [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of expert lateral reading so learners see the moves in action

#### Constraints
- Fails when learners lack basic search skills; "find better coverage" presumes fluency with query formulation and result scanning
- Less effective if taught as a memorized sequence rather than applied to real pages — transfer requires varied practice with authentic misinformation [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~M]
- The "Stop" move depends on metacognitive awareness that novices often lack; without explicit modeling, learners skip it and react to content emotionally
- Time pressure and strong prior beliefs can override the strategy — motivated reasoning reduces willingness to investigate sources that confirm one's views [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Taught as a full sequence or as individual micro-moves ("just do the S and the I")
- Adapted for younger learners as simplified heuristics ("leave the page and see what others say")
- Embedded in discipline-specific contexts: evaluating journal articles in science, primary sources in history, datasets in statistics
- Delivered via Caulfield's open course materials, or integrated into library information-literacy instruction

### Target Learners
- Secondary and postsecondary students who get most information through social media and search [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of web sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Adults in professional contexts (journalism, health, finance) who must triage sources quickly
- Less necessary for learners already fluent in lateral reading; instruction should fade as evaluation becomes automatic [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Source evaluation: judging the credibility of an unfamiliar source in seconds, not minutes
- Media literacy: recognizing when content is stripped of its original context
- Metacognitive regulation: pausing before sharing or trusting emotionally charged content

### Instructions
1. **Stop.** Pause when encountering an unfamiliar or emotionally provocative claim; notice the reaction before engaging. (Metacognitive [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
2. **Investigate the source.** Open a new tab and search for what known outlets say about the source or author — read laterally, not vertically.
3. **Find better coverage.** Search for the claim itself on fact-checking sites and trusted outlets; the best fact-check of a claim is often independent of the original source.
4. **Trace the claim.** Follow quotes, images, and statistics back to their original context using reverse image search and citation chasing.
5. Follow with distributed [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on fresh, authentic pages so the moves become automatic [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
