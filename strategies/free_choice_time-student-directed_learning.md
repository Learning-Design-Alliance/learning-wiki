# Free Choice Time Student Directed Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Free choice time designates a scheduled block in which learners choose what to work on from a curated (or open) set of activities, materials, or inquiry topics. The instructor's role shifts from directing activity to designing the choice environment, conferring with individuals, and helping learners plan and reflect on their self-directed work. It is carried out through routines such as [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md), interest-based project time, and structured planning–working–reflecting cycles such as [Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md).

## Design Implications

Choice taps learners' needs for autonomy, one of the three basic psychological needs in [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md), and autonomy-supportive contexts reliably increase intrinsic motivation and engagement [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. However, choice only improves learning outcomes when the options are meaningfully distinct, all instructionally viable, and matched to learners' ability to self-regulate; unstructured choice among low-value options consumes time without producing learning [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of options that are all worth doing — choice over *how* or *what among good options*, not whether to engage
- Enough time for learners to reach a productive state in their chosen activity (frequent interruption undermines self-directed work)
- Planning and reflection routines so learners make deliberate choices rather than defaulting to habit ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), [Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md))
- Instructor availability for conferring, observation, and just-in-time [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)
- Clear norms and boundaries so autonomy does not collapse into off-task behavior

#### Constraints
- Novices and learners with weak self-regulation often make poor choices or flounder without structure; pure discovery during choice time underperforms guided alternatives for novices [~S]
- Excessive options create decision overload and time loss; a bounded set (roughly 3–8 viable options) works better than unlimited choice [-M]
- Choice of superficial features (fonts, colors, seat location) does not produce the motivational or learning benefits of choice over content, method, or pace [~M]
- Without accountability structures, some learners consistently avoid challenging work, choosing only comfortable activities [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Bounded choice**: learners pick from a teacher-designed menu ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) — appropriate for novices and younger learners
- **Open choice with conferencing**: learners propose their own projects, negotiated with the teacher — appropriate once self-regulation is established
- **Choice of process, not product**: all learners reach the same goal but choose their route (read vs. watch vs. build)
- **Incremental release**: short choice blocks with heavy scaffolding early in the year, expanding in duration and openness as learners demonstrate self-management

### Target Learners
- Learners with developing self-regulation benefit most when choice is bounded and paired with planning routines [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Disengaged or low-motivation learners, for whom autonomy is often the missing condition for investment [+M]
- Very young learners and novices need narrower option sets and more frequent check-ins; open-ended choice can exceed their self-management capacity [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own work ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))
- Intrinsic motivation and sustained engagement with content
- Independent application and extension of previously taught skills ([Application](../elements/application.md))
- Interest-driven inquiry and knowledge building

### Instructions
1. **Teach the options first.** Ensure every choice has been introduced through [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) or [Explicit Teaching](../patterns/explicit-teaching.md) so learners can actually use what they choose.
2. **Present a bounded menu.** Offer 3–8 viable options via a [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md), varying by content, process, or product.
3. **Have learners plan.** Each learner states or writes what they will do and why before starting ([Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md)).
4. **Confer during work.** Circulate, observe, and coach; use [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) to redirect off-track choices without taking the choice away.
5. **Reflect and share.** Close with brief reflection on what was accomplished and what to choose next time, building self-regulation over successive cycles.

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom's Academic Choice** ([https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)) — a planning–working–reflecting routine in which K–8 students choose among teacher-designed tasks, used widely in US elementary schools.
- **Montessori classrooms** ([https://amshq.org](https://amshq.org)) — extended (2–3 hour) work periods in which children select from prepared materials; the longest-studied implementation of student-directed choice time.
- **Choice boards in blended learning** — teachers post tiered activity menus (e.g., in a HyperDocs workflow) letting students choose their path through practice on a shared objective.
