Use mental practice as an addition to physical practice for procedural skill learning in medical education¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q1–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The review concludes there is some evidence to recommend mental practice — mental rehearsal of a procedure without physically performing it, potentially including imagery techniques — for procedural learning in medical education, especially surgical skills. It states this motor learning principle "should be considered for implementation" to improve learning of procedures. Mental practice is framed as a support of physical practice rather than a replacement.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Mental practice sessions in the included studies were structured, e.g. scripts enumerating procedure steps, relaxation and imagery exercises, or sessions with an expert educator

Constraints¶

  • The review notes two included studies had no active comparator, which might have introduced a bias in favour of mental practice; evidence comes mostly from surgical education and simulated environments

Target Learners¶

  • medical and physiotherapy students and residents learning procedural skills

Target Learning Goals¶

  • acquisition of procedural (motor) skills, especially surgical skills

Affordances¶

  • Post Acquisition Retention Transfer Test Framework

Claims¶

  • Mental practice significantly improves procedural skill performance on post-acquisition tests in medical education (SMD 0.43) [+W]
  • Evidence for transfer and long-term impact of mental practice is sparse but includes positive trends: combined MP plus physical practice improved suturing transfer, and mental rehearsal lowered endovascular error rates [+W]
  • Reviews report that mental practice effectiveness may depend on individual imagery strength, and that MP interventions vary widely in duration, delivery mode and setting [~W]
