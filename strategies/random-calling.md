# Random Calling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Random calling (also called cold calling or random nomination) is a questioning strategy in which the instructor selects respondents at random — by name cards, a spinner, an app, or a shuffled roster — rather than accepting volunteers. It is typically paired with wait time and often with think-pair-share so that learners have an opportunity to prepare an answer before being called on.

## Design Implications

Random calling distributes participation across the whole class and keeps every learner cognitively engaged, since anyone may be asked next [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on a low-threat climate: when paired with adequate thinking time, scaffolding, and a norm that partial answers are acceptable, it raises engagement; when used punitively, it suppresses it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely random selection mechanism (name cards, [random.org](https://www.random.org), apps like ClassDojo or Wheel of Names) so learners cannot predict who will answer
- Sufficient wait time (3–5 seconds minimum) after posing the question before naming a respondent
- Established norms that make answering safe: right to pass initially, acceptance of partial answers, follow-up prompts rather than judgment
- Questions calibrated to learners' current understanding — answerable with the preparation provided

#### Constraints
- High-anxiety use (no preparation time, public correction of wrong answers) increases stress and reduces participation, particularly for learners with social anxiety [-M]
- Random calling without prior think time or pair discussion becomes a spotlight rather than a thinking prompt [~M]
- In large lecture settings without discussion structures, it can devolve into rapid-fire quizzing that benefits only the named learner
- Inequitable if questions consistently require background knowledge some learners lack

#### Implementation Variability
- **Warm calling**: announce the question to all, allow think/pair time, *then* draw a name — the most robust variant
- **Random calling with passes**: learners may pass a limited number of times per term, preserving accountability while reducing threat
- **Group-level random calling**: draw a team rather than an individual, useful early in team-based courses
- **Digital randomization**: tools like Wheel of Names or LMS rosters add perceived fairness and transparency

### Target Learners
- Reluctant or quiet learners who would otherwise disengage during discussion — provided the climate is supportive [~M]
- Whole classes where a small subset of volunteers dominates discourse
- Less suitable, at least initially, for learners with acute communication anxiety; pair or written-response variants are better entry points

### Target Learning Goals
- Formative assessment: surfacing the whole class's understanding rather than the confident few [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Discussion quality and accountability in [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- Attention and engagement maintenance during direct instruction

### Instructions
1. Pose a question to the entire class before selecting anyone; give explicit think time.
2. Optionally have learners discuss with a partner first (think-pair-share), so every learner rehearses an answer.
3. Draw a name using a visible random mechanism, reinforcing that selection is fair and unpredictable.
4. Apply wait time after the learner responds; use follow-up prompts ("What makes you say that?") rather than evaluating immediately.
5. Normalize error: thank the attempt, probe reasoning, and redirect to peers to build on the answer.

## Examples
- **Wheel of Names** ([wheelofnames.com](https://wheelofnames.com)) — a free spinner teachers use to draw names publicly and transparently.
- **EL Education / Responsive Classroom norms** — random calling embedded within established discussion norms so accountability is paired with psychological safety.
- A statistics instructor poses a conceptual question, gives 60 seconds of silent think time, then two minutes of pair discussion, then spins the wheel — every learner has rehearsed an answer before anyone is named.
