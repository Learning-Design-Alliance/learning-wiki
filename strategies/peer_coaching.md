# Peer Coaching

## Description
Peer coaching is a reciprocal helping relationship between equals who support each other's learning through mutual trust and open discussion. Participants alternate between coach and client roles — observing one another's practice, offering feedback, sharing experiences, and holding each other accountable. Unlike hierarchical mentoring, peer coaching distributes expertise: each partner is both a source of support and a learner, which builds reflective practice alongside the target skill.

## Design Implications

Peer coaching works because articulating feedback to a peer forces the coach to organize and verbalize their own understanding, while the client receives task- and process-level feedback from someone close to their own performance level [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The reciprocal structure also builds self-efficacy: seeing a similar peer succeed makes improvement feel attainable [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Effectiveness depends on structured roles and training — unstructured "just talk" pairings rarely produce instructional gains.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trusting environment and explicit norms for open, non-evaluative discussion
- Training in observation and feedback protocols so partners give specific, behaviorally anchored input rather than vague praise
- Scheduled, recurring sessions with rotating coach/client roles
- A shared focus — a lesson, project, or skill — that gives each session a concrete object of analysis

#### Constraints
- Without facilitation or protocol training, feedback drifts toward social nicety and avoids substantive critique [-M]
- Personality conflicts or perceived status differences within a pair suppress honest disclosure and reduce the relationship to compliance theater [-M]
- When tied to formal evaluation or administrative judgment, trust collapses and partners perform rather than reflect [-S]
- Coaches with only marginally more expertise may reinforce each other's misconceptions; pairing across complementary strengths mitigates this [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal dyads** — equal partners alternate roles on a fixed schedule (the classic Joyce & Showers model)
- **Team coaching** — small cross-functional groups meet monthly; each member takes a turn as client while others coach
- **Instructional coaching with peer elements** — a trained coach works alongside peer pairs, common in teacher professional development
- **Peer observation cycles** — pre-observation conference, observation, post-observation debrief, common in higher education faculty development

### Target Learners
- Professionals, teachers, and managers refining complex practice (teaching, leadership, clinical work) where no single expert is available for every learner [+M]
- Students in higher education and professional programs working on projects, presentations, or clinical skills [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]
- Novices benefit most when paired with structured protocols; fully open-ended coaching favors more experienced participants [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reflective practice: analyzing one's own performance with a partner's perspective
- Communication and feedback skills: both giving and receiving process-level critique
- Transfer of training: coaching after initial instruction is what converts workshop learning into classroom or workplace practice [+M]
- Professional identity and persistence: a support system that sustains effort through difficult transitions [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Prepare** — train participants in observation and feedback protocols; establish norms of confidentiality and non-evaluation
2. **Model** — demonstrate a coaching conversation using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) so partners see what substantive, non-evaluative feedback sounds like
3. **Pair and contract** — form dyads or triads around shared goals; agree on focus, schedule, and rotation of roles
4. **Observe and gather data** — the coach observes the client's practice or reviews their work, collecting specific evidence rather than impressions
5. **Debrief** — hold a structured conversation in which the client reflects first, then the coach offers process-level feedback ([Peer Interaction](../elements/peer-interaction.md))
6. **Apply and iterate** — the client sets a concrete adjustment for the next cycle; roles rotate so both partners experience coaching and being coached ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))

## Examples
- **Joyce & Showers teacher coaching** — after training in a new instructional strategy, teacher pairs observe each other's lessons and debrief weekly; coaching is the component that turns strategy knowledge into consistent classroom use.
- **Medical education peer coaching** — residents review recorded patient encounters with peers using structured checklists (e.g., programs built on the Stanford Faculty Development feedback model), improving clinical communication skills.
- **Cross-functional leadership circles** — small groups of managers from different departments meet monthly; each session coaches one member through a live leadership challenge, broadening organizational perspective while building coaching skill.
