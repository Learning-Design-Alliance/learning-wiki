# Amplifying Key Ideas

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Amplifying involves deliberately drawing learner attention to the most important ideas in course materials, discussions, and student work — through signaling (headings, emphasis, verbal cueing), summarizing, providing additional examples, or publicly elevating insightful student contributions. The goal is to guide selective attention so that limited working memory and study time are spent on what matters most.

## Design Implications

Amplification works because learners cannot attend to everything; cues that signal relevance direct attention and, when well designed, improve selection, organization, and integration of content [Signaling cues improve learning by guiding attention to relevant information.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+S]. However, the technique is easily overused: when everything is emphasized, nothing is, and learner-generated highlighting is far less effective than experimenter- or instructor-placed emphasis [Experimenter-placed underlining is more effective than student underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prior analysis identifying the small set of ideas that are essential, so amplification is selective rather than pervasive
- Multiple amplification channels working together: visual signaling in materials, verbal cueing during instruction, and summaries that restate key ideas ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
- Consistency between what is amplified and what is assessed — learners quickly learn to ignore cues that do not predict assessment focus

#### Constraints
- Student self-highlighting during first exposure is unreliable; learners highlight poorly chosen material and gain little [Experimenter-placed underlining is more effective than student underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [-M] — rereading highlighted text is among the least effective study techniques [Highlighting and rereading show low utility relative to other study strategies.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [-S]
- Over-emphasis creates clutter that increases rather than reduces extraneous load [Signaling helps only when cues are sparse and genuinely diagnostic of importance.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [~M]
- Amplifying surface features rather than underlying principles can encourage shallow encoding; cues should point at relationships and structure, not just isolated facts [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **In materials:** headings, bolding, margin notes, and advance organizers that preview the key idea before the detail
- **In instruction:** verbal signposting ("if you remember one thing from today…"), board work that visually isolates the core idea, and strategic repetition at the start and end of a session
- **In student work:** publicly amplifying exemplary student contributions — quoting a student's comment and unpacking why it is insightful — which both reinforces the idea and raises the [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) of the contributor
- **In multimedia:** segmenting videos and adding on-screen text or callouts at key moments [Signaling and segmenting improve learning from instructional video.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09792-9) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novices, who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content on their own and benefit most from external signaling [+S]
- Learners with high working-memory demands in the task, where cues reduce unnecessary search [Chunking and signaling reduce working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less necessary for experts, who already detect structure and may find heavy cueing redundant or distracting [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept discrimination: helping learners identify which ideas are central
- Schema formation: cueing the structural relationships among ideas rather than isolated facts
- Retention of essential content: focusing study effort on high-value material

### Instructions
1. Identify the 3–5 essential ideas for the unit before designing any materials; everything else is candidate for trimming, not amplifying.
2. Build signaling into materials up front — headings, emphasis, and an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that frames the key idea before details appear.
3. During instruction, verbally signpost importance and pause to summarize at transitions ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md)).
4. Amplify student thinking: select one or two student contributions to quote and unpack publicly, modeling why the idea matters ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Align assessment with what was amplified, so cues remain trustworthy signals of what counts.

## Related Strategies
- **Signaling in multimedia** — the same attention-guiding function applied to video and graphics
- **Advance organizer sequencing** — amplification placed *before* content rather than during it

## Examples
- **3Blue1Brown (YouTube)** — uses animation, color, and on-screen annotation to visually isolate the key idea in each explanation; motion and color act as amplifiers aligned with the narration.
- **Instructor practice in case teaching (Harvard Business School method)** — the instructor amplifies a student's comment by writing it on the board and connecting it to the session's central concept, elevating both the idea and the contributor.
- **Annotated course readers** — instructor-provided margin notes and bolded key claims in assigned readings, replacing unreliable student highlighting.
