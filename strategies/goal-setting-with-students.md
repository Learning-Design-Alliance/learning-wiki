# Goal Setting With Students

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Goal setting with students is the practice of jointly establishing specific, measurable, and appropriately challenging learning targets — rather than imposing them — so that students understand what success looks like and can monitor their own progress toward it. It is carried out through structured conversations, written goal records, and periodic [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) in which goals are revisited, revised, and connected to feedback.

## Design Implications

Goal setting is one of the most robustly supported motivational interventions in organizational and educational psychology: specific and challenging goals reliably produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals [Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.126.5.626) [+S]. In educational settings, its power comes from coupling goals to [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): goals give learners a reference standard against which to evaluate progress, which feeds the forethought–performance–reflection cycle [+M]. Involving students in setting the goals matters — participation supports perceived [autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md), which sustains intrinsic motivation and commitment to the goal [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Goals that are specific and proximal ("complete three practice problems with correct unit conversions by Friday") rather than distal and vague ("get better at chemistry")
- A challenge level calibrated to current ability — goals must be difficult enough to motivate but attainable enough to sustain commitment
- A mechanism for monitoring progress and connecting it to [feedback](../elements/assessment.md), so goals function as evaluation standards rather than decoration
- Student voice in the goal-setting conversation, not merely ratification of teacher-set targets

#### Constraints
- Goals imposed without student input can undermine autonomy and reduce intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]
- Overly difficult or unattainable goals lead to abandonment and disengagement, especially for low-efficacy learners [~S]
- Goals focused narrowly on performance outcomes (grades, rankings) can encourage surface strategies and reduce interest compared with learning-oriented goals [~M]
- Public goal sharing can backfire for some learners: announcing goals can create a premature sense of accomplishment [~W]
- Goal setting alone, without progress monitoring or feedback, shows weak effects — the goal must be embedded in a self-regulation cycle [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Individual goal conferences**: brief one-to-one conversations setting a weekly learning target, common in writing conferences and advisory systems
- **Goal-setting journals / learning logs**: students record goals, monitor progress, and reflect at intervals — a core component of self-regulated learning instruction
- **Contract grading / learning contracts**: goals formalized in an agreement between student and instructor
- **Proximal sub-goals within larger projects**: breaking a term project into staged targets with checkpoints
- **Digital dashboards**: platforms like Khan Academy display progress against goals, externalizing the monitoring function

### Target Learners
- Struggling or low-achieving students benefit disproportionately: a structured goal-setting intervention improved GPA and satisfaction for academically struggling college students [Morisano et al. (2010) elite-struggling students goal-setting intervention.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020720) [+S]
- Adolescents and adults with emerging self-regulation skills, who need external structure to scaffold the forethought phase [~M]
- Less effective as a standalone for very young children, who lack the metacognitive capacity to monitor against abstract targets [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: building the habit of forethought, monitoring, and self-evaluation
- Sustained engagement on long-horizon tasks (projects, portfolios, independent reading)
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own progress and gaps
- Not well suited to rapid, discrete skill acquisition where immediate [practice](../elements/application.md) dominates

### Instructions
1. Open with a diagnostic conversation or [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) to establish the student's current performance and what they want to achieve.
2. Co-construct one to three specific, proximal goals; make the success criteria explicit and visible (link to [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) practices, which improve achievement when criteria are transparent [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]).
3. Break distal goals into proximal sub-goals with scheduled checkpoints.
4. Establish a monitoring routine — learning log, dashboard, or conference — where progress is compared against the goal and [feedback](../elements/assessment.md) is actioned.
5. Revise goals based on evidence: raise challenge when goals are met early, recalibrate when they are out of reach.

## Examples
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — learners set mastery goals per skill; the dashboard tracks progress against them, externalizing the monitoring loop.
- **Morisano et al. (2010)** — struggling university students wrote structured goal-setting essays (elaborated goals, plans, contingencies); the intervention raised GPA relative to controls. [doi:10.1037/a0020720](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020720)
- **Expeditionary Learning / EL Education (https://eleducation.org)** — K-12 crews set and track individual and collective learning targets each expedition cycle, with revision at checkpoints.
- **Writing conferences in workshop models** (e.g., Units of Study, https://www.unitsofstudy.com) — teacher and student set one specific writing goal per conference, revisited at the next.
