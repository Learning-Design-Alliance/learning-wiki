# Model Drawing Strategy

## Description
The model drawing strategy teaches learners to solve word problems — particularly fraction and percentage problems — by drawing a schematic diagram (typically bar or part-whole models) that represents the qualitative relations described in the text, then using the diagram to plan and execute the arithmetic. The strategy is taught explicitly: the instructor demonstrates how to translate each sentence into a visual element, and learners practice the full read–draw–solve cycle until it becomes routine. It originates in Singapore's bar-model approach and in schema-based instruction research for students with learning disabilities.

## Design Implications

Model drawing externalizes the problem structure, offloading quantitative relations from working memory onto the diagram so learners can reason about them [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on teaching *schematic* representations (bars showing part–whole and comparison relations) rather than decorative pictorial drawings; schematic representations correlate with solution success while pictorial ones do not [~S]. The strategy must be taught through [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) followed by guided [Practice](../elements/practice.md), with the drawing routine faded as competence grows.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Pencil and paper or whiteboard, plus explicit instruction in the drawing routine itself
- A worked sequence of problems sharing the same underlying schema (part–whole, comparison, change) so learners can abstract the model type
- Instructor [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) that narrates how each sentence maps onto the diagram, followed by [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on the diagram before the arithmetic

#### Constraints
- Learners with weak spatial or fine-motor skills may find drawing itself a burden; pre-printed model frames or digital tools reduce this load [~M]
- Pictorial (literal) drawings — drawing the objects in the story — do not help and can consume working memory without aiding solution [-S]
- If all practice problems share one surface story, learners may bind the model to the story rather than the schema; varying surface features is necessary [~S]
- Requires foundational number sense; learners who cannot yet compute fractions or percentages will produce correct diagrams and wrong answers

#### Implementation Variability
- Pre-printed bar-model templates vs. freehand drawing — templates lower the entry barrier for learners with motor or spatial difficulties
- Digital implementations (e.g., [Thinking Blocks](https://www.mathplayground.com/thinkingblocks.html), [Bar Model apps](https://www.didax.com)) allow drag-and-drop model construction
- Fading sequence: full worked model → partially completed model → learner-drawn model from scratch
- Extendable from fractions/percentages to ratio, rate, and early algebra problems

### Target Learners
- Students with learning disabilities in mathematics, who benefit from explicit, structured representation routines [+M]
- Middle and high school students struggling with multi-step word problems despite adequate computation skills
- Less beneficial for learners who already spontaneously construct schematic representations; for them the routine can be redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Word-problem solving: translating text into a solvable representation
- Conceptual understanding of part–whole and comparison relations underlying fractions and percentages
- Transfer of a general representation routine across problem types [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Read the problem aloud and identify what is known, unknown, and the unit of the whole [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)
2. Draw a bar representing the whole; divide or extend bars to represent the stated relations (parts, comparisons, percentages)
3. Label each segment with quantities and unknowns, using a question mark for the unknown
4. Use the diagram to plan the computation, then solve and check the answer against the model
5. Repeat across a sequence of problems with the same schema, fading support from worked models to independent drawing [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on the diagram before the arithmetic

## Examples
- **Singapore Math bar models** — the national curriculum's model method, in which students draw unit bars to solve fraction, ratio, and percentage problems from primary grades onward ([Singapore Math](https://www.singaporemath.com))
- **Thinking Blocks** ([Math Playground](https://www.mathplayground.com/thinkingblocks.html)) — interactive tool where learners drag blocks to build part–whole and comparison models for addition, multiplication, fraction, and ratio problems
- **Xin's PGBM-COMPS intervention** — research-based program teaching students with learning disabilities to represent word problems with diagrams before formulating equations
