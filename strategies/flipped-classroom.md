# Flipped Classroom

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The flipped classroom moves direct instruction outside of class — typically as short videos or structured readings — and reserves synchronous time for application: problem-solving, discussion, and feedback with the instructor present. The instructor's role shifts from delivering content to diagnosing and correcting understanding during [Practice](../elements/practice.md).

## Design Implications

The model aligns with [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md): class time spent applying content produces better exam performance than time spent listening to lectures [Active learning improves exam performance over lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Pre-class materials also allow learners to control pacing — pausing and rewinding — which reduces the transience problem of live explanation [Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory is exceeded.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. The flip only pays off if class time is genuinely interactive; a flipped lecture that merely relocates passive video-watching gains little [Active learning improves exam performance over lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Short, focused pre-class materials (ideally under ~10 minutes per video, segmented by topic)
- An accountability mechanism — embedded questions, pre-class quizzes, or entry tickets — so learners arrive prepared
- Redesigned in-class activities centered on application, not re-explanation ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), problem sets, [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
- A plan for learners who did not prepare, so class does not collapse back into lecturing

#### Constraints
- Weak or absent accountability for pre-work is the most common failure mode; without it, students arrive unprepared and instructors re-teach, erasing the flip [Active learning improves exam performance over lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [-M]
- Increases upfront production effort and requires student access to reliable technology and connectivity
- Less effective when in-class time remains instructor-led; the benefit comes from the active use of face-to-face time, not the videos themselves
- Novices may struggle with unguided pre-class materials if the videos are not well-structured [Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory is exceeded.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Partial flip**: only some units flipped; useful for piloting and for courses with heterogeneous topic difficulty
- **Flipped mastery**: learners progress through units at their own pace, advancing only after demonstrating competence
- **Readings instead of videos**: text-based first exposure works when video production is impractical; pair with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)
- **Peer-instruction style**: pre-class exposure followed by in-class individual-then-peer voting on conceptual questions (Mazur's model)

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and professional students in content-heavy courses where class time is currently consumed by lecturing [Active learning improves exam performance over lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Learners who benefit from self-paced first exposure — those who need to rewind, or who want to preview at their own speed
- Less suited to learners with weak self-regulation unless strong accountability structures are in place; the model shifts more responsibility onto the learner [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural and problem-solving skill: class time is spent doing what was demonstrated
- Conceptual understanding and application, rather than simple recall of transmitted content
- Preparation for collaborative, discussion-based, or case-based work

### Instructions
1. **Select first-exposure content.** Identify what is genuinely transmission (definitions, demonstrations, procedures) and record or curate it as short segments; apply [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) to keep each segment focused.
2. **Add accountability.** Attach 2–3 low-stakes questions to each pre-class asset ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)); use results to plan class time.
3. **Open class with a check.** Begin with a brief quiz or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) to surface misconceptions from the pre-work.
4. **Run application activities.** Spend the bulk of class on problems, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or cases with the instructor circulating and coaching — enacting [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md).
5. **Close with consolidation.** End with a synthesis or exit task; schedule follow-up [Practice](../elements/practice.md) distributed over subsequent sessions [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S].

## Examples
- **[Flipped Learning](../patterns/flipped-learning.md)** — the general pattern this strategy instantiates; see also [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md).
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — video first-exposure content widely used as the at-home layer in flipped K-12 math classrooms, with built-in practice and teacher dashboards.
- **Eric Mazur's Peer Instruction at Harvard** — introductory physics students read before class and spend class time answering conceptual questions with peer discussion; the model that popularized the flip.
- **[ACUE / flipped STEM courses](https://www.stemflipped.net)** — the STEM Flipped Learning Consortium documents discipline-specific implementations across engineering and science courses.
