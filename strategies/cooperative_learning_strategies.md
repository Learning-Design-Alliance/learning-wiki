# Cooperative Learning Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Cooperative learning strategies are structured group activities in which students work toward shared learning goals under conditions of positive interdependence — each member's success depends on the group's success — combined with individual accountability. Common formats include jigsaw, think-pair-share, round robin, gallery walks, and simultaneous round table. Unlike unstructured group work, cooperative learning assigns roles, specifies interaction protocols, and requires every member to demonstrate individual mastery.

## Design Implications

Cooperative learning reliably improves achievement when it enforces both positive interdependence and individual accountability; groups without these structures show much weaker or null effects [~S]. The mechanism is partly elaborative: explaining to peers forces verbalization and organization of knowledge [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M], and the social interaction itself raises engagement and task value [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit group goals and success criteria so interdependence is real, not nominal
- Assigned or taught roles (facilitator, recorder, checker) to distribute participation
- An individual accountability mechanism — individual quizzes, random reporter selection, or individually graded components
- Sufficient time and [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) so groups know exactly what to produce

#### Constraints
- Without individual accountability, free-riding and social loafing reduce learning for capable members [-S] — achievement gains largely disappear in loosely structured "group work"
- Groups formed with large ability gaps can disadvantage low-status members unless roles rotate and norms are taught [~M]
- High working-memory demands of coordinating social interaction can overwhelm novices if the task itself is also novel; simplify either the task or the interaction structure [~M]
- Poorly designed tasks with one obvious answer allow one student to do all the work while others watch [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jigsaw**: each member masters a segment and teaches it to the group; maximizes interdependence
- **Think-pair-share**: low-overhead pairing before whole-class discussion; usable in large lectures
- **Gallery walk / round robin**: movement-based variants suited to idea generation and critique
- **Formal vs. informal**: multi-week team projects vs. minutes-long in-class structures

### Target Learners
- Learners across K-12 and higher education; effects on achievement are positive but modest on average, with larger effects for collaboration skills and social outcomes [+M]
- Students with low academic self-concept benefit from structured peer support that raises persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+W]
- High-achieving students gain from explaining, but only when tasks require elaboration rather than recall [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through peer explanation and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)
- Collaboration, communication, and perspective-taking skills
- Increased engagement and participation in [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) contexts [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Set a group goal that cannot be completed by one person alone, establishing positive interdependence.
2. Assign roles or use [Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) to distribute responsibility.
3. Structure the interaction with a protocol (round robin, pair discussion) so every member contributes.
4. Build in [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) with prompts that require explanation, not just answer comparison.
5. Hold individuals accountable via random reporting, individual write-ups, or quizzes.
6. Debrief with [Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md) and process reflection on how the group worked.

## Examples
- **Kagan Structures** ([https://www.kaganonline.com](https://www.kaganonline.com)) — a published set of cooperative structures (Round Robin, Simultaneous Round Table, Numbered Heads Together) with built-in role and participation rules.
- **Jigsaw Classroom** ([https://www.jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)) — Aronson's original interdependence design, in which each student masters one segment and teaches it to teammates.
- **Peer Instruction** (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — think-pair-share built around conceptual clicker questions, forcing students to convince peers during discussion.
