# EQ Feeling Chart

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q3`–`q4` · 1 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
The EQ Feeling Chart is a structured [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) activity in which students identify and name their current emotional state using a visual chart of feeling words, then discuss it with a partner using facilitation questions ("What is the feeling? Where does it come from?"). Pairs then share observations with the whole class. The activity operationalizes emotional granularity — the ability to label emotions with precision — which is the foundation of most social-emotional learning curricula.

## Design Implications

Naming an emotion (affect labeling) reliably reduces amygdala reactivity and self-reported distress, creating the regulatory space students need to engage with learning [Lieberman et al. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]. Structured peer sharing also builds empathy and emotional vocabulary, both core outcomes of SEL programs such as RULER [Brackett et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2011.12.001) [+M]. The chart's value depends on the discussion prompts: without questions that probe the *origin* of the feeling, the activity stays at surface labeling and does not build self-monitoring.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible feeling chart with a graded vocabulary of emotion words (beyond "good/bad"), e.g., the Yale RULER Mood Meter or Six Seconds' EQ materials
- Time for pair discussion (5–10 minutes) and brief whole-class sharing
- Facilitation questions that move from identification ("What are you feeling?") to attribution ("What caused it?") to agency ("What might you do with it?")
- A classroom climate where disclosure is voluntary and low-stakes
- An EQ Feeling Chart (visual emotion vocabulary display) visible to all students
- 5–10 minutes for paired discussion plus a brief whole-class debrief
- A classroom climate of psychological safety; voluntary sharing norms established in advance
- Teacher modeling of the routine the first several times, including the teacher's own check-in
- An EQ Feeling Chart (or equivalent emotion-vocabulary display) visible to all learners
- Discussion prompts that push beyond identification to origins ("What happened that made you feel this way?")
- Protected time for both partner exchange and whole-class synthesis
- Norms of psychological safety; sharing must be voluntary, never coerced

#### Constraints
- Students may be hesitant to disclose feelings publicly, especially adolescents in front of peers [-M] — sharing must be opt-in ("share what your partner said" or "share a feeling, not the story")
- Students with limited emotional vocabulary or alexithymia-like difficulties may not be able to identify or articulate feelings at all [~M] — the chart scaffolds this, but some students need one-on-one support first
- Routine use without variation can produce performative, ritualized responses rather than genuine reflection [-W]
- In classrooms without established psychological safety, forced sharing can increase anxiety rather than reduce it [-M]
- Students hesitant to disclose emotions will not benefit from the discussion component; forced sharing can increase social threat rather than reduce it [-M] — participation in the pair discussion must remain voluntary in content even if the check-in itself is routine
- One-off use produces little benefit; the labeling skill develops only through repeated, distributed practice [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- In groups with low trust or recent conflict, whole-class sharing can expose students to ridicule; the debrief should focus on "what did you notice" rather than "who felt what"
- Students with limited emotion vocabulary may point at faces without internalizing labels; the chart must be paired with explicit vocabulary teaching, not used as a substitute for it
- If the routine becomes perfunctory (same answer every day), the labeling practice loses its regulatory effect; teachers should vary prompts and push for granular labels [~W]
- Learners with limited emotion vocabulary or alexithymia may find the chart overwhelming rather than supportive; a smaller starter set of feelings reduces load [~M]
- Compulsory public sharing of feelings can produce performative or defensive responses and damage trust [-M] — keep whole-class sharing at the level of observations, not disclosures
- Cultural display rules vary: in some contexts naming negative emotions publicly is discouraged; the chart must be adapted rather than assumed universal [~W]
- Without follow-up, the activity remains a one-off vocabulary exercise; benefits depend on repeated use embedded in routines such as [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Silent entry: students point to or mark their feeling on the chart rather than speaking, useful for early trust-building
- Private variant: students journal from the chart before optional sharing
- Student-authored charts: older students build their own feeling vocabulary charts, deepening word knowledge
- Digital check-ins (e.g., a shared slide or form) for large classes or remote settings
- **Silent entry variant:** students mark their feeling on a posted chart or personal slip on arrival — lower social cost, suitable for adolescents
- **Mood-meter variant:** a two-axis (energy × pleasantness) grid such as Yale's RULER Mood Meter supports more differentiated labeling than a face chart
- **Writing variant:** students journal the label, source, and one coping strategy — adds a self-monitoring record
- **Escalation variant:** the same chart is reused during conflict moments as a de-escalation tool, not just at class start
- **Silent entry point:** learners point to or mark the chart privately before any discussion, lowering the barrier for reluctant sharers
- **Fictional framing:** learners identify a character's feelings from a story or case before their own, using [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) as a safe proxy
- **Intensity scaling:** learners rate the selected feeling on a 1–5 scale, connecting the chart to self-monitoring of escalation
- **Age adaptation:** younger learners use faces and colors; older learners use nuanced vocabulary (e.g., distinguishing frustration from disappointment from resentment)

### Target Learners
- K–12 students broadly; especially effective in upper elementary and middle school where emotional vocabulary is still developing [+M]
- Students who benefit from external structure for self-monitoring [Naming emotions supports self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a whole-class disclosure activity for students with trauma histories; provide private alternatives
- All ages, adaptable from pre-K through college; younger learners need face-based charts, older learners benefit from granular vocabulary
- Students with weak interoceptive awareness or alexithymia-like presentation, for whom the external chart supplies labels they cannot generate [Affect labeling reduces distress even when the label is supplied externally.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]
- All ages, but especially children and adolescents still building emotion vocabulary
- Learners with weak interoceptive awareness or those who default to undifferentiated labels ("fine," "bad") [+W]
- Groups with recurring interpersonal friction, where shared vocabulary enables de-escalation
- Less immediately useful for learners already fluent in emotion language, for whom the chart may feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional identification and granularity: precise labeling of internal states
- Self-regulation: creating a pause between feeling and response [Naming emotions supports self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Empathy and perspective-taking: hearing peers' emotional experiences [Building empathy through structured perspective exchange.](../principles/building-empathy.md) [+W]
- Classroom community: normalizing emotion talk as part of academic life
- Emotional literacy: building differentiated emotion vocabulary
- Self-awareness and self-regulation: recognizing and naming internal states before they drive behavior
- Relationship skills: practicing empathic listening and normalizing emotional conversation
- Classroom climate: establishing that emotions are discussable, which supports engagement and task value [+M]
- Emotional literacy: identifying and precisely naming internal states
- Self-awareness and [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): recognizing feelings as data that influence attention and behavior [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Empathy and perspective-taking: understanding that others experience and label feelings differently
- Communication: articulating causes and sources of emotional states to a listener

### Instructions
1. Display the EQ Feeling Chart and briefly model identifying your own feeling and its source.
2. Pair students; each partner identifies their feeling on the chart ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md)).
3. Partners ask the facilitation questions in turn, practicing [Active Listening](active-listening.md) — listening to understand, not to respond.
4. Pairs share one observation with the whole class ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), keeping disclosures voluntary.
5. Close by connecting the feeling to the work ahead: "Given how you're arriving today, what do you need to do your best work?"

## Description
Learners use the EQ Feeling Chart — a visual display of emotion vocabulary organized by intensity and valence — to identify and name their current feelings. In pairs, they discuss what they feel and where the feeling comes from, then share observations with the whole class. The strategy converts diffuse internal states into precise, communicable language, building emotional literacy through structured [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) and [Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md).

## Design Implications

Partner discussion adds a social-comparison layer — hearing how a peer labels the same situation broadens learners' own emotional vocabulary and builds [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md).
