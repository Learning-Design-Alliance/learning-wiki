# Assessing Needs and Wants with Likert Scales

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy uses short Likert-scale surveys (typically five-point, from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree") to gather quantitative data on what learners need — prior knowledge, confidence, perceived relevance — and what they want — interests, preferences, motivations. Responses are aggregated to inform instructional decisions such as topic sequencing, pacing, grouping, and emphasis, and are typically collected at the start of a unit, at [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) during it, and at closure.

## Design Implications

Likert scales convert subjective learner states into comparable data, making them a lightweight form of [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) that feeds instructional adjustment — the core move of [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [+M]. Their value depends entirely on what happens next: data that informs teaching improves learning, while data collected and ignored does not [Formative assessment requires acting on the information gathered.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Item wording matters as much as the scale itself — ambiguous or double-barreled items produce noise that looks like data.

### Context
#### Requirements
- 3–8 focused, single-idea items ("I can explain X" not "I understand and enjoy X")
- A consistent, labeled response scale; unlabeled midpoint categories invite inconsistent interpretation [Krosnick's survey-methodology work shows response-scale design systematically biases answers]
- A planned decision the data will drive — grouping, reteaching, topic choice, pacing
- Anonymity or low-stakes framing where honesty about confusion is the goal

#### Constraints
- Self-report measures perceived, not actual, understanding; learners with low skill often overestimate it, so scale data should be triangulated with performance evidence [-M]
- Acquiescence and central-tendency biases inflate mid-scale responses, especially with young learners or low-stakes contexts [~M]
- Ordinal responses are not interval data; computing means and treating a 4 as "twice" a 2 misrepresents the measurement [Jamieson (2004) documents common statistical misuse of Likert data] [-W]
- Repeated identical surveys produce response fatigue and drifting answers rather than genuine change data [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-unit needs assessment:** items on prior knowledge and confidence to calibrate starting points
- **Interest/wants inventory:** items on topic preferences and relevance to support choice and [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of motivation
- **Mid-unit pulse check:** 2–3 items as a quick [check-in](../principles/check-ins.md) to catch confusion early
- **Exit self-rating:** confidence ratings that learners compare against actual performance to build accurate self-assessment

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult learners, who interpret scale items more consistently than young children [~M]
- Learners building self-assessment accuracy — comparing their confidence ratings to results exposes miscalibration and supports [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+W]
- Reluctant participants, for whom anonymous scales surface needs that [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) will not

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive monitoring: calibrating confidence against performance
- Affective and motivational diagnosis: identifying interest and perceived relevance to inform engagement design
- Instructional planning: locating the group's zone of need before teaching

### Instructions
1. Define the decision the data will inform (regroup, reteach, choose examples, adjust pace).
2. Write 3–8 single-idea statements with a labeled five-point scale; avoid double-barreled and negatively worded items.
3. Administer anonymously before or during the unit; keep completion under three minutes.
4. Aggregate responses and identify the distribution, not just the average — a bimodal split calls for differentiated grouping, not a middle path.
5. Act on the data: adjust instruction, then tell learners what changed because of their responses, closing the [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) loop.
6. Re-administer a parallel form at closure and have learners compare confidence ratings with actual performance to build calibration.

## Related Strategies
- Entry and exit tickets — the performance-based complement to self-report scales
- Interest surveys and learner inventories — the "wants" side in more open-ended form
- Confidence ratings before answer submission — item-level rather than unit-level calibration

## Examples
- **Pre-unit needs check in a science class:** "I can explain the difference between weather and climate" (1–5), used to decide whether to open with the review lesson or skip it.
- **[Plickers](https://www.plickers.com)** and **[Mentimeter](https://www.mentimeter.com)** — tools for collecting anonymous scale responses in class and displaying the aggregate distribution instantly.
- **Course-design feedback in higher education:** mid-semester Likert pulse surveys (e.g., via institutional course-evaluation platforms) that instructors act on within the term rather than after it.
