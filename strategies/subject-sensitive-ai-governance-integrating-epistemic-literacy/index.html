Transition from restrictive AI governance to frameworks that formally integrate operational and epistemic workflow competencies with subject-sensitive guidance¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 mixed) · 2 studies (1 associational, 1 qualitative), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends moving beyond restrictive governance toward "a framework that formally integrates overlapping operational and epistemic workflow competencies, emphasizing the need for subject-sensitive guidance that ensures AI enhances rather than displaces disciplinary rigor". Because perceived restrictions co-occur with poor rule clarity and clandestine use, the strategy calls for clear, discipline-specific guidelines that foster productive scaffold use rather than blanket prohibition, and for cultivating epistemic literacy so responsible integration depends less on access than on critical evaluation skills.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Formal, documented guidelines for acceptable AI use per subject, since guidance currently often relies on implicit norms of individual instructors

Constraints¶

  • Cross-sectional associations cannot establish that governance shapes behavior; reverse causality, where clandestine use shapes perceptions of ambiguity, is equally plausible per the article

Target Learners¶

  • secondary students in STEM subjects
  • teachers in business-oriented secondary schools

Target Learning Goals¶

  • responsible and epistemically vigilant AI use
  • disciplinary rigor in STEM problem solving

Affordances¶

  • Dual Ai Literacy Interdependent Workflow Phases

Claims¶

  • In joint analysis, Practice codes correlate most strongly with AI pedagogical orientation, while Epistemic codes show a weaker positive association [~W]
  • Practitioner governance activity in Australian Group of Eight institutions outpaces what the published GenAI literature has documented, while Indonesian participants report less formal scaffolding [~W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Develop AI literacy frameworks that build metacognitive awareness distinguishing system-assisted performance from independent competence
  • Institutions should respond to generative AI in education with guided critical engagement rather than prohibition
  • Institutional governance should establish practical conditions supporting AI literacy, learner agency, and critical evaluation rather than relying on prohibition and detection
  • Move beyond generic AI policies to method-based ethical policies and process-oriented assessment
  • Ground AI integration in higher education on core principles of human agency, academic freedom, transparency, ethics, inclusivity, and critical thinking
  • Higher education leaders should develop more inclusive and future-oriented GenAI policies integrating social equity, interdisciplinary experimentation, and sustainability considerations
  • Foster communities of practice for AI governance instead of rigid top-down policies
  • Teach the pedagogical rationale behind AI policies rather than only stating rules
  • Move beyond permit-or-restrict GenAI policies toward policies that clarify appropriate use, support equitable access, and extend rather than replace independent reasoning
  • Provide explicit pedagogical guidance on effective and ethical GenAI use within specific assessment contexts, beyond prohibitions
  • Build educator AI literacy and pedagogical competence alongside student critical engagement with AI-generated content
