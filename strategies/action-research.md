# Action Research

## Description
Action research is a cyclical inquiry process in which practitioners (usually teachers) systematically study their own practice: they identify a problem, plan and implement an intervention, collect evidence of its effects, and reflect on the results before planning the next cycle. The classic cycle is plan → act → observe → reflect, repeated iteratively [Lewin, 1946]. Unlike external evaluation, the practitioner is both the researcher and the beneficiary of the findings, and the knowledge produced is situated in a specific context rather than intended as generalizable causal claims.

## Design Implications

Action research operationalizes formative evaluation at the level of one's own teaching: evidence collection and reflection convert everyday impressions into systematic data, supporting instructional adjustment [feedback is most effective when it targets processes rather than outcomes](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. Its iterative structure mirrors self-regulated learning applied to teaching — goal setting, monitoring, and strategic revision — and teachers who engage in it model [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) practices for their students [self-monitoring improves self-regulation](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Because findings are context-bound, action research is best treated as professional learning and local improvement, not as a substitute for experimental evidence about what works generally.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A focused, answerable question about one's own practice (e.g., "Does structured peer feedback improve draft quality in my writing block?")
- Baseline evidence before the intervention — student work, observation notes, [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) data — so change can be judged
- Multiple data sources (triangulation): artifacts, observations, student voice
- Time and institutional support for cycles that typically span weeks, not single lessons
- A reflective record: journals, [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) with colleagues, or structured [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of what changed and why

#### Constraints
- Researcher bias is pervasive: teachers invested in an intervention tend to see improvement; triangulation and a critical colleague reduce but do not eliminate this [-M]
- Small, uncontrolled samples mean results cannot be generalized; treating a local success as proof of a general method overreaches [-S]
- Cycles demand sustained time; without protected time, action research degrades into anecdotal reflection [-M]
- Ethical obligations apply — collecting student data requires consent and care, especially when findings may be published [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Individual teacher inquiry** — one practitioner studies their own classroom; most common and lowest cost
- **Collaborative/participatory action research** — teams or whole schools inquire together, often within [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md); increases perspective-taking and data richness
- **Lesson study** (Japan-originated) — a structured variant in which a team co-plans, one teaches while others observe, and the group revises the lesson
- **Critical action research** — explicitly targets equity outcomes, examining who is underserved by current practice

### Target Learners
- In-service and pre-service teachers developing reflective, evidence-based practice [+M]
- Teacher candidates in practicum, where a small inquiry cycle builds habits of data use and professional [Self-Efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+W]
- Graduate students in education programs, where it serves as a capstone methodology
- Students can participate as co-researchers (youth participatory action research), which supports [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) and agency [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Professional skill development: diagnostic, data-literacy, and reflective practice
- Metacognition about teaching: making one's instructional assumptions explicit and testable
- Local improvement of specific outcomes (engagement, discourse quality, achievement gaps)

### Instructions
1. **Identify a puzzle** — a recurring problem or question in your own classroom, framed as an inquiry question rather than a complaint.
2. **Gather baseline evidence** — collect student work, [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) results, or observation notes before changing anything.
3. **Plan an intervention** — design a concrete change, ideally drawing on an established principle (e.g., [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)) so the cycle also tests theory in context.
4. **Act and observe** — implement the change over a defined period, documenting what happens through multiple data sources.
5. **Reflect and analyze** — compare evidence to baseline; involve a colleague or [Collaborative Inquiry](../patterns/collaborative-inquiry.md) group to check interpretations against bias.
6. **Revise and re-cycle** — adjust the intervention based on findings and begin the next cycle; share results with a professional community.

## Related Strategies
- [Lesson Study](../strategies/lesson-study.md) — a highly structured, team-based variant of the same cycle
- [Reflective Practice](../strategies/reflective-practice.md) — the reflective habit action research formalizes with data
- [Formative Assessment Loops](../strategies/formative-assessment-loops.md) — the same plan–act–observe logic applied to student learning rather than teaching

## Examples
- **Lesson Study in Japanese elementary mathematics** (e.g., through [Mills College Lesson Study Group](https://www.lessonresearch.net)) — teams cycle through co-planning, observation, and revision of a single research lesson.
- **Teacher inquiry in the National Writing Project** ([nwp.org](https://www.nwp.org)) — teachers study their own writing instruction, collect student writing as evidence, and publish practitioner findings.
- **Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR)** programs (e.g., UCLA's [Council of Youth Research](https://idea.gseis.ucla.edu)) — students research problems in their own schools and present findings to policymakers.
