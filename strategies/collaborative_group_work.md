# Collaborative Group Work

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Collaborative group work organizes learners into small groups that pursue a shared task requiring interdependent contribution — discussing, explaining, negotiating meaning, and producing a joint product. It is carried out through deliberately composed groups, structured roles or protocols, and tasks that cannot be completed by one person working alone. For multilingual learners in particular, it creates authentic opportunities to use academic vocabulary and language structures in low-stakes peer talk.

## Design Implications

Collaboration improves achievement when it is genuinely cooperative — positive interdependence plus individual accountability — rather than merely seating students together [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. The task design matters more than the grouping: discussion and explanation among peers drive learning only when the task elicits elaborated talk, not answer-copying [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Teachers must actively structure and monitor the dialogue; unstructured group work frequently produces unequal participation and off-task interaction [The teacher's role in promoting collaborative dialogue is critical to its effectiveness.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task with genuine joint purpose — one product, shared information, or divided expertise — so interdependence is structural, not rhetorical
- Individual accountability mechanisms (numbered heads, individual write-ups, random reporter selection) so no member can free-ride
- Group norms and roles (facilitator, recorder, skeptic) taught explicitly before the first task
- Teacher monitoring with planned intervention points, using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines

#### Constraints
- Groups without individual accountability allow social loafing and unequal learning [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Poorly structured collaboration can overload working memory for novices, who must coordinate both the content and the social demands of the task [Guidance should be reduced only as expertise develops; minimal guidance disadvantages novices.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Heterogeneous grouping without language scaffolds can silence ELL students rather than support them; status differences suppress participation of lower-status members
- Tasks that are easily divisible (or easily done solo) collapse into parallel individual work [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Think-pair-share / peer instruction**: brief low-structure collaboration embedded in whole-class instruction
- **Jigsaw**: each member masters a piece and teaches it to others — maximal interdependence
- **Project teams**: longer-duration groups producing a shared artifact, common in project-based learning
- **Language-focused pairing**: strategically pairing ELLs with supportive peers and providing sentence frames and structured protocols for academic talk

### Target Learners
- Multilingual/ELL students, who gain authentic oral practice with academic vocabulary and language functions in a lower-stakes setting than whole-class talk [+W]
- Novices benefit when group work is tightly structured with clear roles; open-ended collaboration suits them less [Guidance should be reduced only as expertise develops; minimal guidance disadvantages novices.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Learners with strong prior knowledge benefit from explaining to peers, which consolidates their own understanding [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through verbalization, explanation, and peer critique
- Academic language development: using target vocabulary and discourse structures in authentic communication
- Collaboration and communication skills as outcomes in their own right
- Problem-solving on tasks too complex or multifaceted for individuals

### Instructions
1. Define the shared goal and the joint product; design the task so it requires every member's contribution.
2. Compose groups deliberately — typically heterogeneous, 3–4 members — and assign roles or use structured protocols.
3. Teach collaboration norms and academic language supports (sentence frames, word banks) before starting.
4. Launch the task with clear success criteria; circulate to monitor participation and prompt deeper explanation rather than giving answers.
5. Build in individual accountability: random reporters, individual exit writes, or separately graded components.
6. Debrief both the content and the collaboration itself, so group process improves over time.

## Examples
- **Jigsaw classroom** (Aronson) — students become "experts" in one segment and teach it to their home group; the original design specifically used interdependence to reduce intergroup bias ([jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)).
- **PBLWorks (Buck Institute) project teams** — K–12 projects with defined team roles, norms, and shared rubrics for group products ([pblworks.org](https://www.pblworks.org)).
- **Peer Instruction in physics (Mazur, Harvard)** — students answer individually, discuss with neighbors, then revote; widely replicated in large STEM courses.
