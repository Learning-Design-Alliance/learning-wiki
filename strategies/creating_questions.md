# Creating Questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Creating Questions is a strategy in which learners generate questions about content they are listening to. The instructor pauses at intervals during a speech, documentary, podcast, or read-aloud, and learners write one or two questions about what they just heard. This shifts learners from passively receiving information to actively monitoring their own comprehension — listening for points of confusion, gaps, or curiosity. Because learners are typically trained to answer questions rather than pose them, brief modeling of effective question formulation is essential.

## Design Implications

Self-questioning is one of the most consistently supported comprehension strategies: teaching learners to generate questions about material improves comprehension and retention across age groups and domains [Teaching students to generate questions improves comprehension.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. The mechanism is twofold: question generation forces active processing of the material, and the resulting questions serve as metacognitive signals that reveal what was not understood. Pausing during listening also functions as a form of [Activation](../elements/activation.md), prompting learners to connect new input to what they already know [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Structured pause points during the listening activity, spaced closely enough to prevent overload but not so frequent that the flow of the material is destroyed ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the input)
- Modeling of question formulation — the instructor should think aloud while writing their own questions first
- A follow-on use for the questions (peer discussion, instructor selection, or self-directed re-listening) so question generation does not feel like a dead-end task

#### Constraints
- Learners accustomed to answer-seeking may initially produce shallow or off-target questions; quality improves only with modeling and practice [~M]
- Pausing too frequently fragments the material and degrades comprehension of extended discourse [~M]
- Without a follow-on activity, generated questions are often discarded and the metacognitive benefit is lost [-M]
- Learners with very limited prior knowledge may not know enough to ask substantive questions; front-loading background knowledge helps [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Question stems:** provide sentence starters ("Why did…?", "What would happen if…?", "How does this connect to…?") for learners who struggle with formulation
- **Question types:** target different cognitive levels — clarifying questions (confusion), predictive questions (what comes next), inferential or "why" questions (deeper processing)
- **Social formats:** individual writing, pair generation, or whole-group question walls; [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) can follow to address the best questions
- **Asynchronous adaptation:** in video-based courses, embed pause prompts at fixed timestamps and collect questions in a shared document or discussion thread

### Target Learners
- Effective across all levels, but especially valuable for intermediate learners who have enough background to notice gaps but not enough to resolve them independently [~M]
- Struggling comprehenders benefit from explicit question stems; unstructured question generation can overload them [~M]
- Less immediately productive for complete novices, who lack the knowledge base to detect what they do not understand

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension monitoring: recognizing gaps and confusion in real time
- Active listening: engaging with extended spoken input rather than transcribing or drifting
- Metacognitive development: building the habit of self-assessment central to [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Question-asking as a disciplinary skill (inquiry, journalism, research interviews)

### Instructions
1. **Model first:** before the listening activity, the instructor plays a short excerpt and thinks aloud while writing their own questions, showing how to notice confusion and formulate it.
2. **Set the pause structure:** tell learners the material will stop at intervals; each stop, they write one or two questions ([Individual Response](../elements/individual-response.md)).
3. **Listen and generate:** play the material, pausing at planned points. Early pauses should come sooner; spacing can lengthen as learners warm up.
4. **Share and select:** learners compare questions in pairs, then a few are surfaced to the whole group ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)); the instructor answers or flags questions for re-listening.
5. **Close the loop:** revisit the best questions after the listening ends, using them to structure review or a follow-up [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md).

## Examples
- **Listening comprehension in language classes:** the instructor plays a podcast segment, pausing every 2–3 minutes; learners write questions in the target language, which doubles as output practice.
- **Documentary viewing in secondary science:** students generate "why" and "what if" questions at pause points; the teacher selects several to open the next lesson, enacting [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by using learner questions to diagnose understanding.
- **Question Formulation Technique (Right Question Institute):** a structured protocol in which learners produce, improve, and prioritize their own questions about a prompt (https://rightquestion.org).
