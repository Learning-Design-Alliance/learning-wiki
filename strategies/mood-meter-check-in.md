# Mood Meter Check In

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Mood Meter Check In is a routine in which learners plot how they are feeling on a color-coded grid organized by pleasantness (valence) and energy (arousal), then optionally share and briefly discuss. Developed as part of the [RULER](https://ei.yale.edu/ruler) approach at the Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence, the routine is typically used at the start of a class session or day, taking 2–5 minutes. It serves both an instructional function — priming self-regulation and readiness to learn — and a formative one, giving the teacher a snapshot of the class's emotional climate.

## Design Implications

Affective states influence attention, working memory, and willingness to engage, so surfacing them at the outset lets both learners and instructors adjust before instruction begins [~M]. Naming emotions with precise vocabulary (emotional granularity) supports regulation better than undifferentiated "good/bad" reports [+W]. The routine also functions as a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) that builds relational trust and belonging when responses are acknowledged rather than merely collected [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible Mood Meter display (poster, slide, or digital tool such as the RULER app) with the four quadrants: red (high energy, unpleasant), blue (low energy, unpleasant), green (low energy, pleasant), yellow (high energy, pleasant)
- A consistent, low-stakes routine — same time, same format — so participation becomes automatic
- An agreed norm of privacy: learners may pass or share only what they choose
- A teacher response plan for what to do with the information (acknowledge, adjust pacing, follow up privately)

#### Constraints
- Repeated mandatory public sharing can feel surveilling and depress honest reporting [~M] — anonymity or opt-out options preserve validity
- If the data is collected but never acted on, learners disengage from the routine within weeks [~W]
- Poorly facilitated discussion of negative moods can amplify rumination rather than support regulation [~W]
- Adds time pressure in short sessions; the routine must stay under ~5 minutes or it competes with instructional time

#### Implementation Variability
- **Analog:** sticky notes or magnets on a wall-mounted grid; fastest and preserves anonymity
- **Digital:** the RULER Mood Meter app or LMS polls; enables trend tracking over time
- **Private journal variant:** learners plot privately and write one sentence on *why* and one regulation strategy — deepens the [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) connection
- **Young learners:** replace valence/energy axes with faces or characters (see ['What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In](whats_my_emotion_game_check-in.md))

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, where routine emotion labeling builds foundational self-awareness [+W]
- Adolescents and adults in high-stress programs (e.g., medical training), where chronic unexamined stress degrades performance
- Learners with limited emotion vocabulary, who benefit most from the quadrant scaffolding [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: emotion recognition, vocabulary, and regulation strategies
- Metacognitive: noticing one's own readiness state and selecting strategies accordingly
- Classroom climate: building psychological safety and [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md)

### Instructions
1. Display the Mood Meter grid and briefly re-teach the two axes (energy and pleasantness) until learners can use them without prompting.
2. Ask each learner to place themselves on the grid — anonymously or by name — using sticky notes, a poll, or the RULER app.
3. Invite (never require) a few learners to name the specific emotion word and its cause; model precise vocabulary ("frustrated" rather than "bad").
4. Briefly acknowledge the class pattern ("I see a lot of yellow — energized and pleased") and adjust the session plan if a large cluster is red or blue.
5. Connect to regulation: ask learners to identify one strategy to shift quadrants if their current state interferes with learning (movement, breathing, reframing), linking to [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) as an ongoing practice rather than a one-off.

## Examples
- **RULER (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the Mood Meter is one of four RULER anchor tools; whole-school implementations include staff, student, and family Mood Meter check-ins ([https://ei.yale.edu/ruler](https://ei.yale.edu/ruler)).
- **RULER Mood Meter app** — digital plotting with emotion-word menus and built-in regulation strategy suggestions ([https://moodmeterapp.com](https://moodmeterapp.com)).
- **Secondary classroom use** — a teacher opens each class with sticky-note plotting on a door-mounted grid; a cluster in red prompts a two-minute reset before starting new content.
