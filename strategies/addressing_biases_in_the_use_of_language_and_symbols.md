# Addressing Biases in the Use of Language and Symbols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy involves auditing and revising the language, examples, names, imagery, and symbolic cues used in learning materials, assessments, and classroom discourse to remove stereotypes, exclusionary defaults, and identity-based assumptions. It is carried out through deliberate choices: gender-neutral phrasing, diverse and non-stereotyped example personas, culturally varied problem contexts, and visual environments that signal multiple identities are welcome. The goal is to create a learning environment in which no learner's identity is implicitly marked as out of place.

## Design Implications

Environmental and linguistic cues shape who feels they belong, and belonging in turn shapes persistence and performance [~S]. Learners who perceive identity-based threat in a setting disengage or underperform even when the content itself is neutral [~S]. Because these cues operate largely outside conscious awareness, they must be addressed by design rather than left to individual goodwill.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An audit process for existing materials: names used in examples, gendered defaults ("the engineer… he"), culturally narrow contexts, and imagery or symbols that signal a single demographic
- Replacement examples that represent diverse identities without tokenism — diversity in roles, contexts, and contributions, not just in decorative detail
- A norm-setting discussion with learners about inclusive language in discussion and peer feedback
- Ongoing review, since bias creeps back through unexamined defaults ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and learner feedback loops)

#### Constraints
- Superficial or tokenistic representation can backfire: learners notice when diversity is cosmetic, and it can reinforce rather than reduce stereotype threat [~S]
- Learners may resist language changes they perceive as political, particularly when the rationale is not explained; framing changes around accuracy and audience reach reduces resistance
- Overly sanitized language can strip examples of the contextual richness that supports [Analogical Reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) and transfer; the goal is accurate, varied contexts, not context-free ones
- One-time audits decay — materials drift back to defaults without periodic re-review

#### Implementation Variability
- Materials-level: revising textbooks, problem sets, slide imagery, and interface icons
- Discourse-level: instructor modeling and gentle correction of biased language in live discussion
- Assessment-level: ensuring scenario-based items do not require cultural background knowledge unrelated to the construct being measured
- Learner-facing: engaging students in auditing and rewriting biased materials themselves, which doubles as critical literacy practice

### Target Learners
- Learners from marginalized or underrepresented groups in a domain, for whom cue removal reduces identity threat and supports belonging [~S]
- All learners benefit from clearer, more accurate materials; stereotype threat effects are strongest for learners who are highly identified with the domain [~S]
- Belonging interventions are especially effective at transition points (first year, first course in a discipline) where belonging uncertainty is highest [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and climate goals: fostering belonging, respect, and willingness to participate
- Persistence goals: reducing identity-based disengagement that undermines retention [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Critical literacy goals: when learners audit materials themselves, they build skills in analyzing how language constructs social meaning

### Instructions
1. Audit existing materials for biased language, stereotyped examples, and exclusionary symbols; log each instance and its likely effect on different learner groups.
2. Revise examples and personas to represent diverse identities in non-stereotyped roles — e.g., vary the gender, culture, and role of named characters across a problem set rather than defaulting to one group for "expert" roles.
3. Replace gendered and ableist defaults with neutral phrasing ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
4. Set explicit norms for inclusive language in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and peer feedback, with the instructor modeling correction without shaming.
5. Provide [Practice](../elements/practice.md) opportunities in which learners identify and rewrite biased examples themselves, followed by [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on their revisions.
6. Re-audit periodically and gather learner feedback on whether materials feel inclusive.

## Examples
- **Physics problem-set revision** — Replacing a problem set in which all "engineers" and "scientists" were male and all "assistants" female with varied role–identity pairings; studies of classroom cues show such representation affects women's engagement in STEM settings [~S].
- **CS curriculum imagery** — Redesigning computer science classroom decor and course websites so that objects and imagery do not signal a single (male, tech-geek) culture; ambient cues measurably affect women's interest in entering computing environments [~S].
- **Belonging-framing at course start** — Pairing inclusive materials with a brief message that identity-based doubts are common and temporary; such interventions improved academic outcomes for marginalized students in a randomized field study [~S].
- **Learner-led audits** — Having students in a technical writing course audit a company's user documentation for biased language and propose revisions, turning the strategy into authentic practice with a real audience.
