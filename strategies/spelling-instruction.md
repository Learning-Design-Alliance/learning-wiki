# Spelling Instruction

## Description
Spelling instruction teaches learners to encode words accurately by making the orthographic system explicit — phoneme–grapheme correspondences, morphological patterns, and etymological conventions — rather than relying on memorizing arbitrary word lists. Effective programs combine explicit explanation of patterns, analysis of word structure, immediate corrective feedback, and distributed practice with feedback until words are stored in long-term memory as unitized orthographic representations.

## Design Implications

Explicit, systematic spelling instruction produces measurable gains in spelling accuracy and contributes to reading and writing development, because accurate word encoding and decoding draw on the same orthographic knowledge [Explicit spelling instruction improves spelling and writing outcomes.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Instruction works best when it teaches *why* a word is spelled as it is — mapping sounds to letters, identifying morphemes, and noting etymology — so learners build generalizable knowledge instead of item-by-item memorization. Weekly word-list testing alone, without instruction and feedback, yields little durable learning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A scope and sequence that moves from simple, regular patterns to complex and irregular ones
- Explicit explanation of the logic behind spellings (phonology, morphology, etymology) ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md))
- Word study activities that require analysis, sorting, and comparison, not just copying ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) if available; otherwise word sorts)
- Immediate corrective feedback on attempts, including self-correction routines ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
- Distributed review of previously taught words so spellings consolidate into automatic orthographic representations

#### Constraints
- Copying words repeatedly without analysis produces weak retention [-M] — copying is a low-level motor task that bypasses orthographic analysis
- Rote memorization of unrelated word lists does not transfer to new words [-M]; instruction must teach patterns, not just items
- Whole-word visual memorization alone is inefficient for irregular-heavy languages and fails learners with weak orthographic memory [~M]
- Overemphasis on correctness during early composing can suppress writing fluency and willingness to attempt words [-M]; invented spelling during drafting should be tolerated with correction deferred to editing
- Effectiveness drops when instruction is not coordinated with reading instruction; spelling and decoding share knowledge and reinforce each other [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Word sorts / developmental spelling (Words Their Way)**: learners categorize words by pattern, inducing the rule from contrast sets
- **Synthetic phonics-aligned spelling**: explicit phoneme–grapheme mapping with dictation, common in UK systematic programmes
- **Morphological instruction**: teaching bases, prefixes, and suffixes so learners spell by meaning units (e.g., *sign → signature*)
- **Test–study–test cycles with feedback**: pretest, targeted study of missed items, retest — more efficient than uniform study of all words

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and writers building initial orthographic knowledge [+S]
- Struggling spellers and students with dyslexia, who need more explicit, structured, and cumulative instruction than typically developing spellers [~S]
- Older students benefit less from general spelling programs but still gain from morphological and etymological instruction on academic vocabulary [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Automatic, accurate word encoding that frees working memory during composition [Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension; the same holds for production.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Generalizable orthographic knowledge: applying patterns to untaught words
- Reading support: spelling knowledge strengthens decoding and word recognition

### Instructions
1. Diagnose current spelling knowledge with a pretest or developmental inventory; group or sequence instruction accordingly ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Explicitly teach the target pattern, explaining the phonological, morphological, or etymological logic ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md)).
3. Have learners sort, compare, and analyze words exemplifying the pattern against contrasts ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — teach patterns in small sets, not long lists).
4. Practice through dictation and sentence writing with immediate corrective feedback ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Distribute review of previously taught words across subsequent weeks until spelling is automatic.
6. Hold learners accountable for taught patterns in authentic writing, with correction during editing rather than drafting.

## Examples
- **Words Their Way** (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — developmental word study built on sorting by spelling pattern; widely used in US elementary classrooms.
- **Spelling Mastery** (Dixon & Engelmann, McGraw-Hill) — a Direct Instruction program combining phonetic, morphemic, and whole-word strategies with scripted, cumulative lessons.
- **Read Write Inc. Spelling** (Oxford University Press, https://global.oup.com/education) — UK programme pairing systematic phonics with spelling rules and dictation for Years 2–6.
