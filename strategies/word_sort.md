# Word Sort

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students work in small groups, each given an envelope containing key terms on separate slips of paper. Before reading or lecture, they discuss what the terms might mean and organize them into categories based on perceived relationships. After encountering the terms in text or lecture, groups re-sort the words and compare their new arrangements with their initial ones, making conceptual revision visible.

## Design Implications

Word sort is a pre-instruction activation activity: by forcing learners to organize unfamiliar terms, it surfaces prior knowledge and creates a readiness for the explanations that follow. The before/after re-sort structure turns the sort into a form of contrasting cases — learners experience why the expert categorization is better than their naive one, which supports abstraction of the underlying structure [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep features.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. Struggling productively with the initial sort before receiving definitions also improves subsequent learning from instruction [Productive failure improves conceptual learning compared with direct instruction first.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+M]. The discussion component adds a self-explanation benefit: articulating *why* two terms belong together forces relational processing rather than rote pairing [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A carefully chosen term set (roughly 10–25 terms) with genuine relational structure worth discovering
- Slips of paper or cards that can be physically rearranged
- A follow-up exposure phase (reading, lecture) that supplies authoritative definitions and relationships
- A scheduled re-sort with group comparison of before/after arrangements

#### Constraints
- Students may object to "figuring out definitions for themselves" and perceive the activity as the instructor shirking work [-W] — framing the sort as preparation, not replacement, for authoritative explanation mitigates this
- If the term set is arbitrary or the categories are idiosyncratic, students waste effort constructing meaningless groupings [-M]
- With no closure or expert debrief, students may consolidate incorrect relationships from the initial sort [-M]
- Groups with no relevant prior knowledge may sort superficially (alphabetically, by word length), reducing the activation benefit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Closed sort**: instructor specifies the number or nature of categories; **open sort**: students devise their own, which demands deeper relational reasoning
- **Pre/post sort** (as described) versus **post-instruction sort only**, used as review and consolidation
- Digital variants (drag-and-drop boards, Padlet, Miro) allow asynchronous or large-class use
- Individual pre-sort as homework followed by group negotiation in class

### Target Learners
- College students and older learners encountering a new technical vocabulary domain
- Learners with partial, fragmented prior knowledge — the sort reveals what they already know and exposes gaps [Activation of prior knowledge prepares learners to integrate new information.](../principles/activation.md) [+M]
- Less suitable for absolute novices with no experiential foothold, who lack material to activate [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Vocabulary acquisition and retention in a content domain
- Relational knowledge: understanding how concepts relate, not just what they mean
- Conceptual change: revising naive categorizations after instruction
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own prior knowledge and its limits

### Instructions
1. Select 10–25 key terms from the upcoming unit and write each on a separate slip; verify the set has meaningful category structure.
2. Form small groups and distribute envelopes; instruct students to discuss what the terms might mean and sort them into categories ([Peer Collaboration](../elements/peer-collaboration.md)).
3. Have groups record or photograph their initial arrangement.
4. Deliver the exposure phase — reading, lecture, or [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) — that defines the terms and models expert relationships.
5. Reconvene groups to re-sort the terms ([Application](../elements/application.md)), then compare new arrangements with the originals and explain what changed and why ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
6. Debrief with an expert categorization, addressing the most productive disagreements from the sorts.

## Examples
- **Words Their Way** (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — the foundational K–12 spelling and vocabulary program built entirely around developmental word sorts; students categorize words by spelling pattern, sound, and meaning (https://www.pearson.com/words-their-way).
- An introductory psychology course sorts terms from an upcoming unit on memory (encoding, storage, retrieval, amnesia, rehearsal…) before lecture, then re-sorts after; the re-sort discussion surfaces the encoding/retrieval distinction students initially collapsed.
- A nursing pharmacology course has students sort drug names by guessed mechanism before the pharmacokinetics unit, then re-sort by actual mechanism after instruction.
