# Establishing Rules and Expectations

## Description
Establishing rules and expectations means explicitly defining — and ideally co-constructing — the behavioral, procedural, and academic norms of a learning environment, then teaching them as deliberately as content. It is carried out at the start of a course or term through clear statements of what success and appropriate participation look like, modeled routines, and consistent follow-through.

## Design Implications

Clear expectations reduce uncertainty and off-task behavior, freeing working memory and instructional time for learning; classroom management practices that establish norms proactively show one of the larger effect sizes on achievement in educational research [Marzano's meta-analysis of classroom management.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164X03065002004) [+S]. Expectations work best when they are few, positively framed, explicitly taught, and consistently enforced — vague or punitive rule lists do not produce the same benefit [Kounin's work on withitness and smoothness.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164X03065002004) [+M]. Co-constructing norms with learners supports buy-in and autonomy rather than mere compliance [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A small set (typically 3–7) of clearly stated, positively framed rules or norms
- Explicit teaching of routines: what the expectation looks, sounds, and feels like in practice
- Consistent, low-drama responses when expectations are not met
- Alignment between stated expectations and how the instructor actually behaves and assesses

#### Constraints
- Rules imposed without explanation or student input can undermine autonomy and produce compliance only under surveillance [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]
- Overly long or ambiguous rule lists dilute attention and are inconsistently applied [-M]
- Expectations established once and never revisited decay; they must be re-taught after transitions and disruptions [-M]
- Harsh, exclusionary enforcement (e.g., zero-tolerance discipline) is associated with worse engagement and disproportionate harm to marginalized students [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Instructor-authored rules vs. co-constructed norms (the latter trades speed for ownership)
- Behavioral norms vs. academic expectations (e.g., "what good discussion looks like" protocols)
- Whole-class agreements vs. small-group or team norms in collaborative settings
- Digital adaptations: netiquette agreements, discussion-forum guidelines, camera/mic norms in online courses

### Target Learners
- Novice learners and younger students, who lack internalized models of academic participation [+M]
- Learners from backgrounds whose prior schooling norms differ from the instructor's assumptions — explicit expectations prevent inequitable "hidden curriculum" penalties [+M]
- Anxious or new students, for whom clarity reduces uncertainty and supports [belonging](../elements/belonging.md) [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: internalizing norms so external rules become personal standards
- Collaborative participation: establishing the discourse norms that make [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and group work productive
- Metacognitive habits: expectations about revision, effort, and error normalize productive struggle

### Instructions
1. Identify the 3–7 behaviors most essential to learning in this specific environment (not a generic rule list).
2. Frame each as a positive, observable behavior ("We build on each other's ideas") rather than a prohibition.
3. Where feasible, co-construct norms with learners and explain the rationale for each [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].
4. Model and rehearse each routine explicitly, as in [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — treat the routine itself as content to be taught.
5. Post and reference expectations at moments of use (before discussion, group work, assessment).
6. Respond to violations consistently, privately where possible, and with the [empathic discipline](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615570362) approach that maintains relationship while restating the expectation [+M].
7. Revisit and revise norms periodically, especially after transitions or when the group composition changes.

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom (Center for Responsive Schools)** — K–8 program in which teachers and students co-create classroom rules during the first weeks, then practice routines ("Interactive Modeling"); see [responsivereads.com](https://www.responsivereads.com) / [centerforresponsiveschools.org](https://www.centerforresponsiveschools.org).
- **PBLWorks discussion norms** — project-based learning classrooms establish "norms of collaboration" posters and protocols that students self-assess against during team work ([pblworks.org](https://www.pblworks.org)).
- **University syllabus co-construction** — instructors of large lecture courses devote the first session to generating a shared "community agreement" for discussion and online forum conduct, then link peer-feedback rubrics to it.
