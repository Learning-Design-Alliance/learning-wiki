# Use Quotations to Cite Textual Evidence

## Description
Using quotations to cite textual evidence involves teaching students to select relevant passages from a text and integrate them into their own writing to support claims. The strategy typically pairs explicit instruction in selection and integration mechanics (framing quotes, citing, commenting) with repeated practice and feedback, so that quoting becomes a purposeful analytical move rather than a decorative one.

## Design Implications

Quotation instruction works because it makes the abstract norm of "support your claim" concrete and checkable: students can see whether a quote actually warrants a claim, and teachers can give precise feedback on the claim–evidence link [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis identifies explicit strategy instruction in writing as consistently effective.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. The critical design decision is requiring commentary: a quote dropped in without explanation does not build the reasoning skill, and instruction that stops at "find a quote" produces formulaic writing [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts worth quoting — rich enough that evidence selection requires judgment, not just retrieval
- Explicit modeling of the full move: claim → framed quotation → citation → commentary ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) or [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of the selection decision)
- Repeated [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback targeted at the claim–evidence relationship, not surface citation mechanics
- A visible structure students can internalize (e.g., claim–evidence–commentary frames) that is later faded

#### Constraints
- Over-quotation: students substitute quoted text for their own analysis, producing patchwriting or "quote sandwiches" with no original thinking [-M]
- Formulaic application: rigid templates (e.g., a fixed sentence frame) can produce compliance without understanding; frames must be faded as competence grows [~M]
- Selection is the hard part — students with weak comprehension choose quotes that are topically adjacent but not logically supportive, so quotation instruction alone cannot compensate for comprehension gaps [-M]
- Less applicable to narrative or personal writing genres where textual evidence plays a different role

#### Implementation Variability
- **Annotated models:** study mentor texts where published writers integrate quotes, comparing strong and weak integrations [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying criterion.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- **Quote selection first:** students propose candidate quotes and justify choices in discussion before writing, separating selection from integration
- **Gradual release:** teacher-modeled integration → collaborative integration → independent writing
- **Cross-disciplinary transfer:** adapt the evidence move to history (primary sources), science (data claims), or mathematics (justifying solutions)

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and early-college writers encountering evidence-based argument for the first time; explicit strategy instruction in writing shows the largest effects for these writers [+M]
- English learners and students with weaker academic language, who benefit from concrete frames for integrating source material [~M]
- Advanced writers need fading of templates quickly, or the scaffolding suppresses voice and analysis [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Argumentative writing: constructing claims warranted by textual evidence
- Close reading: justifying interpretation with specific textual support ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) feeds this directly)
- Analytical reasoning: explaining *how* evidence supports a claim, not just that it does [Explaining one's own reasoning improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Model the full move with a think-aloud: read a passage, weigh candidate quotes aloud, select one, and explain why it is the strongest evidence ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
2. Provide a claim–evidence–commentary frame and have students integrate a quote into a shared class argument ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can present the frame)
3. Have students practice on short texts, then exchange drafts and evaluate whether each quote genuinely supports its claim ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
4. Give feedback focused on the claim–evidence–commentary link rather than citation formatting [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
5. Fade the frame and assess independent integration in full essays ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

## Examples
- **Reading and Writing Project (Teachers College, Columbia University)** — argument units in which students collect "boxes and bullets" evidence from texts, then draft essays where each box becomes a claim supported by a quoted passage. https://www.readingandwritingproject.org
- **AP English Language and Literature courses** — the College Board's evidence-based essay rubrics explicitly reward quotations that are integrated and analyzed, making the claim–evidence–commentary move the scored construct. https://apcentral.collegeboard.org
- **History classrooms using Document-Based Questions (DBQs)** — students quote primary-source documents to support a thesis, with rubrics penalizing quotes that are dropped in without analysis.
