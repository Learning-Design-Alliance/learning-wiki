# Revision Cycles

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (1 for, 2 mixed) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 3 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
A revision cycle structures learning around iterative production: learners create a draft or attempt, receive feedback (from instructor, peers, or self-assessment), and revise the work before it is assessed or published. The cycle may repeat two or more times, with each iteration narrowing the gap between current and target performance. Revision is treated as part of the learning activity itself, not as error correction after learning has ended.

## Design Implications

Revision cycles operationalize [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): feedback arrives while learners can still act on it, and the act of revising forces learners to process feedback deeply rather than merely receive it [Formative assessment improves achievement by closing the gap between current and desired performance.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The revision step is what distinguishes the cycle from feedback alone — learners must diagnose what to change and how, which builds evaluative judgment and self-regulation.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Low-stakes or ungraded early drafts, so revision is not penalized
- Feedback that is specific, actionable, and delivered in time to be used ([Actionable Feedback](action-oriented-feedback.md))
- Explicit criteria or rubrics so learners know what "better" looks like
- Protected time and a required revision deliverable — feedback without a mandated revision is typically ignored
- A low-stakes (or ungraded) initial submission so learners can risk imperfection
- Feedback that identifies *what* to change and *how*, not just quality judgments
- Protected time and explicit expectation for the revision step — a deadline for the revised version, not merely an invitation to improve
- A way to see change over time (draft comparison, revision memo) so learners notice their own improvement

#### Constraints
- Feedback that arrives after grades are posted is largely unused [-M] — learners attend to the grade, not the comments
- Vague feedback ("expand this," "unclear") produces cosmetic revision rather than substantive improvement [-M]
- Too many cycles with heavy feedback load can cause cognitive overload and disengagement [Cognitive overload degrades learning when demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Peer feedback of uneven quality can mislead novices unless criteria and training are provided [~M]
- Feedback without a required revision step is largely ignored; learners attend to the grade and move on [-S]
- If the final grade averages or penalizes early drafts, learners optimize for first-attempt polish and the cycle collapses [~S]
- Unstructured peer feedback ("I liked it") gives revisers nothing to act on; peer reviewers need protocols or rubrics [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-led:** instructor comments on drafts; strongest for high-stakes disciplinary accuracy
- **Peer review:** structured protocols (e.g., calibrated peer review) where learners critique each other's drafts against a rubric; reviewing others' work builds evaluative judgment even when the feedback received is imperfect
- **Self-assessment:** learners compare their draft against exemplars or rubrics before submitting
- **Conference-based:** brief individual meetings (e.g., writing conferences) targeting one or two revision priorities per cycle
- **Instructor-feedback cycles**: teacher comments on drafts; highest quality but least scalable
- **Peer-review cycles**: structured protocols (e.g., Calibrated Peer Review) where students review each other against a rubric; doubles as learning through evaluating others' work
- **Self-assessment cycles**: learners compare their draft against exemplars or rubrics and plan their own revisions; builds evaluative judgment
- **Automated cycles**: systems like Revision Assistant (Turnitin) deliver instant draft feedback, enabling more cycles per term

### Target Learners
- Developing writers and novice producers of complex work, who lack the evaluative judgment to self-correct without external feedback [+M]
- Learners building [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) habits; cycles externalize the plan–monitor–revise loop until it can be internalized
- Less effective for learners who treat revision as optional busywork unless revision is required and consequential
- Learners prone to treating assignments as one-shot performances; cycles reframe error as expected
- Advanced learners may need fewer, deeper cycles — over-scaffolding revision for experts adds little [Contingent scaffolding improves learning when support is matched to current learner need.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improving quality of complex products (writing, designs, code, arguments) through iteration
- Developing evaluative judgment: the ability to judge the quality of one's own work
- Metacognitive and self-regulatory skill: interpreting feedback and planning improvements
- Normalizing drafting and imperfection as part of expert practice
- Product quality: iteratively improving writing, design, code, or arguments
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing the criteria used to assess one's own work
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and adjusting work across attempts

### Instructions
1. Set the task with clear criteria and an exemplar; make explicit that the first attempt is a draft ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
2. Learners produce a complete draft under realistic constraints ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
3. Deliver or orchestrate feedback — instructor comments, structured peer review, or rubric-based self-check — focused on a small number of high-leverage issues ([Actionable Feedback](action-oriented-feedback.md))
4. Require a revision with a brief change memo ("what I changed and why") to force processing of the feedback ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
5. Repeat for one or more cycles, shifting feedback responsibility progressively from instructor to peers to self ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))
6. Conclude with publication or final assessment so revision has an authentic endpoint ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (https://calibratedpeerreview.org)** — learners first calibrate their reviewing skill against instructor-graded sample essays, then review peers' drafts and revise their own based on received comments.
- **Writing-across-the-curriculum draft–feedback–revision sequences** — typical composition courses require a graded proposal, a commented draft, and a final revision, with the grade weighted toward the revision delta.
- **Studio critique in art and design education** — public critique of work-in-progress followed by a revision presented at the next session; the cycle is the core pedagogy of the studio model.
- **Agile code review in programming courses** — pull-request feedback on student code followed by required commits addressing reviewer comments before merge.
- **Calibrated Peer Review (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu)** — discipline-based assignments where students first calibrate their reviewing skill on sample texts, then peer-review and revise in structured cycles.
- **Writing-across-the-curriculum "draft → peer review → revision" sequences** — standard in composition courses (e.g., first-year composition curricula), where the revision memo makes the learning from the cycle visible.
- **Revision Assistant (Turnitin)** — automated formative feedback on student drafts during writing, enabling multiple revision passes before teacher evaluation.

## Description
A revision cycle structures learning around repeated produce–feedback–revise loops: learners create a draft or attempt, receive specific feedback (from instructor, peers, or self-assessment), and rework the product before final evaluation. The cycle treats the first attempt as raw material for learning rather than a summative judgment, making iteration an explicit expectation of the task design.

## Design Implications

Revision cycles operationalize [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by positioning feedback as input to further work rather than a verdict [Formative assessment practices improve achievement by making learning gaps actionable.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Their effectiveness depends on feedback being specific and actionable, and on learners actually doing the revision — feedback that is received but not acted on produces little learning. Spacing revision across time also converts each cycle into a retrieval and reprocessing opportunity [Distributed practice improves long-term retention compared with massed practice.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S].
