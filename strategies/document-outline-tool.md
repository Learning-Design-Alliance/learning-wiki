# "Document Outline" Tool

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 causal, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
Students use the "Document outline" tool in Google Docs to extract evidence supporting the main ideas they've found in a text. They write headings for paragraphs or groups of paragraphs and select "Heading 1" from the drop-down menu; sentences or phrases representing supporting evidence are summarized and labeled "Heading 2." The tool automatically populates a navigable outline that can be exported to the top of the document, giving students a ready-made scaffold for review and writing initiation.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines structural text analysis with external organization: assigning headings forces students to discriminate main ideas from supporting detail, and the auto-generated outline makes that structure visible and reusable. Summarizing evidence into heading-level phrases is a form of generative processing that supports comprehension better than rereading or highlighting alone [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Because the outline is produced as a byproduct of the work rather than composed separately, it lowers the startup cost of writing from sources.

### Instructions
1. Assign the source text ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)) and model the heading-labeling decision once on a shared document.
2. Students read and, for each paragraph or paragraph group, write a heading naming the main idea and apply "Heading 1."
3. Students summarize supporting evidence into short phrases, apply "Heading 2," and place them under the relevant main-idea heading — a digital form of [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md).
4. Students open the auto-generated outline, review it for gaps or mislabeled ideas, and revise headings — a self-explanation pass over their own structure [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
5. Students use the exported outline to begin drafting ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)), turning each Heading 1 into a section and each Heading 2 into a point with evidence.

## Description
A document outline tool supports learners in planning a written product before drafting: they organize the document into sections, headings, and bullet-level key points, establishing the logical skeleton first. The tool may be a structured template, a drag-and-drop outliner (e.g., Workflowy, Notion, Google Docs outline view), or an instructor-provided planning frame. The core move is separating *structural planning* from *prose generation*, so working memory is spent on organization rather than on composing sentences and arranging ideas simultaneously.

Outlining reduces the simultaneous demands of ideation, organization, and transcription during writing, easing cognitive load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Planning at the document level also shifts feedback earlier in the writing process: instructors can review and correct an outline before learners invest effort in full drafts, making feedback more efficient and less costly to act on [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. # Document Outline Strategy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 1 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Using the "Document outline" tool in Google Docs, students identify the main ideas in a text and the evidence supporting them, then encode that structure as headings. A main idea becomes a "Heading 1"; each summarized piece of supporting evidence becomes a "Heading 2" placed at the relevant sentence or paragraph. When complete, the outline can be exported to a table of contents at the top of the document, and each heading acts as a hyperlink that jumps to the corresponding passage — turning the finished outline into a navigable map of the text and a jumping-off point for writing.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines three well-supported moves: marking text to direct attention, summarizing to force generative processing, and organizing ideas hierarchically to build a coherent schema. Because headings must be *written* by the student rather than selected, the tool functions as a lightweight [graphic organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that the learner constructs rather than receives [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The hyperlink structure adds a navigation payoff: students can revisit the argument's skeleton without rereading the full text, which supports revision and essay planning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to Google Docs (or an equivalent editor with an automatic outline/heading feature)
- A text worth structuring — typically expository or argumentative, with identifiable main ideas and evidence
- Time for reading, summarizing, and outlining; brief modeling of what counts as a "main idea" versus "evidence"
- A follow-on task that uses the outline (e.g., drafting an essay from the table of contents), so the outline serves a purpose rather than becoming an end in itself

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops when students simply relabel existing sentences instead of summarizing in their own words — verbatim copying bypasses the generative processing that drives comprehension [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [-M]
- Less effective for narrative or highly associative texts where a hierarchical main-idea/evidence structure does not map onto the content
- Students with weak summarization skills may produce headings that are too broad or too granular; without feedback the outline can encode a misunderstanding and make it harder to undo later
- Tool dependence: the workflow is specific to editors with heading-based outlines, and the skill does not automatically transfer to outlining on paper or in plain text

- **Collaborative outlining:** pairs negotiate headings, forcing discussion of what the main idea really is
- **Progressive fading:** teacher supplies Heading 1s early on; students supply only Heading 2s, then both

- Less beneficial for advanced readers who already impose structure automatically; for them the procedure can feel like busywork [~W]

- Metacognitive monitoring: the outline makes gaps in understanding visible — a heading that can't be written signals a paragraph that wasn't understood

### Instructions
1. Turn on the Document outline tool (Tools → Show outline) in Google Docs and model the process on a short shared text, thinking aloud about how you distinguish a main idea from evidence.
2. Students read the text and, for each major section, write a heading summarizing the main idea and apply "Heading 1" [Summarization and Synthesis](../elements/summarization-and-synthesis.md).
3. Students locate sentences or phrases that serve as evidence for each main idea, write a brief summary heading for each, and apply "Heading 2" [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md).
4. Export the outline to the table of contents and use the hyperlinked headings to review the text's structure, checking that each main idea is supported [Practice](../elements/practice.md).
5. Use the outline as a planning scaffold for writing — each Heading 1 becomes a section or paragraph claim, each Heading 2 a piece of evidence — and provide feedback on outline accuracy before drafting [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md).

## Related Strategies
- **Reverse outlining** — the same heading-labeling move applied to a student's own draft for revision
- **[Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)** — the broader family of text-marking strategies; the outline tool is a structured, navigable form of annotation
- **[Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)** — the outline groups text into meaningful units, reducing the load of holding a long text in mind

## Examples
- A middle school ELA class outlines a *Newsela* article in Google Docs, then drafts an argumentative paragraph using the exported table of contents as its plan.
- A first-year composition course uses reverse outlining: students apply Heading 1/Heading 2 labels to their own essay drafts to check that every paragraph has a clear claim and support before revision.
- A high school history class collaboratively outlines a primary-source document, negotiating headings in pairs and comparing outlines as a class to discuss interpretation of evidence.
