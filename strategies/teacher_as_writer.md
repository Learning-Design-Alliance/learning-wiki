# Teacher as Writer

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Teacher as Writer is a professional learning strategy in which teachers engage in their own writing — drafting, revising, and sharing — in order to experience the writing process their students face. Teachers write personally meaningful pieces (about family, self, or interests), receive feedback, and reflect on the emotional and cognitive demands of composing. The goal is not publication but embodied understanding: teachers who write develop empathy for struggling writers and more credible instructional judgment about what writing instruction requires.

## Design Implications

The strategy rests on the premise that teaching a complex process benefits from firsthand experience of that process — a core move in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../principles/cognitive-apprenticeship.md), where the teacher re-enters the learner role. Teachers who write themselves are better positioned to [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) their own drafting and revision, making expert composing processes visible rather than presenting only finished products. Experiencing the vulnerability of sharing unfinished writing also builds the empathy that underpins effective feedback practices [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Protected time for teachers to write regularly (writing time must be scheduled, not aspirational)
- A low-stakes audience — a writing group, PLC, or institute — willing to share drafts and respond constructively
- A facilitator who writes alongside participants; the facilitator's own vulnerability sets the norm
- Prompts that invite personal, low-risk topics (family, self, place) before professional or analytical writing

#### Constraints
- Teachers resistant to writing, or who see writing as belonging to English teachers only, may disengage; mandating participation without building a supportive community tends to produce compliance writing rather than genuine experience [-W]
- Time pressure is the most common failure mode: one-off workshops without sustained writing time produce little change in classroom practice [-W]
- If the experience stays at the personal level without structured reflection connecting it to instruction, empathy gains do not transfer to pedagogy [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Summer writing institutes (the National Writing Project model): multi-week immersion with peer response groups
- Embedded PLC practice: brief regular writing during existing team meetings with rotating sharing
- Cross-disciplinary adaptation: math and science teachers write explanations, lab narratives, or problem-solving journals rather than essays
- Teacher-as-writer paired with [5-minute writing conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md) to connect the teacher's experience directly to conferring practice with students

### Target Learners
- Teachers of all subjects and grade levels, especially those who assign writing but do not write themselves
- Early-career teachers building instructional identity, for whom experiencing learner discomfort builds durable empathy
- Teacher educators and coaches who must [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) composing processes for others

### Target Learning Goals
- Pedagogical empathy: understanding the cognitive and emotional load of drafting and revising from the student's perspective
- Instructional modeling capacity: developing authentic material and process knowledge to demonstrate writing moves
- Teacher self-efficacy as a writer, which supports persistence in teaching writing [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Write first, personally: begin with low-risk prompts about family, self, or place; expect discomfort and name it explicitly as the point of the exercise.
2. Share drafts in a small peer response group, experiencing the vulnerability students feel when submitting unfinished work.
3. Revise in public: revisit a draft over multiple sessions so teachers feel the effort of making writing "efficient and interesting."
4. Reflect on the experience against instructional practice — where did you stall, avoid, or seek reassurance, and what does that imply for how you respond to student writers?
5. [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) for students: compose or revise in front of the class, verbalizing decisions and uncertainty, connecting the teacher's experience to visible demonstration.
6. Sustain the practice through a writing group or [Communities of Practice](../principles/communities-of-practice.md) so the identity of "teacher who writes" persists beyond the initial institute.

## Examples
- **National Writing Project** (https://www.nwp.org) — the canonical implementation: summer institutes where teachers across disciplines write, share, and respond in groups, then lead similar work in their own schools.
- A math department PLC that opens each meeting with five minutes of journal writing about a problem-solving experience, then discusses how the struggle felt — mirroring what students face with unfamiliar problems.
