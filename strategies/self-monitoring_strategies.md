# Self Monitoring Strategies

## Description
Self monitoring strategies ask learners to systematically observe and record aspects of their own learning — attention, accuracy, strategy use, time on task, or comprehension — and compare what they observe against a standard or goal. Typical implementations include accuracy self-recording (tallying correct answers), comprehension checks ("Can I explain this paragraph?"), attention checks, and structured self-questioning during reading or problem solving. Monitoring is the bridge between goal-setting and strategy adjustment in self-regulated learning: without accurate monitoring, learners cannot tell when to persist, switch strategies, or seek help.

## Design Implications

Self monitoring improves learning primarily when it is *accurate* and *cued*: learners are systematically miscalibrated, and unaided judgments of learning rely on fluency cues that often mislead [~M]. External structure — recording sheets, prompts, rubrics — converts vague self-assessment into specific, standard-referenced observation, which is what drives behavioral change. Monitoring works as part of a cycle (observe → compare to goal → adjust), not as a standalone activity; pairing it with [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) gives learners the external standard they need to calibrate against [assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear performance standard or goal against which monitoring is compared
- A concrete recording method (checklist, tally, log, rubric) that is low-effort enough not to displace the learning task itself
- Prompts timed to natural checkpoints (end of a problem, paragraph, or work session)
- Follow-through: opportunities to act on what monitoring reveals ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), strategy adjustment, help-seeking)

#### Constraints
- Monitoring adds cognitive load; for novices, self-questioning during a demanding task can degrade performance rather than improve it [~M] — introduce monitoring at low-stakes or low-load points first
- Learners frequently overestimate their comprehension and readiness (illusions of knowing), so self-report alone is unreliable [-S]; monitoring must be checked against objective performance, not trusted as-is
- Recording can become a compliance ritual with no effect on behavior if learners never receive feedback on the accuracy of their monitoring
- Over-monitoring (excessive check-ins, logging every action) can undermine motivation and autonomy [autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Accuracy self-recording**: learners tally correct/incorrect responses; common in behavior-analytic and special education applications
- **Comprehension monitoring**: stop-and-summarize or keyword prompts during reading; feeds directly into rereading decisions
- **Attention/engagement monitoring**: brief check-ins or attention ratings during independent work
- **Process monitoring**: checklists tracking strategy steps (e.g., "Did I check my work?") rather than outcomes
- **Technology-mediated**: dashboards and progress indicators in adaptive platforms externalize monitoring data for the learner

### Target Learners
- Students with weak self-regulation, including many learners with ADHD or executive function difficulties, who benefit from externalized monitoring structure [+M]
- Older elementary through adult learners; young children lack the metacognitive capacity for accurate self-judgment and need heavily scaffolded, concrete monitoring forms
- High-achieving learners may gain less, since their monitoring is already relatively accurate [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive skill development: accurate judgment of learning and comprehension
- Sustained attention and on-task behavior during independent work
- Strategy regulation: knowing when to persist, switch approaches, or seek help
- Long-term retention, via better-timed restudy and [Distributed Practice](distributed-practice.md) decisions

### Instructions
1. Set a specific, observable goal with the learner so monitoring has a comparison standard.
2. Choose or co-design a simple recording tool (tally sheet, checklist, exit prompt) matched to the target — accuracy, attention, or comprehension.
3. Model the monitoring itself: think aloud while checking your own work or assessing your own understanding, as in [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md).
4. Insert monitoring prompts at natural checkpoints — after each problem, paragraph, or work interval — using structures like [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md).
5. Have learners compare monitored performance against the goal and select an adjustment (reread, retry, seek help, change strategy).
6. Provide feedback on monitoring *accuracy*, not just performance, so learners calibrate their self-judgments over time.

## Examples
- **Self-monitoring checklists in special education** — students with ADHD track on-task behavior with a MotivAider or paper tally and compare against a co-set goal; one of the most consistently supported classroom interventions in the behavior-management literature.
- **Stop-and-jot comprehension checks** — during reading, students pause at paragraph ends to write a one-line summary and rate "I get it / I'm confused," triggering rereading decisions.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery progress bars and skill dashboards externalize monitoring, showing learners exactly which skills are proficient versus struggling.
- **Exam wrappers** — post-exam reflection forms (used widely in undergraduate STEM courses) prompting students to record how they studied and compare that with their results, feeding forward into next-exam planning.
