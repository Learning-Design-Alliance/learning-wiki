Mechanical Binet testers should report findings as tables of related chronological and mental ages, not as judgments of normality or abnormality¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 8 claims (5 for, 3 mixed) · 5 studies (3 theoretical, 1 review, 1 associational), q1–q2 · 0 of 5 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

Description¶

This strategy directs examiners who can do good mechanical Binet testing without being clinical psychologists to report their examinations of children or groups in tables of related chronological and mental ages rather than in terms of normality or abnormality. The article argues this is the satisfactory way of reporting Binet results, since only the expert can evaluate lesser degrees of retardation for diagnosis or prognosis. Within this format, examiners may state with high certainty that children showing retardation of more than 3 years are feeble-minded.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Examiners must be capable of doing good Binet testing of the mechanical sort and report both chronological and mental ages for each case examined.

Constraints¶

  • Examiners using this reporting format should not say that children testing less than 3 years retarded are backward or normal, since only the expert can evaluate lesser degrees of retardation.

Target Learners¶

  • children examined with the Binet Scale

Target Learning Goals¶

  • accurate classification of children's intellectual development

Claims¶

  • Trained assistants with extended institutional experience may differentiate feeble-minded children as well as, and sometimes better than, clinical psychologists on the Binet test [+W]
  • In expert hands the Binet Scale can be relied on to diagnose about 90 per cent of cases, with the remaining 10 per cent requiring other tests and criteria [~W]
  • The mental quotient is misleading as a general expression of mental development and is valuable only when cases are of the same age [+W]
  • The Binet-Simon scale accurately graded fairly minute individual differences in mental ability within same-age groups, as validated against teacher and principal judgments [+W]
  • Binet-Simon results fit normal children's chronological ages better in early years, while the Yerkes-Bridges point scale gives better results from age eight or nine on, due to scoring methods [~W]
  • Yerkes-Bridges coefficients of intelligence of 1.20 or more are attained by very different percentages of children at successive ages (34.5% at ages 4-5 down to 0% at ages 16-17 and 18-on), so they do not represent the same degree of brightness at all ages. [+W]
  • IQs obtained by dividing mental by chronological age remain fairly constant up to the age of 14, which supports treating yearly increments of intelligence as equal absolute units up to about age 14. [+W]
  • The Binet-Simon metric scale works well with normal and slightly atypical children but is less satisfactory with imbeciles and idiots and almost worthless for dements [~W]
