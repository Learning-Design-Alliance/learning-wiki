Uncluttered Environment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

An uncluttered environment is a physical learning space — classroom, workspace, or home study area — deliberately stripped of visual distractions: supplies stored out of sight, surfaces cleared, and wall displays limited to a small number of currently relevant items. The rationale is attentional: every visible object competes for limited visual attention and working memory resources, so reducing irrelevant stimuli preserves capacity for the learning task itself.

Design Implications¶

Visual clutter imposes measurable costs on attention: laboratory work shows that cluttered displays force the visual system to compete for neural representation, degrading recognition of targets within the display [McMains & Kastner's clutter study] [+S]. In classrooms specifically, children in decorated classrooms spent more time off-task and scored lower on learning outcomes than children in sparsely decorated ones [Fisher, Godwin & Seltman classroom decoration study] [+M]. The mechanism parallels the coherence principle in multimedia design: decorative but irrelevant material reliably reduces learning, especially for novices [Clark & Mayer coherence principle] [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Dedicated storage (shelves, cabinets, bins) so materials are accessible but out of the visual field during instruction
  • A curated display policy: only materials supporting the current unit remain visible; rotate rather than accumulate
  • Ongoing maintenance routines, since clutter re-accumulates quickly in active classrooms

Constraints¶

  • Overly sterile environments can reduce engagement and belonging; some personal and culturally relevant display supports motivation [~M]
  • Disorder is not uniformly harmful: Vohs et al. found tidy settings promoted convention and healthy choices, while disorderly settings promoted creative thinking [-M] — a fully ordered space may cost creative ideation
  • Effects are strongest for young children and novices; adults with developed attentional control show smaller benefits [~W]
  • Decluttering cannot compensate for acoustic or social distraction; it addresses only the visual channel

Implementation Variability¶

  • Zoned displays: keep one "focus wall" for current content and one student-work area; clear everything else
  • Temporary visual barriers (trifold dividers, facing-away seating) for learners with attention difficulties during independent work
  • Digital parallel: apply the same principle to slides and LMS pages — remove decorative images and off-topic links (Cognitive Load Management)

Target Learners¶

  • Young learners, whose attentional filtering of irrelevant visual information is still developing [Fisher, Godwin & Seltman classroom decoration study] [+M]
  • Learners with attention difficulties or sensory sensitivities, for whom irrelevant stimuli are harder to suppress [~M]
  • Novices generally, since irrelevant decorative material competes for the same limited working memory as to-be-learned content [Clark & Mayer coherence principle] [+S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Sustained attention and on-task behavior during instruction and independent work
  • Retention of lesson content (less attentional capture by irrelevant stimuli)
  • Self-regulation: an ordered environment cues ordered behavior and reduces stress [~W]

Instructions¶

  1. Audit the space. Inventory every visible item and classify it as instructional, organizational, or decorative. Remove or store anything not serving current learning.
  2. Curate displays. Keep only content tied to the current unit; rotate displays rather than layering them. Relevance, not quantity, is the criterion — emphasis directs attention only when it signals what matters Relevant emphasis directs attention to what matters. [+M]
  3. Clear work surfaces. Store supplies in labeled bins or cabinets; desks hold only materials for the current task.
  4. Chunk and organize what remains. Group remaining materials and displays into clearly bounded, labeled zones so the space is scannable at a glance Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]
  5. Maintain. Build a short daily reset routine and a deeper weekly reorganization; assign learners ownership of zones to build responsibility.
  6. Pair with other sensory management. Combine with Acoustics and Noise Management — visual decluttering alone leaves auditory distraction intact.

Related Strategies¶

  • Acoustics and Noise Management — the auditory counterpart; both reduce extraneous load from the physical environment
  • Clear Structure Presentation — the same decluttering logic applied to instructional materials and slides

Related Elements¶

  • Cognitive Load Management — the uncluttered environment is the physical-space enactment of reducing extraneous load

Examples¶

  • Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014) — Kindergarten classrooms compared in decorated vs. sparsely decorated conditions; children in the sparse condition were more attentive and learned more in six science lessons (CMU summary)
  • Montessori classrooms — Prepared environments with low open shelving, ordered materials, and minimal wall decoration; orderliness of the environment is a core design feature (Association Montessori Internationale)
  • Reggio Emilia "environment as third teacher" — a counterpoint: deliberately rich environments, but curated and purposeful rather than cluttered — illustrating that the goal is intentional visual content, not emptiness
