# Cognitively Guided Instruction

## Description
Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI) is a professional development program and instructional stance in which teachers learn the research-based taxonomy of children's mathematical problem-solving strategies (direct modeling, counting strategies, derived facts, recall) and the classification of problem types (e.g., join, separate, part-part-whole, compare), then use that knowledge to listen to and build instruction on students' own informal strategies. Rather than teaching a prescribed procedure first, teachers pose problems, elicit multiple solution strategies, and sequence problems to support students' progression toward more efficient reasoning.

## Design Implications

CGI rests on the constructivist premise that learners arrive with informal, meaningful strategies that instruction should build upon rather than replace [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Its distinctive move is making the *teacher's* knowledge the target of intervention: CGI professional development does not script lessons but deepens teachers' models of student thinking, on the theory that teachers who understand the developmental trajectory will make better moment-to-moment instructional decisions than any curriculum script could [~S]. Longitudinal studies show CGI-trained teachers gain greater knowledge of student thinking, spend more time on problem solving, and their students show stronger problem-solving and number-sense outcomes with no loss in computational skill [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teachers trained in the taxonomy of problem types (additive structures, then multiplication/division structures) and the developmental sequence of solution strategies
- A classroom culture where students explain their reasoning aloud and strategies are publicly compared
- Rich, contextualized word problems rather than bare computation exercises
- Time for students to solve problems in their own way before formal procedures are introduced

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends on teacher uptake: teachers who adopt the vocabulary but not the underlying attention to student thinking show little classroom change [-S]
- Requires substantial professional development time; one-shot workshops do not produce durable changes in practice [-M]
- Can be misread as "never teach procedures," leaving students without efficient strategies; CGI research supports building *toward* fluency, not stopping at informal methods [-M]
- Less directly applicable outside well-structured domains like early number and arithmetic, where the strategy taxonomy is well mapped [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class problem solving with strategy sharing, small-group work, or one-on-one conferences
- Integrated with curriculum materials (posing CGI-style problems within an adopted textbook) or as a standalone problem-solving routine
- Extended versions (e.g., "CGI fraction" work) apply the same stance to later-grade topics

### Target Learners
- Elementary students, especially K–3, developing number sense and additive/multiplicative reasoning [+S]
- Lower-achieving students benefit particularly, because their informal strategies are honored rather than bypassed [+M]
- Teachers as the primary learners: the professional development targets teachers' knowledge of student thinking

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of number operations and problem structure
- Problem-solving flexibility: selecting and inventing strategies suited to a problem
- Procedural fluency built on understanding rather than rote recall [~S]

### Instructions
1. Learn the problem-type taxonomy (join, separate, part-part-whole, compare; later multiplication/division structures) and the developmental sequence of children's strategies.
2. Pose a contextual word problem without demonstrating a procedure first, allowing [activation](../principles/activation.md) of students' informal knowledge.
3. Circulate and observe, using the taxonomy to interpret each student's approach (direct modeling → counting → derived facts → recall).
4. Facilitate a strategy-sharing discussion in which students explain their methods; sequence sharing from concrete to efficient.
5. Pose follow-up problems chosen to nudge students toward the next strategy in the progression — the teacher's knowledge of student thinking drives problem selection, not a fixed script.

## Examples
- **CGI professional development program** (Carpenter, Fennema, and colleagues, University of Wisconsin–Madison) — summer workshops plus yearlong support in which teachers analyze videos of children solving problems and plan instruction from student work.
- **Children's Mathematics: Cognitively Guided Instruction** (Carpenter, Fennema, Franke, Levi, & Empson, Heinemann) — the practitioner book and accompanying classroom video cases used widely in district CGI implementations.
- A first-grade CGI classroom solving "There were 8 birds on a branch; 3 flew away; how many are left?" — some students model with cubes, some count back, some recall the fact — followed by sharing that surfaces the counting-back strategy for peers still using direct modeling.
