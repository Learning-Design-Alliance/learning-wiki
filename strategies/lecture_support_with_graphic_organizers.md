# Lecture Support with Graphic Organizers

## Description
Instructors present lecture content using graphic organizers — concept maps, matrices, flowcharts, hierarchical trees, comparison tables — rather than bullet-point slides or prose outlines. The organizer externalizes the relational structure of the content (hierarchies, sequences, cause–effect links, contrasts) so that learners see how ideas connect while hearing them explained, rather than reconstructing those connections later from linear notes.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers exploit the [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) advantage of pairing verbal explanation with spatial-visual structure, and they reduce extraneous load by making the lecture's organization explicit instead of leaving learners to infer it [Nesbit & Adesope's meta-analysis found instructor-provided concept-map instruction outperforms text-based instruction on retention and transfer.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2006.02.001) [+M]. The organizer functions as an [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) scaffold when presented before content, and as a running structural map when built up during the lecture. Because the visual must be processed alongside the spoken explanation, organizers should be progressively revealed rather than shown all at once, consistent with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) principles for multimedia presentation [Mayer, R. E. (2009). *Multimedia Learning* (2nd ed.)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511811678) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Pre-designed organizers matched to the actual structure of the content (a hierarchy for taxonomies, a matrix for comparisons, a flowchart for processes)
- Progressive disclosure during the lecture — building the organizer node by node rather than displaying the finished diagram
- Verbal explanation that elaborates the links, not just the nodes; the relationships are the learning target
- A way for students to reproduce or complete the organizer themselves (handout, LMS copy, or partially blank version)

#### Constraints
- Organizers impose their own structure; a poorly chosen format (e.g., a hierarchy for content that is really a causal network) can distort understanding [~M]
- Presenting a complete, dense organizer all at once overloads working memory and encourages copying rather than processing [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M]
- Instructor-provided organizers can produce passive engagement; students who merely view a finished organizer learn less than those who generate or complete one [Stull & Mayer found learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided ones in several comparisons.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.4.660) [~S]
- Less suitable for narrative, affective, or highly idiosyncratic content that resists relational structure

#### Implementation Variability
- **Completed organizer** shown at the start as an advance organizer for the whole lecture
- **Progressively built** organizer, revealed node by node as content unfolds
- **Partial organizer** with blank nodes or unlabeled links that students fill in during the lecture (cloze-style)
- **Student-generated** organizers as a post-lecture consolidation activity, which shifts toward [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and generative processing
- **Matrices and comparison tables** for multi-concept contrasts, which support abstraction better than single-concept diagrams [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novices who lack the prior knowledge to impose structure on the content themselves; the organizer substitutes for missing schemas [+M]
- Learners with weaker note-taking or listening skills, who otherwise capture fragments without the connective tissue
- Less beneficial for experts or learners with strong prior knowledge, for whom the imposed structure may be redundant or constraining [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Relational and structural knowledge: hierarchies, sequences, cause–effect, and part–whole relationships
- Conceptual comprehension and integration across lecture segments
- Retention of organized content, since structured retrieval cues outperform linear notes for recall [+M]

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content to identify its dominant structure (taxonomy, process, comparison, causal chain) and select a matching organizer format.
2. Design the organizer with [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) in mind — no more than a handful of nodes per screen or reveal.
3. Open the lecture by presenting the empty or skeletal organizer as an [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) preview of where the session is going.
4. Build the organizer progressively during the lecture, narrating the *links* between nodes explicitly ("this factor feeds into that one because…") rather than only naming the nodes.
5. Use [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) or worked examples to anchor abstract nodes in familiar content as they are added.
6. Close with a consolidation step: students complete a partially blank copy, or redraw the organizer from memory, converting passive viewing into generative processing.

## Examples
- **Anatomy lectures using concept maps** — instructors build a body-systems concept map across a multi-week unit, with each lecture adding and linking nodes; students receive the cumulative map as a study artifact.
- **History lectures using comparison matrices** — a matrix of revolutions (causes × events × outcomes × actors) filled in across lectures lets students see structural parallels rather than isolated narratives.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — many lesson pages pair video explanation with structured diagrams and relationship maps rather than prose summaries.
- **Biology "concept maps as lecture backbone"** — a partially blank map distributed via the LMS that students complete during class, then compare against the instructor's completed version.
