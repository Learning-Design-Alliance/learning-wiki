Repeating Key Points in Different Ways¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 11 studies (4 causal, 4 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 11 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Repeating key points in different ways means restating core content through varied representations — paraphrase, concrete examples, visuals, gestures, or analogies — rather than verbatim repetition. Each restatement encodes the idea from a new angle, building redundant retrieval routes while giving learners who process slowly a second or third pass at the material.

Design Implications¶

Varied repetition works because it exploits dual coding and elaboration: presenting the same idea in verbal and visual form creates two memory codes, and paraphrase forces learners to abstract the invariant meaning across surface forms Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]. Verbatim repetition, by contrast, adds little once the words are understood and can invite shallow word-level processing. Spacing the repetitions across a lesson or across days outperforms massing them together Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A small set of genuinely key points — repetition multiplies whatever is repeated, so the target ideas must be selected first (Chunking)
  • At least two distinct representations per key point (e.g., verbal restatement plus a diagram or worked example)
  • A plan for when repetitions occur: opening preview, mid-lesson check, and closing summary, then spaced review in later sessions

Constraints¶

  • Verbatim repetition of an already-understood point wastes time and can reduce attention [-M] — learners disengage from what they perceive as redundant
  • Repeating too many points dilutes the effect; repetition of everything is repetition of nothing
  • Adding a second modality does not help if it duplicates rather than complements — redundant on-screen text narrated aloud can impair learning compared with narration plus a relevant graphic Media combinations affect recall and retention. [~S]
  • Repetition within a single massed block produces short-lived gains; without spacing, retention decays quickly Spaced repetition improves retention. [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Within-lesson: preview → restate with example → student paraphrase → closing summary
  • Across lessons: brief retrieval of the key point at the start of subsequent sessions (Spaced repetition)
  • Across learners: students generate their own restatements or examples (Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]), shifting repetition from teacher-provided to learner-generated

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with processing speed or working memory challenges who benefit from a second, differently-coded pass at content
  • Novices, who lack the prior knowledge to extract the key point on first exposure Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less valuable for experts, who extract the point immediately and find repetition redundant (expertise reversal)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of core concepts and vocabulary
  • Conceptual understanding, when repetitions use contrasting examples or analogies
  • Transfer, when varied restatements help learners abstract the deep structure across surface forms

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the 2–4 key points of the lesson and write a one-sentence statement of each (Clear Structure).
  2. Open with a preview of the key points (Advance Organizers).
  3. During instruction, restate each key point at least twice in different forms: a paraphrase, a concrete example, or an analogy (Analogies).
  4. Pair verbal statements with a supporting visual so the idea is dual-coded (Chunking).
  5. Ask learners to restate the point in their own words or generate their own example (Practice) — learner-generated repetition beats teacher-provided repetition Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].
  6. Close by revisiting the key points, then schedule brief spaced reviews in later sessions Spaced repetition improves retention. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Chunking — repetition only works on a small, well-chosen set of points
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the opening preview doubles as activation of related knowledge
  • Spaced Repetition — the scheduling principle that determines whether repetition sticks

Examples¶

  • Explicit Direct Instruction lessons (e.g., DataWORKS EDI) structure every lesson with a "concept development" phase in which the key concept is stated, restated with a non-linguistic representation, and checked through whole-class choral response and individual whiteboard checks.
  • Khan Academy (https://www.khanacademy.org) videos restate each step of a procedure verbally while writing it, then the accompanying exercise repeats the same concept across multiple problem variants.
  • Language teaching recasting: a teacher repeats a learner's utterance in corrected, expanded form — the same meaning delivered in a slightly different structure — a core technique in communicative and immersion classrooms.
