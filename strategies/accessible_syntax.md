# Accessible Syntax

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Accessible syntax is the deliberate adjustment of sentence structure — length, embedding, voice, and connective density — in instructional materials, teacher talk, and learner-facing tasks so that language form does not become an extraneous source of cognitive load. In practice this means simplifying instructor syntax when presenting new content, and scaffolding learner production with sentence starters, frames, and progressively more complex target structures.

## Design Implications

Syntactic complexity competes with content for working memory; when learners must parse convoluted sentences *and* learn new concepts simultaneously, comprehension suffers [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Shorter, subject-verb-object sentences with one idea per clause let learners allocate capacity to the content rather than the parsing. For learner production, sentence frames and starters lower the barrier to expressing disciplinary ideas, which is especially important for multilingual learners who often have conceptual understanding that their English syntax cannot yet express.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Knowledge of learners' language proficiency and reading levels, gathered through assessment or [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md)
- Willingness to revise instructional language: active voice, common connectives, defined terms, one clause per idea where possible
- Sentence frames or starters matched to the target discourse (e.g., claim-evidence-reasoning frames in science)

#### Constraints
- Oversimplification can deny learners exposure to the complex syntax of authentic disciplinary texts, delaying academic language development [-W] — the goal is temporary scaffolding, not permanent simplification
- Simplified syntax without vocabulary support is insufficient; syntax and word choice must be addressed together (see [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Sentence starters can become crutches if never faded; learners may produce formulaic prose that satisfies the frame without the underlying reasoning [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Input simplification**: rewriting instructions, problems, and readings into shorter sentences with explicit logical connectives
- **Output scaffolding**: sentence starters, paragraph frames, and "because/but/so" routines for learner writing and discussion
- **Gradual release**: beginning with heavy framing and fading toward independent complex syntax as proficiency grows
- **Bilingual supports**: allowing learners to draft in a stronger language before recasting in the target language

### Target Learners
- Multilingual learners and emergent bilinguals, who benefit most when language demands are reduced while conceptual demands are preserved [+W]
- Learners with language-based learning disabilities or processing speed challenges
- Young learners whose syntactic comprehension is still developing
- Less necessary for learners reading well above grade level in the language of instruction, for whom simplified syntax may add no value [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of complex content delivered through text or talk
- Disciplinary communication: expressing reasoning, argument, and explanation in conventional academic forms
- Reduced extraneous [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) during language-heavy tasks

### Instructions
1. Audit the language of the task: identify long sentences, nested clauses, passive constructions, and ambiguous pronouns in instructions and readings.
2. Rewrite for clarity: one idea per sentence, active voice, explicit connectives ("because," "however") rather than implicit juxtaposition.
3. Pair with vocabulary support: flag and pre-teach terms alongside the syntactic simplification, per [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md).
4. Provide production frames: give sentence starters matched to the target discourse move (e.g., "The data show… because…").
5. Fade the scaffolds: reduce frame support as learners demonstrate control, moving toward independent composition of complex sentences.

## Examples
- **WIDA-supported instruction** ([https://wida.wisc.edu](https://wida.wisc.edu)) — multilingual learner frameworks that pair content objectives with language objectives and sentence frames for academic discourse.
- **"Because, But, So" routine** (The Writing Revolution) — learners complete sentence stems using causal, contrastive, and consequential connectives, building syntactic control alongside content understanding.
- **Rewritten science prompts** — converting "Given the observations recorded during the preceding investigation, formulate a hypothesis that accounts for the anomalous result" into "Look at your data. Why was Trial 3 different? Write: 'Trial 3 was different because…'"
