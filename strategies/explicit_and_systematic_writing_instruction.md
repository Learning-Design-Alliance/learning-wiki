# Explicit and Systematic Writing Instruction

## Description
Explicit and systematic writing instruction directly teaches the strategies, skills, and knowledge proficient writers use, rather than expecting them to be absorbed through exposure. The instructor models writing strategies — planning, drafting, revising, self-regulation — making decisions visible, then guides learners through supported practice toward independent application across purposes (recording, communicating, persuading, self-expression, reflection). Foundational skills such as spelling, handwriting, and keyboarding are taught to automaticity so working memory is freed for composing [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M] — the same logic applies to transcription fluency in writing. Skilled writing is heavily a planning-and-revising activity: accomplished writers spend roughly half their composing time planning and revising text, so instruction must treat these as teachable strategies, not afterthoughts.

## Design Implications

Explicit strategy instruction works because it converts tacit expert processes into learnable procedures and then scaffolds learners' regulation of those procedures [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Its effects are among the largest in writing research: meta-analyses of explicit strategy instruction show substantial gains in text quality for adolescents, and comparable effects for students with learning difficulties [Graham & Perin meta-analytic evidence.](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.99.3.445) [+S]. Instruction must be *systematic* as well as explicit — sequenced from simple to complex, with each strategy taught, modeled, practiced, and revisited — because isolated mini-lessons without coordinated practice do not accumulate into writing competence.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A structured, sequenced curriculum that specifies which strategies and skills are taught when
- [Modeling](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) of strategies with instructor think-alouds that make planning and revision decisions visible
- Extended time for authentic writing practice across purposes and audiences
- Explicit feedback on strategy use and text quality, not just surface errors
- Instruction to automaticity of foundational transcription skills (spelling, handwriting, keyboarding)

#### Constraints
- Requires significant instructional time; effects shrink when strategy lessons replace rather than accompany extended writing [Process-writing approaches without explicit strategy support yield weaker effects.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2011.593691) [~M]
- Less effective when learners lack motivation or a reason to write; engagement and authentic purpose are prerequisites, not extras
- Overly formulaic strategy teaching (e.g., rigid essay templates) can produce formulaic writing and transfer poorly to new genres [~M]
- Foundational-skill drill that dominates instructional time crowds out composing practice, which is where strategy use consolidates

#### Implementation Variability
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)**: pairs strategy instruction with explicit teaching of self-regulation — goal setting, self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement — and is the most heavily validated variant, especially for struggling writers [+S]
- **Sentence-level instruction**: explicit teaching of sentence combining and construction, effective as a complement to whole-composition strategy instruction [+M]
- **Reading–writing integration**: teaching text structures through reading and then having students write in those structures capitalizes on the relationship between the two modalities [+M]
- **Prewriting and organizing**: explicit instruction in planning procedures (e.g., outlining, graphic organizers, goal setting) before drafting [+M]

### Target Learners
- Struggling writers and students with learning difficulties benefit most; explicit strategy instruction produces some of the largest effects for these learners [+S]
- Adolescents and older students who have not internalized planning and revision strategies [+S]
- Beginning writers building transcription automaticity, for whom cognitive load during composing is otherwise prohibitive [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- Capable writers still benefit from strategy instruction, though effects are smaller and depend on matching strategies to their current repertoire [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: producing clearer, better-organized, more persuasive text
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, evaluating, and revising one's own composing process
- Procedural fluency: automating transcription so attention shifts to ideas and audience
- Genre knowledge: understanding how purpose and audience shape text structure

### Instructions
1. **Teach the strategy explicitly.** Name the strategy, explain when and why to use it, and connect it to what proficient writers do ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md)).
2. **Model it.** Demonstrate the strategy through think-aloud while composing, making planning and revision decisions visible ([Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)).
3. **Guide practice.** Support learners as they apply the strategy with prompts, checklists, and collaborative composing, fading support as competence grows ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. **Teach self-regulation.** Train goal setting, self-monitoring, and self-evaluation so learners can deploy and adjust strategies independently across tasks.
5. **Extend to independent writing.** Provide sustained writing practice across purposes and genres, with feedback on both strategy use and text quality.

## Examples
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)** — Graham and Harris's extensively validated framework (e.g., the POW + TREE strategy for persuasive writing), in which students learn a planning strategy alongside self-regulation procedures; see [SRSD Online](https://srsdonline.org).
- **The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler)** — a systematic, sentence-level method that sequences instruction from sentence construction to composition, explicitly taught across content areas; see [The Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org).
- **Graham & Perin's Writing Next recommendations** — the meta-analysis identified explicit strategy instruction, summarization, collaborative writing, and sentence combining as the highest-effect practices for adolescent writers.
