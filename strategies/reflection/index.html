    Reflection¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Reflection is a structured activity in which learners deliberately revisit an experience, artifact, or problem to examine what happened, why it happened, and what should change next time. It is carried out through prompts, journals, structured protocols (e.g., 3-2-1 Reflection), or discussion, and works best when it produces a concrete artifact or commitment rather than unstructured rumination.

    Design Implications¶

    Reflection converts experience into transferable knowledge by forcing learners to articulate the reasoning behind outcomes — a mechanism closely related to self-explanation, which reliably improves understanding when learners explain their steps to themselves Chi et al.'s self-explanation work supports this. [+M]. Unprompted reflection is rare and shallow; effective designs supply specific prompts, time, and a follow-through mechanism such as Action Planning. Reflection also functions as formative Assessment data, revealing misconceptions and self-regulation gaps the instructor can act on Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A specific object of reflection (a task, artifact, decision, or experience) — vague "reflect on the unit" prompts produce vague output
    • Structured prompts or protocols that direct attention to reasoning, not just feelings (Articulation)
    • Protected time in the schedule; reflection squeezed into the last two minutes is skipped or superficial
    • A loop back into action: revised plans, next attempts, or instructor response

    Constraints¶

    • Unstructured journaling without prompts tends toward description and venting rather than analysis [-M]
    • Reflection on failure without guidance can depress self-efficacy rather than improve strategy use [~M]
    • When graded on length or polish, reflective writing becomes performance, undermining honesty and diagnostic value [-M]
    • For novices with little domain knowledge, reflection has little to operate on; it works best after sufficient experience or Practice [~M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Written (journals, exit tickets, 3-2-1 Reflection) — low cost, creates a record for later comparison
    • Oral/social (structured debriefs, Check-In rounds) — adds exposure to peers' reasoning
    • Comparative — reflecting across multiple cases or one's own earlier work sharpens discrimination Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]
    • Embedded vs. terminal — brief mid-task reflection ("why did that fail?") beats end-of-unit retrospection for correcting errors quickly

    Target Learners¶

    • Learners with enough experience to have something to examine — intermediate learners and above [~M]
    • Learners developing self-regulation, who benefit from prompts to monitor strategy use and outcomes
    • Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge base to generate useful explanations

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Metacognition: monitoring one's own understanding and strategy effectiveness
    • Transfer: extracting general principles from specific experiences
    • Professional judgment: learning from practice in complex, ill-structured domains
    • Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and revising one's approach

    Instructions¶

    1. Anchor the reflection to a concrete experience or artifact learners just completed (Practice, a project, a case discussion)
    2. Supply 2–3 specific prompts targeting reasoning and strategy, not just feelings (e.g., "What was your key decision, and what would you do differently?")
    3. Give protected time and a low-stakes format (Articulation)
    4. Require one forward-looking commitment or revision (Action Planning)
    5. Close the loop: revisit the reflection at the next attempt, or use it as formative data (Assessment)

    Related Strategies¶

    • 3-2-1 Reflection — a lightweight structured protocol for end-of-session reflection
    • Action Planning — the forward-looking half of the reflection cycle
    • Activating Prior Knowledge — reflection before learning serves the same retrieval function

    Examples¶

    • Project-based science curricula (e.g., Project Lead The Way) build design-journal reflections into engineering units, requiring students to justify design decisions and record revisions.
    • Medical education debriefing after simulation uses structured protocols (e.g., PEARLS) to guide team reflection on clinical decisions; reviews identify structured debriefing as more effective than unstructured discussion.
    • 3-2-1 Reflection exit tickets — three things learned, two questions, one application — as a five-minute end-of-class routine.
