# Cold Calling

## Description
Cold calling is the practice of asking a question, allowing brief thinking time, and then directing it to a named student rather than accepting only raised hands. It is carried out as a normative classroom routine — predictable, evenly distributed, and framed as an expectation of thinking rather than a test — so that all students prepare to respond rather than only volunteers.

## Design Implications

Cold calling converts whole-class questioning from a volunteer-selected activity into a distributed one, increasing the proportion of students who actively process the question [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on pairing the call with adequate wait time and a climate in which a partial or tentative answer is safe; used punitively, it suppresses rather than elicits thinking.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions pitched within reach of the called student (differentiate by knowing who is asked what)
- Sufficient wait time (3+ seconds) after the question and after the student begins answering
- A response climate where "I'm still working on it" or a partial answer is acceptable, with follow-up prompts rather than immediate redirection
- Even, predictable distribution so students cannot opt out by anonymity or opt in by dominance

#### Constraints
- Without established psychological safety, cold calling raises anxiety and reduces voluntary participation over time [Belonging interventions improve outcomes partly by reducing threat in evaluative settings.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]
- Instructors who use cold calling inconsistently or as a "gotcha" see students disengage and prep only when called on [~M]
- Less effective for high-stakes or emotionally loaded content, where public performance threat crowds out cognitive processing [-M]
- Requires small-to-moderate class sizes or name knowledge; in very large classes, name-level cold calling is impractical without tools

#### Implementation Variability
- **Warm calling**: signal the question to the class, give think/pair time, then call a name — reduces threat while keeping accountability
- **Randomized selection** (name cards, wheel, LMS picker): increases perceived fairness and prevents instructor bias toward confident students
- **Pre-briefed calling**: tell students in advance which question they will answer, trading spontaneity for preparation quality
- **Follow-up chains**: call a second student to build on or challenge the first answer, shifting from recall to [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)

### Target Learners
- Reluctant or quiet students who would otherwise never volunteer — the largest participation gains accrue to them [~M]
- All students when norms are established: perceived engagement and in-class participation rise across the distribution [~S]
- Students with high communication anxiety need scaffolding (pair time, warning, partial-credit norms) or the strategy backfires [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retrieval and consolidation: being called on forces retrieval, which strengthens memory more than re-exposure [+S]
- Formative assessment: hearing from a representative sample of students, not just volunteers, gives the instructor accurate evidence of class understanding
- Discussion skills and academic discourse: sustained use increases students' willingness to speak and the quality of contributions

### Instructions
1. Establish the norm explicitly on day one: "I will call on people by name; you may pass or say where you are in your thinking." Frame it as thinking practice, not evaluation.
2. Pose one clear question to the whole class before naming anyone, so all students engage.
3. Insert wait time or a brief [think-pair-share](../elements/class-discussion.md) step so every student forms an answer before one is selected.
4. Call a name, ideally tracking distribution to equalize frequency across students.
5. Respond to the answer at the task or process level — prompt for elaboration ("What makes you say that?") rather than judging correctness immediately [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
6. If the student is stuck, scaffold (narrow the question, offer a starting point) rather than passing immediately; return to them later in the term.

## Examples
- **Harvard Business School's case method** — cold calling is the backbone of every session; students are told the norm at admission, and roughly 50% of the class grade is based on called-on contributions, demonstrating that institutional framing and grading alignment make intensive cold calling sustainable.
- **Dallimore, Hertenstein & Kettner's classroom studies** — business school courses where moderate, routine cold calling increased voluntary participation and perceived learning over a semester, especially among initially reticent students.
- **K-12 "no hands up" practice (Dylan Wiliam's formative assessment work)** — teachers replace hand-raising with randomized selection (e.g., popsicle sticks) so every student prepares an answer, as a core formative assessment technique.
