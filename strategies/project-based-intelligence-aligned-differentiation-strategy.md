# Use project-based activities aligned with students' dominant intelligence domains to differentiate mathematics instruction.

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (7 for) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article's differentiation approach implements a specific instructional strategy: administer a multiple intelligences inventory to identify each student's dominant intelligence domains, then have students select project topics from alternatives that suit those domains, the subject objectives, and creativity strategies. Students complete all project stages (problem, method, presentation) and receive feedback from teachers, peers, and researchers based on video-recorded presentations. Teachers guide the projects and use evaluation forms based on information-process skills to score them. The article reports that this strategy increased achievement for both gifted and non-gifted students across six implementations.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A multiple intelligences inventory must be administered to identify students' dominant intelligence domains.
- Teachers must be informed about the activities, creativity, project preparation steps, and the project evaluation process before implementation.
- An evaluation form based on information-process skills is needed to score projects.
#### Constraints
- Teachers reported difficulties with group work, time, presentation preparation, and classroom size.
- One teacher noted that using the same activities across all mathematics subjects could waste time.

### Target Learners
- gifted middle school students
- non-gifted middle school students

### Target Learning Goals
- mathematics achievement
- research and presentation skills
- social skills such as cooperation and self-confidence
