# Headings and Highlight Strategy

## Description
Students read an excerpt of a difficult article in Google Docs. First, they highlight words they don't know. Then, in pairs, they read a paragraph together and highlight key ideas, discussing why those parts are important. Next, each student individually devises a four-word heading for that portion of the text based on the highlighted main ideas, and pairs compare headings — if they differ, each must justify why theirs is better. The teacher then regroups pairs so four students must reach consensus on the single best heading, optionally culminating in group presentations or a class vote. Students finish with an individual reflection on how the heading helped them understand the reading and answer the unit's driving question.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it converts passive reading into generative processing: selecting ideas (highlighting), organizing them (heading construction), and elaborating through justification and consensus [Generative activities that require learners to select, organize, and integrate material outperform passive reception.](../claims/generative-learning-improves-retention.md) [+S]. The four-word constraint is the critical design move — it forces abstraction beyond what highlighting alone achieves, since unstructured highlighting is among the weakest studied learning techniques [Highlighting and underlining as typically practiced show low utility for learning outcomes.](../claims/highlighting-alone-low-utility.md) [-S]. Collaborative negotiation of headings adds peer explanation and error detection, which deepens comprehension beyond solitary summarizing [Collaborative writing and joint text construction improve text quality and comprehension.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared digital text (Google Docs or similar) that supports visible, concurrent highlighting
- A text at an appropriate challenge level — difficult enough that main ideas are not self-evident
- Time for paired reading, heading negotiation, cross-group consensus, and individual reflection
- A driving question or unit purpose to anchor the final reflection

#### Constraints
- Highlighting without the heading task adds little: students who highlight indiscriminately or verbatim show minimal comprehension gains [Highlighting and underlining as typically practiced show low utility for learning outcomes.](../claims/highlighting-alone-low-utility.md) [-S]
- The full routine consumes substantial instructional time; truncating the consensus and justification phases removes the elaboration that drives the effect [Generative activities that require learners to select, organize, and integrate material outperform passive reception.](../claims/generative-learning-improves-retention.md) [~M]
- Groups dominated by one strong reader can short-circuit negotiation; assigned roles or individual pre-writing of headings before discussion mitigates this
- Very dense or unfamiliar-vocabulary texts can overload working memory during the heading task; pre-teaching key vocabulary or [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the text into shorter sections helps [Chunking reduces working memory load during learning.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Analog version: printed text, highlighters, headings written on sticky notes and posted for a gallery consensus walk
- Scaffolded version: provide sentence stems for justification ("Mine is better because it captures…") for students new to academic argument
- Asynchronous version: students draft headings in shared docs; the teacher curates a heading comparison for the next synchronous discussion
- Extension: students compare their self-generated headings to the author's actual headings, analyzing what the author emphasized differently

### Target Learners
- Middle school, high school, and adult learners building reading comprehension and analysis skills with challenging disciplinary texts
- Emergent multilingual learners benefit from the explicit vocabulary-identification phase, provided headings may be drafted in multiple languages before negotiation
- Less suitable for beginning readers still decoding; the strategy assumes fluent word recognition so attention can go to meaning [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Identifying and prioritizing main ideas in expository text
- Summarization and abstraction: compressing a passage into a constrained representation
- Argumentation and justification: defending an interpretation against alternatives
- Collaborative meaning-making and metacognitive awareness of one's own reading process

### Instructions
1. Distribute the text in a shared document and have students independently highlight unfamiliar words ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)); address high-frequency blockers before proceeding
2. Pair students; they read a paragraph together and highlight key ideas, discussing why each matters ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) at pair scale)
3. Each student individually writes a four-word heading for the passage, then pairs compare and justify differences ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) in summarization)
4. Regroup pairs into foursomes; groups must reach consensus on one heading, with each member defending their version ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) through peer critique)
5. Groups present headings or the class votes; the teacher highlights how strong headings capture causal or thematic structure, not just topics
6. Students individually reflect: how did the heading change their understanding, and how does it help answer the unit's driving question?

## Examples
- A middle school science class reading a dense article on ocean acidification in Google Docs: pairs highlight evidence of cause and effect, negotiate four-word headings ("Carbon dioxide acidifies oceans"), and the class votes on headings for each section before writing lab conclusions
- An adult ESL course using the printed sticky-note variant on news articles, with multilingual heading drafts permitted in phase 3
- A high school history department adopting the routine as a recurring protocol for primary-source analysis, with headings compared against historians' own section titles
