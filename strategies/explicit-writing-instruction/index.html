Explicit Writing Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 16 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 5 review, 4 causal, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 5 of 16 report an effect size

Description¶

Explicit writing instruction teaches the components of skilled writing — planning, drafting, revising, sentence construction, and genre structure — through direct explanation, modeling, guided practice, and independent application. The instructor makes invisible expert decisions visible (e.g., thinking aloud while planning a paragraph), then supports learners as they apply the same moves to their own text. It contrasts with immersion-only approaches that assume students will absorb writing skill from reading and frequent writing alone.

Design Implications¶

Explicit writing instruction is among the most consistently supported approaches in writing research: teaching writing strategies, summarization, and sentence-combining produce measurable gains in text quality across grade levels Graham & Perin, 2007 meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on making expert reasoning observable and then fading support — modeling without guided application produces recognition without production. Because writing imposes heavy simultaneous demands on working memory (generating ideas, transcribing, monitoring), explicit instruction reduces the load by automating sub-skills and sequencing them Cognitive overload degrades learning when demands exceed working memory capacity. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear model of the target genre or skill, with the instructor's reasoning made visible (articulation)
  • Guided practice with feedback before independent writing (application)
  • Instruction sequenced from sentence-level to whole-text skills, respecting working memory limits Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Mentor texts that exemplify the target structure, ideally compared and contrasted Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Overly scripted, formulaic instruction (e.g., rigid five-paragraph templates) can produce writing that satisfies the formula but transfers poorly to authentic tasks and audiences [~M]
  • Whole-class explicit lessons pitched at one level misfit learners far above or below it; effectiveness drops when instruction ignores prior skill [~M]
  • Explicit strategy instruction without extended opportunities to write and revise shows weak maintenance; strategies must be practiced in the context of real writing purposes [~M]
  • Sentence-level drill disconnected from meaningful composing does not improve overall text quality [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Strategy instruction (SRSD): teaching self-regulated strategy development — planning strategies plus self-monitoring statements — the best-evidenced variant [+S]
  • Sentence combining: learners combine short kernel sentences into complex ones; strong effects on syntax and quality [+S]
  • Text-structure instruction: teaching genre schemas (narrative, argument, compare-contrast) with graphic organizers Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]
  • Process writing with explicit minilessons: brief direct teaching embedded in a workshop model, rather than a fully scripted sequence [~M]

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling writers and students with limited prior knowledge, who benefit most from modeled strategies and reduced search Graham & Perin, 2007 [+S]
  • Elementary and adolescent learners across content areas; effects hold in second-language writing contexts [+M]
  • Proficient writers need less explicit structure; excessive scaffolding can suppress their own planning and voice [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Genre and text-structure knowledge: knowing how a genre is organized
  • Procedural fluency: planning, drafting, and revising routines that become automatic Automaticity frees resources for higher-level processing. [+M]
  • Self-regulation: goal setting, self-monitoring, and revising one's own composing process
  • Sentence-level craft: syntax variety, cohesion, and vocabulary use

Instructions¶

  1. Set a purpose with a mentor text. Analyze an exemplar of the target genre, ideally contrasting strong and weak versions Comparing contrasting cases improves learning. [+M]
  2. Model the strategy. Think aloud while planning or drafting a short piece, verbalizing decisions ("I'll state my claim first because…") — the writing analogue of articulation in Cognitive Apprenticeship
  3. Guide practice. Co-construct a text with the class or have students complete a partially written model (application) with prompts and feedback
  4. Fade support. Move from shared writing to independent writing as competence grows, removing organizers and sentence frames
  5. Teach self-regulation. Add goal setting and self-checking so students run the strategy without the teacher — the core of SRSD [+S]
  6. Assess and reteach. Use writing samples to identify which sub-skill broke down, and reteach that component Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+M]

Related Strategies¶

  • Sentence Combining — a high-impact explicit technique for syntactic fluency
  • Process Writing Workshop — the workshop structure into which explicit minilessons are embedded
  • Retrieval Practice — spaced retrieval of taught structures supports maintenance

Examples¶

  • SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development) — the most researched explicit writing framework; e.g., POW+TREE for persuasive writing, with strong meta-analytic support (ThinkSRSD resources) [+S]
  • The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler) — explicit sentence-level instruction (because/but/so activities, sentence combining) embedded across content areas (thewritingrevolution.org)
  • Graham & Perin's Writing Next recommendations — explicit strategy instruction, summarization, and sentence combining top the effect-size rankings for adolescent writing (Carnegie Corporation report)
