# Purposeful Scheduling for Learner Connections

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Purposeful scheduling treats relationship-building as an instructional activity with dedicated time, rather than something left to chance. Instructors embed structured get-to-know activities, one-on-one conferences, and informal contact (before/after class, sitting with groups during work) into the course schedule. The information gathered is then used to personalize content and connect instruction to learners' experiences.

## Design Implications

Relationship quality between teacher and learner is consistently associated with engagement, participation, and achievement, with the strongest effects for at-risk and younger learners [Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with engagement and achievement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543073001137) [+S]. The mechanism is partly motivational: feeling known and supported satisfies learners' needs for relatedness and autonomy, which sustains intrinsic motivation [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Crucially, the strategy pays off only when gathered information feeds back into instruction — connection time that never changes teaching becomes a cost with no learning return.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Protected, scheduled time slots (openers, conferences, work-time circulation) that are not sacrificed to content coverage under pressure
- A mechanism for capturing what is learned about learners (notes, interest inventories, conference records) so it can inform later instruction
- Instructor follow-through: visibly integrating learner interests and experiences into examples, tasks, and discussions

#### Constraints
- Time spent on connection activities reduces content time; in tightly packed curricula the trade-off can outweigh relational benefits [-M]
- Superficial or performative rapport-building (icebreakers with no instructional follow-through) can read as inauthentic and does not improve engagement [~W]
- Effects of positive teacher–student relationships on achievement are weaker for older/secondary learners than for younger children, so expectations should be calibrated [~S]
- One-on-one conferences scale poorly; in large classes, unscheduled attempts collapse first under time pressure [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured conferences:** brief scheduled one-on-ones (5–10 minutes) during independent work time
- **Circulation:** sitting with groups during [collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) to learn learners while they work
- **Edge-time contact:** purposeful before/after-class conversations, effective in small classes
- **Digital equivalents:** check-in surveys, discussion-board introductions, and office hours in online courses

### Target Learners
- Learners who are disengaged or at risk, for whom teacher relationship quality shows the strongest association with engagement [Learner-centered teacher–student relationships are associated with engagement and achievement.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543073001137) [+S]
- Adult and returning learners, whose motivation rises when content connects to their real-world experience and goals [Autonomy support fosters intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Anxious or low-confidence learners, for whom a known, trusted instructor lowers the cost of participation; self-efficacy supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and motivational goals: engagement, belonging, persistence
- Prerequisite to personalization: surfacing prior knowledge and interests that enable relevant task design
- Indirect support for all content goals through increased participation and instructional relevance

### Instructions
1. **Open with structured check-ins.** Begin class with brief [check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) that surface learner state and interests; keep them short and purposeful rather than purely social.
2. **Schedule conferences during work time.** Hold short one-on-one conversations while learners work, using [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) moves (questions, listening) to learn about goals and experiences.
3. **Circulate during group work.** Sit with different groups each session during [collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md), treating group work time as dual-purpose: content support plus relationship data collection.
4. **Capture and use what you learn.** Record interests and prior experiences; feed them into examples, task contexts, and [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) prompts so learners see their input reflected in instruction.
5. **Use informal edge time deliberately.** Arrive early and stay after class with a plan for whom to talk to, rather than leaving connection to chance.

## Examples
- **Writing conferences in workshop models** (e.g., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project, https://www.readingandwritingproject.org): scheduled one-on-one conferences during independent writing are a core structural element, combining relationship-building with formative feedback.
- **First-week interest inventories in K-12 classrooms** followed by deliberately themed word problems, reading selections, and examples drawn from learner interests throughout the term.
- **Online course "connection weeks":** instructors in asynchronous courses schedule individual video or phone check-ins during week 1–2 and use discussion-board introductions to personalize later examples.
