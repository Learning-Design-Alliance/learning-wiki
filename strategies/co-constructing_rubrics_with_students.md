# Co Constructing Rubrics With Students

## Description
Co-constructing rubrics involves students in drafting, negotiating, or refining the criteria and performance-level descriptions by which their work will be judged, rather than receiving a finished rubric from the instructor. The process typically begins with students analyzing exemplars (strong and weak), naming the qualities that distinguish them, and translating those qualities into explicit criteria and descriptors. The instructor's role shifts from author to facilitator, ensuring the resulting criteria are accurate, assessable, and aligned with learning goals.

## Design Implications

Co-construction works because it makes evaluative criteria explicit and personally meaningful before students produce work, converting assessment from a black box into a transparent standard students internalize [Rubrics support learning when they make criteria transparent and are used formatively.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. The activity itself is a form of [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): students who help author criteria develop the evaluative judgment needed to monitor and improve their own work, a core component of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. Participation also builds ownership — students perceive co-developed criteria as fairer and more legitimate, which supports engagement and [intrinsic motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Exemplars of the target performance at varying quality levels, so students have concrete material from which to induce criteria ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md))
- Instructor expertise in the domain to keep student-generated criteria valid and complete — students often omit criteria they cannot yet perceive
- Time in the schedule for negotiation; the process cannot be compressed into a handout
- A plan for using the rubric formatively (self-assessment, peer feedback, revision), not just summatively

#### Constraints
- Co-construction without exemplar analysis produces vague, unmeasurable criteria ("be creative," "good effort") that degrade assessment quality [-M]
- If the instructor treats the activity as token consultation and later grades against unstated criteria, perceived fairness and trust collapse [-M]
- Novices may lack the evaluative judgment to distinguish quality dimensions; without instructor scaffolding the resulting rubric can be incomplete or misdirected [~M]
- Time cost is substantial relative to instructor-authored rubrics; for one-shot, low-stakes tasks the investment rarely pays off [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full co-construction:** students draft all criteria from exemplars; instructor refines. Highest ownership, highest time cost.
- **Criteria negotiation:** instructor supplies a draft; students critique, revise wording, and add missing criteria. Faster, retains most of the transparency benefit.
- **Descriptor co-construction:** criteria fixed by instructor; students write the performance-level descriptions, which builds understanding of quality gradients.
- **Ongoing refinement:** rubric is revisited after each assignment cycle as students' evaluative judgment develops.

### Target Learners
- Most valuable for novices who cannot yet articulate what quality looks like — the process builds the evaluative vocabulary they lack [~M]
- Effective in higher education and secondary settings where students can engage in criterion-level discussion [+M]
- Less beneficial for very young learners unless heavily scaffolded with concrete exemplars and simplified language [~W]
- Particularly useful for students with low [academic self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md), for whom transparent, self-authored standards reduce the sense that assessment is arbitrary [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: the capacity to judge the quality of one's own and others' work
- Metacognitive monitoring: using criteria to plan, self-assess, and revise ([Self-Monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md)) [+M]
- Deep understanding of task demands and quality standards in a domain
- Not well suited to objectives with fixed, non-negotiable standards (e.g., safety protocols, citation formats), where criteria should be given, not negotiated [~M]

### Instructions
1. Present exemplars at several quality levels without revealing grades; have students analyze differences ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)).
2. Facilitate nomination of quality dimensions; consolidate into a draft set of criteria, checking alignment with learning goals.
3. Co-write performance-level descriptors, pushing students toward observable, specific language ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
4. Pilot the rubric: students apply it to a sample or peer draft ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)), then revise ambiguous descriptors.
5. Use the final rubric for self-assessment and revision before submission, so it functions as a learning tool rather than only a grading tool ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).

## Examples
- **Andrade's rubric-referenced self-assessment studies** — middle and high school students co-developed writing rubrics and used them to self-assess drafts, improving essay quality and students' understanding of "good writing" (see Reddy & Andrade, 2010).
- **Writing program at the University of Hawaii (Manoa)** — composition courses engage students in deriving grading criteria from sample essays before drafting, a documented practice in Andrade's work on instructional rubrics.
- **Project-based courses using critique protocols** (e.g., EL Education / Expeditionary Learning schools) — students analyze models, generate quality criteria ("what makes a powerful infographic?"), and refine a class rubric used for peer critique and revision. See [https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org).
