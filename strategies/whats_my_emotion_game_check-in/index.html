'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Students use the EQ Feeling Chart to identify their feelings. One student acts out a chosen emotion through body language and facial expressions while the rest of the class guesses it, then the group names and briefly discusses the feeling. The activity functions as a low-stakes check-in that doubles as practice in emotion recognition and expression.

Design Implications¶

The game builds emotional granularity — the ability to distinguish and label fine-grained feelings — which underpins self-regulation and empathy in social-emotional learning (SEL) programs [~M]. Verbal labeling of emotions ("affect labeling") reliably reduces amygdala reactivity and the intensity of felt emotion [~M], supporting the claim that naming feelings helps students regain control before academic work begins. Because the emotion is conveyed non-verbally and inferred by peers, the activity also exercises theory-of-mind and perspective-taking [~W].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An EQ Feeling Chart or equivalent emotion vocabulary display with varied, graded emotion terms (not just happy/sad/angry)
  • Open space for acting out emotions and a format that lets every student participate at a comfortable level
  • A safe, supportive classroom climate; the game fails quickly in environments where students mock expressive peers
  • A brief debrief in which the felt emotion is named and, optionally, connected to a situation ("When do you feel this?")

Constraints¶

  • Students with social anxiety, autism spectrum profiles, or trauma histories may find public emotional performance threatening [-M] — forced acting-out can raise rather than reduce arousal; offer observer, card-pointing, or written roles instead
  • Guessing can collapse into stereotyped, exaggerated displays that reinforce shallow emotion categories (happy = smiling) rather than granularity [-W] — counter this by requiring increasingly subtle or mixed emotions as the game matures
  • Without a naming/debrief step, the activity is entertainment: the regulatory benefit of affect labeling depends on the emotion actually being named in words [~M]
  • Cultural display rules vary; an emotion expressed the way one student expects may read differently across cultural backgrounds [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Charades format (full acting) for confident groups; card-pointing format (point to the feeling on the chart) for reluctant or younger students
  • Guess-the-situation variant: students act out an emotion and peers guess both the emotion and a situation that evokes it, deepening the emotion-context link
  • Daily mood check-in variant: each student points to or acts out their current feeling at the start of class, converting the game into a routine check-in
  • Literacy integration: extend the chart vocabulary each week (frustrated, anxious, proud, relieved) to grow emotion word banks

Target Learners¶

  • Pre-K through upper elementary students, for whom emotion vocabulary and facial-expression recognition are still developing [~W]
  • Adolescents and college students as a warm-up check-in, provided the format is framed as optional-performance (pointing or writing rather than acting) [-M]
  • Students needing support with non-verbal communication decoding and empathy; adaptable for inclusive and special education settings

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: identifying, naming, and distinguishing feelings
  • Non-verbal communication: producing and reading facial expressions, posture, and tone
  • Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' internal states from observable cues
  • Self-regulation readiness: labeling current affect to reduce its intensity before instruction [~M]

Instructions¶

  1. Display the EQ Feeling Chart and briefly review the emotion words with the class (Advance Organizers).
  2. Invite one volunteer to choose an emotion (privately, via card) and act it out with face and body only — no words (Demonstration).
  3. Classmates guess the emotion; the actor confirms or redirects, and the class locates the word on the chart (Whole-Class Discussion).
  4. Ask the actor (or, in the check-in variant, each student) to name their feeling and, if willing, one situation that evokes it (Practice).
  5. Close with a one-minute debrief linking the labeled feeling to regulation strategies ("What helps when you feel frustrated?").

Related Strategies¶

  • Act It Out — the same embodied enactment applied to academic content rather than emotions
  • Acting-Role Play — extended role-play structures that build on the same non-verbal expression skills
  • Acceptance, Responsibility, and Sharing — a companion community-building routine that pairs well with emotional check-ins

Examples¶

  • Elementary classrooms using the Responsive Classroom approach run daily emotion check-ins at morning meeting; the charades variant is a common community-building adaptation.
  • Second Step (Committee for Children) teaches emotion-face recognition with photo cards; teachers frequently gamify these cards into a "guess my feeling" format identical to this strategy.
  • Middle school advisory programs use a mood-meter variant (e.g., the RULER Mood Meter, Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) in which students locate their current feeling on a two-axis grid — a low-exposure version of the same labeling move.
