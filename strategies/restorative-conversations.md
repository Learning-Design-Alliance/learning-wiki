# Restorative Conversations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A restorative conversation is a facilitated dialogue — often between a teacher and student, or among affected peers — structured around restorative questions: *What happened? Who was affected and how? What do you need to make things right?* It shifts the adult role from judge to facilitator, aiming to repair relationships and rebuild norms rather than simply assign consequences. It is the smallest unit of restorative practice, sitting between informal everyday check-ins and formal restorative conferences or circles.

## Design Implications

Restorative conversations operationalize [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) and self-determination principles: they give students voice and autonomy in resolving conflict, which supports intrinsic motivation and internalization of norms [Autonomy-supportive environments strengthen intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Brief empathic-discipline approaches built on dialogue rather than punishment have been shown to cut suspension rates, particularly for adolescents [Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]. The conversation only works when the relationship is intact enough for honest dialogue — it presupposes, and simultaneously builds, a sense of belonging and trust [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A facilitator trained in restorative questioning and de-escalation, who can hold neutrality rather than leading the student to a predetermined apology
- Sufficient time and privacy — a rushed hallway exchange defeats the purpose
- [Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) on the facilitator's part: reflecting, paraphrasing, and withholding judgment until the student has fully narrated events
- Follow-through: agreed repair actions must be monitored, or the process reads as performative

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when used coercively — a "restorative" conversation that is really a lecture with restorative vocabulary trains students to give scripted answers [-M]
- Requires relational capital; with a student who does not trust the facilitator, the conversation can escalate conflict [~M]
- Inappropriate as the sole response to serious harm (violence, bias incidents) without trained specialists and systemic support [-M]
- Poorly implemented programs that substitute conversation for accountability can leave victims feeling unheard and increase perceived unfairness among peers [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Affective statements** — brief "I felt… when…" expressions used in everyday interactions, the lowest-intensity form
- **Restorative check-in** — a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine using restorative questions to surface issues before they escalate
- **Restorative conference** — a formal multi-party meeting with an agreement document, used after significant harm
- **Peer mediation** — trained students facilitate conversations between peers, building facilitation skills themselves

### Target Learners
- Adolescents, who respond especially well to respectful, autonomy-supportive discipline dialogue [Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]
- Students at risk of exclusionary discipline, where punitive responses compound disengagement
- Whole classrooms when embedded as routine practice, not only as crisis response [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: perspective-taking, emotional regulation, accountability
- Community norms: co-constructing and repairing shared behavioral expectations
- Conflict-resolution and communication skills transferable beyond school

### Instructions
1. **Prepare** — ensure emotional safety and privacy; do not attempt the conversation while anyone is dysregulated.
2. **Open with the harm, not the rule** — ask "What happened?" and listen without interrupting ([Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md)).
3. **Surface impact** — ask "Who was affected, and how?" to shift focus from blame to consequence.
4. **Elicit repair** — ask "What do you need to do to make things right?" letting the student propose the repair to preserve autonomy [Autonomy-supportive environments strengthen intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].
5. **Agree and follow up** — record the agreement and check in on it; a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) a few days later signals the relationship, not the incident, was the point.

## Examples
- **[Oakland Unified School District](https://www.ousd.org/restorative-justice)** — a district-wide restorative justice program associated with reduced suspensions and improved school climate; its whole-school model layers affective statements, circles, and formal conferences.
- **Restorative questions card in advisory periods** — many schools embed the four restorative questions into daily advisory check-ins so the language is familiar before any conflict occurs.
