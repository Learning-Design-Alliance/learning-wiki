# Guided Discovery with Word Sorting

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students sort word cards by spelling pattern under teacher questioning that directs attention to critical features, inducing the rule themselves rather than receiving it stated upfront. For example, sorting *page, cage, badge, edge, bridge* reveals the –ge/–dge convention: /j/ after a short vowel is spelled –dge, after a long vowel or consonant –ge. The teacher's questions ("What do you notice about the letter before the g?") scaffold the discovery without giving the answer away.

## Design Implications

Discovery of orthographic patterns through structured comparison is more effective than rote memorization of rules because it forces active analysis of letter–sound relationships [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The sorting task reduces working memory load by externalizing words as manipulable objects, letting students compare spellings side by side [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Crucially, the guidance component is what makes it work: unguided sorting without teacher questioning or a follow-up generalization discussion produces weak learning, and the guidance should be explicit and systematic [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Curated word sets that cleanly contrast the target pattern (e.g., –ge/–dge, –ck/–k/–ke, –ve endings) with minimal distractors
- Sorting materials (word cards, columns, or digital sort boards)
- Teacher questioning that directs attention to the critical feature before the rule is named
- A closing step where students state the generalization in their own words, followed by [Practice](../elements/practice.md) applying it to new words

#### Constraints
- Unguided "pure discovery" sorting — students left to sort with no prompts or debrief — is ineffective for novices and can entrench wrong categorizations [-S]
- Patterns that are irregular or have too few exemplars (e.g., *said, one*) do not lend themselves to inductive sorting; these need direct teaching of the specific word
- Students with very weak phonemic awareness or severe cognitive deficits may not perceive the contrast the sort is built on; they need prerequisite [activation](activation.md) of the target sound first
- If the sort includes too many patterns at once, students attend to surface features (word length, initial letters) rather than the target feature [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Closed sort** (teacher-defined categories) → **open sort** (students devise their own categories) as expertise grows
- **Speed sorts** and **blind sorts** (sorting by listening without seeing the word) shift attention from visual to phonological features
- Word hunts — students find new examples in texts — extend the pattern to authentic reading
- Digital implementations (e.g., drag-and-drop sorts) allow immediate feedback and automatic record-keeping

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (typically Grades 1–4) at the within-word pattern stage of orthographic development
- Struggling spellers who memorize word lists without generalizing; sorting builds the analogical reasoning that transfers to untaught words [+M]
- English learners, who benefit from the explicit contrast of English position-based conventions (e.g., why English words rarely end in *v*, hence –ve) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Inductive rule formation: deriving position-based and convention-based spelling generalizations
- Word-reading automaticity: knowledge of orthographic patterns supports rapid word recognition [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Orthographic mapping: connecting phonemes to spellings rather than memorizing whole words

### Instructions
1. Select a target pattern with sufficient regular exemplars and prepare 10–20 word cards contrasting the pattern.
2. Model one or two sorts, thinking aloud about the feature being considered.
3. Have students sort the remaining words, asking guiding questions ("Say the word. Where do you hear /j/? What letter comes just before it?") rather than confirming or correcting directly.
4. Ask students to state the generalization in their own words; refine it as a class.
5. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on new, untaught words to test transfer, and provide corrective [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) on misapplied patterns.
6. Revisit the sort across days (open sorts, speed sorts, word hunts) for spaced review [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]

## Examples
- **Words Their Way** (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton, & Johnston) — the published word-study curriculum built entirely around developmentally sequenced picture and word sorts with teacher-guided generalization discussions. [https://www.pearson.com/words-their-way](https://www.pearson.com)
- **–ge/–dge sort** — students sort *age, huge, stage* against *judge, fudge, lodge*, discover the short-vowel condition, then state: "English words don't end in j, and –dge follows a short vowel."
- **LETRS** (Moats) — professional development that trains teachers to run guided-discovery sorts for position-based conventions like –ck/–k/–ke and doubled consonants. [https://www.lexialearning.com/letrs](https://www.lexialearning.com/letrs)
