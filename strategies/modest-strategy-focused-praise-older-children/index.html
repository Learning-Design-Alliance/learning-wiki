Phrase praise modestly and focus on strategies rather than effort to prevent ability-denigrating inferences¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 review), q3 · 0 of 4 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that, for older children (from about age 10) who understand that ability and effort are compensatory, teachers "phrase their praise modestly and focus the praise on children's strategies rather than their effort". Inflated praise, even without mentioning effort, implicitly signals that a student had to work hard because of low ability, leading peers to see the student as less smart. Focusing praise on strategies (e.g., "You found a good way to do this") is offered as a way to avoid these ability-denigrating inferences.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Applies to children from about age 10, who have acquired the understanding that ability and effort can be compensatory

Constraints¶

  • The article notes effort praise can be beneficial for young children's motivation by communicating that effort is a path to improving ability; the recommendation targets older children only

Target Learners¶

  • Primary school children ages 10-13

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Maintaining perceptions of academic ability while giving positive feedback

Affordances¶

  • Attribution Theory Inflated Praise Inequality

Claims¶

  • Children perceive students receiving praise as more hardworking than equally performing students receiving less praise [+M]
  • Teachers give low-SES students more inflated praise than equally performing high-SES students, though not more total praise [+M]
  • Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure [+M]
