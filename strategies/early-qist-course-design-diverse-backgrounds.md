# Design early QIST courses for diverse backgrounds: emphasize applications and career pathways, develop confidence with competence, and do not teach them as physics courses

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q1` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article's educators recommend structuring introductory interdisciplinary QIST courses so that students from engineering and computer science, not only physics, succeed. One educator insisted "They shouldn't be punished for not being a physicist", and advised emphasizing applications in quantum computing, communication and sensing that solve societal problems, discussing career pathways, and offering enrichment projects or special modules for deeper dives. Confidence development should accompany competence from early on.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Courses should mainly focus on learners with diverse background preparations and interests, with enrichment options via projects or special modules
#### Constraints
- The article notes many ways of structuring interdisciplinary QIST courses exist; these are educator suggestions, not evaluated designs

### Target Learners
- high school students
- first- and second-year college students in science and engineering

### Target Learning Goals
- foundational quantum information science and technology concepts
- confidence and competence in QIST
- awareness of QIST career pathways
