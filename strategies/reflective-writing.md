# Reflective Writing

## Description
Reflective writing asks learners to write about their own learning — what they did, what confused them, what strategies worked, and what they would do differently — rather than only about subject-matter content. It is carried out through prompts, journals, learning logs, or post-task debriefs, typically with structure (e.g., Gibbs' cycle, "What? So what? Now what?") that moves learners beyond description toward analysis and planning.

## Design Implications

Reflective writing works by forcing elaboration and metacognitive monitoring: translating experience into language exposes gaps in understanding and makes strategy use visible to the learner [Writing-to-learn tasks produce modest but consistent gains in content learning.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543074001029) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on structure and follow-through — unguided "write about your feelings" prompts produce description without analysis, and reflection without application rarely changes behavior [Self-regulated learning strategy instruction improves achievement, with effects strongest when strategies are explicitly taught and practiced.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2014.02.002) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Focused prompts that direct attention to process and reasoning, not just content summary ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame what to attend to)
- A structural frame (Gibbs' cycle, What/So what/Now what, or a claim-evidence-plan format) that pushes past description
- Instructor modeling of what good reflection looks like, ideally via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or annotated exemplars
- A loop back into action: reflections should inform subsequent tasks or [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)

#### Constraints
- Pure description or emotional venting without analysis yields little learning benefit [-M] — prompts must demand explanation and planning
- Reflection becomes ritualistic and shallow when graded on completion alone or repeated with identical prompts [~M]
- Novices often lack the vocabulary to diagnose their own understanding; without scaffolding they misjudge what they learned [-W]
- High-frequency mandatory journaling can reduce motivation when learners see no purpose or audience [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-task** (activation of prior knowledge and planning) vs. **post-task** (consolidation and error analysis) placement
- **Dialogue journals** with instructor or peer responses add an audience and accountability [+W]
- **Exam wrappers** — short post-assessment reflections on preparation and error patterns — are a low-cost, high-frequency variant
- **Multimodal reflection** (audio, video, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) one's own work) for learners who struggle with extended writing

### Target Learners
- Adolescents through adults; effects of self-regulation strategy instruction are strongest for older learners with some metacognitive foundation [Self-regulated learning strategy instruction improves achievement, with effects strongest when strategies are explicitly taught and practiced.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2014.02.002) [+S]
- Learners in complex, multi-stage domains (clinical practice, design, teaching practica) where experience must be converted into transferable principles
- Struggling learners benefit when reflection is heavily scaffolded; unsupported open journals favor already-skilled writers [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition and self-regulation: monitoring comprehension, evaluating strategy effectiveness
- Consolidation of learning: elaborative processing of recent experience
- Professional judgment: connecting theory to practice in clinical, field, and work-integrated settings
- Transfer: extracting generalizable principles from specific cases ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) pair naturally with reflective debriefs)

### Instructions
1. **Frame the purpose.** Explain why reflection is being asked for and how it will be used; autonomy over topics or format supports engagement [Autonomy-supportive contexts increase intrinsic motivation and engagement.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
2. **Model a reflection.** Demonstrate an expert-level reflection on a sample experience, making the move from description to analysis visible ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. **Provide a structured prompt.** Use a cycle such as Gibbs (describe → analyze → evaluate → plan) or What/So what/Now what; require at least one "why" and one "next time" component.
4. **Set a manageable cadence.** Brief, frequent reflections (5–10 minutes, tied to specific tasks) outperform long, infrequent journals [~W].
5. **Respond or pair.** Provide brief instructor feedback, peer dialogue, or dialogue-journal exchange so reflection has an audience.
6. **Close the loop.** Require learners to reference prior reflections in planning the next task, and revisit patterns at mid-term and end of term ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).

## Examples
- **Exam wrappers** (e.g., as implemented in Carnegie Mellon's Eberly Center guidance, [https://www.cmu.edu/teaching](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching)) — students answer 4–5 questions about exam preparation and errors, then the instructor opens the next class with the aggregated patterns.
- **Medical education reflective portfolios** — structured case reflections after clinical encounters, following Gibbs' (1988) cycle, used in programs such as those accredited under ACGME requirements.
- **Design studio crits with written debriefs** — architecture and engineering programs pairing verbal critique with a short written "what I will change" memo before the next iteration.
