# Hands-On Laboratory Procedures

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Hands-on laboratory procedures place learners in direct contact with the materials, instruments, and phenomena of a discipline — pipetting, wiring circuits, dissecting, titrating — under structured protocols. The strategy pairs physical execution with observation and interpretation, so that procedural actions and conceptual reasoning develop together rather than as separate tracks.

## Design Implications

Manipulating real equipment grounds abstract concepts in perceptual-motor experience and exposes learners to the messiness of real data — error, variability, unexpected results — that simulations and readings smooth away [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. But unstructured "cookbook" labs, where learners follow steps without understanding why, produce procedural fluency without conceptual gain; the design task is to connect the doing to the thinking [Cognitive overload degrades learning when demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) or worked protocol before independent execution, so learners have a model of correct technique
- Pre-lab preparation that activates relevant concepts and orients attention to the critical observations ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
- [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) during execution — circulating instructors who correct technique and ask probing questions in the moment
- Post-lab [Practice](../elements/practice.md) in interpreting results, error analysis, and reporting, not just completing the procedure

#### Constraints
- Step-by-step recipe protocols without prediction or interpretation tasks yield poor conceptual transfer [-M] — learners execute correctly and learn little
- High equipment complexity combined with new content overloads working memory; novices should not learn a new instrument and a new concept simultaneously [Cognitive overload degrades learning when demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Physical labs are resource-intensive (equipment, safety supervision, scheduling) and scale poorly; virtual simulations can substitute for some goals but not for developing fine motor technique [~W]
- Learners with strong prior knowledge may gain little from routine procedural repetition [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured inquiry labs**: learners predict outcomes before running the procedure, then reconcile predictions with data
- **Faded protocols**: early labs give full step-by-step instructions; later labs give only the goal and available materials, requiring learners to design the procedure
- **Hybrid simulation-first**: virtual environments (e.g., PhET, Labster) let learners rehearse the procedure cheaply before touching real equipment
- **Station rotation**: multiple short procedures at stations rather than one long lab, managing attention and equipment constraints

### Target Learners
- Novices in a scientific or technical discipline who need to connect textbook concepts to physical phenomena [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]
- Learners preparing for professional practice where technique itself is assessed (nursing, chemistry, engineering)
- Less valuable for learners who already have procedural fluency and need conceptual depth or experimental design practice instead

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural skill: correct, safe, reproducible execution of techniques
- Conceptual understanding: linking observable phenomena to underlying theory
- Scientific practices: measurement, error analysis, data interpretation, documentation

### Instructions
1. Assign pre-lab reading and prediction questions to activate relevant knowledge ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
2. Demonstrate the procedure with narrated reasoning, highlighting critical steps and common errors ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
3. Have learners execute the procedure in pairs or small groups while instructors circulate and coach ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
4. Require in-lab checkpoints — learners must justify a reading or decision before proceeding ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
5. Close with structured analysis: compare results to predictions, identify sources of error, and connect findings to theory ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md))

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — labs can be framed as authentic cases where the procedure answers a driving question
- [Simulation](../elements/simulation.md) — a lower-cost rehearsal path before or alongside physical labs

## Examples
- **PhET Interactive Simulations** (https://phet.colorado.edu) — research-based virtual labs used before or in place of physical equipment for circuit-building and chemistry procedures
- **Labster** (https://www.labster.com) — immersive virtual lab simulations with embedded theory checkpoints, used in university biology and chemistry courses
- **Chemistry guided-inquiry labs (POGIL-style)** — students design and execute procedures to answer a question rather than following a recipe, then report and defend results
