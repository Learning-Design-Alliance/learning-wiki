# Spelling Instruction Through Encoding

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Encoding instruction teaches spelling by requiring learners to *produce* written words — typically from dictation — rather than merely reading, copying, or recognizing them. Learners segment a spoken word into phonemes, map each phoneme to plausible graphemes, and write the word, then compare their attempt against the correct form. This production requirement converts spelling practice into a form of retrieval practice with feedback, and builds the orthographic representations that support both accurate spelling and automatic word recognition [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M].

## Design Implications

Encoding is more effective than exposure-only methods because producing a spelling forces active reconstruction of the word's orthographic form, whereas copying can be performed with minimal attention to letter order [~S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows explicit, systematic spelling instruction improves both spelling and, to a lesser degree, writing quality and word reading [+S]. Instruction should follow a phonics-like scope and sequence: teach sound-letter correspondences first, then patterns, then irregular high-frequency words, so that learners can generate spellings rather than memorize strings of letters.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A systematic sequence of taught patterns (phoneme–grapheme correspondences, morphological patterns, common irregular words)
- Dictation or word-building routines that require production, not recognition ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with immediate feedback)
- Learners who have at least emerging phonemic awareness; encoding presupposes the ability to segment spoken words
- Corrective feedback that shows the target form and explains the pattern, not just marks errors

#### Constraints
- Ineffective if learners lack phonemic segmentation skills — they cannot map sounds to letters they cannot isolate [-S]
- Rote memorization of whole-word lists without pattern analysis produces poor retention and minimal transfer to writing [-M]
- Copying words or unscrambling letters allows learners to complete tasks without encoding letter sequences [-M]
- English's deep orthography means sound-first strategies fail for many irregular words; instruction must explicitly teach which words are irregular and why [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dictation (traditional):** teacher dictates words/sentences; learners write and self-correct against a model
- **Simultaneous oral spelling:** learner spells aloud while writing, then verifies — used in structured literacy programs
- **Word building / letter tiles:** learners construct words from a fixed letter set, forcing attention to sequence
- **Spelling-focused writing:** learners apply taught patterns in authentic composition with teacher feedback, supporting transfer
- **Coverage of morphology:** for older learners, encoding shifts toward morphemes (e.g., *sign* → *signal*) rather than phoneme-grapheme mapping

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and spellers in K–2, for whom phoneme–grapheme mapping is the primary mechanism [+S]
- Struggling spellers and students with dyslexia, who need more explicit and cumulative encoding practice than typically developing spellers [+M]
- Older learners benefit more from morphological and etymological encoding than from phonics-style drill [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Accurate spelling in independent writing
- Orthographic mapping: building the stored word representations that underpin automatic reading [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Transfer of phonics knowledge from reading to writing

### Instructions
1. **Teach the pattern explicitly** — present the target sound-letter correspondence or spelling pattern with example words ([Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md)).
2. **Segment and map** — learners say the word, segment its phonemes, and identify the grapheme for each.
3. **Encode from dictation** — learners write the word without a visible model, then check against the correct form and self-correct ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback).
4. **Distribute review** — retest previously taught words across sessions rather than massing practice within one ([Spaced practice improves retention.](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]).
5. **Apply in writing** — hold learners accountable for taught patterns in composition so encoding transfers to authentic writing.

## Examples
- **Orton-Gillingham-based programs** (e.g., [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com), [SPIRE](https://www.schoolspecialty.com/spire)) — use simultaneous oral spelling and dictation as core routines for struggling readers.
- **[UFLI Foundations](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations/)** — free explicit phonics lessons each ending with dictation of words and sentences, with a systematic scope and sequence.
- **[Read Write Inc.](https://www.ruthmiskin.com)** (UK) — "Hold a sentence" and dictation routines requiring learners to encode taught graphemes from memory.
- **Spelling through morphemes** (e.g., [Word Study / Words Their Way](https://www.pearson.com) sorts plus writing) — upper-elementary encoding organized around spelling patterns rather than arbitrary lists.
