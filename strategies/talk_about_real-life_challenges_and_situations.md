# Talk About Real-Life Challenges and Situations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Facilitating structured discussions in which learners describe real challenges they are facing, articulate how those situations make them feel, and reframe them constructively — focusing on what went right, what can be learned, and how the experience transfers to future situations. The facilitator guides with open questions rather than advising, positioning learners' own experiences as the curriculum.

## Design Implications

Discussing authentic challenges connects learning to learners' lived experience, increasing relevance and engagement [~M], and verbalizing difficulties supports self-regulation by forcing learners to monitor and articulate their coping strategies [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Positive reframing of setbacks builds coping resources: learners who can find meaning or benefit in difficulty show greater persistence and resilience [~M], and self-efficacy developed through narrating successful coping predicts academic persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Peer sharing also normalizes struggle, reducing the isolation that often accompanies difficulty.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A psychologically safe environment with established norms for confidentiality and respectful listening
- A skilled facilitator who can guide discussion without lecturing, redirecting negativity, or forcing disclosure
- Open, reflective question prompts (e.g., "What did you do right?", "What can you learn from that?", "How could that help you next time?")
- Adequate time — meaningful sharing cannot be compressed into a few minutes at the end of a session

#### Constraints
- Learners uncomfortable with emotional disclosure may disengage or experience the discussion as intrusive [~M] — participation should be invitational, with alternatives such as written reflection
- Poorly facilitated sessions can devolve into unstructured venting that reinforces negative framing rather than reframing it [-W]
- Group sharing of personal difficulties requires facilitator skill in managing distress; without it, the activity can harm rather than help vulnerable learners [-W]
- Effectiveness drops when discussions are generic or hypothetical rather than anchored to challenges learners actually face [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class circle discussions, small-group [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) formats, or one-on-one [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) conversations
- Written variants (journals, discussion boards) for learners reluctant to speak publicly
- Regular brief [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) at the start of sessions versus scheduled deeper reflection sessions
- Structured protocols (e.g., "situation → feeling → what worked → what I'd do differently") to keep sharing constructive

### Target Learners
- Learners navigating transitions, setbacks, or high-stakes situations (new programs, exam periods, work placements)
- Adolescents and adults developing self-regulation and coping skills [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Learners with low academic self-efficacy who benefit from hearing peers' successful coping narratives [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as a mandatory activity for learners who process privately; offer written or opt-out alternatives

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional processing and constructive reframing of difficulty
- Self-regulated learning: articulating strategies, monitoring progress, and planning adjustments
- Social-emotional learning: empathy, perspective-taking, and help-seeking within a supportive community

### Instructions
1. Establish discussion norms (confidentiality, no fixing/advising unless invited, right to pass) before any sharing begins.
2. Open with a brief [Check-In](../principles/check-ins.md) to surface current challenges and set the tone.
3. Invite learners to describe a real situation, using [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or small-group [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md) formats.
4. Guide reflection with reframing questions — "What went right?", "What can you learn from that?", "How could that help you in the future?" — keeping focus on learning rather than dwelling on the negative.
5. Support learners in [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of the strategy or insight they take away, converting the discussion into a transferable plan.
6. Close by connecting insights forward: how will the learner apply what they identified next time?

## Related Strategies
- [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — the brief, regular form of this strategy; opens sessions by surfacing challenges before they escalate
- [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — the one-on-one form, where the same reflective questioning is applied individually

## Examples
- **Advisory / circle-time discussions** — Secondary advisory programs open each session with a check-in question ("What's one thing that went wrong this week and one thing you did about it?"), followed by peer sharing with reframing prompts.
- **Medical and clinical education debriefs** — Post-case debriefing sessions (e.g., in simulation-based training) ask trainees to discuss what was difficult, how they felt, and what they would do differently, converting emotional experience into clinical learning.
- **Resilience curricula** — Programs such as the Penn Resilience Program (Pennsylvania) use structured group discussion of real setbacks to teach cognitive reframing skills to students.
