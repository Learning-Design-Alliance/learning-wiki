# Circle Time Emotions

## Description
End each week with a group circle where students discuss different topics and share their feelings. Students can use Circle Time to discuss conflict, repair fractured relationships, and connect with their emotions. Start with "What makes you happy?" and add other emotions as students become familiar with the format.

## Design Implications

Structured social-emotional discussion builds emotional vocabulary and peer relationships, and well-implemented social-emotional learning programs produce measurable gains in both behavior and academic achievement [Social-emotional learning programs improve achievement and prosocial behavior.](../claims/social-emotional-learning-programs-improve-achievement.md) [+S]. The circle format matters: equal participation, a talking object, and clear norms create the psychological safety that makes self-disclosure productive rather than risky [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Dedicated, predictable time (weekly works well); consistency itself signals safety
- Facilitation skills: open questions, neutral framing, and the ability to redirect without shaming
- Clear participation norms, including the right to pass — mandatory disclosure suppresses honest sharing [~M]
- A talking object or turn-taking structure so all voices are heard

#### Constraints
- Poorly facilitated circles can escalate conflict or expose students to peer judgment; facilitators must de-escalate rather than adjudicate in the moment [-M]
- Students who have experienced trauma may find public emotional disclosure distressing; opt-out and private alternatives are essential [-M]
- Gains fade without reinforcement during the week — a weekly circle alone, with no [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) or follow-up, has weak effects [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Topic circles**: a single prompt ("What makes you happy?") with brief turns; best for early grades and new groups
- **Repair circles**: used after conflict, drawing on restorative-practice questioning (What happened? Who was affected? What will make it right?)
- **Appreciation circles**: students name something a peer did well, shifting from disclosure to affirmation
- **Sentence stems**: "I felt ___ when ___ because ___" scaffold emotional vocabulary for younger or less verbal students

### Target Learners
- Early grade students, who are still building emotion vocabulary and benefit from modeled expression [Dialogic discussion improves expressive language development.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]
- Classrooms with relational conflict or low cohesion, where structured peer sharing builds intergroup attitudes [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as-is for adolescents without adaptation — older students often respond better to smaller circles or written-first formats [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: naming and differentiating feelings
- Community building: establishing trust and belonging that support all other learning [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Conflict resolution: practicing perspective-taking and repair
- Speaking and listening: turn-taking, active listening, responding to peers

### Instructions
1. Establish norms: sit in a circle, introduce a talking object, and state the right to pass.
2. Open with a low-stakes warm-up prompt ("What makes you happy?") so students learn the format before tackling harder emotions.
3. Pose the week's topic and invite responses around the circle; model emotional vocabulary in your own brief share ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md)).
4. Use [Active Listening](../strategies/active-listening.md) norms — listeners attend without interrupting and may reflect back what they heard.
5. When conflict surfaces, shift to restorative questions and defer resolution to a follow-up if needed rather than resolving mid-circle.
6. Close with an appreciation or one-word check-out, and carry themes into the week through individual [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md).
