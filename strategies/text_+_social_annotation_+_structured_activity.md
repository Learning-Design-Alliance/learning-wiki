# Text + Social Annotation + Structured Activity

## Description
Students read a shared digital text in a social annotation platform (e.g., Perusall, Hypothesis), posting margin comments, questions, and replies that are visible to classmates. A structured activity — a writing task, problem set, or discussion deliverable — then requires learners to apply or extend ideas surfaced in the reading and annotation exchange. The three components are deliberately sequenced: annotation makes reading interactive and social, and the activity converts that engagement into durable learning.

## Design Implications

The strategy works because it raises the interactive quality of engagement with text: passive reading becomes peer dialogue anchored to specific passages, and the follow-on activity prevents annotation from remaining a shallow social gesture [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture-only formats.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Social annotation is most effective when prompts require elaboration, questioning, or connection-making rather than summary, consistent with the ICAP prediction that interactive engagement outperforms constructive, which outperforms passive [Chi & Wylie, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037417) [+M]. The structured activity supplies the accountability and application layer that annotation alone lacks.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared text in a platform that supports threaded, in-context comments visible to the class (e.g., [Perusall](https://www.perusall.com), [Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is))
- Annotation prompts tied to specific cognitive moves — question a claim, connect to prior reading, apply a concept — not "highlight something interesting"
- A graded or otherwise accountable follow-on activity that references the annotation discussion
- Instructor presence in the margins, especially early, to model annotation quality and sustain the conversation [Garrison et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1096-7516(00)00016-6) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Unstructured annotation ("great point!", isolated highlights) produces social presence without cognitive engagement and little measurable learning gain [Novak et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2011.09.002) [~M]
- Annotation quality degrades when reading load is high; long or dense texts lead to skimming and perfunctory comments [-M]
- If the follow-on activity does not build on the annotations, students perceive annotation as busywork and disengage [-M]
- Asynchronous margin discussion can fragment into parallel monologues unless replies are required or threads are curated [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-reading annotation**: students annotate before class; the instructor synthesizes margin themes into the subsequent lesson
- **Annotation-as-drafting**: students draft essay claims or problem solutions in the margins, then develop them in the structured activity
- **Role-based annotation**: rotating roles (questioner, connector, skeptic) distribute cognitive moves across the group
- **Peer instruction loop**: instructor selects high-quality annotations anonymously for whole-class discussion before the activity

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and graduate students in reading-intensive courses, where annotation distributes comprehension monitoring across peers [Novak et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iheduc.2011.09.002) [+M]
- Students hesitant to speak in whole-class discussion, who participate more readily in text-anchored asynchronous exchange [~M]
- Less effective for novices lacking the background knowledge to generate substantive comments; these learners benefit from instructor-seeded annotations or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) first [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and critical reading: interrogating claims, evidence, and argument structure in source texts
- Conceptual application: transferring ideas from text to problems, cases, or writing
- Discourse and community building: establishing academic dialogue norms in online and blended courses [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md)

### Instructions
1. Select a text of manageable length and load it into a social annotation platform; seed it with 2–3 instructor annotations modeling the desired cognitive moves.
2. Assign annotation prompts requiring specific moves — question, connect, apply — and require at least one reply to a peer, raising engagement from passive to interactive [Chi & Wylie, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037417) [+M].
3. Monitor the margin discussion and intervene to deepen threads that stall at agreement or summary.
4. Launch the structured activity — a short analytic write-up, problem set, or [Case Studies](../principles/case-studiescase-based-learning.md) response — that explicitly requires students to use ideas from the annotations.
5. Close the loop with feedback that addresses both the activity product and the quality of the annotation exchange [Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

## Related Strategies
- Structured peer review of drafts extends the same social-feedback logic from margins to full documents
- Jigsaw reading distributes text segments across groups before collaborative synthesis, complementing whole-class annotation

## Examples
- **[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** in a large introductory psychology course: students annotate an assigned chapter, Perusall's scoring rewards substantive engagement, and the weekly quiz draws on concepts flagged in the margins.
- **[Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is)** in a graduate seminar: students annotate a journal article before class; the instructor opens the session by projecting contested passages from the margin thread, then assigns a position paper as the structured activity.
- Annotated close reading in literature courses, where students mark textual evidence in shared editions and then write interpretive arguments grounded in the class's margin debate.
