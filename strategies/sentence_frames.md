# Sentence Frames

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Sentence frames are partially completed sentence templates ("I disagree with ___ because ___") that learners use to structure their spoken or written responses. They make the *discourse moves* of a discipline — agreeing, disagreeing with evidence, building on a peer's idea, hedging, citing — explicit and available to learners who have ideas but not yet the language to express them. Frames are typically introduced, modeled, practiced in low-stakes exchanges, and faded as learners internalize the structures.

## Design Implications

Sentence frames are a language scaffold in the Vygotskian sense: they lower the linguistic demands of a task so cognitive effort can go to the content of reasoning rather than to formulating syntax under pressure. This is most valuable when the target discourse genre is unfamiliar — academic argument, scientific explanation, respectful disagreement — because learners cannot be expected to infer its language patterns from exposure alone [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Frames work best when paired with modeling of their use and with opportunities for structured peer exchange, not as standalone handouts [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Frames matched to the actual discourse moves of the task (agree, counter, elaborate, cite evidence), not generic sentence starters
- Teacher modeling of frame use before independent or peer use ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) or live demonstration)
- A structured exchange context where frames are genuinely needed — e.g., [Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or structured academic controversy
- A plan for fading: shifting from full frames to word banks to independent production as fluency grows

#### Constraints
- Overuse without fading produces formulaic, mechanical responses and can suppress authentic voice [-M] — learners may fill frames without genuine engagement when the frame no longer matches their thinking
- Frames that exceed learners' current syntactic competence shift the load back to language production and defeat the purpose [~M]
- In online or asynchronous settings, frames can feel artificial and invite perfunctory compliance unless community norms and modeling establish their purpose
- Less useful for learners already fluent in the target register, for whom frames add redundancy rather than support [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Speaking frames** for structured discussion protocols (e.g., Socratic seminars, debate prep) versus **writing frames** for constructed responses
- **Word banks + frames** for multilingual learners at earlier proficiency levels; open frames ("One thing that complicates this is…") for advanced learners
- **Digital adaptations**: discussion-board templates, chat starters in collaborative platforms, or comment stems embedded in peer-review tools
- Frames can be co-constructed with students from an anchor text, which builds awareness of the discourse genre itself

### Target Learners
- Multilingual learners and students acquiring academic language, where the gap between ideas and expression is largest [+M]
- Novices to a discipline's discourse conventions (e.g., students learning to argue from evidence in science) [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Reluctant participants in discussion, for whom the frame lowers the social risk of entering conversation
- Less beneficial for learners already fluent in the register, who may find frames constraining [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic language development: producing discipline-appropriate syntax and vocabulary [Accessible vocabulary and syntax supports comprehension.](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+M]
- Dialogic skill: disagreeing respectfully, building on others' ideas, citing evidence
- Argumentation quality: structuring claims with reasons and evidence [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Participation equity: distributing talk beyond confident volunteers

### Instructions
1. Identify the target discourse moves for the task (e.g., "disagree with evidence," "extend a peer's idea") and draft one frame per move.
2. Model each frame in use, thinking aloud about when and why it fits ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Have pairs or small groups rehearse using the frames in a low-stakes exchange ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)).
4. Run the main activity — discussion, debate, or collaborative writing — with frames visible as a reference, not a script.
5. Fade support: remove frames or reduce them to word banks as learners internalize the structures, and celebrate student-generated alternatives.

## Examples
- **Academic conversations (Zwiers & Crawford, 2011)** — content-area teachers use prompt, respond, and follow-up frames ("What do you mean by ___?" "I agree/disagree with ___ because ___") to build sustained peer dialogue across subjects.
- **Structured academic controversy in online forums** — digital citizenship lessons supply disagreement frames ("I see it differently because ___," "Can you say more about ___?") so that online debate stays evidence-based and respectful rather than adversarial.
- **Science explanation frames** — claim-evidence-reasoning stems ("The data show ___, which supports my claim that ___ because ___") scaffold scientific writing in curricula such as those from the [Claim-Evidence-Reasoning framework](https://k12science.org/).
