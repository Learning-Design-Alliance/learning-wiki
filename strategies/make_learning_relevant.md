# Make Learning Relevant

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Making learning relevant means deliberately connecting instructional content to learners' interests, goals, communities, and prior experience so that the material carries personal value rather than relying solely on external incentives. In practice this ranges from contextualizing problems in familiar scenarios, to giving students authentic audiences and purposes for their work, to offering meaningful choices that build ownership of the learning process.

## Design Implications

Relevance works primarily through task value: when learners perceive that content is useful or connected to their identity and goals, they invest more effort and persist longer [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Relevance is most powerful when it is *structural* — authentic tasks, real audiences, learner choice — rather than cosmetic dressing (e.g., swapping names in word problems), which produces shallow engagement gains that fade quickly [~W]. Giving students voice and choice also supports autonomy, a core driver of intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine knowledge of students' interests, communities, and aspirations (surveys, conferences, community input) — not assumptions
- Learning goals that can be authentically connected to those interests without distorting the content
- Mechanisms for student input and choice (topic selection, product format, classroom decisions)
- Willingness to revise examples, contexts, and materials over time

#### Constraints
- Superficial relevance ("edutainment" framing) can distract from the target content and add extraneous processing load [~M]
- Individualizing relevance for every student is unsustainable at scale; design for shared contexts and offer choice points instead
- Relevance claims that students do not believe (forced "when will I use this" justifications) can backfire and reduce value perception [-W]
- Designing around "learning styles" (matching instruction to visual/auditory/kinesthetic preferences) is not supported by evidence [X] — tailor to interests, prior knowledge, and goals instead

#### Implementation Variability
- **Contextualization**: anchor content in real-world scenarios ([Anchored Instruction](../patterns/anchored-instruction.md), [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md))
- **Authentic purpose**: connect work to real audiences and consequences ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))
- **Choice architecture**: structured options within fixed learning goals (choice of topic, product, or partner)
- **Community connection**: draw problems and data from students' own communities ([Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md))

### Target Learners
- Disengaged or low-task-value learners who see no connection between schoolwork and their goals [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and adults, who respond strongly to utility value and autonomy; young children benefit more from novelty and play than explicit relevance arguments [~W]
- Learners whose self-efficacy is fragile — relevant, attainable contexts support persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement outcomes: sustained effort, participation, persistence
- Transfer: knowledge anchored in authentic contexts is more likely to be applied outside the classroom [~M]
- Conceptual understanding when relevance is used to activate prior knowledge rather than merely decorate content [Activation improves comprehension.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [~W]

### Instructions
1. Learn about your students — survey interests, conduct conferences, and note community and cultural touchpoints before designing units.
2. Identify the natural connection points between the learning goal and those interests; discard connections that distort the content.
3. [Activate prior knowledge](../elements/activation.md) by opening with a problem or scenario drawn from students' experience.
4. Anchor instruction in an authentic task or context — a [case study](../elements/case-studies.md), community problem, or real product for a real audience.
5. Build in structured [choice](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md) points (topic, format, pathway) so students own decisions without losing goal alignment.
6. Debrief the relevance explicitly — help students articulate how the skills transfer beyond the classroom, since relevance perceived by the teacher does not automatically become relevance perceived by the student [~W].

## Examples
- **[Anchored Instruction (Jasper Woodbury Project)](https://peabody.vanderbilt.edu/research/legacy_projects.php)** — Vanderbilt's Jasper Adventure series anchors mathematics problem solving in extended video narratives, giving abstract math a compelling story context.
- **[Case-Based Learning (Harvard Business School method)](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md)** — every concept is taught through a real organizational dilemma, so relevance is built into the curriculum rather than added on.
- **Classroom example** — A statistics teacher lets students choose a dataset from their own lives (music streaming, sports, local transit) for the same regression unit, preserving identical learning goals while varying the context.
