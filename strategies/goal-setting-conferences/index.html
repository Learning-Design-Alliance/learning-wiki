Goal Setting Conferences¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (5 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

A goal setting conference is a structured one-on-one (or small-group) conversation in which a teacher and learner jointly identify a specific, challenging-but-attainable learning goal, agree on success criteria and strategies, and schedule a follow-up to review progress. The conference converts abstract expectations ("do better in reading") into concrete commitments ("read two chapter books this month and track summaries in my journal"), and positions the learner — not the teacher — as the owner of the goal.

Design Implications¶

Goal setting conferences operationalize two well-supported findings: specific and challenging goals produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory [+S], and proximal subgoals raise self-efficacy and persistence, especially for young or struggling learners Bandura & Schunk, 1981 [+S]. The conference format matters because goals set with learners, rather than for them, support autonomy and increase commitment Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]. The conference is only the visible tip of the cycle — its value depends on the monitoring and feedback that follow Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Evidence about the learner's current performance (work samples, data, Self-Assessment) so goals are grounded in a realistic baseline
  • A predictable conference routine with a short protocol: review progress → set/revise goal → name strategies → record the goal
  • A visible record of the goal (goal card, tracker, portfolio) that the learner can refer to between conferences
  • Scheduled follow-up — a conference without a check-in cycle is a conversation, not an intervention (Check-In)
  • A predictable conference routine (e.g., 3–5 minutes per learner on a rotating schedule) so conferences are frequent enough to sustain momentum
  • Visible evidence of current work (portfolio, draft, data dashboard) to ground the conversation in specifics
  • A simple recording system — the goal, the strategy, and the follow-up date — that both parties can revisit
  • Teacher skill in coaching moves: open probes, restating, and withholding premature advice

Constraints¶

  • Goals imposed by the teacher without learner input reduce ownership and can undermine intrinsic motivation Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [-M]
  • Goals that are outcome-only ("get an A") rather than process-oriented ("use the revision checklist") can encourage performance-avoidance and surface strategies [~M]
  • Conference time is expensive; without a tight protocol, conferences consume instructional time disproportionate to their effect [-W]
  • Public goal displays can backfire for learners motivated to avoid looking incompetent; keep goals private or learner-controlled [~W]
  • Conferences consume substantial instructional time; at scale they must be brief and rotational or they crowd out practice
  • Goals set without a monitoring mechanism are quickly abandoned; without a scheduled follow-up, self-monitoring decays Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Learners with very low prior knowledge may set goals misaligned with what would actually help; the teacher must supply enough structure to prevent unproductive goal choice [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reading/writing workshop conferences (e.g., Lucy Calkins' Units of Study): 3–5 minute conferring sessions during independent work, research-decide-teach structure
  • Data meetings: longer conferences built around progress data, common in Competency-Based Learning schools
  • Advisory/ILP conferences: periodic goal reviews against a personal learning plan, as in Summit Public Schools' personalized learning model
  • Peer conferences: learners run the same protocol with a partner once the routine is established
  • Writing conferences (e.g., Lucy Calkins' workshop model): conferring over a draft with a teaching point and a goal for the next revision
  • Data conferences: reviewing progress data (reading levels, mastery dashboards) before setting the next goal
  • Peer conferences: structured peer-to-peer goal talk using a protocol, with the teacher circulating
  • Micro-conferences: 60–90 second check-ins during work time, trading depth for frequency

Target Learners¶

  • Developing self-regulators in upper elementary through adulthood; goal setting is a trainable component of self-regulated learning Zimmerman, 2002 [+M]
  • Struggling or disengaged learners, for whom proximal subgoals build self-efficacy Bandura & Schunk, 1981 [+S]
  • Very young learners need heavy scaffolding — teacher-narrowed goal menus rather than open-ended goal choice [~W]
  • Developing self-regulated learners who can articulate preferences but need help forming strategy-linked goals Self-regulated learning can be taught and improves achievement. [+S]
  • Struggling learners who benefit from proximal, achievable commitments that rebuild efficacy Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Less useful for learners who already self-regulate effectively and may find routine conferences redundant [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning
  • Metacognition: articulating what "good work" looks like and where one stands relative to it
  • Motivation and ownership: building commitment through learner-set, proximal goals
  • Skill growth in any domain where progress can be made visible (reading level, writing traits, math fluency)
  • Metacognition: articulating what one knows and what to do next
  • Motivation and persistence: building commitment through ownership of proximal goals

Instructions¶

  1. Gather baseline evidence — recent work samples, assessment data, or Self-Assessment — before the conference so the conversation starts from shared facts.
  2. Open with the learner's self-report: "How do you think it's going? What's working?" (Check-In)
  3. Co-construct one specific, proximal goal with visible success criteria; offer a constrained menu of options rather than open choice when learners are novice goal-setters (Achievable Micro-Goals)
  4. Name 1–2 concrete strategies the learner will use, and record the goal and strategy in a tracker the learner owns (Action Planning)
  5. Schedule brief follow-up Check-Ins to monitor progress and deliver Feedback tied to the goal criteria.
  6. At the next conference, review progress against the goal, celebrate or revise, and set the next goal — closing the cycle.

Examples¶

  • Reading workshop conferring (Teachers College Reading & Writing Project, https://readingandwritingproject.org): teachers hold 3–5 minute goal conferences during independent reading, each ending with a recorded goal the student pursues until the next conference.
  • Summit Public Schools (https://summitps.org): students set weekly goals in personalized learning plans and review them in mentor conferences, pairing goal setting with progress data.
  • Responsive Classroom (https://www.responsiveclassroom.org): teacher-student goal setting conversations embedded in advisory and academic choice routines.
  • Reading/writing workshop conferring (Teachers College Reading & Writing Project, https://www.readingandwritingproject.org) — teachers hold brief 1:1 conferences during independent work, naming one teaching point and one goal per conference.
  • Data-driven goal conferences in competency-based schools (e.g., Summit Public Schools' mentoring model, https://summitps.org) — weekly mentor–student meetings review progress dashboards and set the next week's goals.
  • Physical education goal talks — students set personal fitness targets with the teacher before a unit and revisit them at mid-unit check-ins.
