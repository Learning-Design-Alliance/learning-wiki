Self-Monitoring of Behavior¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Self-monitoring is a strategy in which learners assess and record their own behavior — whether they were on-task, followed rules, or met a performance target — at regular intervals. Two common variants are self-monitoring of attention (SMA: "Was I paying attention?") and self-monitoring of performance (SMP: "Did I complete and correctly answer the work?"). The act of observation itself tends to change the behavior being observed, and the practice builds the foundational self-regulation skill of evaluating one's own actions against a standard.

Design Implications¶

Self-monitoring works primarily through reactivity: simply attending to and recording a behavior shifts its frequency, making it a low-cost, active intervention that also offloads monitoring work from the instructor Self-regulation interventions, including self-monitoring, produce moderate positive effects on behavior and academic outcomes. [+M]. Effectiveness depends on precise operational definitions of the target behavior, a simple recording mechanism, and a reliable cue (timer, tone, checklist prompt) that signals when to self-assess. Accuracy matters: training learners to match their ratings to instructor or video-standard ratings improves both accuracy and behavioral outcomes, and accuracy training can be faded once behavior improves.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An operationally defined target behavior with observable indicators (e.g., "eyes on the speaker and pencil moving" rather than "paying attention")
  • A self-monitoring form, tally sheet, or digital equivalent matched to the learner's age and developmental level
  • A cueing mechanism — timer, audio tones, or scheduled check-ins — that prompts self-assessment at consistent intervals
  • Initial training: modeling the rating process, practice ratings against a standard, and feedback on accuracy (Practice, Assessment)
  • A baseline measure of the behavior for later comparison
  • An operational definition of the target behavior, stated in observable terms
  • A recording form or tally sheet matched to the learner's age and skill level
  • A cueing method for signaling monitoring intervals (timer, audio tones, vibrating watch)
  • A goal or criterion so the learner can evaluate the record, not just collect it

Constraints¶

  • Learners often inflate self-ratings, especially when ratings carry consequences; positive-accuracy training (reinforcing accurate or overestimated ratings) mitigates this but does not eliminate it [-M]
  • Effectiveness drops when the target behavior is vaguely defined or when cueing intervals are too long for the learner's attention span [-M]
  • Requires sustained instructor support during initial implementation; abandoning the scaffold before the behavior is established leads to regression [~M]
  • Less useful for behaviors that are covert, low-frequency, or that the learner is not motivated to change [-W]
  • Learners often rate their behavior more favorably than observers do; inflated ratings reduce the intervention's effect until accuracy training is added Self-monitoring accuracy training improves outcomes. [~M]

  • Reactive effects can be temporary — behavior may revert once monitoring stops unless paired with self-reinforcement or goal-setting Self-monitoring effects fade without maintenance supports. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • SMA vs. SMP: attention monitoring suits engagement problems; performance monitoring suits work-completion and accuracy problems
  • Cue type: audio tones, vibrating timers, or self-initiated checkpoints at task transitions
  • Fading: move from instructor-cued to self-cued monitoring, then to intermittent monitoring, as behavior stabilizes
  • Combined forms: pairing self-monitoring with self-evaluation (comparing self-ratings to a standard) or goal-setting strengthens effects
  • SMA vs. SMP: monitoring attention/on-task behavior vs. monitoring academic output (problems completed, words written, accuracy)
  • Cueing: teacher-signaled, audio-signaled, or self-initiated (learner decides when to check in) — self-initiated monitoring transfers better but requires more training
  • Matching vs. counting: learner marks whether the behavior occurred at the cue vs. tallies every occurrence
  • Self-evaluation and self-reinforcement: adding a comparison of the record against a goal, and learner-administered rewards, strengthens maintenance

Target Learners¶

  • Students with attention difficulties, including ADHD, for whom self-regulation interventions show consistent benefits [+M]
  • K–12 learners needing support with on-task behavior, work completion, or social interactions
  • Adult and higher-education learners building study habits and independent work routines
  • Younger learners need simpler forms, shorter intervals, and picture-based rating scales
  • Students with attention difficulties or ADHD, where self-monitoring is among the best-supported self-regulation interventions Self-regulation interventions improve academic performance for students with ADHD. [+S]
  • K–12 learners needing support with task completion, compliance with routines, or social behavior
  • Adult learners building study habits or professional self-regulation (e.g., tracking practice time, safety behaviors)
  • Less appropriate as a standalone strategy for learners who cannot yet accurately discriminate the target behavior — discrimination training must come first

Instructions¶

  1. Define the target behavior in observable terms and collect baseline data.
  2. Choose the monitoring focus — attention (SMA) or performance (SMP) — and design a simple recording form.
  3. Train the learner: model a rating, have them practice rating against a standard, and give feedback on accuracy.
  4. Set the cueing interval (start short, e.g., every 5–10 minutes) and begin monitoring with the Practice routine in place.
  5. Review the record with the learner, compare self-ratings with instructor observations, and adjust intervals or definitions.
  6. Fade cues and instructor verification as accuracy and the target behavior stabilize.

Related Strategies¶

  • Self-Evaluation — adds a comparison against a standard or goal, strengthening the reactive effect
  • Goal-Setting — gives the monitored behavior a target to move toward
  • Check-Ins — scheduled instructor–learner conversations that review self-monitoring records (Check-In)
  • Token Economies / Reinforcement Systems — self-monitoring records can feed reinforcement contingencies, though rewards for accuracy must be handled carefully
  • Teach students to monitor and reflect on their own behavior

Examples¶

  • SMA with audio cueing: a teacher gives a student who loses focus during whole-class instruction a tape or app that plays tones at random intervals; at each tone the student marks "Was I paying attention?" on a card. On-task behavior typically rises within the first weeks of implementation.
  • SMP for work completion: a student tallies correctly completed math problems per work period and graphs the daily total against a self-set goal, making progress visible.
  • Digital study tools: apps such as Forest or RescueTime operationalize self-monitoring for adult learners by logging focused time and prompting reflection on session goals.
  • SMA with audio cueing: A teacher uses a tape of random tones; at each tone a student with attention difficulties marks "yes/no — was I attending?" on a card, then compares the total to a daily goal.
  • SMP for written work: A student tallies correctly completed math problems per work period and graphs the count, targeting an improvement over their own baseline rather than peer comparison.
  • ClassDojo-style self-report: Digital behavior-tracking tools let students self-log points against class expectations, with the teacher spot-checking agreement.
