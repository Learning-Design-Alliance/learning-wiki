Misconception Repair¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal), q3–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Misconception repair begins by eliciting learners' existing intuitive ideas — often flawed but robust mental models built from everyday experience — and then deliberately creating cognitive conflict between those ideas and evidence or expert explanations. Learners must recognize the inadequacy of their prior conception before an alternative, scientifically accurate model can be integrated. The strategy treats misconceptions not as gaps to be filled but as coherent frameworks that must be actively restructured.

Design Implications¶

Misconceptions are resistant to simple transmission of correct information; learners frequently assimilate new content into their existing faulty framework rather than replacing it [~M]. Repair therefore requires two phases: activation of the flawed idea, followed by structured confrontation. Refutation-style instruction — explicitly stating the misconception, marking it as wrong, and explaining why — outperforms expository instruction that simply presents the correct version [+M]. Conceptual change is motivated when learners experience disequilibrium between prediction and observation Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A documented inventory of common misconceptions for the topic (e.g., physics education research on force and motion)
  • An elicitation task that makes the flawed idea visible before instruction (Activating Prior Knowledge, prediction tasks, or diagnostic multiple-choice items with distractors keyed to known misconceptions)
  • Evidence or demonstrations that directly contradict the elicited idea, not merely restate the correct one
  • Time and support for learners to articulate and compare old and new models (Class Discussion, Analogies)

Constraints¶

  • Presenting correct information without first eliciting the misconception typically fails; learners reinterpret it to fit their existing model [-M]
  • Cognitive conflict alone is insufficient — learners who cannot construct an intelligible, plausible alternative revert to the original conception [-M]
  • Overly strong conflict without support can cause learners to dismiss the evidence rather than the misconception [-W]
  • Less effective when the target "misconception" is actually a fragmentary or inconsistent belief rather than a coherent framework; fragmented knowledge responds better to Cognitive Load Reduction approaches than to confrontation [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Refutation texts: written passages that name the misconception, refute it, and present the canonical explanation
  • Predict–Observe–Explain: learners predict an outcome, observe a discrepant event, and reconcile the difference
  • Bridging analogies: a sequence of intermediate cases connecting the intuitive idea to the target concept
  • Peer discussion: comparing conflicting predictions in small groups before instructor resolution

Target Learners¶

  • Learners with well-formed but inaccurate intuitive models (common in physics, biology, statistics, and programming)
  • Intermediate learners who have enough prior knowledge to hold a coherent misconception — complete novices may lack a framework to repair [~M]
  • Learners who report high confidence in their wrong ideas benefit most from explicit refutation, since confidence blocks passive correction [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding and restructuring, especially where everyday intuition conflicts with the discipline
  • Discrimination between superficially similar correct and incorrect explanations
  • Epistemic habits: treating one's own ideas as testable rather than fixed

Instructions¶

  1. Elicit: Use a prediction task or diagnostic item whose distractors map to known misconceptions (Activating Prior Knowledge).
  2. Confront: Present a discrepant event, demonstration, or dataset that contradicts the elicited idea, creating cognitive conflict Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M].
  3. Refute explicitly: Name the misconception, state why it fails, and mark the boundary between it and the correct model (Cognitive Conflict).
  4. Replace: Offer an intelligible and plausible alternative, often via Analogies or Advance Organizers.
  5. Consolidate: Require learners to apply the repaired model to new cases and re-answer the original elicitation item (Application, Practice).

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the elicitation phase that makes misconceptions visible before repair
  • Refutation Texts — a written-instruction variant of the same mechanism
  • Predict-Observe-Explain — a structured elicitation-confrontation cycle for demonstrations

Examples¶

  • Physics education research curricula such as Tutorials in Introductory Physics (University of Washington) and RealTime Physics use pre-tutorial predictions, discrepant experiments, and guided reconciliation to repair force-and-motion misconceptions. https://www.phys.washington.edu/groups/peg/
  • Diagnostic question banks such as Diagnostic Questions and concept inventories (e.g., the Force Concept Inventory) key distractors to documented misconceptions, letting instructors target repair to the specific ideas a class holds. https://diagnosticquestions.com
  • Statistics instruction: confronting the gambler's fallacy by having learners predict and then observe actual sequences of coin flips before introducing independence formally.
