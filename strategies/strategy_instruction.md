# Strategy Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Strategy instruction teaches learners how to learn effectively by applying principles, rules, or multi-step processes to solve problems or accomplish learning tasks. Examples include phonics rules, comprehension-monitoring routines, procedures for decoding multi-syllabic words, using context clues to define unknown words, and test-taking strategies. Instructors model their thought processes — demonstrating when and how to use the strategy — then prompt and cue learners as they apply it, gradually releasing responsibility until learners deploy the strategy independently.

## Design Implications

Strategy instruction works because it converts tacit expert procedures into explicit, teachable steps, reducing the unguided search that overwhelms novices [chunking-reduces-working-memory-load]. Its signature feature is the pairing of explicit teaching with modeling: learners must see *when* and *why* a strategy applies, not just its steps, or they acquire a procedure without the conditional knowledge needed to use it [contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning] [+M]. Effects are strongest when instruction includes modeling, guided practice, and feedback on strategy use — not merely telling learners about a strategy [Dignath & Büttner meta-analysis] [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructors knowledgeable about a range of learning strategies and the conditions under which each applies
- Allocated time for [Modeling](../elements/think-aloud.md), guided practice, and independent application ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Explicit "when to use it" teaching — conditional knowledge about task types that call for the strategy
- Prompts, cue cards, or checklists that support use until the strategy becomes [Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md)

#### Constraints
- Introducing too many strategies at once overloads learners and produces shallow, non-durable use [cognitive-overload-degrades-learning] [-M]
- Strategies taught as rigid scripts without conditional knowledge fail to generalize to new tasks [~S] — learners apply them mechanically or not at all
- Effects shrink for well-prepared learners who already use effective strategies spontaneously; strategy instruction can add little beyond existing repertoires [~M]
- Benefits fade if instruction stops at awareness ("here's a strategy") without extended practice to automaticity [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Embedded vs. standalone:** strategies taught within content-area lessons (e.g., reading comprehension in science) versus in dedicated study-skills sessions; embedded approaches show stronger transfer [~S]
- **Reciprocal formats:** learners take turns leading strategy application in [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) groups, as in reciprocal teaching
- **Self-regulated variants:** pair strategy steps with self-monitoring prompts (goal setting, self-evaluation), extending instruction into [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) training
- **Intensity:** from brief single-strategy lessons to multi-week programs with [Fading](../elements/fading.md) of prompts over time

### Target Learners
- Novices and struggling learners who lack effective default approaches, including learners with learning disabilities [~S]
- Adult basic-skills learners building academic study repertoires
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already possess and select appropriate strategies [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency in domain tasks (decoding, problem solving, writing planning)
- Comprehension monitoring and metacognitive control
- Transfer: equipping learners with portable tools for independent learning [~S]

### Instructions
1. **Assess current strategy use** — identify what learners already do and where gaps lie; avoid teaching what is already in place
2. **Name and explain the strategy** — describe its steps and, critically, when and why to use it ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame how the strategy fits the task)
3. **Model it** — the instructor performs the task while verbalizing decisions and monitoring via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)
4. **Guide practice** — learners apply the strategy with prompts, cue cards, or [Scaffolding](scaffolding.md); instructor supplies corrective [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) on strategy use, not just task outcomes
5. **Fade support** — remove prompts as use becomes reliable ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)), then require learners to select among taught strategies for new tasks to build conditional knowledge
6. **Assess transfer** — check application in new contexts and learners' articulation of when and how to use each strategy

## Related Strategies
- Reciprocal Teaching — a canonical strategy-instruction program for reading comprehension that cycles modeling, guided group practice, and independent use
- Self-Questioning — a specific comprehension-monitoring strategy frequently taught through this approach
- Spaced Practice — a learning strategy whose instruction benefits from the same modeling-and-fading sequence

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown, 1984) — seventh graders with poor comprehension learned four strategies (summarizing, questioning, clarifying, predicting) through instructor modeling then student-led group practice; gains of roughly two standard deviations on comprehension assessments
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)** (Graham & Harris) — writing instruction in which teachers model planning strategies (e.g., POW + TREE for persuasive essays) with self-talk and goal setting; one of the most strongly supported writing interventions
- **Context-clue demonstration** — instructor reads an unfamiliar-word sentence aloud, verbalizing how surrounding words and word parts narrow the meaning, then learners practice the routine on new sentences with a cue card
