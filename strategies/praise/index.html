Praise¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Praise is positive evaluative feedback following a learner's behavior, effort, or outcome. It is one of the most common teacher moves, but research shows it is not uniformly beneficial: praise directed at the person ("You're so smart") undermines persistence after setbacks, while praise directed at process and strategy ("You found a good way to check your work") supports motivation and achievement Mueller & Dweck, 1998 [+S]. Praise also functions as an operant reinforcer, strengthening the behavior it follows Behaviorism [+M].

Design Implications¶

Praise is a form of Feedback — plain text — and inherits its core requirement: it must convey information about the task or the learner's strategy, not just approval Hattie & Timperley, 2007 [+S]. Generic or inflated praise ("Good job!", "Amazing!") carries little information and can signal low expectations, particularly to struggling learners.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Specificity: identify what was done well, linking praise to observable actions or strategies
  • Credibility: praise must be earned and proportionate; learners discount praise that appears indiscriminate Brophy, 1981 [+M]
  • Attribution to controllable causes: effort, strategy use, and persistence rather than fixed ability
  • Sincerity and low-key delivery; effusive praise reads as manipulation or low expectations

Constraints¶

  • Person-praise ("You're brilliant") creates fragile self-concepts: after failure, praised children choose easier tasks, persist less, and lie about scores more often Mueller & Dweck, 1998 [-S]
  • Inflated praise to low-ability students ("This is incredibly beautiful work!") implicitly communicates low standards and leads them to avoid challenge [-M]
  • Overuse dilutes effectiveness — praise that accompanies most responses loses informational and reinforcing value Brophy, 1981 [-M]
  • Praise for effort alone, without strategy guidance, can teach learners to persist ineffectively rather than to change approach [~M]
  • Public praise can backfire for learners sensitive to social evaluation or peer standing [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Process praise targets strategies, effort, and choices ("Your revision addressed the counterargument directly")
  • Product praise targets the quality of the outcome against clear criteria
  • Private vs. public: private praise sustains behavior without social costs; public praise works best for behaviors with peer-modeling value
  • Nonverbal praise (smiles, nods, proximity) substitutes in early-childhood and low-verbal contexts
  • Self-praise routines: teaching learners to evaluate their own work against criteria shifts the locus from teacher approval to internal standards

Target Learners¶

  • Young children and novices, who are especially sensitive to ability attributions Mueller & Dweck, 1998 [+S]
  • Learners with low prior achievement, who benefit from credible, specific recognition of genuine progress — but are most harmed by inflated praise [-M]
  • Older and high-achieving learners respond less to praise and more to informational feedback; excessive praise can feel patronizing [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Motivation and persistence: sustaining engagement through difficulty
  • Self-regulation: reinforcing strategy use and monitoring behaviors
  • Classroom behavior: shaping conduct through contingent reinforcement Behaviorism [+M]
  • Not a substitute for informational feedback on the quality of academic work Hattie & Timperley, 2007 [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Observe the specific behavior, strategy, or product before responding — praise only what you can name.
  2. Attribute success to controllable causes (effort, strategy, revision), not fixed traits, to support a growth orientation and Autonomy [+M].
  3. Pair praise with next-step information: "Your diagram shows the cycle clearly — now label the feedback loop."
  4. Keep delivery low-key, sincere, and proportionate; reserve public praise for behaviors peers should model.
  5. Fade praise as competence grows, shifting learners toward self-evaluation against criteria.

Related Strategies¶

  • Action-Oriented Feedback — the informational counterpart; praise should open the door to it
  • Growth Mindset Framing — process praise is the classroom-level enactment of mindset theory
  • Check-Ins — relationship-building that makes praise credible

Examples¶

  • Responsive Classroom (https://www.responsiveschools.org) trains teachers in "reinforcing language" — specific, descriptive acknowledgment of student behavior rather than generic approval.
  • Teacher-student interaction coding (CLASS framework) (https://teachstone.com) distinguishes quality of feedback from generic praise and shows the former predicts achievement gains.
  • A mathematics teacher replaces "Great job!" with "You checked your solution by substituting back — that's what mathematicians do," reinforcing the strategy rather than the person.
