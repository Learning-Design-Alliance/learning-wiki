Low Stakes Testing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

Low stakes testing is the practice of administering frequent, short assessments — quizzes, clicker questions, exit tickets, practice tests — that carry little or no consequence for grades. The act of retrieving information from memory strengthens retention more than restudying, a phenomenon known as the testing effect Retrieval practice produces durable learning beyond restudying. [+S]. Because consequences are low, the assessments function primarily as learning events and feedback opportunities rather than evaluation.

Design Implications¶

Testing is not merely measurement: retrieval itself is a powerful learning event, and low stakes conditions let learners benefit from it without the motivational and anxiety costs of graded assessment Retrieval practice produces durable learning beyond restudying. [+S]. Effectiveness depends on learners actually attempting retrieval — open-book or immediately-answered formats reduce the benefit — and on timely feedback following each attempt.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Questions that require genuine retrieval (short answer, free recall) rather than recognition or rereading
  • Low or zero grade consequences, clearly communicated to learners
  • Feedback delivered promptly after the attempt, so errors are corrected before they consolidate
  • Sufficient frequency and spacing — a single pre-exam practice test captures little of the benefit

Constraints¶

  • Benefits shrink sharply when questions are answerable without retrieval (open notes, peer answering before individual response) [~M]
  • Repeated testing on the same items can produce rote item-specific learning rather than flexible understanding if question formats never vary [~M]
  • Poorly aligned quiz items can encourage surface memorization of isolated facts at the expense of integration [~M]
  • If stakes creep upward, anxiety and grade-orientation displace the learning function [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • In-class: clicker questions, think-pair-share polls, minute papers, exit tickets
  • Online: auto-graded quizzes in an LMS (e.g., Canvas quizzes, Mobius), spaced retrieval apps like Anki
  • Pre-class: reading quizzes that incentivize preparation and enable just-in-time teaching
  • Cumulative quizzing: each quiz includes items from earlier units, converting quizzing into spaced review

Target Learners¶

  • Learners of all ability levels benefit from retrieval practice, though lower-performing learners gain the most because testing exposes gaps they would not notice while rereading Retrieval practice produces durable learning beyond restudying. [+S]
  • Learners prone to illusions of knowing — fluency from rereading is mistaken for mastery; quizzing calibrates their self-assessment
  • Anxious learners benefit specifically from the low-stakes framing, which removes evaluative threat while preserving the retrieval benefit

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Retention of factual and conceptual knowledge over the long term
  • Metacognitive calibration: accurate judgment of what is and is not yet known
  • Transfer preparation: cumulative, varied quizzing supports flexible application better than blocked review

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the core knowledge and concepts learners must retain; write short retrieval questions at varied cognitive levels.
  2. Schedule frequent brief quizzes (weekly or per-unit), explicitly framed as practice, not evaluation.
  3. Require individual retrieval before any collaboration or note consultation.
  4. Provide immediate feedback on errors, and briefly reteach items the majority missed.
  5. Make quizzes cumulative and vary question formats to promote flexible, spaced retrieval.
  6. Pair with Spaced Practice and Feedback; use results to adjust instruction (Assessment for Learning).

Related Strategies¶

  • Spaced Practice — distributing quizzes over time multiplies the retention benefit
  • Feedback — the corrective half of the quizzing cycle; testing without feedback can entrench errors
  • Formative Assessment — the broader practice of using assessment evidence to adapt teaching
  • Mastery Learning — low stakes quizzes can serve as the mastery checkpoints
  • Increase the use of feedback and correctives through mastery learning procedures

Examples¶

  • Retrieval Practice (Agarwal & Bain) — classroom guides and research summaries for implementing low stakes quizzing across grade levels.
  • Two-stage cooperative exams (e.g., as used at the University of British Columbia; see Carl Wieman Science Education Initiative) — students take a quiz individually, then retake it in groups; the individual stage is low stakes retrieval, the group stage adds discussion.
  • Anki / spaced flashcards in medical education — programs like Firecracker schedule daily low stakes retrieval questions across the curriculum.
