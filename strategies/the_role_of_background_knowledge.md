# The Role of Background Knowledge

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Background knowledge is the pre-existing store of facts, experiences, and vocabulary that learners bring to a new topic, and it largely determines what new instruction can accomplish. Learners may have relevant experiences that just need to be drawn out; otherwise, students may lack the needed knowledge entirely, and instruction must build it before new content can stick. Practical moves include identifying connections to students' experiences, modifying linguistic complexity, rephrasing problems, and using manipulatives purposefully — e.g., red cubes as "hot" (positive) and blue cubes as "cold" (negative) numbers when teaching integers. In one documented case, a teacher struggling to teach grid coordinates to three Vietnamese ESL students nearly gave up until one student realized, "Oh, I know — same same you drive a boat!" — he had navigated by stars while fleeing Vietnam, and explained the concept to the others in Vietnamese with gestures and drawings. The teacher reflected: "I had failed to activate their prior knowledge, and in the process had almost given up."

## Design Implications

Comprehension and learning depend far more on prior knowledge than on general ability: readers with low reading skill but high topic knowledge often outperform skilled readers with low topic knowledge [Prior knowledge is a stronger predictor of comprehension than general ability.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Because new information is encoded by connecting to what is already known, instruction that fails to surface or supply relevant knowledge leaves learners memorizing disconnected surface features [Advance organizers improve learning.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Where knowledge is genuinely absent, activation alone is insufficient — the missing knowledge must be taught, often through [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) or concrete experiences.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Diagnostic awareness of what each learner actually knows — through pre-assessment, discussion, or [Activation](../elements/activation.md) activities — not assumptions based on grade level or language background
- Instructional materials that connect new content to familiar experiences, or supply the missing knowledge explicitly ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- Willingness to treat learners (especially ELLs and students from non-dominant cultures) as holding valuable knowledge that may not match school formats

#### Constraints
- Inaccurate or fragmented prior knowledge actively interferes with new learning; misconceptions must be confronted, not just activated [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [~M]
- Hard to accommodate each student's background individually in whole-class settings; activation activities that assume shared experience can exclude learners whose experiences differ
- Learners with low prior knowledge are also more vulnerable to overload, so simply adding content does not help without [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]
- Activation prompts that are generic ("think about what you know about X") show weaker effects than prompts tied to the specific target content [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Activation** for existing knowledge: quick-writes, discussion, [Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md)
- **Building** missing knowledge: field experiences, manipulatives, video, read-alouds, vocabulary pre-teaching with [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)
- **Bridging**: rephrasing problems to reduce linguistic load while preserving the mathematical demand (e.g., for ELLs, crossing out unnecessary vocabulary)
- **Cultural bridging**: inviting students to explain concepts in their home language or through community practices, as in the navigation example above

### Target Learners
- English language learners, whose conceptual knowledge often exceeds what English-only instruction reveals
- Novices in a domain, who lack the schemas experts use to organize new information [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- Students from non-dominant backgrounds whose experiential knowledge is routinely overlooked by standard curricula
- Advanced learners need this less; over-activation of knowledge they already have wastes time [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: connecting new concepts to existing mental models
- Transfer: analogical mapping from familiar to unfamiliar contexts [Analogical reasoning improves transfer.](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+M]
- Vocabulary and discourse development in content areas (especially for ELLs)

### Instructions
1. Diagnose what learners know about the topic before instruction — pre-assessment, quick-write, or discussion ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
2. Surface relevant experiences deliberately: ask for specific, content-linked memories or applications rather than generic recall ([Recall Prior Knowledge](../elements/recall-prior-knowledge.md))
3. Where knowledge is missing, build it with concrete experience — manipulatives, visuals, video, or read-alouds — before abstract instruction
4. Bridge familiar to new with analogy or metaphor ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md)), checking that the mapped features actually align
5. Reduce linguistic load without reducing conceptual demand: rephrase problems, cross out unnecessary vocabulary, pre-teach key terms ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
6. Confront predictable misconceptions directly rather than hoping new instruction will overwrite them

## Examples
- **Negative-number cubes**: red cubes as "hot" (positive) and blue cubes as "cold" (negative) numbers give learners a concrete experiential anchor for abstract integer operations.
- **The navigation anecdote**: a student's experience navigating by stars made grid coordinates comprehensible to peers in his home language — knowledge the teacher's visuals, games, and manipulatives had failed to reach.
- **[Word Generation](https://wordgeneration.org)** (SERP Institute) — middle-school curriculum that builds academic vocabulary and background knowledge through discussion of real-world controversies, designed to close knowledge gaps across classrooms.
- **[Core Knowledge Language Arts](https://www.coreknowledge.org/curriculum/language-arts-curriculum/)** — sequenced read-alouds in early grades explicitly designed to build the domain knowledge that later comprehension depends on.
