# Collaborative Learning

## Description
Collaborative learning organizes instruction so that small groups of learners work interdependently toward a shared goal, constructing knowledge through explanation, negotiation, and mutual critique rather than receiving it individually. It differs from mere group seating: the task must require genuine joint work, and individual accountability must be preserved.

## Design Implications

Collaboration improves outcomes when it is structured — with assigned roles, individual accountability, and tasks that genuinely require multiple perspectives — and when it is not, effects shrink or reverse [Structured cooperative learning outperforms individualistic and competitive structures.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The mechanism is largely elaborative: explaining to peers, being questioned, and resolving disagreement force learners to articulate and reorganize their understanding [Collaborative writing improves text quality through negotiation of meaning.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]. Group composition and task design matter more than group presence; poorly designed group work produces social loafing and division of labor without learning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task with a genuine joint product or decision that cannot be completed efficiently alone
- Positive interdependence plus individual accountability (e.g., roles, individual assessment, randomized reporting)
- Group size of roughly 2–5; larger groups invite free-riding
- Norms and training for productive talk ([Active Listening](active-listening.md), turn-taking, [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) norms)

#### Constraints
- Unstructured group work degrades into social loafing and status hierarchies; effects on achievement are unreliable without accountability structures [-S]
- Novices collaborating on complex new material can overload working memory and reinforce errors — group discussion of poorly understood content can entrench misconceptions [Unguided or minimally guided approaches are less effective for novices than explicit guidance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [~S]
- Groups dominated by one member suppress the elaboration benefits for others; unequal participation is the most common failure mode [-M]
- High-stakes individual grading layered on top of group work can convert collaboration into competition [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- [Jigsaw](../strategies/jigsaw.md) — each member masters a piece and teaches it, enforcing interdependence
- Peer instruction — individual answer, group discussion, revote (Mazur's method)
- [Collaborative writing](../strategies/collaborative-writing.md) — joint text production with negotiated revision
- Think-pair-share — low-structure entry point for large classes

### Target Learners
- Learners with some prior knowledge, who can contribute to and benefit from peer explanation [Collaboration benefits are strongest when group members have complementary partial knowledge.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [~M]
- Anxious or low-status learners benefit from structured low-stakes participation before whole-class discussion
- Less effective for complete novices on complex material, who need [Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md) first [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and conceptual change through [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) and [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- Transfer and application of knowledge to novel problems
- Communication, perspective-taking, and teamwork as goals in themselves
- Less suited to rapid acquisition of procedural basics, where [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback is more efficient

### Instructions
1. Set a task with a single joint product and clear success criteria ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
2. Form groups of 3–4 with deliberately mixed ability; assign rotating roles
3. Brief the group on talk norms and the [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine for equal airtime
4. Monitor groups, probing with questions rather than answering ([Ask Experts](../principles/ask-experts.md) reversed — the instructor asks, the group answers)
5. Hold individuals accountable via random reporter selection or individual exit tickets
6. Debrief both the content and the collaboration itself ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

## Examples
- **Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard)** — students answer individually, discuss in small groups, then revote; widely adopted in physics education ([https://blog.iclicker.com/peer-instruction/](https://blog.iclicker.com/peer-instruction/))
- **Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson)** — originally designed to reduce intergroup tension in desegregated Texas classrooms ([https://www.jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org))
- **PBL (Problem-Based Learning, Maastricht University)** — small tutorial groups drive inquiry through authentic problems ([https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl](https://www.maastrichtuniversity.nl))
