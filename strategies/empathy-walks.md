# Empathy Walks

## Description
An empathy walk asks learners to move through an environment, task, or scenario as if they were another person — a user with a disability, a new immigrant, a struggling customer, a character in a text — attending to barriers, emotions, and decisions that person would encounter. The embodied experience is followed by structured reflection that converts the experience into articulated insight about the other person's perspective.

## Design Implications

Empathy walks operationalize perspective-taking, which reliably improves intergroup attitudes and reduces stereotyped reasoning when the target's perspective is vividly imagined rather than abstractly described [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]. The physical or simulated traversal matters because it generates first-person affective data — frustration, confusion, exclusion — that lecture or reading about the same situation does not. The reflection debrief is where learning consolidates: without it, the experience remains an anecdote rather than a transferable frame.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-specified persona or real person whose situation learners will inhabit, with enough detail to guide attention
- A route, scenario, or simulation that surfaces authentic barriers and decision points, not a staged tour
- A structured debrief ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) or written reflection) that asks learners to name what they noticed, felt, and would now do differently
- Facilitator framing that distinguishes *approximating* another's experience from *fully knowing* it

#### Constraints
- Perspective-taking can backfire when learners are prompted to imagine *themselves* in the target's situation rather than the target's own experience — this increases self-focused stereotyping rather than empathy [~M]
- Brief simulated experiences can produce overconfidence ("now I understand disability") that dismisses the depth and variability of others' lived experience; facilitators must explicitly bound the claim
- Effects on attitudes tend to decay without reinforcement or contact; a single walk is not durable [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [~M]
- Poorly chosen personas can reinforce deficit framing (e.g., experiencing disability only as loss) rather than structural analysis

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical walks**: navigating campus or city in a wheelchair, with limited vision, or while carrying a child — common in universal design and service-design education
- **Service-design / UX variants**: journey-mapping walks where learners trace a customer's path through a service, noting friction points (e.g., IDEO.org's Human-Centered Design field guides)
- **Narrative variants**: walking in a literary or historical figure's footsteps through a text or site, connecting to [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)
- **Clinical variants**: patient-experience walks in medical and nursing education, tracing a patient's path from parking to discharge

### Target Learners
- Professional learners whose work serves diverse populations (designers, clinicians, teachers, public servants) [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Learners who hold unexamined assumptions about a group they will serve; embodied disconfirmation is more memorable than being told
- Less effective for learners who already have deep familiarity with the target population, for whom the simulation adds little new information

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective and attitudinal goals: reducing stereotyping, building intergroup empathy [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Professional identity formation: internalizing the service user's perspective as a design or care criterion
- Situational awareness: noticing environmental and procedural barriers that are invisible from the provider's position

### Instructions
1. **Define the persona and purpose.** Specify whose perspective learners will take and what the walk should reveal; ground the persona in real accounts or data rather than stereotype.
2. **Design the route.** Choose a path or scenario with authentic friction points; decide what learners carry, wear, or are denied.
3. **Brief learners.** Frame the walk as *approximation, not expertise*; give observation prompts (what slows you down, what excludes you, what would you ask for).
4. **Walk.** Learners move through the scenario individually or in pairs, recording observations in situ.
5. **Debrief.** Use a structured [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) and written reflection: What did you notice? What surprised you? What will you change in your own work? Connect observations to structural causes, not just personal feelings.
6. **Follow through.** Have learners apply the insight to a concrete artifact — a redesign, a care plan, a lesson — so empathy converts to action.

## Examples
- **Patient experience walks in health professions education** — nursing and medical programs have students navigate admission, waiting, and discharge as a patient would, then debrief communication and dignity gaps (see Kumagai's framework for narrative and experiential methods in medical education).
- **[IDEO.org Human-Centered Design field guides](https://www.ideo.org/tools)** — field immersion exercises in which designers spend time in the daily routines of the people they design for, then synthesize observations into journey maps.
- **Universal design audits in teacher education** — pre-service teachers navigate their own campus or a lesson sequence using assistive technology or with simulated sensory limitations, then redesign materials for accessibility.
