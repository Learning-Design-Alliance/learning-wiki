# Positive Affirmations

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Positive affirmations are short, positive self-statements ("I am capable of learning this") that learners repeat aloud or in writing, with the goal of raising self-esteem, confidence, and motivation. They are distinct from [self-affirmation](../principles/self-affirmation.md) in the social-psychological sense, which involves reflecting on core personal values rather than repeating praise-like statements.

## Design Implications

The evidence for affirmation repetition as a learning intervention is weak and conditionally negative: for people with low self-esteem, repeating statements that contradict their self-views produces worse mood and less persistence, not better [~M]. The mechanism appears to be that implausible positive statements trigger resistance and highlight the gap between statement and self-concept. Value-based self-affirmation — writing about personally important values — has stronger experimental support for reducing defensiveness and stereotype threat effects in academic settings [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Statements that are credible and consistent with the learner's actual self-view; incremental, process-focused statements ("I can improve with practice") are more plausible than global trait claims ("I am brilliant")
- A rationale connecting the statement to effort and strategy, not outcome
- Pairing with concrete learning activity — affirmations alone do not build skill

#### Constraints
- Learners with low self-esteem feel worse and persist less after repeating exaggerated positive statements [~M] — the statement clashes with their self-concept and invites counter-arguing
- No reliable effect on academic achievement when used as a stand-alone intervention [-W]
- Can promote complacency or reduce effort if learners interpret the affirmation as evidence that no further work is needed [-W]
- Global trait praise ("you're so smart") undermines persistence relative to process feedback [-S]; affirmations framed as fixed-trait claims inherit this problem

#### Implementation Variability
- **Value affirmation** (Cohen & Sherman tradition): learners write briefly about a core value before a threatening task — the best-supported variant
- **Process affirmations**: statements tied to effort and strategy ("My effort determines my progress") aligned with [growth mindset](../principles/growth-mindset.md) framing
- **Self-talk restructuring**: replacing negative self-talk with neutral, instructional self-statements, common in sport psychology

### Target Learners
- Learners with moderate-to-high self-esteem may see small mood benefits [~W]
- Learners with low self-esteem or low prior confidence are the group most likely to be harmed by implausible affirmations [~M]
- Value affirmation benefits are largest for students under identity threat (e.g., stereotype threat in mathematics) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Affective: reducing anxiety and defensiveness before high-stakes assessment
- Motivational: supporting persistence when paired with process-oriented framing
- Not appropriate as a goal-serving strategy for knowledge or skill acquisition itself

### Instructions
1. Replace global trait statements with credible, process-focused statements ("I can get better at this with practice") rather than fixed-trait claims.
2. For assessment-anxious or stereotyped learners, use a **value-affirmation** writing exercise (10 minutes on a personally important value) before the task instead of repeated statements.
3. Pair any affirmation with immediate, concrete learning activity and [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the process level.
4. Monitor for backfire effects: if learners express skepticism or discomfort, drop the affirmations rather than increasing repetition.

## Examples
- **Value-affirmation interventions in schools**: Cohen, Garcia, et al.'s middle-school studies had students write about cherished values at the start of the year, narrowing the achievement gap for stereotyped students over two years.
- **Sport psychology self-talk**: athletes replace negative self-statements with instructional cues ("smooth swing") — effective because the statements are task-specific and plausible.
- **Contrast case**: poster-based "I am amazing" affirmations repeated daily in classrooms have no supporting achievement evidence and risk backfiring for the least confident students.
