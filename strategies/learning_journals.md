# Learning Journals

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A learning journal is a recurring, learner-owned record in which students document what they did, what they understood, what confused them, and how their thinking changed. Entries are typically short, structured by prompts or frameworks, and revisited over time so the journal itself becomes an artifact of learning progress rather than a one-off assignment.

## Design Implications

Learning journals work primarily by forcing elaboration and self-monitoring: translating experience into words exposes gaps that passive participation hides, and rereading earlier entries makes growth and misconceptions visible. The evidence base is strongest for metacognitive and self-regulated learning outcomes, with moderate effects on achievement when prompts are specific and entries receive feedback [assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]. Unprompted "dear diary" free-writing tends to produce summary rather than reflection, so prompt design is the central lever.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A regular cadence (weekly or per-session) with low stakes so honesty is not penalized
- Structured prompts targeting specific reflective moves — e.g., "What was the muddiest point?" or "How does this connect to what you already knew?" — rather than "Write about today's class"
- Occasional instructor or peer response, so reflection is consequential and not performative
- A mechanism for revisiting entries ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines, mid-course reviews) so the record feeds forward into future learning

#### Constraints
- Without specific prompts, journals devolve into event summaries with negligible learning benefit [-M]
- Grading journals on length or polish incentivizes fabrication and performative reflection rather than honest self-assessment [-M]
- High-frequency mandatory journaling produces compliance fatigue; quality and engagement decline after the first weeks in mandatory daily formats [-W]
- Reflection without follow-up action (no feedback, no revisiting) yields little durable gain [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dialogue journals**: instructor responds to each entry, creating a written tutorial conversation
- **Double-entry journals**: left column for source material or observations, right column for the learner's response — enforces the separation of evidence and interpretation
- **Error/bug logs** (programming, math): learners catalog their own mistakes and diagnoses, turning errors into study material
- **Multimedia journals**: audio or video entries lower the writing barrier for younger learners or those with writing difficulties
- **Exam wrappers**: single-session mini-journals completed after graded assessments, focusing reflection at the moment feedback arrives

### Target Learners
- Adolescents through adults; younger learners need heavily scaffolded prompts and oral alternatives
- Particularly valuable for learners with weak self-monitoring, who otherwise misjudge their own understanding [~M]
- Less useful for novices who lack the domain knowledge to diagnose their own gaps — their reflections will misattribute difficulty; pair with [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and instructor feedback to compensate [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition and self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own understanding
- Elaborative encoding: connecting new material to prior knowledge and across topics
- Formative assessment: surfacing misconceptions for the instructor ([Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md))
- Dispositional growth: tracking changes in attitude, confidence, and identity over long projects

### Instructions
1. Choose a cadence and format (notebook, LMS tool such as a discussion board or blog) and keep stakes low — completion-based credit, not quality grades.
2. Design 2–3 specific prompts per entry targeting distinct reflective moves (recall, connection, self-diagnosis, planning).
3. Model a reflective entry once using a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) so learners see what genuine self-questioning sounds like.
4. Collect and skim entries, responding briefly to a sample each cycle; use patterns across entries to adjust instruction.
5. Build in structured revisiting — mid-course and end-of-course rereading with a "what changed?" prompt — so the journal functions as longitudinal evidence of growth.
6. Fade prompts over time as learners internalize the reflective routine, shifting toward self-generated questions.

## Examples
- **Exam wrappers** (widely used in postsecondary STEM, e.g., the Eberly Center at Carnegie Mellon: [https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html)) — post-exam reflection sheets asking students to categorize their errors and plan study changes.
- **Science notebooks in inquiry curricula** (e.g., FOSS, [https://www.fossweb.com](https://www.fossweb.com)) — students record predictions, observations, and revised claims, making the journal a working scientific record.
- **Dialogue journals in writing-intensive courses** — instructor responds to weekly entries, sustaining an individualized feedback loop across a semester.
