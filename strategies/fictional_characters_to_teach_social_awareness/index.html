Fictional Characters to Teach Social Awareness¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy uses fictional characters and narratives — from literature, film, or purpose-built scenarios — as safe proxies through which learners examine social situations, perspectives, and ethical dilemmas. Because the characters are not real, learners can discuss sensitive topics (prejudice, exclusion, conflict, inequity) without the interpersonal risk of naming real peers or communities. The fiction is deliberately connected to current events or learners' lived contexts so that insights transfer outward from the story to the world.

Design Implications¶

Fictional narrative engages the same social-cognitive machinery used to understand real people; readers construct situation models of characters' goals, emotions, and beliefs much as they model real interlocutors [~M]. Well-designed character study supports perspective-taking and empathy, particularly when learners are prompted to imagine how a character feels rather than merely judge what they did Perspective-taking instructions that focus on the target's feelings increase empathic concern and helping. [+S]. The fictional frame lowers defensiveness: learners who might resist direct discussion of bias or conflict will analyze a character's choices, allowing productive cognitive disequilibrium without personal threat [~W].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A narrative with psychologically realistic characters facing a genuine social or ethical dilemma
  • Explicit prompts that direct learners to adopt, articulate, and critique character perspectives (Class Discussion, Act It Out)
  • A bridge to reality: discussion questions or texts connecting the fictional situation to current events or learners' communities
  • Facilitation that separates analysis of the character from evaluation of learners who share the character's situation

Constraints¶

  • Empathy gains from fiction are modest and often fail to generalize beyond the specific character or outgroup depicted [-M] — single-story exposure can even reinforce stereotypes if the character is the learner's only representation of a group
  • Perspective-taking exercises can backfire when learners are told to "imagine being" a person whose disadvantage they cannot accurately model, producing overconfident claims of understanding [-M]
  • If the fiction is too distant from learners' experience, or too didactic (characters as obvious morality props), engagement and transfer collapse [-W]
  • Discussion can drift into plot retelling; without structured prompts the social-awareness goal is lost [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Literature circles on novels featuring characters navigating identity, migration, or injustice
  • Role-play and hot-seating, where learners answer questions as the character (Acting/Role-Play)
  • Film and media clips paired with structured perspective-analysis worksheets
  • Comparing multiple narratives featuring different characters in parallel dilemmas, which supports abstraction of the underlying social principle Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Children and adolescents, for whom fictional characters provide a developmentally appropriate entry into abstract social concepts [~M]
  • Learners in heterogeneous classrooms where direct discussion of real conflicts would expose or implicate particular students
  • Reluctant discussants — the fictional frame gives low-risk participation entry points; sustained engagement builds the confidence that supports persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Perspective-taking: articulating another person's goals, emotions, and constraints
  • Social-emotional learning: recognizing and naming emotions and social dynamics
  • Ethical reasoning: evaluating choices and consequences in ambiguous situations
  • Empathy and attitude change toward stigmatized or unfamiliar groups [~M]

Instructions¶

  1. Select a narrative whose central dilemma maps onto a current event or recurring social issue in the classroom or community.
  2. Establish comprehension of the situation before analysis; use Advance Organizers to frame the social concepts at stake.
  3. Pose perspective-taking prompts ("Why did the character do that? What were they feeling? What else could they have done?") in structured Class Discussion or small groups.
  4. Deepen embodiment through role-play — hot-seating the character or re-enacting a decision point (Act It Out).
  5. Introduce a contrasting narrative or real-world case presenting the same dilemma differently, and have learners abstract the shared social principle.
  6. Bridge outward: connect the fictional analysis to a current event, school issue, or learner action (letter-writing, campaign design, community project).

Related Strategies¶

  • Acting/Role-Play — embodiment of the character deepens the perspective-taking this strategy initiates
  • Case-based discussion — the non-fictional counterpart, using real cases instead of invented characters

Examples¶

  • Facing History and Ourselves (https://www.facinghistory.org) — uses literary characters (e.g., in To Kill a Mockingbird) alongside historical case studies to teach students about prejudice, bystander behavior, and civic responsibility.
  • Second Step (Committee for Children, https://www.secondstep.org) — scripted video characters model social situations (peer conflict, exclusion) that elementary students analyze and rehearse.
  • The Choices Program (Brown University, https://www.choices.edu) — curriculum units pairing historical narratives with role-play deliberations on contested policy dilemmas.
