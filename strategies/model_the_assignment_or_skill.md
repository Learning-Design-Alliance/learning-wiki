# Model the Assignment or Skill

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Modeling means the teacher performs the target task — writing a paragraph, solving a problem, annotating a text, critiquing an argument — while verbalizing the decisions, checks, and revisions that expert performance involves. The goal is to make invisible expert thinking observable, so learners acquire not just the product but the process that produces it.

## Design Implications

Modeling works because observation of a competent performance provides a template learners can imitate and internalize [Bandura, 1977], and because worked models reduce the unguided search that overwhelms novices [Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. The critical ingredient is the verbalization: silent demonstration shows *what* to do, but think-aloud narration shows *how experts decide*, which is what learners cannot infer from the finished product alone [van Gog & Rummel, 2010] [+M]. Modeling must be paired with guided practice and faded over time, or learners remain dependent on the model [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine, accurate performance of the task — not a polished artifact presented after the fact
- Explicit verbalization of reasoning, including self-monitoring and error recovery ([articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of the expert's decisions)
- A planned handoff: modeling → guided practice → independent practice (the gradual release of responsibility cycle [Fisher & Frey, 2011])
- Task complexity matched to novice working memory; segment or [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) long demonstrations

#### Constraints
- Observation without subsequent practice produces illusions of competence; learners overestimate what they learned from watching [van Gog & Rummel, 2010] [-M]
- Overly fluent expert performance can be hard to follow; novices benefit when the model works at a realistic pace, makes and corrects plausible errors, and explains why alternatives fail
- Less effective for learners with strong prior knowledge, for whom explicit modeling is redundant and can slow performance [expertise-reversal-effect](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Modeling a single fixed approach can anchor learners; contrasting two solved cases supports abstraction better than one [multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full modeling**: teacher completes the entire task with narration — appropriate when the task is entirely new
- **Partial modeling**: teacher models the opening moves, then the class or group completes the rest — bridges toward independence
- **Error-focused modeling**: the model deliberately makes a high-frequency error and repairs it, which builds error-detection skill [erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **Peer modeling**: a near-peer performs the task, which can raise self-efficacy by making the performance seem attainable [self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novices encountering a task type for the first time, who would otherwise flounder in unguided search [example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]
- Learners low in self-efficacy for the task, who benefit from seeing a attainable path [self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for advanced learners, who profit more from independent problem solving [expertise-reversal-effect](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural skill acquisition: learning the steps and sequencing of a task
- Strategic and metacognitive knowledge: seeing how experts monitor, evaluate, and revise their own work
- Schema construction: building a mental model of what a finished performance looks like and how it is assembled

### Instructions
1. Choose a task learners must soon perform independently; identify the hidden decisions an expert makes.
2. Perform the task live while verbalizing reasoning — including uncertainty, checking, and self-correction (cognitive apprenticeship's modeling phase [Collins et al., 1989]; see [cognitive-apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)).
3. Check understanding with targeted questions before releasing responsibility.
4. Move immediately to guided practice with [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and prompts, fading support as competence grows [fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
5. End with independent application of the same task type ([application](../elements/application.md)).

## Related Strategies
- [activating-prior-knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) — front-loads the knowledge that makes a model comprehensible
- Gradual release of responsibility ("I do, we do, you do") — the instructional frame that sequences modeling, guided practice, and independence [Fisher & Frey, 2011]

## Examples
- A writing teacher drafts a persuasive paragraph on the board, narrating choices ("I need evidence here, but this source is weak, so I'll qualify the claim") before students draft their own.
- **Khan Academy** (https://www.khanacademy.org) — narrated step-by-step problem-solving videos that model mathematical reasoning before learners attempt practice sets.
- A statistics instructor models how to select an appropriate test by thinking aloud through the decision tree, then has student pairs work a new scenario with the same reasoning prompts.
