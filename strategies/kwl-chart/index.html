KWL Chart¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

A KWL chart is a three-column graphic organizer completed across a lesson: before instruction, students record what they already Know (K) and what they Want to know (W); after instruction, they record what they Learned (L). Developed by Ogle (1986) for expository reading, it front-loads Activation of prior knowledge and goal-setting, then closes with structured summarization.

Design Implications¶

KWL charts function as a lightweight Advance Organizers variant: the K and W columns prime relevant prior knowledge and orient attention toward specific questions, which improves encoding of new material Activating prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]. The W column converts passive reception into goal-directed reading or listening, and the L column forces retrieval and summarization, which strengthens retention beyond re-reading or passive review Advance organizers improve learning outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A pre-designed KWL chart (paper or digital), one per student or group
  • A topic with enough familiar surface features that the K column is non-empty
  • Access to the learning materials (readings, video, lecture) the W questions will target
  • Time to revisit the chart after instruction — an L column filled in a rush defeats the purpose
  • A visible chart (paper, whiteboard, or digital) with K, W, and L columns
  • Time before instruction for genuine K and W generation — rushing this step reduces the routine to fill-in-the-blank compliance
  • Instructional content that plausibly answers the questions students raise; the instructor should be prepared to address misconceptions surfaced in the K column
  • A structured debrief when completing the L column, ideally asking students to justify each entry ("How do you know?")
  • A visible three-column chart (paper, whiteboard, or digital), completed in the K/W phase before instruction and the L phase after
  • Source material or a lesson to organize — the chart frames a text, unit, or case study
  • Teacher facilitation: students' K entries often contain misconceptions that need gentle surfacing, not silent recording
  • Time for discussion so the W column generates genuine questions rather than compliance answers
  • A three-column chart (Know, Want to know, Learned) on a shared display or individual sheet
  • A brief class discussion for the K and W phases, so misconceptions surface publicly
  • Instructional content that genuinely answers some of the W questions — the chart must connect to what follows
  • Time reserved for the L phase; skipping it reduces the routine to a warm-up activity

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on the accuracy of students' initial knowledge; misconceptions recorded in the K column and never challenged can be reinforced rather than corrected [~M]
  • Entries can become superficial lists if the W column is not revisited during instruction and the L column is not compared against it
  • Less useful for entirely novel topics where students have no prior knowledge to activate — the K column produces guessing rather than priming
  • As a generic organizer, it adds little for learners who already have strong self-questioning and summarization strategies [~W]
  • The K column can reinforce misconceptions if students list inaccurate "facts" that are never explicitly corrected during instruction [-M]
  • The L column invites copying from the text rather than generative summarization; without prompts to paraphrase or connect, entries become shallow extraction [-W]
  • Effectiveness drops when the W column is ignored — if instruction does not visibly answer students' questions, the goal-setting benefit is lost and engagement declines [~M]
  • Less effective for topics where students have genuinely no prior knowledge to activate; the K column then produces guessing that may be encoded as fact [-W]
  • Unfacilitated K columns can reinforce misconceptions by having students record and commit to inaccurate prior knowledge [~M] — the teacher must treat K entries as hypotheses to test, not facts to preserve
  • Becomes a rote fill-in-the-sheet ritual when the W column is not genuinely used to steer the lesson [-W]
  • The L column invites listing of surface facts rather than reflection unless prompts push for "how does this change what I thought?" [~W]
  • Low payoff for learners with substantial prior knowledge, for whom the K phase adds little activation value [~M]
  • If the K phase is done silently and uncorrected, students may consolidate misconceptions rather than surface them [-M]
  • Listing "what you know" can consume instructional time without adding learning when students have little relevant prior knowledge [~M]
  • The L column often becomes a copy-out-the-facts exercise; without prompts to compare L against K and W, it produces shallow listing rather than reflection [-W]
  • Effectiveness drops when the chart is treated as a compliance worksheet rather than a genuine discussion record [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • KWL+ / KWHL: add an H column ("How will I find out?") to make the search process explicit, or a final "+" for new questions raised
  • Individual, pair, or whole-class: whole-class charting surfaces diverse prior knowledge and misconceptions for discussion; individual charts add retrieval practice
  • Digital variants: shared documents or whiteboard tools (e.g., Jamboard, Padlet) allow live addition to the L column during instruction
  • KWL+ / KWHL: add a fourth column — How will I find out (KWHL) or How does this connect to what I know (KWL+) — to make the inquiry process explicit
  • KWLQ: a final Question column for new questions raised, sustaining inquiry beyond the lesson
  • Digital variants: shared documents or tools like Padlet allow collaborative K/W brainstorming and revisiting the chart asynchronously
  • Individual vs. collective: whole-class charts surface diverse prior knowledge and misconceptions for discussion; individual charts elicit more honest self-assessment
  • KWL+ / KWHL: add a fourth column — How will I find out (KWHL) or What more do I want to know (KWL+) — to extend the routine into inquiry planning and self-regulated learning
  • Group KWL: complete the chart collaboratively on a shared board, using it as a running anchor chart across a multi-week unit
  • Digital KWL: shared documents or tools like Padlet allow revisiting and annotating the chart throughout the unit rather than completing it once
  • KWL+ / KWHL: add a fourth column — How will I find out (KWHL) or What more do I want to know (KWL+) — to extend the routine into inquiry planning and metacognitive monitoring
  • KLEW: replace L with Learning evidence and add Evidence and We still wonder columns, suited to science inquiry
  • Digital versions (shared documents, Padlet boards) allow asynchronous completion and make the W questions visible throughout a unit
  • Individual charts suit reading comprehension; whole-class charts suit unit launches and class discussion

  • Multilingual learners, when the K column lets them contribute knowledge in either language and lowers the entry barrier to a new topic
  • Less valuable for advanced learners with well-organized domain knowledge, for whom the K column adds little and may feel redundant [~M]
  • Novices and intermediate learners encountering an unfamiliar topic, where activation and structure matter most Graphic organizers support comprehension for novice learners. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Distribute the chart and have students list everything they know about the topic in the K column (Activation); discuss entries to surface misconceptions.
  2. Have students generate questions they want answered in the W column; keep these visible during instruction.
  3. Deliver the lesson (reading, video, lecture), pausing to check which W questions have been answered (Annotating the chart mid-lesson helps).
  4. After the lesson, students complete the L column, ideally cross-referencing each entry against a W question and noting unanswered questions (Application).
  5. Use the completed chart as a formative assessment: gaps between W and L reveal what needs reteaching.

Examples¶

  • Before a unit on rainforests, students fill the K column with facts ("it rains a lot," "there are monkeys") and W questions ("why are there so many species?"), then complete L after the unit — the classic Ogle (1986) classroom implementation.
  • Understood.org — provides downloadable KWL templates and guidance on using advance organizers with diverse learners.
  • Reading Rockets (readingrockets.org) — publishes KWL classroom routines for expository text in elementary reading instruction.
  • Expository reading (Ogle's original use): before a unit on rainforests, students list known facts and questions in K and W; after reading, they complete L and verify which questions were answered. See Reading Rockets' KWL overview.
  • Understood.org — KWL charts as an accommodation for students with learning and attention differences, providing structure for organizing new information.
  • Science inquiry (KWHL): before a unit on electricity, students add a "How will we find out?" column, turning the chart into a plan for hands-on investigation.
  • Elementary science unit on weather: Students list known weather facts (K), ask "why do tornadoes happen?" (W), and after the unit revise their K list, crossing out misconceptions — making conceptual change visible.
  • Reading Rockets — publishes classroom-tested KWL procedures and printable templates for literacy instruction.
  • Adult ESL / EFL reading courses: KWL is widely used to frame expository texts; the W column sets a purpose for reading, and the L column doubles as a summary-writing scaffold.
  • Ogle's original DRTA-derived routine (Ogle, 1986): fifth-grade students completed group KWL charts before reading expository passages, with the teacher modeling how to generate W questions from the topic.
  • Science unit launches (KLEW variant): teachers using the KLEW chart (e.g., in elementary science methods texts based on Hapgood & Magnusson, 2007) have students tie each "Learning" entry to specific evidence from investigations, connecting the chart to scientific practices.
  • Content-area literacy programs: widely used in reading workshop models and district literacy frameworks (e.g., Reading Rockets' KWL templates, https://www.readingrockets.org) as a standard pre-reading organizer for informational text.
