# Creating a Classroom Library

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A classroom library is a curated, accessible collection of books within the learning space, organized so learners can browse, select, and read independently. Setup involves acquiring a diverse collection, organizing it (by genre, topic, theme, or level), labeling bins so covers face outward, and involving learners in categorization decisions. The goal is to make self-selected reading frictionless and appealing.

## Design Implications

Access to books is one of the strongest environmental predictors of reading volume, and reading volume in turn predicts comprehension and vocabulary growth [~M]. The library functions as an autonomy-supportive structure: when learners choose their own books, motivation and engagement rise relative to assigned-only reading [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Displaying covers in bins and keeping the collection current increases browsing and selection rates [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A collection that is diverse in genre, topic, format, and difficulty, with enough titles that every learner finds something appealing
- Shelving or bins with front-facing display and clear labels; a defined, comfortable space for browsing and reading
- A system learners can navigate independently (genre labels, topic bins, or level markers)
- Ongoing maintenance: rotating titles, repairing books, and refreshing displays

#### Constraints
- Level-only organization (e.g., strict Accelerated Reader or guided-reading leveling) can stigmatize struggling readers and narrow their choices; research on reading motivation favors interest- and genre-based organization with leveling used lightly, if at all [~M]
- A large but stale or unappealing collection yields little benefit — volume of books matters less than relevance and accessibility [~W]
- Without protected time to actually read (e.g., an independent reading block), a library becomes decoration rather than instruction [-M]
- Significant upfront cost and ongoing curation effort; under-resourced classrooms may need grants, book drives, or public-library partnerships

#### Implementation Variability
- Organization scheme: genre bins, topic/theme bins, author studies, or hybrid genre-plus-level labels
- Learner roles: students can serve as librarians, recommend titles, write shelf-talkers, and vote on new purchases
- Digital extensions: paired [audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md) or e-book access extends the library for emerging and striving readers

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, with the strongest documented effects in elementary and middle grades
- Striving readers benefit especially from high-interest, low-barrier titles and front-facing display, which lower selection cost [~M]
- Learners with low reading self-concept gain when choice is genuine rather than constrained to a visible "low" shelf [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Independent reading volume and stamina
- Book selection and self-regulation of reading [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Reading motivation and engagement, which mediate comprehension growth [~M]

### Instructions
1. Audit and acquire: inventory existing titles, identify gaps in genre, topic, cultural representation, and difficulty; source books through school budgets, grants (e.g., [DonorsChoose](https://www.donorschoose.org)), book drives, and public-library partnerships.
2. Decide the organization scheme with learners — co-creating categories builds ownership and teaches genre knowledge.
3. Label bins and shelves so learners can return books independently; display covers face-out wherever possible.
4. Create a comfortable browsing and reading space (seating, lighting, defined boundaries).
5. Establish routines for checkout, return, and shelf maintenance; rotate student librarian roles.
6. Protect time for learners to browse and read from the library regularly, and connect selections to response activities such as [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or book talks.
7. Refresh the collection on a schedule; retire worn or unappealing titles.

## Related Strategies
- Independent reading — the library supplies the materials that make sustained silent reading possible
- Book talks — brief recommendations that drive circulation from the library
- Reading conferences — one-on-one check-ins that connect library choices to instruction
