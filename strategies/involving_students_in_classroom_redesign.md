# Involving Students in Classroom Redesign

## Description
Involving students in classroom redesign means treating the physical environment — layout, furniture, displays, materials organization — as a co-designed artifact rather than a teacher-determined given. Students gather evidence about how the environment affects attention and learning, deliberate over competing proposals, and implement changes. The strategy pairs environmental decision-making with instruction about the research behind environmental effects, so choices are informed rather than purely preferential.

## Design Implications

The strategy works through two mechanisms: the motivational value of meaningful choice and the learning value of an authentic, consequential design problem. Providing choice reliably increases intrinsic motivation and persistence, though the effect is strongest when choices are meaningful and not overwhelming [The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation: a meta-analysis.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Framing redesign as a genuine problem also raises task value — students see their decisions matter to a real audience — which supports engagement [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A teacher willing to share real decision-making authority, not just solicit token input
- Basic evidence about environmental effects on attention and learning (e.g., visual clutter, acoustics, lighting) presented in accessible form
- A structured deliberation process so decisions are made collectively rather than by the loudest voices ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
- Some budget, flexibility, or administrative permission to actually implement changes

#### Constraints
- Choice without structure can overload working memory and reduce motivation rather than increase it; a small set of well-described options outperforms open-ended choice [The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation: a meta-analysis.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]
- Highly decorated or student-curated wall displays can compete for attention during instruction, particularly for young learners and learners with attention difficulties [-M] — redesign should deliberately reduce, not add, visual distraction
- Conflicting preferences among students can produce frustration or perceived unfairness if the decision process is not transparent
- Fixed furniture, safety codes, or shared rooms limit feasibility; token consultation with no implementation erodes trust and can reduce perceived autonomy [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Scope ranges from a single learning corner to full-room redesign, or from one-off consultation to an ongoing design cycle with iterative evaluation
- Can be run as a project-based unit (data collection, proposal, budgeting, implementation) or as lightweight periodic check-ins on the environment
- In higher education and adult training, redesign often shifts to co-designing norms, room configuration, and tool selection rather than furniture

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, who benefit from both the ownership experience and the authentic design problem [The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation: a meta-analysis.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Learners with low engagement or weak sense of belonging, for whom visible influence on the environment signals membership and agency [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Less suitable as a stand-alone intervention for learners who need direct support in self-regulation; environmental control alone does not build [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) skills without explicit coaching [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivational goals: ownership, autonomy, and belonging
- Applied problem-solving: gathering evidence, weighing trade-offs, budgeting, and persuading an audience
- Metacognitive goals: noticing how environment and conditions affect one's own attention and work habits

### Instructions
1. Present accessible evidence on how the physical environment affects attention and learning (visual clutter, noise, seating, lighting), using [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) to frame the design problem.
2. Have students observe and document current pain points (photos, sketches, surveys), then generate proposals through [Question Formulation](../elements/question-formulation.md) and structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
3. Support deliberation and consensus-building with [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) routines and, where needed, [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) on negotiation and trade-off reasoning.
4. Implement the agreed changes, then evaluate their effects with students using observation and [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) data, iterating on the design.

## Examples
- **Erin Klein's second-grade classroom (Michigan, USA)** — Klein involved her students in evaluating and redesigning the room, replacing traditional desks with flexible seating and reducing visual clutter based on what students said helped them focus; documented on her blog *Kleinspiration* (http://www.kleinspiration.co).
- **The School I'd Like project (UK)** — *The Guardian* invited school students to imagine and describe their ideal learning environments (2001, repeated 2011), producing a widely used archive of student-authored design critiques used in teacher education.
- **Project-based redesign units** — middle-school STEM classes treating classroom redesign as an engineering problem: measuring the room, surveying users, prototyping layouts with scale models, and presenting budgeted proposals to school leadership.
