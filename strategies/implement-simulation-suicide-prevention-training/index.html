Implement simulation-based education for suicide prevention training across health students and gatekeepers, with complexity adapted to trainee level¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 7 claims (5 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 2 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Based on its encouraging findings, the review recommends implementing simulation-based education for suicide risk assessment and crisis intervention training. It states "the benefit to implement simulation-based education for all health students as is done for somatic first aids", and that "targeting people most likely to be close with person with suicidal idea may be an efficient public health approach". It also advises adapting training complexity to the educational level of trainees and combining simulation with lectures, since theoretical knowledge remains necessary.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Training complexity should be adapted to the educational level of trainees, and effectiveness assessment adjusted accordingly

Constraints¶

  • Clear and robust benefits of simulation over simpler modalities such as lectures or videos are required, and simulation requires writing authentic scenarios, training facilitators and actors, with additional costs and ethical considerations when working with actors

Target Learners¶

  • health students
  • gatekeepers such as school staff and hotline workers
  • healthcare professionals

Target Learning Goals¶

  • suicide risk assessment
  • suicidal crisis intervention skills

Affordances¶

  • Simulation Techniques Taxonomy Mental Health

Claims¶

  • RCTs report objective behavioral changes after simulation training, including increased peer referral, longer crisis-line calls, and improved management of suicidal crises [+W]
  • Meta-analysis of RCTs finds no significant effect of simulation training on factual knowledge about suicide [-W]
  • Non-randomized and pre/post-test studies broadly support simulation training benefits for attitudes, skills, knowledge and behaviors, though subject to natural learning effects [+W]
  • Training school staff with simulation reduced student suicide attempts and severe suicidal ideation at 12 months, among the few RCTs measuring patient outcomes [+W]
  • Simulation training improves self-perceived skills at six-month follow-up compared with active comparators, but shows no significant skill advantage at immediate post-test [+W]
  • Simulation training improves attitudes toward suicide risk assessment compared with active comparators immediately after training and compared with inactive comparators at 2–4 months [+W]
  • Virtual standardized patient training improved alcohol screening and brief intervention skills and diagnostic accuracy for depression and PTSD in two RCTs, while a suicide risk assessment RCT found no significant effect [~W]
