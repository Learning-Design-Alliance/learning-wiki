# Jigsaw Classroom

## Description
The Jigsaw Classroom, developed by Elliot Aronson in 1971 for desegregating classrooms in Austin, Texas, divides a lesson into segments and assigns each member of a "jigsaw" group one segment to master. Learners first meet in temporary "expert groups" with peers who share the same segment, then return to their home groups to teach their piece. Because no learner holds the full picture alone, the structure creates genuine positive interdependence: each student's learning depends on listening to and learning from every teammate.

## Design Implications

Jigsaw operationalizes cooperative learning by combining individual accountability (each member is the sole source of one segment) with positive interdependence (the group task cannot be completed without all segments) [Group rewards combined with individual accountability are central to cooperative learning's effectiveness.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Its distinctive contribution is the teaching role: preparing to explain material to peers produces deeper processing than preparing only to be tested, and listening to a peer restructures the social dynamics of mixed groups. Aronson's original motivation was intergroup relations — the structure gives marginalized students a legitimate, necessary role and gives majority students a reason to attend to them [Structured cooperative contact improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Material that divides cleanly into interdependent segments (a narrative, a set of causes, a set of cases)
- Time for two phases: expert-group meetings and home-group teaching
- Individual accountability for the *whole* content (e.g., a quiz covering all segments), not just one's own piece
- Norms for peer teaching: check for understanding, invite questions ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Weak or unprepared "experts" propagate gaps: if a segment is taught poorly, the whole group inherits the misconception [-M]
- Segments that are genuinely independent (parallel facts with no synthesis) reduce interdependence to turn-taking, undermining the design [~M]
- Dominant students can still take over home-group discussion unless roles and norms are enforced [-M]
- Heavy working-memory demands for novices who must both comprehend and prepare to teach; segment texts may need [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and accessible language [Cognitive overload degrades learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jigsaw II (Slavin)**: all students read the same base text, expert groups specialize in a topic within it, and individual quiz scores convert to team scores — adds team rewards and reduces the risk of a weak expert
- **Reverse jigsaw**: experts teach in the expert group first, then home groups synthesize
- **Jigsaw in large/online classes**: breakout rooms in two rounds; expert groups can be supported with instructor-provided segment guides

### Target Learners
- Mixed-ability and culturally heterogeneous classrooms, where status differences otherwise depress participation [Structured cooperative contact improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Upper elementary through adult learners; younger learners need more scaffolding for the teaching role
- Less suitable for learners with very low reading fluency unless segments are provided in accessible formats ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))

### Target Learning Goals
- Content mastery across a broad, divisible body of material [Active learning structures improve exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]
- Communication and explanation skills — articulating understanding to peers
- Intergroup understanding and classroom climate reduction of prejudice

### Instructions
1. **Segment the material** into 4–6 interdependent parts aligned to a single synthesizing question.
2. **Form home groups** of one learner per segment; assign segments deliberately, giving lower-status students segments of genuine importance ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)).
3. **Expert groups**: learners with the same segment meet to master it, using an instructor-provided study guide; instructor circulates and coaches ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. **Teach-back**: learners return to home groups and teach their segment, checking peers' understanding rather than just reciting.
5. **Synthesize and assess**: the group or class integrates the segments; each learner completes an individual assessment covering *all* segments, preserving individual accountability.

## Related Strategies
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — a lighter-weight interdependence structure; jigsaw adds the expert-teaching layer
- [Peer Tutoring](peer-tutoring.md) — shares the "learning by teaching" mechanism but without the interdependence across segments

## Examples
- **Aronson's original design (1971, Austin, TX)**: fifth-grade history lessons split into segments; documented reductions in prejudice and increases in minority students' achievement ([jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)).
- **Jigsaw II in reading (Slavin's TAI/CII research program)**: teams earn points from individual quiz scores covering all topics, combining expert specialization with team reward.
- **University flipped courses**: pre-class readings split by segment; expert groups convene in the first 15 minutes of class, home groups synthesize for the remainder.
