# Peer Discussion

## Description
Peer discussion places learners in dialogue with one another — explaining their reasoning, answering each other's questions, and resolving disagreements — rather than interacting only with an instructor. It ranges from brief structured exchanges (think-pair-share, peer instruction votes) to extended formats such as [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), seminars, and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) tasks. The instructor's role shifts from delivering content to designing prompts, monitoring talk, and orchestrating the synthesis that follows.

## Design Implications

Discussion works because articulating ideas to a peer forces retrieval and elaboration, and because exposure to a peer's differing reasoning can create the productive conflict that drives conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. Active-learning formats that embed peer discussion improve exam performance relative to lecture alone [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. However, the quality of talk matters more than its presence: discussion only produces learning when prompts are genuinely discussable and when students are accountable for engaging, not merely adjacent to it [Cooperative learning requires group rewards paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A question or task with genuine ambiguity or multiple defensible positions — discussion of a single settled fact collapses into answer-checking
- Sufficient shared background knowledge that peers can evaluate each other's reasoning
- Individual thinking or writing time before talk begins, so discussion builds on retrieval rather than replacing it
- A whole-group synthesis afterward in which the instructor consolidates what the discussion surfaced

#### Constraints
- Unstructured "discuss with your neighbor" often produces task-irrelevant social talk within minutes; time limits, roles, and named prompts are needed [-M]
- Groups without individual accountability allow free-riding, where stronger students do the cognitive work and weaker students copy conclusions without the reasoning [Cooperative learning requires group rewards paired with individual accountability.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Discussion between two peers who share the same misconception can entrench it rather than correct it [~M] — instructor follow-up or access to authoritative feedback is needed to resolve stalemates
- Students with low status in a group are talked over or ignored unless structures (assigned roles, wait time, turn protocols) distribute airtime [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Peer Instruction**: students answer individually, discuss with neighbors, then re-answer — the vote-revote cycle makes disagreement visible ([Mazur's Peer Instruction](https://peerinstruction4seers.github.io))
- **Think-Pair-Share**: individual thinking, paired exchange, whole-class sharing; the most portable variant
- **Reciprocal teaching roles**: students alternate leading questioning, summarizing, and predicting in small groups
- **Structured controversy**: assigned positions in a [Debate](../patterns/debate.md) force engagement with the strongest opposing case
- **Asynchronous peer discussion**: online forums with required reply protocols (e.g., "one agreement, one extension, one question") for distributed or blended settings

### Target Learners
- Students who have enough prior knowledge to generate and evaluate explanations, but not enough to be certain — the zone where peer reasoning is both useful and contestable
- Underrepresented or quieter students benefit when structured turn-taking replaces voluntary hand-raising [~M]
- Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge to distinguish plausible from correct peer claims [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and misconception repair, especially in domains with common intuitive errors
- Argumentation and epistemic practices: justifying claims, evaluating evidence, responding to counterarguments
- Transferable communication and collaboration skills

### Instructions
1. Pose a conceptually demanding question and give students 1–2 minutes of individual response time ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) or a quick write).
2. Pair or group students and require each to articulate their reasoning, not just their answer ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
3. Direct students to reach agreement or a clearly stated disagreement, with reasons ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)).
4. Circulate and listen, intervening with questions rather than answers ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Reconvene for whole-group synthesis in which the instructor names the key ideas and resolves remaining misconceptions ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).

## Examples
- **[Peer Instruction](https://peerinstruction4seers.github.io)** (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — ConcepTest questions in physics: individual clicker vote, peer discussion, revote, instructor explanation. Replicated learning gains across many STEM disciplines.
- **[Reciprocal Teaching](https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching)** (Palincsar & Brown) — small-group dialogue in which students take turns leading prediction, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing of a text.
- **[Piazza](https://piazza.com)** — online Q&A platform where students answer each other's questions with instructor endorsement, structuring asynchronous peer discussion in large courses.
