Deploy 360° VR videos as a self-directed learning tool where instructor access is limited or content is hard to demonstrate¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (4 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 1 of 3 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article suggests organizations use 360° VR videos where traditional instruction is impractical: "Organizations that provide education and training programs (such as water safety organizations and museums) could use this type of technology to support their education programs and make it accessible to more children." Teachers noted VR overcomes barriers of cost, travel time, and documentation required for excursions. VR also allows learners to repeat experiences as needed.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A VR-capable device such as a cardboard headset with smartphone and headphones, and 360° video content covering the target topics

Constraints¶

  • The article cautions that VR "should be not adopted in educational settings without a careful analysis of instructional design and the integration process in the educational curricula"

Target Learners¶

  • Primary school children in Years 5 and 6

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Specialized topics difficult to replicate in real-life settings, such as identifying rip currents

Claims¶

  • 360° VR videos can serve as an effective self-directed learning tool, with children learning comparably without an instructor [+W]
  • Children learning water-safety content via 360° VR videos, traditional video, or posters show similar learning gains, with 95% improving knowledge and retaining it at one and eight weeks [+W]
  • Teachers perceive 360° VR videos as engaging, feasible, and useful for explaining concepts difficult to demonstrate traditionally [+W]
  • Participants in the 360° VR video condition report higher interest and enjoyment than those learning via traditional video or posters [+W]
  • Prior single-exposure IVR studies show mixed cognitive outcomes: affective gains without cognitive benefits (Parong & Mayer) and short-lived retention gains (Christopoulos) [-W]
  • Immersive lessons show a small effect size advantage over less immersive technology, but increased presence does not necessarily lead to more learning [~W]
