Promoting Belonging Through Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Promoting belonging through instruction means designing everyday teaching practices — task design, feedback, grouping, discourse norms — so that students experience themselves as capable, respected members of the academic community. Rather than treating belonging as a fixed student trait, this strategy treats it as a malleable perception shaped by instructional signals: who gets to contribute, how struggle is framed, and whether errors are treated as normal parts of learning.

Design Implications¶

Belonging is a strong predictor of persistence, engagement, and achievement, and brief interventions that target students' sense of belonging produce measurable gains in GPA and course completion, especially for marginalized students Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [+S]. Instructional practices matter because students infer belonging from daily cues: whether their ideas are taken up in discussion, whether feedback conveys high expectations with confidence in their ability, and whether difficulty is normalized as universal rather than diagnostic of fixed ability Autonomy-supportive teaching increases intrinsic motivation and engagement. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Consistent instructional routines that give every student legitimate opportunities to contribute (e.g., Class Discussion with structured turn-taking, Peer Discussion)
  • Feedback practices that pair high standards with explicit affirmation of the student's capacity to meet them
  • Framing of struggle and error as normal and informative, not as evidence of inability
  • Teacher awareness of how status hierarchies form in classrooms and deliberate practices to disrupt them

Constraints¶

  • One-off belonging activities without changed instructional practice can backfire: students who then experience contradicting cues (public failure, dismissive feedback) may disengage more than if nothing had been said [~M]
  • Belonging interventions show the strongest effects for students at risk of feeling marginalization; for students already secure in their belonging, effects are near zero Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [~S]
  • Requires sustained effort and sensitivity; a single inclusive lesson cannot offset a semester of status-based participation patterns [-M]
  • Teachers may need additional resources and training to support students who have experienced marginalization or discrimination

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class approaches: equity-based task design, Check-In routines, re-engagement norms for Whole-Class Sharing
  • Individualized approaches: Provide Guidance during work time, targeted feedback that attributes difficulty to strategy rather than ability
  • Structured social-belonging framing: brief reading-and-writing exercises in which students learn that worries about belonging are common and temporary (the Walton & Cohen social-belonging intervention)

Target Learners¶

  • Students entering a transition or gatekeeping context (first-year college, tracked math courses, STEM pathways) where belonging is most fragile Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [+S]
  • Students from groups stereotyped as low-ability in the domain, who are most likely to attribute everyday setbacks to a lack of belonging Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [+M]
  • All students benefit from equity-based instructional practices, but effects on outcomes concentrate among those at risk of marginalization [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and motivational goals: sense of belonging, academic self-efficacy, persistence through difficulty
  • Dispositional goals: viewing struggle and error as normal components of learning
  • Equity goals: equitable participation and access to rigorous content across student groups

Instructions¶

  1. Open with routines that surface every student's thinking, such as a Check-In or Individual Reflection before whole-group talk, so participation is not dominated by a few voices.
  2. Structure Peer Discussion with roles and protocols that distribute authority, so students hear their ideas valued by peers as well as the teacher.
  3. During work time, Provide Guidance in ways that attribute difficulty to strategy and effort rather than ability ("try a different representation" rather than "this may be too hard for you").
  4. Normalize error publicly: treat incorrect answers in Whole-Class Sharing as contributions that advance the discussion, and share stories of productive struggle.
  5. Deliver feedback that combines high standards with expressed confidence in the student's capacity — the "wise feedback" formulation ("I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know you can reach them") [~S].
  6. Early in a course, use a brief social-belonging framing activity in which students read about and reflect on older students' experiences of initial belonging worries that faded with time.

Related Strategies¶

  • Building Empathy — empathy practices and belonging practices both target students' sense of being understood and valued in the classroom
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — valuing students' existing knowledge signals that their backgrounds are assets, a direct belonging cue
  • Cultivate mathematics belonging by publicly recognizing every student as a mathematical thinker

Examples¶

  • Social-belonging intervention (Walton & Cohen) — Students read survey results showing that worries about fitting in are common and diminish over time, then write about their own experience; a single session improved GPA and halved the racial-achievement gap among marginalized students years later.
  • Wise feedback (Cohen, Steele & Ross) — Teachers attach a note to assignment feedback stating high standards and confidence in the student; students revise more and trust the feedback more.
  • Complex instruction in mathematics — Status-based grouping is disrupted through assigned group roles and multiple-ability tasks (e.g., as implemented in Youcubed curricula), so every student has a legitimate way to contribute.
