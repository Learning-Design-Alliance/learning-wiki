Social Emotional Learning Curricula¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 1 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Social Emotional Learning (SEL) curricula are structured, sequenced instructional programs that explicitly teach five interrelated competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making (the CASEL framework). Delivery typically combines direct instruction of skills, structured practice through role-play or discussion, and integration into academic content and classroom routines.

Design Implications¶

Well-implemented SEL programs produce measurable gains in social-emotional skills, attitudes, and academic achievement, with effects persisting years after program completion Durlak et al.'s meta-analysis of 213 school-based programs. [+S]. Effectiveness depends on the SAFE features — Sequenced, Active, Focused, Explicit — rather than on program branding alone; programs using active skill practice outperform those relying on lecture or discussion alone [+S]. Skills must be modeled and rehearsed, not merely described, consistent with observational learning mechanisms Social learning theory. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A sequenced scope-and-lesson sequence rather than isolated activities
  • Active practice formats: role-play, rehearsal, feedback (Act It Out, Acting-Role Play)
  • Adult modeling — teachers demonstrating the skills they teach, since students learn from observed behavior [+M]
  • Integration into classroom management and academic tasks, not a stand-alone block disconnected from the rest of the day
  • Implementation support: training and coaching for teachers, since effects shrink sharply under poor implementation fidelity [+M]

Constraints¶

  • Effects are substantially weaker when implementation fidelity is low or when teachers lack buy-in and training [-M]
  • One-off workshops or assemblies without sequencing and practice show negligible effects [-M]
  • Curricula that teach skills without addressing classroom climate or Belonging show weaker transfer to real peer interactions [-W]
  • Overly scripted delivery can crowd out responsive discussion and reduce student Autonomy, undermining internalization of the skills [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Universal (Tier 1) classroom curricula (e.g., PATHS, Second Step, RULER) delivered to all students
  • Targeted (Tier 2/3) small-group interventions for students with elevated needs
  • Embedded approaches that integrate SEL into academic instruction and discipline practices rather than a separate period
  • Brief targeted interventions can be surprisingly durable — a 2-session empathic discipline program for teachers cut suspensions significantly Brief empathic discipline intervention reduces suspensions. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 students generally, with the strongest academic effects in elementary grades [+S]
  • Students experiencing adversity or low Belonging, for whom explicit social-skills instruction compensates for less modeling at home [~M]
  • Students with behavioral or self-regulation difficulties benefit most from targeted small-group formats [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-regulation and emotion management skills
  • Interpersonal skills: cooperation, conflict resolution, help-seeking
  • Responsible decision-making and ethical reasoning
  • Prerequisites for academic learning — attention, persistence, and classroom engagement [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Establish a safe climate first — use Check-In routines and community-building so students are willing to practice vulnerable skills publicly.
  2. Name and model the skill — the teacher demonstrates the competency (e.g., a calm-down routine, an "I-statement"), consistent with Social Learning Theory.
  3. Rehearse actively — students practice through structured role-play (Act It Out) with peer and teacher feedback, not just discussion.
  4. Debrief and generalize — connect the skill to real classroom situations and academic work; prompt students to plan when they will use it.
  5. Reinforce in context — cue and praise the skill during authentic moments throughout the day rather than confining it to the SEL lesson block.
  6. Offer choice in application — letting students select practice scenarios or goals supports ownership Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M].

Related Strategies¶

  • Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting) — structures student autonomy, which supports the self-management competencies SEL curricula target
  • Acceptance, Responsibility, and Sharing — a classroom-routine variant that embeds SEL practice in daily community meetings
  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a low-stakes emotion-labeling activity usable as a warm-up within an SEL sequence
  • Deliver social-emotional skills through short, frequent structured lessons with teacher manuals and parallel parent handbooks
  • Implement Connect Science through a staged professional development sequence with coaching and reflective practice
  • Embed brief daily wellbeing check-ins across academic courses to build student-teacher connection

Examples¶

  • PATHS (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies) — sequenced elementary curriculum teaching emotion vocabulary and self-control; among the most heavily evaluated universal SEL programs.
  • Second Step (Committee for Children) — K–8 curriculum with scripted lessons, puppet/video modeling, and skill rehearsal cards.
  • RULER (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — an approach-based curriculum built around emotion "anchors" (Charter, Mood Meter) integrated into school climate rather than isolated lessons.
  • CASEL — the framework and program-clearinghouse organization; its SAFE criteria summarize the design features that predict effectiveness.
