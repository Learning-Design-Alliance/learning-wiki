# Mistake-Friendly Classroom

## Description
A mistake-friendly classroom deliberately normalizes error as information: learners are expected to make mistakes, errors are surfaced and analyzed publicly rather than hidden, and the social climate rewards intellectual risk-taking over error avoidance. The strategy combines teacher framing (how errors are responded to), task design (activities where errors are likely and useful), and routines (error analysis, revision, and discussion) so that mistakes become objects of study rather than sources of shame.

## Design Implications

Error tolerance changes what learners do with failure: instead of concealing or avoiding errors, they examine them, which supports conceptual change and retention. Errors made with high confidence are especially valuable, because the surprise of being wrong triggers reprocessing and better memory for the corrected answer [Errors made with high confidence improve retention when corrected.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]. Analyzing erroneous examples — flawed worked solutions presented for critique — builds conceptual knowledge better than studying only correct solutions [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]. The climate also protects self-efficacy: learners who see mistakes as normal rather than diagnostic of ability persist longer [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teacher responses that treat errors as expected and informative — asking "what does this mistake teach us?" rather than moving quickly past it
- Tasks with genuine error potential, such as [Productive Failure](productive-failure.md) designs or [Erroneous Examples](../elements/erroneous-examples.md) for critique
- Routines for public error analysis: discussing anonymous mistakes, comparing solution attempts, normalizing revision
- Feedback that targets the task and the reasoning process rather than the person [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Error tolerance without correction is harmful: leaving errors unaddressed lets misconceptions consolidate; every surfaced error needs timely, accurate feedback [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-M]
- Public error discussion can backfire for learners with low self-efficacy or in classrooms with weak trust — the climate must be established before errors are shared publicly
- Less effective for high-stakes procedural skills where errors carry real cost (e.g., safety-critical procedures); simulation or [Fading](../elements/fading.md) is needed there instead
- If grading punishes early errors, the framing collapses — formative assessment must be separated from evaluative grading [Assessment for learning requires low-stakes opportunities to act on feedback.](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Anonymous error analysis: teacher collects and presents common mistakes without attribution, lowering social risk
- Erroneous-example routines: learners find and fix a deliberately flawed solution ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) applied to problem solving)
- Revision-based grading: credit is tied to improvement after error, not first-attempt accuracy
- Teacher modeling: instructors share and analyze their own mistakes via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), demonstrating that error is part of expert practice

### Target Learners
- Novices, whose errors are frequent and often reveal productive misconceptions [Analyzing erroneous examples builds conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Learners with fragile self-efficacy or math/science anxiety, who otherwise avoid risk and disengage from challenge [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- High-confidence learners who hold misconceptions — their confident errors are the most instructive [Errors made with high confidence improve retention when corrected.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: diagnosing and remediating misconceptions through error analysis
- Problem-solving and transfer: attempting problems before instruction, then learning from the gap [Productive failure improves conceptual learning.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+S]
- Metacognition and self-regulation: learning to monitor, diagnose, and correct one's own reasoning

### Instructions
1. Establish the climate explicitly: frame errors as expected, share your own mistakes, and respond to learner errors with curiosity rather than correction.
2. Design tasks with productive error potential — problems learners can attempt before full instruction, in the spirit of [Productive Failure](productive-failure.md).
3. Collect and surface errors: gather common mistakes and present them anonymously for whole-class analysis, or use prepared [Erroneous Examples](../elements/erroneous-examples.md).
4. Give feedback at the task and process level — what was wrong, why, and what to try next — not at the personal level [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. Require revision: learners correct their own work and explain the original error, converting the mistake into a studied case.
6. Keep early attempts low-stakes: separate formative practice from graded evaluation so risk-taking is not punished.

## Examples
- **My Favorite Mistake routine** — a common warm-up in [Number Talks](https://www.mathsolutions.com/number-talks/) classrooms: the teacher presents a student's (anonymous) incorrect solution and the class analyzes where the reasoning went wrong before correcting it.
- **Kapur's Productive Failure studies** — Singapore math classrooms where students collaboratively attempt complex problems before instruction; failure on first attempts produced deeper conceptual learning than direct instruction first [Productive failure improves conceptual learning.](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+S].
- **Mastery-oriented platforms** — [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) treats incorrect answers as retry points with hints rather than terminal grades, embedding error tolerance into the product design.
