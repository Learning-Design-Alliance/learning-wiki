# Three-Phase Lesson

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Three-Phase Lesson organizes a single lesson into three sequenced phases: **Before** (activating prior knowledge, framing the task, establishing expectations), **During** (students actively work on a problem, task, or investigation while the teacher circulates, questions, and supports), and **After** (consolidation — students share strategies, the teacher highlights key ideas, and connections are made explicit). The structure is most closely associated with mathematics instruction (e.g., Ontario's "Getting Started / Working On It / Reflect and Connect" framework) but generalizes to any problem- or inquiry-based lesson. It is a lesson-level architecture rather than a single technique: each phase draws on distinct elements, and the value comes from the deliberate handoffs between them.

## Design Implications

The structure operationalizes two well-supported ideas: activating prior knowledge before new content improves learning [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M], and consolidation after active work is where partial understanding gets organized into usable form. The During phase enacts [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md), which improves exam performance relative to lecture alone [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The After phase is frequently truncated in practice, yet it carries much of the conceptual payoff — without it, learners leave with strategies but not the connections among them.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, solvable task for the During phase that permits multiple solution approaches
- A Before phase that activates relevant prior knowledge without giving away the solution path ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
- Time protected for consolidation — typically as much as a third of the lesson
- A teacher role during the During phase of monitoring and questioning, not rescuing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))

#### Constraints
- Compressing the After phase into "here's the answer" forfeits the consolidation benefit and can leave misconceptions intact [-M]
- A Before phase that over-scaffolds (teaching the method the task was meant to elicit) turns the During phase into procedure-following rather than problem solving [~M]
- Low-floor tasks are required; a task most students cannot enter makes the During phase unproductive for much of the class [-M]
- The structure adds time overhead relative to direct presentation; for novices with minimal prior knowledge, a full exploration cycle may overload working memory [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mathematics lesson study versions** (e.g., Toronto District School Board's three-part lesson) emphasize bansho/consolidation boards where student work is sequenced publicly in the After phase
- **Launch–Explore–Summarize** (Connected Mathematics Project) applies the same arc to middle-school investigations
- **5E Learning Cycle** extends the arc to five phases (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate) for science
- In literacy blocks, the arc appears as mini-lesson → independent work → share

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners in problem-based subjects, especially mathematics [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Mixed-ability classes — the During phase permits differentiated support while students work on a common task
- Learners with weak prior knowledge need a deliberately brief, targeted Before phase; too little activation leaves them searching blindly [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through comparing student-generated strategies [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Procedural fluency built on self-generated methods rather than transmitted procedures
- Mathematical discourse and explanation (the After phase's share-and-connect routine)

### Instructions
1. **Before (5–10 min):** Activate relevant prior knowledge with a brief related task or discussion ([Activation](../elements/activation.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)); present the problem and clarify expectations without demonstrating the solution method.
2. **During (15–25 min):** Students work — individually, in pairs, or in groups ([Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)) — while the teacher circulates, asks assessing and advancing questions, and selects students whose work will anchor the consolidation ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
3. **After (10–15 min):** Sequence selected student solutions from concrete to abstract, have students explain and compare approaches ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)), and name the key mathematical idea ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)); assign a consolidation or extension task.

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Connected Mathematics Project (CMP)** — [https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/program/microsites/MKTSP-OIP01M0.html] — middle-school units structured as Launch, Explore, Summarize.
- **Ontario Ministry of Education three-part lesson** — "Getting Started / Working On It / Reflect and Connect," widely used in Ontario elementary mathematics and in lesson study cycles.
- **Illustrative Mathematics (IM K–12)** — [https://illustrativemathematics.org] — lesson plans with a warm-up (Before), activity (During), and lesson synthesis (After), including explicit "cool-down" checks.
