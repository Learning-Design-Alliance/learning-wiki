# Text Talk

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Text Talk is a read-aloud approach developed by Isabel Beck and Margaret McKeown in which the teacher reads a storybook aloud but stops at points of complexity to ask open-ended, text-based questions that require children to reason about ideas rather than respond to pictures. It deliberately separates picture support from text comprehension — children are asked to construct meaning from the language — and weaves in brief, child-friendly explanations of sophisticated "Tier 2" vocabulary encountered in the story.

## Design Implications

Text Talk operationalizes dialogic questioning and vocabulary-in-context instruction: comprehension questions force children to integrate and infer from language, while embedded word explanations leverage the story context to make new word meanings memorable [Dialogic questioning during read-alouds improves comprehension.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [+M]. The strategy's signature move — asking questions *before* showing the page's illustration — prevents children from answering from pictures instead of processing the text [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, well-written storybook with language slightly above children's independent level
- Pre-planned stopping points and open-ended questions targeting inferential or evaluative reasoning
- A shortlist of 2–3 Tier 2 words per book, with brief, contextualized definitions and follow-up usage prompts
- Time for extended discussion — the strategy depends on multi-turn exchanges, not single question–answer cycles

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops sharply when questions are closed, factual, or asked rapid-fire without follow-up probing [~M]
- Less effective when picture support is available during questioning — children answer from images and bypass the text [~M]
- Requires teacher skill in responsive follow-up; scripted questioning without contingent feedback yields weaker gains [-M]
- Vocabulary gains from incidental storybook exposure alone are small; deliberate word explanation is necessary [Incidental exposure to words in storybooks produces limited vocabulary growth without explicit attention.](../claims/incidental-vocabulary-exposure-limited.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interspersed questioning** (classic Text Talk): stops during reading at planned points
- **Post-reading discussion**: questions held until the end for shorter attention spans
- **Vocabulary-forward variant**: post-reading activities extend target words across the week (Text Talk lessons typically revisit words in later activities)
- **Adaptation for older readers**: same questioning logic applied to chapter book read-alouds

### Target Learners
- Children ages 4–7 (K–2), especially those with limited exposure to decontextualized academic language [Dialogic questioning during read-alouds improves comprehension.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-language-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Learners with smaller vocabularies benefit most from explicit Tier 2 word instruction embedded in narrative
- Less suited to fluent independent readers, who should analyze text directly rather than through read-aloud mediation

### Target Learning Goals
- Listening comprehension and inferential reasoning about narrative text
- Receptive vocabulary growth, particularly high-utility Tier 2 words
- Oral language development: extended discourse, justification of answers

### Instructions
1. Select a storybook with rich language and identify 2–3 Tier 2 words and 4–6 stopping points for questions.
2. Read aloud, pausing at stopping points to ask an open-ended question about the story's ideas — hold back the illustration until children have answered from the text.
3. Follow up children's responses with probes ("Why do you think that?" "What in the story tells you that?") to extend reasoning.
4. When a target word appears, give a brief, child-friendly explanation in context and connect it to children's experiences.
5. After reading, revisit target words with questions requiring children to use them in new contexts ([Spaced Retrieval](spaced-retrieval.md) across subsequent days strengthens retention).
6. Extend with follow-up activities such as [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) or drawing/writing that reuses story language.
