# Writing Conferences

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 8 report an effect size

## Description
A writing conference is a brief, scheduled one-on-one conversation between teacher and student focused on the student's own writing, typically while it is still in draft. The teacher asks questions, listens, and offers one or two targeted points of feedback or instruction, treating the student as the writer-in-charge rather than grading a finished product. Conferences range from quick 2–3 minute check-ins to extended 10–15 minute sessions, and are a core component of writing workshop models [Atwell, 1987; Calkins, 1994].

## Design Implications

Writing conferences enact [formative feedback](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) at the process level, which is where feedback has its strongest effects on revision and self-regulation [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because the feedback is dialogic and immediately negotiated, the teacher can diagnose the writer's intention and adjust support contingently rather than delivering generic comments [Contingent scaffolding improves learning outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The conversation format also lets the teacher model expert moves — rereading aloud, questioning a sentence, trying a revision — making the conference a site of [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A writing workshop structure with substantial in-class writing time so students have drafts in progress to discuss
- A manageable conference load — short conferences, status-of-the-class routines, or peer conferences — so the teacher can meet every student regularly
- Records of each conference (one or two teaching points, next steps) so instruction builds across meetings rather than restarting
- A classroom culture where other students work independently during conferences ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) and practiced routines)
- Dedicated class time with students working independently on writing while the teacher rotates through conferences
- A predictable conference structure: research (ask what the student is working on), decide on one teaching point, teach, and link to a goal
- A record-keeping system (conference notebook, sticky notes, or digital log) to track what each student has worked on and follow up over time
- Established routines so students know what to do while waiting and can self-manage during independent writing

#### Constraints
- Conferences consume large amounts of instructional time; without tight routines the rest of the class off-loads or disengages [-M]
- Feedback limited to surface corrections (spelling, grammar) during conferences produces little transfer to future writing [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- Students with no draft or no investment in their topic have nothing to confer about; conferences stall into teacher monologue [-M]
- Whole-class mini-lessons remain necessary — one-to-one instruction alone leaves common needs unaddressed and is inefficient for shared misconceptions [~M]
- Time-intensive: in a class of 30, a full rotation at 5 minutes per student takes multiple sessions, leaving some students unattended for days [-M]
- Effectiveness collapses if the teacher takes over the text and dictates revisions — students learn dependence rather than self-assessment [~M]
- Requires strong classroom management; off-task behavior during independent writing time erodes the conditions for conferencing [-W]
- Whole-class mini-lessons must still cover shared needs; conferences alone cannot deliver a coherent curriculum [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **[5-Minute Writing Conferences](5-minute_writing_conferences.md)** — brief, high-frequency check-ins with a single teaching point, trading depth for coverage
- **Research-decide-teach structure** — the teacher opens with a question about the writer's intention, names one teaching point, and ends with the student applying it
- **Peer conferences** — structured partner conversations using protocols (e.g., "listener asks questions, does not fix"), extending conference benefits when teacher time is scarce [+W]
- **Small-group strategy conferences** — the teacher convenes 3–4 students with a shared need, blending conference responsiveness with small-group efficiency
- **Peer conferences** — students use the same research–suggest structure with partners, trading teacher time for peer feedback quality
- **Roving vs. scheduled conferences** — brief on-the-spot check-ins versus pre-scheduled longer meetings
- **On-demand conferences** — students request a conference when stuck, building self-regulation
- **Small-group conferences** — 3–4 students with a shared need meet together, tripling efficiency

### Target Learners
- Developing writers in grades 3–12 and adult learners, who benefit most from process-level, dialogic feedback [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Reluctant or struggling writers, for whom individual attention and an audience for their intentions builds engagement and willingness to revise [+W]
- Advanced writers, who use conferences to set individual goals; for them the teacher shifts to questioning rather than directing [~M]
- K–12 writers at all stages, especially developing writers who benefit from immediate, dialogic feedback [Feedback is most effective when it answers where the learner is going, how they are doing, and where to next.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Struggling writers who need individualized scaffolding that whole-class instruction cannot provide
- Less effective for students who lack the independent work habits to sustain writing time while the teacher is occupied [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision skills: diagnosing and improving drafts rather than one-shot production
- Self-regulated writing: goal-setting, monitoring, and independent use of strategies [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Writer identity and voice: developing ownership of topics and decisions
- Revision skill: learning to reread and improve one's own drafts
- Self-assessment and goal setting: internalizing the questions a good writing coach asks
- Procedural knowledge of the writing process: planning, drafting, revising, editing [Process-writing approaches improve writing quality across grade levels.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Writer identity and motivation: seeing oneself as a writer with agency over the text

### Instructions
1. Establish workshop routines and in-class writing time so every student has work in progress ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Open each conference with a research question — "Tell me about what you're working on" — to elicit the writer's intention before offering anything.
3. Listen and [observe the draft](../elements/assess-performance.md) to identify one high-leverage teaching point; resist correcting everything.
4. Make the teaching point, often by modeling a quick revision on the student's own text ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
5. Have the student try the move immediately during the conference, then step back as they apply it ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md)).
6. Record the teaching point and a next step; check on it at the next conference and progressively hand responsibility to the writer ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

- **Nancie Atwell's Center for Teaching and Learning** — the workshop classroom documented in *In the Middle*, where daily conferences replaced whole-class grammar instruction.
- **College writing centers** (e.g., [Purdue OWL's on-campus Writing Lab](https://owl.purdue.edu)) — tutor-led conferences on drafts in progress, applying the same dialogic feedback model in higher education.
- **Teachers College Reading & Writing Project (Units of Study, [https://www.unitsofstudy.com](https://www.unitsofstudy.com))** — Calkins' workshop curriculum builds conferences into every writing session, with published conference protocols and record-keeping templates.

## Description
Writing conferences are brief, individualized meetings between a teacher and a student focused on the student's writing. The teacher listens to the student read or discuss their draft, asks questions that prompt reflection, and offers one or two specific, actionable suggestions tied to a clear goal. Rooted in the process-writing tradition of Graves, Atwell, and Calkins, the conference treats the student as the primary decision-maker about their text, with the teacher acting as coach rather than editor.

## Design Implications

Conferences deliver feedback at the moment it can be used — while the student is still composing — which makes them far more actionable than delayed written comments [Feedback is most effective when it answers where the learner is going, how they are doing, and where to next.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Their power comes from dialogic, individualized interaction: the student articulates intentions, the teacher probes, and revision follows immediately. Because each conference is short (3–7 minutes), the design problem is managing the rest of the class and keeping the conference focused on one teaching point rather than correcting everything.

6. Record the teaching point and follow up at the next conference.
