# Hoax Website Analysis

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners analyze famous hoax or fabricated websites (e.g., the DHMO "dihydrogen monoxide" site, the Pacific Northwest Tree Octopus, or satirical news pages) to identify the visual, linguistic, and structural cues that signal a site is not a legitimate source. The strategy typically pairs guided inspection of the hoax with comparison against a credible site, then asks learners to articulate the discriminating features and verify claims through independent searching.

## Design Implications

Hoax websites function as [erroneous examples](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]: analyzing a deliberately flawed artifact directs attention to features learners would otherwise overlook and builds the conceptual criteria underlying source evaluation. The strategy works best when hoaxes are contrasted with credible sites, since [multiple contrasting cases support abstraction](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M] of the underlying evaluation criteria rather than surface heuristics. Research on civic online reasoning shows that students rarely evaluate sources spontaneously; explicit structured analysis is needed [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic hoax artifacts with realistic design (professional-looking hoaxes teach more than obviously absurd ones)
- A comparison set including credible sources, so learners contrast rather than only detect
- Guiding questions that push beyond surface features ("Who is behind this site? What do other sources say?") toward verification behavior
- Internet access for learners to practice lateral moves — leaving the site to check claims elsewhere

#### Constraints
- Teaching surface-feature checklists (e.g., "look for typos, check the .org domain") can backfire: modern hoax sites are professionally designed, and cue-based heuristics fail against sophisticated fabrication [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [-S]
- Learners with high confidence in their own evaluation skill show the largest gaps between perceived and actual ability, so the strategy must confront overconfidence rather than assume it [Breakstone et al., 2021] [-M]
- One-off lessons produce fragile gains; evaluation habits require distributed practice across varied contexts [~M]
- If the hoax is too obviously fake, learners never experience the failure that motivates deeper strategies [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Lateral reading focus:** learners leave the page immediately to investigate the source (the strategy professional fact-checkers use) rather than reading vertically within the page [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019] [+S]
- **Reverse engineering:** learners examine the site's "About" page, domain registration, and linked organizations to trace authorship
- **Creation-based variant:** learners build their own persuasive hoax site, then swap and evaluate peers' sites — deepening understanding of how credibility cues are manufactured
- **Current-events integration:** applying the same analysis to live social media claims rather than archived hoaxes

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students, who research shows perform poorly on source evaluation despite high self-confidence [Breakstone et al., 2021] [+S]
- Learners with high prior confidence benefit especially, because confronting a convincing hoax creates [cognitive conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) that destabilizes unreliable heuristics [~M]
- Not restricted by prior knowledge of the topic; evaluation skill is domain-general but transfers best with practice across domains [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Source evaluation and media literacy: discriminating credible from non-credible information
- Epistemic cognition: understanding how information is produced, funded, and framed
- Transferable inquiry skills: formulating and executing verification strategies ([ask-experts](../principles/ask-experts.md) behavior — checking what independent sources say)

### Instructions
1. Present a professionally designed hoax website without revealing its status; ask learners to assess its credibility and record their reasoning ([self-explanation improves conceptual understanding](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]).
2. Reveal the hoax and debrief: which cues misled learners, and why were those cues unreliable?
3. Contrast with a credible source on a similar topic, guiding learners to abstract the discriminating criteria ([multiple contrasting cases support abstraction](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]).
4. Model lateral reading: demonstrate leaving the site to search for the organization, check fact-checking databases (Snopes, FactCheck.org), and trace domain ownership.
5. Have learners repeat the full analysis on a new, unseen site and compare their verification moves against the modeled strategy.
6. Distribute follow-up practice across the term with varied artifact types (satire, advocacy sites, AI-generated content) rather than a single lesson.

## Examples
- **[Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — free lessons and assessments built on hoax and manipulated-content tasks; the research program behind lateral reading instruction.
- **The Pacific Northwest Tree Octopus (zapatopi.net/treeoctopus)** — the classic study artifact: in the original research, the majority of middle schoolers rated this parody site as credible, making it a powerful discussion anchor.
- **Dihydrogen Monoxide Research Division (dhmo.org)** — a factually accurate but misleadingly framed site used to teach the difference between accuracy and deceptive presentation.
- **Snopes (https://www.snopes.com)** and **FactCheck.org** — verification destinations learners use during the lateral-reading phase.
