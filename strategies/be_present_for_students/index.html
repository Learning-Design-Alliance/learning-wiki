Be Present for Students¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (4 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Being present means making oneself predictably and visibly available to students for individual, informal conversations — before and after class, in hallways, during office hours — particularly when course content is emotionally demanding. When students discuss sensitive topics such as race, they may experience anxiety or discomfort; the instructor's role is to signal that this discomfort is normal, expected, and workable, and that a trusted adult is available to help them process it. Presence is carried out through consistent availability, empathetic listening, and brief check-ins rather than formal counseling.

Design Implications¶

Perceived teacher availability and warmth are among the strongest correlates of student engagement, motivation, and belonging across grade levels Learner-centered teacher-student relationships are associated with higher engagement, motivation, and achievement. [+S]. Presence works by protecting students' sense of belonging at the moments it is most threatened — for example, when a discussion surfaces feelings of stereotype threat or social exclusion — and belonging is a causal lever on persistence and performance, not merely a byproduct of it Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [+S]. The strategy is deliberately relational rather than instructional: it does not teach content, but it keeps students in a psychological state where learning from difficult content is possible.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Predictable, low-barrier availability: consistent times and places where students know they can talk (e.g., office hours, arrival/dismissal windows)
  • Empathetic listening skills: the ability to receive emotional disclosure without immediately problem-solving, judging, or escalating
  • Clear boundaries and referral knowledge: knowing when a conversation exceeds the teacher's role and requires a counselor or other professional
  • Institutional support for the time this takes; presence that exists only when convenient is not perceived as presence

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness depends on relationship history built before the difficult moment; availability introduced only after a crisis reads as performative [~M]
  • Teachers without training in responding to disclosures of trauma or discrimination can inadvertently minimize or amplify distress [-W]
  • In large-enrollment or online settings, individual presence scales poorly; unmediated attempts (e.g., "come talk to me anytime" with no structure) produce low uptake [-M]
  • If availability is perceived as surveillance or as conditional on "correct" emotional responses, it suppresses rather than opens communication [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structured check-ins: brief one-on-one or written check-ins (Check-In) at the start of class, which normalize emotional processing for the whole group rather than singling anyone out
  • Written channels: exit tickets, anonymous question forms, or email for students who will not initiate face-to-face conversation
  • Peer-mediated presence: training peer facilitators so support does not depend solely on the teacher's availability
  • Online equivalents: predictable virtual office hours, discussion-forum presence, and personalized feedback turnaround as proxies for physical availability

Target Learners¶

  • Students navigating emotionally charged content (race, identity, current events) who need a trusted adult to process reactions Brief empathic discipline interventions improve teacher-student relationships and reduce suspensions for marginalized students. [+S]
  • Students at risk of disengagement or exclusion, for whom a single relationship with a teacher can be protective Brief belonging interventions improve academic outcomes for at-risk students. [+S]
  • Students who rarely seek help; presence must be actively offered to them, since they will not self-refer [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Affective and social-emotional goals: emotional regulation, trust, and classroom community
  • Enabling goals: sustaining participation in difficult discussions (Class Discussion) by lowering the emotional cost of engagement
  • Belonging and persistence: keeping students enrolled and engaged through moments of doubt

Instructions¶

  1. Establish availability before it is needed. Announce and keep consistent open times; presence must predate the difficult conversation to be credible.
  2. Normalize discomfort in the group setting. Frame anxiety about sensitive topics as expected and workable, so individual conversations do not feel like exceptions (Check-In).
  3. Listen before responding. In individual conversations, lead with empathetic listening rather than correction or reassurance; students' perception of being understood drives the relationship effect Learner-centered teacher-student relationships are associated with higher engagement, motivation, and achievement. [+S]
  4. Follow up. A brief check-in a day or two later signals the concern was genuinely heard, not merely received.
  5. Refer when appropriate. Recognize the boundary between emotional support and counseling, and have referral pathways ready.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-ins — the structured, group-level form of presence that makes emotional processing routine
  • Building empathy — presence models the empathy that discussions of race ask students to practice
  • Community of inquiry — teacher presence is one of the three presences (social, cognitive, teaching) that sustain online and blended learning communities
  • Build faculty-staff connections and peer community to support persistence of teacher education students

Related Elements¶

  • Check-In — the recurring mechanism through which availability becomes visible
  • Coaching — the one-on-one conversational mode presence often takes
  • Accommodations — individualized adjustments that frequently emerge from presence conversations
  • Class Discussion — the setting whose emotional risks presence is designed to manage

Examples¶

  • A middle-school teacher holds open classroom time 15 minutes before school and stays after dismissal, and students processing feelings after a discussion of racial discrimination know they can drop in without an appointment.
  • In a large lecture course on American history, the instructor pairs each difficult unit with a required short written reflection and responds individually to a sample of entries, creating written one-on-one contact at scale.
  • Facing History and Ourselves curricula explicitly build teacher availability and reflection time into units on race and identity, pairing difficult content with structured opportunities for students to process reactions with the teacher.
