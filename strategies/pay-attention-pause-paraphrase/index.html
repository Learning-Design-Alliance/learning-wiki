Pay Attention, Pause, Paraphrase¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 8 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 1 review), q2–q3 · 4 of 8 report an effect size

Description¶

Pay Attention, Pause, Paraphrase is a structured conversation strategy for pairs or small groups that enforces active listening. Only one learner speaks at a time; listeners do not formulate responses while the speaker talks, but focus solely on understanding. After the speaker finishes, the listener pauses, then paraphrases what was said — and the speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase — before offering their own thoughts. Sentence starters ("What I hear you saying is…") can scaffold the paraphrase move.

Design Implications¶

The protocol converts discussion from a competition for airtime into a comprehension task: because listeners must reproduce the speaker's meaning before responding, they allocate working memory to encoding rather than rehearsing a rebuttal. Requiring paraphrase before reply also slows the exchange, which increases elaborative processing of peers' ideas and reduces the common failure mode of serial monologues [~M]. Structured listening roles are a core feature of effective cooperative designs Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves outcomes. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Groups of two to four, with a clear speaking order (e.g., assigned numbers) so only one voice is active at a time
  • Explicit modeling of the three moves — Coaching or instructor demonstration before first use
  • Optional sentence starters matched to learners' language proficiency
  • A low-stakes discussion prompt worth genuinely understanding, not just answering

Constraints¶

  • Feels stilted and artificial at first; without repeated use, learners treat the paraphrase as a formality rather than a comprehension check [-W]
  • The pause-and-paraphrase cycle roughly doubles turn length, so it is unsuited to rapid brainstorming or time-pressured exchanges [-W]
  • Dominant speakers can still paraphrase superficially and pivot to their own view; facilitation or rotating a designated "listener" role is needed to ensure equal participation [-W]
  • Overly rigid enforcement can suppress spontaneous building on ideas, which is itself valuable in discussion [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pairs (interview format): one speaker, one listener, then swap — lowest floor, good for multilingual learners
  • Triads with roles: speaker, paraphraser, recorder; rotate roles each round
  • Whole-class fishbowl: an inner circle models the protocol while observers tally paraphrase accuracy
  • Written variant: listeners paraphrase on paper or in chat before speaking, adding a brief Individual Reflection step

Target Learners¶

  • All levels; especially valuable for learners still developing discussion and communication skills
  • Multilingual learners, when sentence starters are adapted to their language level — paraphrasing a peer's utterance provides comprehensible input and output practice Dialogic reading improves expressive language through structured conversational turns. [+M]
  • Adolescents and adults in discussion-heavy courses where perspective-taking matters; high-quality listening increases speakers' willingness to articulate and revise their views [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Active listening and accurate comprehension of others' reasoning
  • Clear oral communication and turn-taking norms
  • Perspective-taking and empathy in discussion of contested or personal topics Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [+M]
  • Community-building norms that underpin sustained Collaborative Learning

Instructions¶

  1. Model the protocol. Demonstrate all three moves with a volunteer, thinking aloud about what "paying attention" looks like (body orientation, no rehearsing a reply). See Coaching.
  2. Assign speaking order. Number learners in each group; only the numbered learner speaks, keeping turns short (30–60 seconds).
  3. Pay attention. Listeners hold silence and attend to meaning, not to drafting their response.
  4. Pause. After the speaker finishes, listeners take a deliberate beat before speaking.
  5. Paraphrase. A listener restates the speaker's point ("So your main concern is…") and the speaker confirms or corrects it.
  6. Respond, then rotate. The listener now offers their own view and becomes the speaker; continue through the numbers.
  7. Debrief. Ask groups where paraphrases missed the mark — the mismatches are where meaning broke down.

Related Strategies¶

Related Elements¶

  • Articulation — the paraphrase move is a form of learner articulation of another's reasoning

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Collaborative Learning — supplies the listening norms that make group work genuinely interactive
  • Discussion-Based Learning — establishes turn-taking and comprehension norms before open discussion
  • Community of Inquiry — supports the social presence needed for cognitive exchange

Examples¶

  • Restorative-practice circles (secondary classrooms): students respond to a prompt one at a time; each responder first paraphrases the previous speaker. Protocol descriptions and classroom vignettes appear in Rebecca Alber's Edutopia writing on teaching active listening (https://www.edutopia.org/article/teaching-active-listening).
  • Teacher professional development: facilitators pair participants to paraphrase each other's classroom dilemmas before problem-solving, using adult visitors as extra partners when group sizes are uneven.
  • Multilingual classrooms: sentence starters ("I hear you saying…", "Can you tell me if I got this right?") are posted and adapted to proficiency level.
