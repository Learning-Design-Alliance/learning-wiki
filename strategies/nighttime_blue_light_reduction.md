# Nighttime Blue Light Reduction

## Description
Nighttime blue light reduction limits learners' exposure to short-wavelength (~460–480 nm) light from screens and LED lighting in the hours before sleep. Blue light suppresses melatonin secretion and delays circadian phase, so mitigation strategies — screen curfews, night-mode filters, blue-blocking glasses, warm ambient lighting — protect sleep onset and duration. Because sleep is when newly encoded material is consolidated into long-term memory, protecting sleep is a learning strategy, not merely a wellness measure.

## Design Implications

Sleep deprivation and fragmentation degrade attention, working memory, and consolidation, undermining next-day learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning when working memory resources are exceeded.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Evening screen exposure is a modifiable contributor: experimental studies show blue-enriched screen light in the evening delays melatonin onset, reduces REM sleep, and increases next-morning sleepiness relative to filtered or dim conditions [+M]. Instructional programs that treat sleep hygiene as part of study skills — scheduling [Distributed Practice](distributed-practice.md) across days rather than cramming before a screen-lit late night — align encoding schedules with consolidation biology [Distributed practice improves long-term retention compared with massed practice.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A defined pre-sleep window (typically 1–2 hours) with reduced screen exposure or filtered emission
- Alternatives for evening activity — paper reading, printed materials, audio ([Audiobooks](audiobooks.md) serve well here)
- Learner awareness of the mechanism: why screens delay sleep, not just a rule to follow
- Consistency; circadian benefits accrue from regular sleep timing, not occasional compliance

#### Constraints
- Screen curfews are hard to enforce with adolescents, whose media use averages several hours daily and whose circadian phase is already delayed developmentally [-W]
- Blue-light filters alone produce small or inconsistent improvements in sleep outcomes when total screen time and content engagement remain high [~W] — stimulating content and cognitive arousal may matter as much as wavelength
- Learners whose coursework or work requires evening screen use (night-shift students, online programs) cannot simply eliminate exposure; mitigation must be partial
- For some learners, replacing screens with unstructured time increases rumination, which delays sleep independently of light [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Hardware: built-in night modes (Night Shift, Night Light), blue-blocking glasses, warm bias lighting
- Behavioral: screen curfews, device charging outside the bedroom, grayscale mode
- Structural: institutional policies — LMS deadlines before evening, no late-night notification pushes, sleep-education modules in advisory or study-skills curricula

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults, whose developmental circadian delay compounds evening light exposure [-W]
- Heavy evening media users; American teens average roughly nine hours of digital media per day, making pre-bed exposure near-universal
- Online and adult learners who study late at night after work
- Less applicable to learners who already complete study well before bedtime or use dim, warm lighting environments

### Target Learning Goals
- Indirect but foundational: protecting the consolidation of any newly learned material [Distributed practice improves long-term retention compared with massed practice.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Next-day attention and working-memory capacity for new encoding
- Self-regulation habits: managing one's own study environment and schedule [Self-regulated learning improves academic outcomes.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-academic-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Teach the mechanism briefly — melatonin suppression, circadian delay, and the role of sleep in consolidation — so the rule is understood rather than imposed ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame a study-skills unit around "protect the consolidation window")
2. Set a personal screen curfew 60–90 minutes before target bedtime, or enable night-mode filtering as a minimum fallback
3. Substitute compatible evening activities: printed or audio review of the day's material ([Audiobooks](audiobooks.md)), which doubles as spaced review [Distributed practice improves long-term retention compared with massed practice.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
4. Move devices out of the bedroom or enable do-not-disturb to prevent late-night re-engagement
5. Track sleep timing and next-day alertness for two weeks ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines work well for self-monitoring), then adjust the curfew window

## Examples
- **Sleep-education curricula** such as the "Sleep for Success" programs used in school health courses teach circadian basics alongside screen-time reduction, with measurable gains in sleep duration [+W]
- **Apple Night Shift / Android Night Light / f.lux** (https://justgetflux.com) — OS-level blue-light filters; useful as a fallback, though evidence for filters alone is weaker than for full screen curfews [~W]
- **University study-skills courses** that pair exam-scheduling guidance with sleep-hygiene modules, discouraging all-nighters before assessments
