# Explicit Strategy Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 11 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 2 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 4 of 11 report an effect size

## Description
Explicit strategy instruction teaches learners specific tools and techniques for solving problems and regulating their own learning. The instructor names the strategy, models its use (ideally with visible reasoning), provides guided practice with feedback, and — critically — explains *when* and *why* the strategy applies, so learners can deploy it independently across contexts. The goal is not just possession of a technique but conditional knowledge: knowing how, when, and why to use it.

## Design Implications

Explicit strategy instruction works because it converts tacit expert processes into learnable procedures, reducing unguided search and building self-regulation [Self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md). Meta-analyses of learning-skills and metacognitive-strategy interventions show reliable gains when instruction includes modeling and conditional knowledge, and weaker or even negative effects when strategies are taught as decontextualized tips [Strategy instruction improves outcomes most when it embeds conditional knowledge and content.](../claims/strategy-instruction-needs-conditional-knowledge.md) [+M]. The sequence matters: name → model → guided practice → independent use → fade support [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-specified strategy that can be named, sequenced, and modeled (e.g., "read the problem, identify what's asked, represent it, check")
- Instructor modeling with articulated reasoning ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)) — learners must see the *decision points*, not just the steps
- Multiple practice opportunities with feedback at the process level [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Explicit discussion of when the strategy applies and when it does not (conditional knowledge)
- A plan for fading support as learners take over strategy use [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- A well-specified strategy with a small number of memorable steps
- [Modeling](../elements/coaching.md) of the strategy in use, ideally with the instructor verbalizing decisions ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Guided practice with feedback before independent application ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Feedback](../elements/assessment.md))

#### Constraints
- Strategies taught in isolation from content, without conditional knowledge, show weak or null transfer — and sometimes negative effects [Strategy instruction improves outcomes most when it embeds conditional knowledge and content.](../claims/strategy-instruction-needs-conditional-knowledge.md) [-M]
- Highly able learners may already use the strategies and gain little; over-guidance can depress performance [Guidance effects reverse as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Time-intensive: modeling, practice, and feedback across multiple contexts require significant instructional time
- Learners often abandon strategies when tasks get hard unless instruction includes coping routines and repeated practice across varied contexts
- Strategies taught as rote procedures, without conditional knowledge, show weak transfer to new tasks and content [Strategy instruction effects are weaker when conditional knowledge is not taught.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-learning.md) [-M]
- Time-intensive: modeling and guided practice consume instructional time that competes with content coverage

#### Implementation Variability
- **Embedded vs. stand-alone:** strategy instruction woven into subject-matter teaching outperforms separate "study skills" courses [Strategy instruction improves outcomes most when it embeds conditional knowledge and content.](../claims/strategy-instruction-needs-conditional-knowledge.md) [+M]
- **Reciprocal formats:** learners take turns leading strategy application in dialogue (e.g., reciprocal teaching), which builds both execution and monitoring
- **Strategy inventories:** classes co-construct lists of named strategies ("pull out the important information and organize it into a chart") that learners add to as they discover new moves
- **Duration:** multi-session programs with extended practice outperform brief single-lesson introductions
- **Embedded vs. standalone:** strategies taught within authentic content tasks transfer better than those taught in separate "study skills" lessons [~M]
- **Reciprocal formats:** learners take turns applying the strategy with peer feedback, as in reciprocal teaching
- **Strategy menus:** learners build a repertoire and practice *selecting* among strategies, not just executing one
- **Self-regulated framing:** pair strategy steps with self-monitoring prompts so learners evaluate whether the strategy is working ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices and struggling learners who lack effective strategies or default to ineffective ones (e.g., rereading) [Strategy instruction improves outcomes most when it embeds conditional knowledge and content.](../claims/strategy-instruction-needs-conditional-knowledge.md) [+M]
- Learners with weak self-regulation who benefit from external structure before internalizing it [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for experts or learners who already use the strategy fluently [Guidance effects reverse as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Younger learners, who rarely invent effective strategies spontaneously

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency: executing multi-step problem-solving routines
- Metacognitive skill: monitoring comprehension and self-correcting when stuck
- Transfer: applying strategies flexibly across tasks and domains
- Self-directed learning: building a repertoire learners can select from independently
- Procedural fluency in domain tasks (comprehension, problem solving, writing)
- Metacognitive skill: monitoring comprehension and performance
- Transfer and self-directed learning: independent strategy selection and adaptation

### Instructions
1. **Name and justify the strategy.** State what it is called and why it works, connecting to the task at hand.
2. **Model with visible reasoning.** Demonstrate the strategy on a real task while verbalizing decision points ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)), including how you monitor and recover when stuck.
3. **Guide initial practice.** Work through tasks together, prompting learners to supply steps ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)) and giving process-level feedback [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
4. **Teach conditional knowledge.** Discuss when the strategy fits, when it doesn't, and what alternatives exist; use contrasting cases to sharpen the boundaries.
5. **Fade support.** Shift to independent application with decreasing prompts [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M], then revisit across new content to consolidate transfer.

## Examples
- **Reciprocal teaching of reading** (Palincsar & Brown, 1984): seventh graders taught summarizing, questioning, clarifying, and predicting through teacher modeling followed by student-led group dialogue; comprehension gains of roughly two standard deviations over controls.
- **Mathematics "getting unstuck" routine:** an educator poses a problem designed to make students feel stuck, then names and models recovery moves — "You pulled the important information from the problem out and organized it into a chart" — adding each named move to a class strategy list students can draw on later.
- **Self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) in writing** (Graham & Harris): students taught planning and revising strategies for composition, with modeling, memorization of strategy steps, and self-monitoring; one of the most extensively validated writing interventions.
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown, 1984): seventh-grade struggling readers, taught summarizing, questioning, clarifying, and predicting through teacher modeling followed by student-led group practice, gained up to two years in comprehension over ~20 sessions.
- **Self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) in writing** (Graham & Harris): teachers model planning strategies like POW+TREE for persuasive essays, then fade support as students write independently; a widely replicated approach in special education.
- **A teacher names "get unstuck" moves in math**: after presenting a problem designed to stall students, the teacher elicits and labels what students did ("You pulled the important information out and organized it into a chart"), building a shared, named repertoire of strategies the class can later invoke deliberately.
