Story Map¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 3 studies (2 review, 1 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size

Description¶

A story map is a graphic organizer that scaffolds narrative comprehension by prompting learners to identify and visually arrange the structural elements of a story — setting, characters, problem/conflict, key events, and resolution. Learners complete the map during or after reading, converting a linear text into an explicit spatial representation of narrative structure. Variants range from simple fill-in frames for young readers to complex maps tracking multiple plotlines, character perspectives, or story grammar components.

Design Implications¶

Story maps work by externalizing narrative structure, reducing the working-memory burden of tracking story elements while reading Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Their deeper value is teaching the schema of stories: repeated use of a consistent map helps learners internalize story grammar, which then transfers to unaided comprehension of new texts [+M]. Maps are most effective when completed with learners rather than for them — teacher-modeled completion followed by collaborative and then independent mapping produces stronger gains than handing out pre-filled organizers [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A map template whose categories match the actual structure of the target texts (a simple story grammar frame fits folktales; a multi-perspective map fits complex narratives)
  • Teacher modeling of how to identify and justify each element, not just where to write it (Think-Aloud while mapping)
  • Texts rich enough that the mapped elements are genuinely inferable — maps work poorly when answers are trivially stated or genuinely absent
  • A consistent template across multiple texts so the structure itself becomes learned, not just the content of one story

Constraints¶

  • Becomes a low-level worksheet exercise when learners copy surface details without discussing why an event counts as the turning point — comprehension gains disappear when mapping is mechanical [-M]
  • Can fragment reading into stop-and-fill segments that disrupt the flow and inferential processing narrative comprehension requires [~M] — interrupting reading too frequently undermines the coherent situation model maps are meant to support
  • Less useful for texts with non-canonical structure (in medias res openings, unreliable narrators, multiple timelines) unless the map is redesigned; forcing such texts into a standard frame can produce misunderstanding [-W]
  • Benefits fade for proficient readers who already possess story schemas; continued use becomes redundant busywork [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • During-reading maps support real-time monitoring; post-reading maps support summarization and retelling — the timing changes the cognitive work required
  • Digital story maps (e.g., StoryMapJS for place-based narratives) add geographic and multimedia dimensions for nonfiction and humanities contexts
  • Partially completed maps with key elements blanked function as Fading — support decreases as competence grows
  • Student-authored maps for planning their own writing reverse the direction, using the organizer as a pre-writing scaffold

Target Learners¶

  • Beginning and struggling readers in elementary grades, who benefit most from explicit narrative structure [+M]
  • English language learners, for whom the visual frame anchors vocabulary and discourse structure while text processing demands remain high [~M]
  • Students with reading comprehension difficulties; graphic organizer instruction shows moderate positive effects for learners with learning disabilities [+M]
  • Less beneficial for advanced readers with well-developed story schemas, for whom the organizer adds little beyond what they already do automatically [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Narrative comprehension: identifying and relating story grammar elements
  • Summarization and retelling: the map serves as a retrieval scaffold for oral or written retells
  • Reading comprehension monitoring: noticing when expected elements (a resolution, a motive) are missing or ambiguous
  • Pre-writing planning: applying the internalized story schema to compose original narratives

Instructions¶

  1. Select or design a map template whose categories match the story grammar of the target genre.
  2. Model completion on a short mentor text, thinking aloud about how you decided an event was the conflict rather than a mere complication (Think-Aloud).
  3. Read a new text with students, pausing at natural junctures to complete map sections collaboratively (Class Discussion) — keep pauses few and purposeful.
  4. Fade support: shift to partially completed maps, then blank maps completed independently (Fading).
  5. Use the completed map as a scaffold for retelling or summarizing (Summarization), then remove it to check whether the structure has been internalized.

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — the story map is a domain-specific advance organizer presented before or during text processing
  • Annotating — a complementary text-marking practice; mapping organizes across the whole text while annotating works line-by-line
  • Reciprocal Teaching — pairs well; mapping supplies the structure that prediction, questioning, and summarizing operate on
  • Retelling — the most common immediate application of a completed story map

Examples¶

  • Story grammar maps in basal reading programs — commercial elementary curricula (e.g., Houghton Mifflin Journeys) include story map blackline masters aligned to weekly texts, with teacher guides specifying modeled-to-independent sequencing.
  • StoryMapJS (Knight Lab, Northwestern University) — a digital tool for mapping narratives onto geographic locations, used in journalism and history education for place-based storytelling.
  • Fictional narrative planning in writing workshop — upper-elementary teachers use blank story maps as pre-writing planners before students draft original stories, transferring the comprehension scaffold to composition.
