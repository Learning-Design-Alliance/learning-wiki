# Concentric Circles

## Description
Learners form two circles, one inside the other, with each inner learner paired face-to-face with an outer learner. The teacher poses a question; pairs discuss it briefly; then a signal rotates one circle so every learner faces a new partner, and a new question begins. Two straight lines facing one another work identically — one line slides over one spot and the leftover learner wraps to the start. The format can also run as peer teaching, with each learner responsible for explaining one piece of content to successive partners.

## Design Implications

Concentric Circles converts whole-class discussion into many simultaneous, low-stakes peer exchanges, dramatically increasing the proportion of learners who talk per minute [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Because each rotation pairs learners with a new partner, learners must articulate their ideas repeatedly to different audiences, which promotes rehearsal and self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The rotation also functions as a built-in formative loop: the teacher can sample conversations between rounds and adjust the next question [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A physical space where learners can form two circles or facing lines
- A prepared sequence of 3–6 focused questions, sequenced from lower to higher cognitive demand
- A clear, consistent rotation signal and explicit time limits per round (typically 30–90 seconds)
- Norms for turn-taking so both partners speak in each round

#### Constraints
- Crowded or fixed-seat classrooms make the physical formation difficult; a facing-lines variant or online breakout pairing is a workaround, not an equivalent
- Learners with social anxiety or limited language proficiency may find rapid partner switching stressful; pre-briefing questions or allowing notes reduces this load
- Without strong questions and time limits, rounds degrade into social chat; the structure enforces participation but not substance [-M]
- Repeated articulation of the same answer can produce fluency illusions — learners mistake smooth delivery for understanding; a later individual task checks for this [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Discussion mode:** each question is new; partners exchange perspectives
- **Peer-teaching mode:** each learner owns one piece of content and teaches it to every partner they meet, receiving questions each round
- **Speed-dating review:** rapid rounds used for exam review, with answers revealed after each round
- **Debrief variant:** the inner circle rotates while the outer stays, letting one group hear how successive partners framed the same idea

### Target Learners
- Learners who rarely speak in whole-class discussion — the parallel structure lowers the stakes of contributing [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Adolescents and adults; with younger children, use fewer rounds, shorter questions, and the line variant for easier management
- Less suitable for groups needing sustained, deep single conversations — the format trades depth for breadth of participation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Fluency and retrieval practice: verbal rehearsal of key concepts across multiple partners
- Perspective-taking: hearing how different peers frame the same question
- Communication skills: concise, adaptive explanation to changing audiences
- Formative checking: surfacing misconceptions quickly across the whole class

### Instructions
1. Prepare 3–6 sequenced questions and set a visible timer.
2. Split the class in half; form two circles (or facing lines) with partners matched one-to-one.
3. Pose the first question; give pairs 30–90 seconds, requiring both voices ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
4. Signal the rotation — outer circle moves one place right (or one line slides one spot) — and pose the next question.
5. Circulate and listen during rounds; adjust question difficulty or add a prompt based on what you hear ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
6. Close with a whole-group synthesis in which several learners share the strongest idea they heard from a partner ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md)).

## Related Strategies
- Think-Pair-Share — the same pairing logic without rotation; a good precursor for classes new to structured talk
- Jigsaw — the peer-teaching variant of Concentric Circles generalizes into full jigsaw interdependence
- Gallery Walk — achieves similar breadth of exposure through movement past artifacts rather than partner rotation

## Examples
- **EL Education (Expeditionary Learning)** protocols include Concentric Circles (aka "Circles of Influence") as a standard Crew discussion structure for text-based seminars (https://eleducation.org).
- **Kagan Structures** — the facing-lines variant ("Pairs Compare" family) is used in cooperative-learning classrooms for rapid review rounds.
- **Language classrooms** — the peer-teaching variant is widely used for speaking fluency drills, with each rotation giving learners a fresh conversational partner.
