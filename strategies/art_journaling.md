# Art Journaling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Art journaling asks learners to maintain an ongoing journal in which visual art-making — drawing, collage, diagramming, color work — is combined with written reflection on course content, personal responses, and emotional reactions. The journal functions as a low-stakes, private-to-semiprivate space where learners externalize understanding in two modalities rather than prose alone. Entries are typically prompted (e.g., "sketch the water cycle and annotate where you got confused") but may include open pages for free response.

## Design Implications

Art journaling leverages dual coding: pairing verbal and visual representations creates two retrieval routes for the same content [Combining verbal and visual codes improves recall over either alone.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. The reflective writing component also functions as self-explanation, which improves conceptual understanding when learners articulate connections and gaps [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Because entries accumulate over time, the journal doubles as a record of developing understanding that can support [Spaced Revisiting](../elements/spaced-repetition.md) and formative [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Low-stakes framing: entries are assessed for engagement and reflection, not artistic skill or correctness, or learners will self-censor
- Prompts that connect visual work to content (draw-and-label, concept sketch, emotional color mapping) rather than decoration
- A routine cadence (weekly or per-unit) so the journal becomes sustained practice rather than a one-off activity
- Periodic instructor response or structured peer sharing to keep reflection substantive [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Time-intensive; in content-heavy courses it competes with direct instructional time and can become busywork if prompts are shallow
- Learners with low drawing confidence may disengage if the visual component is framed as artistic quality rather than thinking made visible [~M]
- Emotional content can surface unexpectedly; instructors need protocols for responding to distress, especially in trauma-sensitive contexts [-W]
- Weak prompts ("draw what you learned") produce decorative rather than generative entries; the strategy degrades to coloring without an explanatory requirement [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Science sketching:** draw-and-explain entries for processes and systems (e.g., field notebooks in biology)
- **Emotion-content journals:** color or image mapping of affective responses to literature or history, supporting [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)
- **Visual vocabulary journals:** learners illustrate and define terms, supporting retention through dual coding
- **Digital variants:** tools like [Seesaw](https://web.seesaw.me) or [Book Creator](https://bookcreator.com) allow photo, audio, and drawing entries with teacher feedback loops

### Target Learners
- Learners who struggle to express understanding in prose alone, including multilingual learners [Combining verbal and visual codes improves recall over either alone.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Younger learners whose drawing ability outpaces writing fluency
- Learners developing self-regulation, since ongoing journaling builds monitoring habits [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept consolidation: encoding content in dual verbal-visual formats
- Metacognition and self-monitoring: noticing and recording confusion, growth, and questions
- Affective processing: connecting personal response to academic content
- Formative evidence: surfacing misconceptions the instructor can act on

### Instructions
1. Establish purpose and low-stakes norms; show exemplar entries that model thinking, not artistry ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
2. Provide a prompt that requires both a visual and a written explanatory component ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. Give protected time for entry-making on a regular cadence ([Spaced Revisiting](../elements/spaced-repetition.md)).
4. Respond with brief written or verbal feedback focused on the thinking, or structure peer sharing of selected entries ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md)).
5. Periodically ask learners to revisit earlier entries and annotate what has changed in their understanding ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)).

## Related Strategies

- [Sketchnoting](sketchnoting.md) — real-time visual note-taking; art journaling is reflective and cumulative rather than in-the-moment

## Examples
- **Field journals in place-based science programs** (e.g., [BEETLES](https://beetlesproject.org) at Lawrence Hall of Science) — learners sketch organisms and annotate observations, then reflect on how their thinking changed.
- **Visual journals in ELA units** — students create image-and-text responses to novels, pairing symbolic imagery with written analysis of theme.
- **Seesaw digital journals** — primary classrooms combine drawing, voice annotation, and photos with teacher audio feedback.
