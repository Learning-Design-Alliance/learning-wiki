# Process Writing (Journals)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Process writing positions writing as a developing act rather than a finished product: learners write frequently and fluently — often in journals — prioritizing the communication of ideas over error-free text. The instructor responds with selective, delayed feedback focused on meaning and a small number of recently taught forms, rather than comprehensive error correction. The approach, rooted in Elbow's (1973) freewriting movement and process-oriented composition pedagogy, treats fluency and accuracy as separable goals that develop on different timelines.

## Design Implications

Frequent low-stakes writing builds fluency by reducing the working-memory burden of simultaneous composition and self-monitoring, letting learners generate ideas before attending to form [~M]. Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than at every surface error [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]; comprehensive grammar correction shows weak or null effects on writing improvement [~W], and selective, delayed correction tied to recent instruction is the practical compromise. The journal format also lowers affective barriers — learners write for a known, sympathetic reader rather than an evaluative audience.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A regular writing routine (several short entries per week) with genuine communicative purpose
- A feedback policy that is *selective* — a small set of target forms or meaning-level comments per entry — and *delayed*, so it does not interrupt drafting
- Recently taught language or content that the feedback targets, so correction connects to current instruction
- A response relationship: the teacher (or a peer) reads as an audience, not only as an evaluator

#### Constraints
- Comprehensive error correction on every entry does not improve accuracy and can reduce willingness to write [-W] — Truscott (1996) argues correction's benefits are largely illusory
- Pure fluency emphasis with no form-focused follow-up can fossilize errors; journals alone do not teach structures learners have never been taught [~M]
- If entries are graded for correctness, learners revert to safe, short, formulaic writing, defeating the fluency purpose [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when feedback is generic praise ("good job!") with no connection to instruction or to the learner's own text

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dialogue journals**: teacher writes personal responses, creating written conversation (widely studied in ESL settings)
- **Learning journals / reflective journals**: entries target content-area metacognition rather than language form
- **Writer's workshop**: journals feed into drafting, peer conferencing, and revision cycles for selected pieces
- **Micro-writing conferences**: brief one-to-one [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) on journal entries, as in [5-minute writing conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)

### Target Learners
- Developing L2 writers who need fluency volume before accuracy refinement [~M]
- Adolescent and adult writers; process approaches show measurable benefits for adolescents when combined with strategy instruction [Graham & Perin meta-analysis supports process writing elements.](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.12.1.23) [+S]
- Reluctant writers with high writing anxiety, for whom low-stakes frequency reduces avoidance
- Less suitable as a *stand-alone* method for learners who need explicit instruction in genres, structures, or conventions they have not yet acquired

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing fluency and idea generation: producing text with reduced hesitation
- Voice and audience awareness: writing for a real reader with [authentic audiences and purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)
- Reflective metacognition: using journals to monitor one's own learning and thinking
- Incremental accuracy: targeted forms improving through selective feedback over time

### Instructions
1. Establish the routine: set frequency and length expectations, and explicitly frame journals as low-stakes — assessed for effort and ideas, not correctness.
2. Model entry types with a [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) or shared freewriting session so learners see what "writing to communicate" looks like.
3. Learners write regularly; teacher reads and responds with meaning-focused comments plus at most one or two recently taught target forms per entry.
4. Deliver feedback as [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — questions and responses that invite revision or continuation, not verdicts.
5. Periodically select entries for revision cycles, applying [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) so form-focused work happens on text learners care about.
6. Use occasional brief conferences ([check-ins](../elements/check-in.md)) to individualize the feedback focus.

## Examples
- **Dialogue journals in ESL/EFL classrooms** (Hyland, 2003): students write weekly entries; the teacher responds personally, modeling natural written English without correcting every error — the response itself supplies comprehensible input.
- **National Writing Project sites** ([https://www.nwp.org](https://www.nwp.org)) — teacher-consultants use journaling and writer's workshop cycles in which journal entries seed published pieces.
- **Content-area learning journals** in science and history classes, where students write brief explanatory entries that the teacher responds to for conceptual accuracy, using journals as a window into student thinking.
