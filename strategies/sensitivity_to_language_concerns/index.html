    Sensitivity to Language Concerns¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Sensitivity to language concerns is a strategy in which instructors treat learners' diverse language backgrounds as assets and deliberately adjust instruction — vocabulary load, syntax, discourse norms, and assessment language — so that non-native speakers can access content while continuing to develop academic language. It combines two moves: an affirmational stance toward home languages and dialects, and concrete material design choices such as plain syntax, glossed vocabulary, and multiple representations of key ideas.

    Design Implications¶

    Language proficiency interacts directly with cognitive load: unfamiliar vocabulary and complex syntax consume working memory that would otherwise be spent on content learning Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]. Reducing unnecessary linguistic burden — without diluting content — frees capacity for disciplinary thinking, while connecting new terms to learners' prior knowledge and home language improves encoding Activation improves learning. [+M]. Research on bilingual education shows that continued development of the first language supports, rather than competes with, second-language academic growth Cummins' linguistic interdependence hypothesis. [+M].

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Knowledge of learners' language profiles (home language, proficiency levels, prior schooling)
    • Instructional materials using Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax — short sentences, common words where possible, defined technical terms
    • Explicit teaching of discipline-specific academic language, not just everyday English
    • Multiple representations of key concepts (visuals, manipulatives, gestures, Analogies) so meaning does not depend on a single linguistic channel
    • Classroom norms that position home languages and dialects as resources (e.g., allowing drafting or peer discussion in the first language)

    Constraints¶

    • Simplifying language too far removes exposure to academic syntax and can depress long-term language growth [~M] — the goal is comprehensible input, not simplified input
    • Treating multilingual learners as a homogeneous group fails: newcomers, long-term English learners, and students with interrupted schooling need different supports
    • If home-language use is permitted but never leveraged (no translanguaging tasks, no bilingual resources), the affirmational stance is cosmetic and produces little measurable benefit [~W]
    • Assessment in a learner's weaker language underestimates content knowledge; scores then drive inappropriate placement [-M]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Sheltered instruction (e.g., the SIOP Model): content and language objectives taught together with scaffolded English
    • Translanguaging approaches: learners draft, discuss, or brainstorm in their strongest language before producing output in the target language
    • Materials adaptation: glossaries, dual-language texts, sentence frames, and reduced linguistic density in Advance Organizers
    • Peer structures: pairing multilingual learners for discussion so meaning-making precedes formal output

    Target Learners¶

    • Multilingual learners and non-native speakers of the language of instruction, who otherwise spend disproportionate working memory decoding language rather than learning content Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]
    • Students with strong conversational fluency but underdeveloped academic language — a group easily misidentified as fully proficient Cummins' distinction between BICS and CALP. [+M]
    • All learners benefit from clearer language and multiple representations, though the largest gains accrue to those with the least proficiency in the language of instruction [~M]

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Content-area learning (science, mathematics, social studies) delivered in a second language
    • Academic language development: discipline-specific vocabulary, registers, and discourse conventions
    • Identity and belonging: learners' home languages treated as intellectual resources rather than deficits

    Instructions¶

    1. Survey learners' language backgrounds and proficiency levels at the start of instruction; record home languages and prior schooling.
    2. Audit upcoming materials for unnecessary linguistic load; rewrite for Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax and break dense passages into Chunking units.
    3. Identify the academic language demands of the lesson (key terms, sentence structures) and teach them explicitly alongside content.
    4. Activate learners' prior knowledge, inviting connections to home-language concepts Activating Prior Knowledge Activation improves learning. [+M].
    5. Provide multiple representations — visuals, demonstrations, Analogies — so comprehension does not hinge on one linguistic formulation.
    6. Allow structured use of home languages for drafting and peer discussion; require target-language output at the production stage.
    7. Assess content knowledge with linguistically accessible tasks, or accommodate by separating language proficiency from content mastery (Accommodations).

    Related Strategies¶

    • Accessible Syntax — the material-design counterpart: rewriting prose so syntax does not block comprehension
    • Activate Background Knowledge — connecting new content to what learners already know, including home-language knowledge
    • Accommodate Varying Technology Experience — a parallel strategy for reducing a different access barrier

    Examples¶

    • SIOP Model (Center for Applied Linguistics) — sheltered instruction in which every lesson carries both a content objective and a language objective, with scaffolded vocabulary, visuals, and interaction structures.
    • WIDA standards (wida.wisc.edu) — U.S. framework used by schools to differentiate instruction and assessment by English language proficiency level.
    • Science instruction for English learners — synthesis research shows inquiry-based science with explicit language support improves both science and language outcomes for multilingual learners Lee's synthesis of research on science education with English language learners. [+M]
