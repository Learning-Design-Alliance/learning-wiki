# Slug

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A slug is a brief, distinctive label — typically two to five words — that learners generate or are given to name an idea, example, case, or step. Borrowed from journalism, where a slug names a story before it is written, the strategy asks learners to compress meaning into a cue that can later trigger recall of the fuller content. Slugs function as retrieval handles: a labeled case ("the drowning-beach example") is easier to reinstate in memory than an unlabeled one.

## Design Implications

Slugs exploit the fact that retrieval works best when a memory has a distinctive, well-practiced cue, and that verbal labels bind together the elements of a complex example so they can be handled as one chunk [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. The act of *generating* a label is itself an elaborative encoding activity: learners must decide what the core of an idea is, which forces processing beyond surface features [Generation improves retention relative to passive reading.](../claims/generation-effect-improves-retention.md) [+M]. Labels also support analogical transfer, because a named case can be retrieved and mapped onto a new problem when the label matches the structural relation [Multiple contrasting cases support transfer.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Source material organized into discrete, nameable units (cases, steps, principles, examples)
- A moment in the workflow where learners produce or record the label (annotation margin, index card, shared doc) — see [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)
- Later opportunities to *use* the slugs for retrieval, discussion, or sorting, or they decay into unused jargon

#### Constraints
- Labels imposed by the instructor without learner processing produce weaker encoding than self-generated ones [Generation improves retention relative to passive reading.](../claims/generation-effect-improves-retention.md) [~M]
- Superficial or pun-based labels that capture surface features rather than structure can mislead retrieval toward irrelevant similarity [Analogical transfer depends on structural, not surface, matching.](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [-M]
- Too many slugs compete for attention and fragment the schema; a lesson supports roughly a handful of durable labels, not dozens
- For novices, generating a label before understanding the idea can entrench misconceptions [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [~M] — provide the label first, then ask learners to justify it

#### Implementation Variability
- **Instructor-supplied slugs** for novices; **learner-generated slugs** once basic understanding is in place
- **Case slugs** in case-based teaching (each case gets a name used in later discussion)
- **Step slugs** in procedures (each step compressed to a verb phrase, forming a mnemonic chain)
- **Shared class vocabulary**: a co-developed glossary of slugs reused across discussions builds common reference points

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners who have enough understanding to name ideas meaningfully but not yet the fluency to retrieve them unprompted
- Students in case-heavy or example-heavy domains (law, medicine, teacher education) where a library of named cases is the working toolkit
- Less suitable for absolute novices, who should first study [Worked Examples](../principles/worked-examples.md) or full [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) before compressing

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept and principle retention: creating durable retrieval cues for abstract ideas
- Analogical and case-based reasoning: building a labeled case library for transfer
- Metacognitive organization: forcing learners to identify the "gist" of material

### Instructions
1. Segment the material into discrete units — cases, steps, or principles — using [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md).
2. Model slug-writing once: show a unit and demonstrate compressing it to a two-to-five-word structural label, thinking aloud about why surface-features-only labels fail.
3. Have learners generate their own slug for each unit and record it alongside the material ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
4. Require learners to justify their label in one sentence — what structure does it capture?
5. Reuse the slugs in later activities: sorting tasks, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) prompts, and retrieval practice where the slug is the cue and the content is the target.
6. Curate: as a set, review which slugs the class will adopt as shared vocabulary and retire redundant ones.

## Examples
- **Harvard Business School case teaching** — each case acquires a short name ("the Lincoln Electric case") that students and instructor use for the rest of the course; the slug is the handle for all later analysis and cross-case comparison. See [Case-Based Learning (Harvard Method)](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md).
- **Medical education** — "classic presentations" are taught with canonical labels ("streaking knee," "butterfly rash") that function as retrieval slugs during diagnosis.
- **Writing workshops** — students slug each paragraph of a draft ("problem," "evidence," "turn") to check structural flow before revising.
