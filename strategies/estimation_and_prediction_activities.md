# Estimation and Prediction Activities

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Estimation and prediction activities ask learners to commit in advance to a concrete, checkable judgment — how many problems they will solve correctly, how long a task will take, how confident they are in an answer, or what an experiment will show — before they engage with the task. After completing the work, learners compare their predictions against actual outcomes. The gap between predicted and actual performance becomes the object of reflection, targeting the accuracy of learners' self-assessments rather than the task performance alone.

## Design Implications

Prediction works because it forces learners to consult their current knowledge state before receiving feedback, exposing the difference between what they think they know and what they actually know [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Learners are systematically miscalibrated — typically overconfident — and prediction-then-feedback cycles are one of the few reliable ways to improve calibration, because the discrepancy itself is informative feedback about the reliability of one's own judgments. Predictions also increase attention and engagement with the subsequent task or demonstration, since a committed guess creates a stake in the outcome.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prediction format that is specific and checkable (a number, a confidence rating, a written expected outcome) — vague predictions ("I'll do okay") produce no useful discrepancy
- Timely, accurate feedback on the actual outcome so the comparison can occur while the prediction is still salient [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- A brief structured comparison step (e.g., "Where did your estimate come from? What would you revise?") — without it, learners often dismiss discrepancies
- Repeated cycles across multiple tasks; calibration improves with practice, not from a single prediction

#### Constraints
- Feedback must follow quickly; delayed or absent feedback leaves the erroneous prediction uncorrected and may entrench overconfidence [-M]
- Repeated failure against one's own predictions can depress confidence and willingness to engage, particularly for learners with low prior success [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [~M] — frame discrepancies as information, not verdicts
- Learners with very low metacognitive skill may not know how to generate a grounded estimate; scaffolding the estimation process (e.g., "list the steps first, then judge each") is needed before the activity pays off [-W]
- Predictions about outcomes of demonstrations can be answered passively; require a written commitment to get the benefit

#### Implementation Variability
- **Performance calibration:** estimate score or completion time before an exam or problem set, then compare
- **Confidence-weighted answering:** rate confidence per item (e.g., 1–5) on quizzes; low-confidence correct answers signal fragile knowledge
- **Predict–observe–explain:** in science instruction, learners predict the outcome of a demonstration, observe it, and explain any discrepancy — especially powerful when predictions are wrong [High-confidence errors improve retention.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [+M]
- **Planning estimates:** predict time and effort for a project, then audit the estimate afterward (a work-relevant variant)

### Target Learners
- Novices and intermediate learners, who show the largest calibration errors and thus the most to gain from discrepancy feedback [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Students preparing for high-stakes assessments, where accurate self-assessment drives study allocation
- Less useful for learners who are already well-calibrated; the activity adds overhead without new information [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive accuracy: calibrating confidence and self-assessment to actual performance
- Self-regulated learning: using calibration to decide what to study and when to stop
- Conceptual change: surfacing and revising prior conceptions through predict–observe–explain cycles
- Study skills and planning: realistic estimation of time, effort, and readiness

### Instructions
1. Before the task, have learners record a specific, checkable prediction (score, time, confidence rating, or expected outcome) — a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) format works well for the commitment step.
2. Keep predictions visible but do not discuss them yet; discussion lets learners hedge or conform.
3. Learners complete the task, demonstration, or assessment.
4. Reveal actual outcomes immediately and have learners compute or note the discrepancy.
5. Run a short structured reflection: what information did the prediction rest on, and what would change the next estimate? Pair with self-explanation prompts, since articulating the source of miscalibration deepens the benefit [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
6. Repeat across tasks and track calibration trends over time so learners see their own improvement.

## Examples
- **Predict–observe–explain demonstrations in physics** (e.g., the force-concept sequence in *Physics by Inquiry*, McDermott): students predict what a demonstration will show, watch it, and reconcile surprises — wrong predictions are treated as the most valuable moments.
- **Exam wrappers** (e.g., Carnegie Mellon's Eberly Center exam wrapper templates, [https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers.html)): students predict their score before receiving the exam back, then analyze where their study time went relative to the discrepancy.
- **Confidence-weighted answer sheets** in medical and law exam preparation, where per-item confidence ratings identify "unknown unknowns" — correct answers held with low confidence.
