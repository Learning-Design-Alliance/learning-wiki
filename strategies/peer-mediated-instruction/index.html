Peer Mediated Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (3 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 2 of 3 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

Peer mediated instruction arranges for students to carry instructional functions — explaining, questioning, tutoring, giving feedback — to one another, while the teacher designs the roles, pairings, routines, and materials that structure the interaction. It spans reciprocal teaching, peer tutoring, cooperative goal structures, and structured peer feedback. The teacher's role shifts from delivering content to engineering the conditions under which peer explanation produces learning.

Design Implications¶

Peer mediation works because explaining material to another person forces retrieval, elaboration, and reorganization of one's own understanding, and because the tutor operates closer to the learner's zone of proximal development than the teacher can Peer assessment and peer feedback can improve achievement when structured with criteria and training. [+M]. Unstructured "work in groups" reliably fails; effective designs assign complementary roles, individual accountability, and a shared goal that cannot be met without every member's contribution Cooperative learning with positive interdependence and individual accountability outperforms competitive and individualistic structures. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Structured roles and routines (e.g., reciperocal teaching's four strategies) rather than open-ended group work
  • Training in the peer role itself — tutors need brief instruction in how to explain, prompt, and give feedback
  • Individual accountability so no member can free-ride on the group's product
  • Tasks at an appropriate difficulty band: hard enough to require explanation, easy enough that peers can diagnose errors

Constraints¶

  • Peer tutors with fragile understanding can entrench misconceptions; low-knowledge tutors produce low-knowledge tutees [-M]
  • Unstructured group work allows social loafing and status-based participation inequities, with high-status students dominating airtime [-S]
  • Benefits shrink or reverse when tasks are trivial (no need for explanation) or when group composition is highly heterogeneous without role scaffolds [~M]
  • Peer feedback without criteria or training tends toward vague praise ("good job") that does not improve work [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Reciprocal teaching: roles rotate; all students take both tutor and tutee positions
  • Cross-age tutoring: older students tutor younger ones, trading proximity for the tutor's own consolidation gains
  • Jigsaw: each member masters a unique piece and must teach it; interdependence is built into the task structure
  • Peer review/feedback: structured protocols with rubrics convert peer comments into actionable revision

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 and higher education across content domains; effects are robust from elementary through college [+S]
  • Struggling learners benefit from the lower "affective filter" of peer explanation; high achievers benefit from explaining (the tutor often gains as much as the tutee) [+M]
  • Less suitable when no student in the group has sufficient partial knowledge to seed productive interaction [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding through verbalization and elaboration
  • Procedural fluency through guided peer practice and error correction
  • Social and communicative goals: academic discourse, perspective-taking, giving and receiving feedback
  • Metacognition, especially in reciprocal formats where students model questioning and summarizing for each other

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning goal and select a task that genuinely requires explanation or joint reasoning — not one solvable faster alone.
  2. Choose a structure (Cooperative Learning, jigsaw, reciprocal teaching, peer tutoring) and assign complementary roles with individual accountability.
  3. Train students briefly in the peer moves: how to explain, prompt rather than tell, and give criterion-referenced Feedback.
  4. Launch with a short Demonstration or think-aloud of the peer interaction itself, so students see what a productive exchange sounds like.
  5. Monitor and intervene on content errors and participation inequities; debrief both the content and the collaboration quality.

Related Strategies¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching — the canonical peer-mediated reading routine with rotating roles
  • Jigsaw Method — interdependence by design; each peer holds a unique piece
  • Peer Assessment — structured peer evaluation against criteria
  • Think-Pair-Share — minimal peer mediation embedded in whole-class instruction
  • Train students in tutor and group roles before routine use of peer tutoring or response groups

Examples¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown) — students rotate through predictor, questioner, clarifier, and summarizer roles while reading expository text; documented large comprehension gains with struggling readers.
  • PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies) — a widely implemented structured peer-tutoring program for reading and math in which pairs alternate tutor/tutee roles with scripted activities (https://intensiveintervention.org).
  • Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson) — originally developed to reduce intergroup tension in desegregated Texas classrooms; each student becomes an "expert" on one segment and teaches it to their home group.
