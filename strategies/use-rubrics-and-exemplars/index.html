Use Rubrics And Exemplars¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 9 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

This strategy pairs an explicit rubric — a breakdown of the criteria and performance levels used to judge a task — with one or more exemplars: annotated samples of work at defined quality levels. Learners study the exemplars against the rubric before (and often during) their own production, so evaluation criteria are transparent rather than implicit. The combination works because each component supplies what the other lacks: the rubric gives the abstract criteria, the exemplars give the concrete instantiation.

Design Implications¶

Rubrics and exemplars make quality criteria visible, supporting self-regulated performance: learners can monitor and evaluate their own work against the same standards an assessor will use Assessment that makes criteria explicit improves achievement. [+S]. Exemplars alone risk being interpreted idiosyncratically — learners may copy surface features rather than extract the underlying criteria — so the rubric should drive an explicit analysis of why the exemplar earns its rating. Comparing multiple exemplars at different quality levels is more effective than studying a single model, because contrast makes the discriminating features visible Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A rubric with criteria phrased in language learners can understand and apply, not just assessors (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  • At least two exemplars spanning quality levels, annotated to map features to rubric criteria
  • An activity that requires learners to apply the rubric — e.g., evaluating a sample or peer draft — rather than merely reading it
  • Alignment between the rubric, the exemplars, and the actual assessment task

Constraints¶

  • Rubrics used only as grading tools after submission — never seen or applied by learners — do not improve performance; the benefit comes from use before and during production Assessment that makes criteria explicit improves achievement. [~M]
  • Overly detailed or checklist-style rubrics can fragment attention on surface compliance rather than holistic quality Checklist-style evaluation is ineffective in online contexts. [-W]
  • A single exemplar can anchor learners to one approach or invite imitation of surface features; multiple varied exemplars mitigate this
  • Learners with strong prior expertise may find rubric scaffolding constraining; criteria should be faded or co-constructed as competence grows

Implementation Variability¶

  • Single-point rubrics list only the "at standard" criteria, with space for evidence of falling below or exceeding — reduces the compliance mindset of multi-level grids
  • Co-constructed rubrics — learners help draft criteria from analyzing exemplars — deepen ownership and criterion understanding
  • Rubric + peer review: learners apply the rubric to peers' work, which doubles as calibration practice
  • Exemplar annotation: instructor commentary on exemplars makes the rubric-to-work mapping explicit; alternatively, learners do the annotating themselves

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who cannot yet infer quality criteria from a task description alone Assessment that makes criteria explicit improves achievement. [+M]
  • Learners producing extended, open-ended work (essays, projects, code, portfolios) where "good" is multidimensional
  • Less necessary for routine or well-specified tasks where success criteria are already unambiguous

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Production of complex artifacts: writing, design, research products
  • Self-assessment and self-regulation: internalizing evaluative criteria Assessment that makes criteria explicit improves achievement. [+M]
  • Calibration: aligning learners' judgments of their own work with expert judgment

Instructions¶

  1. Select or draft a rubric with 3–6 criteria phrased in learner-accessible language.
  2. Gather 2–3 authentic exemplars at different quality levels (prior student work, with permission, works well).
  3. Annotate each exemplar, mapping specific features to specific rubric criteria.
  4. Have learners analyze the exemplars against the rubric — rating them and justifying the ratings — before beginning their own work.
  5. Make the rubric available during production for self-monitoring, and again during revision or peer review.
  6. Fade support over successive tasks: move from instructor-annotated exemplars, to unannotated exemplars, to rubric co-construction.

Related Strategies¶

  • Use Worked Examples — the problem-solving analogue: exemplars for well-structured tasks, rubric-and-exemplar pairs for open-ended ones
  • Peer Review With Rubrics — applying the rubric to others' work as calibration practice
  • Self-Assessment — the internalization endpoint of rubric use
  • Teach self- and peer-assessment by starting with rubrics, then glows, then scaffolded grows

Examples¶

  • Explain Everything / model essays in AP courses — College Board publishes scored sample essays with grader commentary for every free-response question; students study scored exemplars against the published rubric before writing.
  • Writing programs using single-point rubrics (e.g., the CUNY WriteSite tradition) — instructors present annotated model texts at adjacent quality levels and have students identify what moves a draft up a level.
  • Capstone and portfolio courses — programs that share exemplary past portfolios with rubric annotations at project launch, then have students self-score drafts before submission.
