Content-Focused Questioning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 against) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q3–q4 · 3 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Content-focused questioning directs students' attention to the central ideas of a text and to how the author supports those ideas with evidence and examples. The instructor poses a planned sequence of questions — beginning with what students already know and moving toward comprehension of main points before discussing rhetorical or analytical techniques. Questions may be answered individually, in peer discussion, or in whole-class sharing, and students can eventually generate their own questions about the text.

Design Implications¶

Questioning is one of the most consistently supported teacher moves in reading instruction: teacher-led questioning that targets main ideas and text structure improves comprehension relative to reading alone [~S], and its effectiveness depends heavily on question quality — higher-order, text-based questions outperform simple factual recall [~M]. Questioning works because it forces active processing and retrieval of the material rather than passive reading Active learning improves exam performance. [+S]. Sequencing matters: comprehension questions should precede evaluative or analytical ones, since students cannot evaluate an author's technique before understanding the content.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Appropriate reading material at a manageable difficulty level
  • Prepared, sequenced questions that move from literal comprehension toward analysis of evidence and argument
  • Sufficient class time for discussion, not just rapid-fire answer checking
  • Facilitation skill to follow up on partial answers and press for textual evidence

Constraints¶

  • Time-consuming; deep questioning of a single text covers less material than lecturing on it
  • Students with limited background knowledge or weak decoding skills may be unable to answer, producing frustration rather than learning Activation of prior knowledge improves learning. [-M]
  • Rapid, low-level questioning routines (call-and-response on facts) show weak or null effects on comprehension [~W]
  • Poor facilitation lets discussion drift or lets a few students dominate, reducing participation and learning

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher-posed questions vs. student-generated questions; training students to generate their own content-focused questions produces reliable comprehension gains [+S]
  • Individual written response, Class Discussion, think-pair-share, or reciprocal-teaching formats
  • Oral questioning vs. embedded written questions in a reading guide or courseware

Target Learners¶

  • Students across all grade levels; particularly beneficial for struggling readers who need explicit attentional guidance through a text [~M]
  • Students with limited background knowledge benefit from questions that activate relevant knowledge before reading Activation of prior knowledge improves learning. [+M]
  • Less effective for advanced readers reading easy texts, where questions add little beyond what independent reading yields [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and supporting evidence
  • Critical reading: analyzing how authors use evidence and examples
  • Question-generation skill: learning to interrogate texts independently [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Select an appropriate text and identify its main points and key evidence in advance.
  2. Activate prior knowledge with a brief opening question about what students already know (Activation).
  3. Pose comprehension questions on main ideas first; require students to point to textual evidence for answers (Direct Instruction can model what a well-evidenced answer looks like).
  4. Move to analytical questions about how the author supports the main points.
  5. Structure responses through individual writing, Class Discussion, or peer exchange before whole-class sharing.
  6. Gradually hand over questioning: have students generate their own content-focused questions and exchange them (Fading [+M]).

Related Strategies¶

  • Reciprocal teaching — a formalized routine in which students take turns leading content-focused questioning of a text
  • Case-Based Learning — questioning applied to extended cases rather than short texts
  • Discussion-Based Learning — the broader pattern this strategy supports with prepared questions

Examples¶

  • A middle-school ELA teacher asks students to identify the central claim of an opinion article, then to list two pieces of evidence the author uses, before discussing whether the evidence is persuasive.
  • Reciprocal teaching routines (Palincsar & Brown) assign students the "questioner" role, generating main-idea questions about a passage segment for their group.
  • Reading-check questions embedded in platforms such as Newsela and Actively Learn deliver content-focused questions at points within the text, with analytics showing the teacher where comprehension breaks down.
