# Jigsaw Method

## Description
The Jigsaw Method, developed by Elliot Aronson in 1971, divides a lesson into segments and assigns each member of a "home" group one segment to master. Learners first meet in temporary "expert" groups with members of other home groups who share the same segment, then return to teach their segment to their home group. Because no learner holds the full picture alone, the structure creates genuine positive interdependence: each student's contribution is necessary for the group's collective understanding.

## Design Implications

Jigsaw converts passive content coverage into active peer teaching, with each learner accountable for a unique, indispensable piece [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Its distinctive mechanism is structural rather than motivational: interdependence is engineered by the information distribution, not by exhortation to cooperate. Requiring learners to organize and explain material to peers also produces elaborative processing and retrieval practice, both of which strengthen retention beyond re-reading or listening.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content that can be genuinely partitioned into interdependent segments (not segments that vary only in difficulty or interest)
- Source materials at readable levels for each segment, or time for experts to process them
- Explicit teaching of peer-explanation skills; many students have never been taught how to teach
- A mechanism for individual accountability on the *whole* topic (e.g., a quiz covering all segments), so experts take teaching seriously
- Sufficient time for two rounds of grouping plus synthesis

#### Constraints
- Weak or disengaged "experts" propagate gaps: misinformation or shallow explanations spread through home groups with no expert filter [-M] — the teacher cannot hear every expert-group discussion, so errors can be institutionalized by the time they reach home groups
- Students with language barriers, social anxiety, or low status in the group may be discounted by teammates, undermining the interdependence design [-M]; Aronson's original work showed status dynamics within groups determine whether jigsaw closes or widens participation gaps
- Poorly partitioned content (overlapping or trivially separable segments) removes the interdependence that drives the method, degenerating into parallel individual study [~M]
- High-stakes individual assessment on peers' teaching without a synthesis check can create resentment rather than responsibility [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jigsaw II (Slavin):** all students read a common base text first; expert groups specialize in subtopics — reduces the risk of a weak segment leaving a student with nothing
- **Reverse jigsaw:** experts teach in expert groups while home-group members question, useful for discussion-heavy subjects
- **Jigsaw in large/online classes:** breakout rooms or discussion boards can host expert groups, but accountability mechanisms must be made explicit since informal monitoring disappears
- **Listening teams:** for younger learners, experts prepare a short presentation with a graphic organizer rather than free-form teaching

### Target Learners
- Students in heterogeneous groups; the original design was built to reduce racial tension and intergroup hostility in desegregated classrooms, and cooperative structures of this kind improve intergroup attitudes and belonging [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Learners who benefit from structured talk and elaboration, including English language learners, provided segment materials are linguistically accessible [~M]
- Less suitable as-is for novices with no prior knowledge of *any* segment, who may struggle to teach before having a schema; a common base text (Jigsaw II) mitigates this

### Target Learning Goals
- Content mastery across a broad, partitionable body of material
- Explanation and communication skills — organizing and transmitting understanding to peers
- Intergroup relations and classroom belonging
- Interdependence and shared accountability as dispositions

### Instructions
1. **Partition** the content into 4–6 interdependent segments, one per group member; verify each segment is necessary for the whole.
2. **Form home groups** and assign segments; give each student their segment's materials.
3. **Expert groups:** students with the same segment from different home groups meet to master the material and rehearse how to teach it; circulate to check for accuracy before errors spread.
4. **Teach-back:** students return to home groups and teach their segment; teammates take notes on each segment using a shared organizer.
5. **Synthesize and assess:** the group or individual integrates all segments (timeline, concept map, solution to a problem requiring all pieces), followed by individual accountability such as a quiz covering every segment.

## Examples
- **The Jigsaw Classroom** (Aronson's original program, [jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)) — 5th-grade history in Austin, TX: each student in a group of six owned one segment of a biography; the structure sharply reduced prejudice and absenteeism in the original studies.
- **Jigsaw II in STAD-style classrooms** (Slavin's Student Teams-Achievement Divisions) — common base text plus expert subtopics, with team scores based on individual quiz improvement.
- **University STEM courses** — jigsaw used for case analysis in large lectures (e.g., expert groups each analyze one stakeholder's position in an environmental case, then home groups integrate into a recommendation).
