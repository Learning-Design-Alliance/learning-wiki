# Story Mapping

## Description
Story mapping is a comprehension strategy in which learners identify and organize the structural elements of a narrative — setting, characters, problem, goal, key events, and resolution — into a visual graphic organizer. It is typically carried out during or after reading: the teacher models completing a map with a think-aloud, then learners complete maps independently or collaboratively, using the map as a basis for retelling, discussion, or writing.

## Design Implications

Story mapping works because it externalizes the *schema* of narrative text, giving learners a reusable frame for organizing incoming information rather than processing sentences serially [Chunking information into organized structures reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. It is most effective when the map is completed *with* the learner under explicit modeling and gradually faded, rather than handed over as a blank worksheet [Contingent scaffolding improves learning outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The map also functions as a retrieval structure: learners who retell or summarize from their maps show better recall than learners who read alone.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A map template whose fields match the actual story grammar of the texts used (simple narratives need fewer fields than complex ones)
- Teacher modeling of how to identify each element, including judgment calls (e.g., distinguishing the central problem from incidental events)
- A follow-on use for the map — retelling, summarizing, comparing across texts — so it is not an end in itself
- Texts at a difficulty level where learners can locate elements with support

#### Constraints
- Overly detailed or generic templates add extraneous load; learners spend effort filling boxes rather than constructing meaning [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when the map is treated as a compliance task — completed once and never used for retelling or discussion
- Less useful for texts that do not follow canonical narrative structure (informational text, vignettes); forcing story-grammar fields onto non-narratives misrepresents the text
- Benefits fade if the map is always provided; the goal is internalization, so support must be faded [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher-completed → collaborative → independent**: the standard fading sequence
- **Pre-reading vs. during-reading**: pre-reading maps act as advance organizers; during-reading maps support monitoring
- **Character/setting maps, problem–solution maps, event-chain maps**: template complexity should scale with text and learner
- **Digital variants**: tools like Storyboard That or Kidspiration support visual story maps with movable elements

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers and learners with language or comprehension difficulties, who benefit most from an explicit narrative frame [Story mapping improves reading comprehension for struggling learners.](../claims/story-mapping-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Young readers (K–5) still internalizing narrative structure
- English learners, for whom the map makes implicit discourse structure visible
- Less necessary for proficient readers who already apply story grammar automatically

### Target Learning Goals
- Narrative comprehension: identifying and relating story grammar elements
- Retelling and summarizing: the map serves as a retrieval scaffold for oral or written retells
- Sequencing and causal reasoning: linking events through problem–goal–action–outcome chains
- Transfer to writing: learners use the same structure to plan their own narratives

### Instructions
1. Select or design a map template matching the story grammar of the target texts; keep fields minimal.
2. Read a short model text aloud and complete the map while thinking aloud, explaining how you decided what counts as the problem or key event ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Have learners complete maps collaboratively on the next text, with the teacher prompting rather than filling ([Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md)).
4. Move learners to independent mapping, then fade the template entirely — retelling from memory.
5. Use completed maps for retelling, comparison across texts, or as planning tools for students' own narrative writing ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).

## Related Strategies

## Examples
- **Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS) for Reading** (Vanderbilt University) — embeds story mapping and retelling in structured peer tutoring routines ([https://pals.vanderbilt.edu](https://pals.vanderbilt.edu)).
- **Boulineau et al. (2004)** — elementary teachers used story maps with explicit modeling and fading; students with learning disabilities improved retelling and comprehension.
- **Storyboard That** ([https://www.storyboardthat.com](https://www.storyboardthat.com)) — digital comic-strip storyboards that function as visual story maps for sequencing and retelling.
