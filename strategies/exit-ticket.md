# Exit Ticket

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An exit ticket is a brief assessment activity — typically 1–3 questions or a single prompt — completed by every learner in the final minutes of a lesson and submitted before leaving. It serves two functions simultaneously: it requires learners to retrieve and consolidate the day's content, and it generates rapid evidence the instructor can use to adjust the next lesson.

## Design Implications

Exit tickets enact [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by making thinking visible at the moment instruction ends, when adjustment is still cheap [Formative assessment practices improve achievement when feedback is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The retrieval demand also gives every learner a brief spaced practice opportunity rather than leaving consolidation to chance [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. The design burden falls on the instructor: tickets must be actually read and acted on, or the routine decays into compliance theater.

### Context
#### Requirements
- 2–5 minutes of protected time at the end of the session
- Questions that target the lesson's core objective, not generic "did you like it" prompts
- A fast review routine (sorting into piles, a quick scan before the next class) so results feed forward into the next lesson's plan
- Low-stakes framing — ungraded or completion-only — so responses reflect actual understanding

#### Constraints
- If tickets are never reviewed or acted on, learners detect this and response quality degrades [Formative assessment practices improve achievement when feedback is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]
- Very short format cannot assess complex reasoning, extended argument, or skill execution — only recall, single-step application, and self-report of confusion
- End-of-class fatigue and time pressure produce rushed, low-quality responses if the lesson runs over
- Self-report items ("I understood this") correlate poorly with actual performance; prefer items that require doing

#### Implementation Variability
- **3-2-1 format**: three things learned, two connections, one question — adds [elaborative interrogation](../claims/elaborative-interrogation-improves-learning.md) [+M] to the retrieval demand
- **Predict-and-check**: learners predict the next lesson's content or apply today's concept to a novel case, priming [Activation](../principles/activation.md)
- **Digital tickets** (Google Forms, Poll Everywhere, Socrative) enable automatic aggregation; paper tickets preserve anonymity for low-confidence learners
- **Muddiest point**: a single open prompt ("What was most confusing?") — fastest to administer, slowest to act on

### Target Learners
- Whole classes in K-12 and higher education; the format scales to any group size
- Especially valuable for learners who will not voluntarily voice confusion in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) — the private, written channel surfaces struggles that public questioning misses
- Less informative for young learners with limited writing fluency; oral or drawing-based variants work better

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation and retention of the day's core content [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Self-assessment and metacognitive monitoring of one's own understanding
- Instructor-side diagnosis of misconceptions before they consolidate

### Instructions
1. Identify the single most important idea from the lesson and write 1–3 items that require learners to *use* it, not restate it ([Application](../elements/application.md))
2. Reserve the final 3–5 minutes; announce the ticket at the start of class so learners monitor for it throughout
3. Keep it low-stakes and brief to avoid end-of-lesson [cognitive overload](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]
4. Collect and sort responses before the next session — into "got it / partial / missing" piles
5. Open the next lesson by addressing the most common gap, and tell learners you did so; visible follow-through sustains the routine
6. For learners whose tickets reveal persistent confusion, follow up individually ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))

## Examples
- **3-2-1 ticket in a biology class**: after a photosynthesis lesson, students write three inputs, two outputs, one question; the instructor sorts tickets and opens the next class by resolving the five most common questions
- **[Socrative](https://www.socrative.com)** — digital exit tickets with auto-aggregated class reports showing per-question accuracy
- **Muddiest point in university lectures** (Mosteller, 1989, Harvard) — a one-sentence prompt collected weekly, with the instructor opening the next lecture by answering the muddiest points
