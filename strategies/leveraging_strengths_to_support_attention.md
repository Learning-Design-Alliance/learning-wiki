# Leveraging Strengths to Support Attention

## Description
This strategy identifies what a learner already does well — strong visual processing, verbal fluency, sustained interest in particular topics, or self-monitoring habits — and uses that strength as the channel through which attention is recruited and sustained. Instead of treating attention as a deficit to be fixed, the instructor designs tasks so that the learner's strong system carries the load for the weaker one: preferential seating paired with visual anchors, directions delivered in the learner's strongest modality, tasks chunked around interest areas, and self-monitoring tools the learner already uses successfully.

## Design Implications

Attention is a limited resource that is allocated, not merely possessed; cues that connect to a learner's existing competencies and interests capture and hold it more effectively than generic prompts [Relevance of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. Strengths-based support also works through motivation: tasks that let learners operate from competence raise task value and engagement, which in turn sustains attention [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Structurally, most implementations reduce extraneous load — chunking, visual organizers, and clear structure free working-memory resources that would otherwise be spent on re-orienting [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine profile of the learner's strengths, gathered through observation, learner interview, or work-sample analysis — not assumed from labels
- A match between the identified strength and the attention demand being supported (e.g., visual strength → visual schedules and anchor charts; verbal strength → self-talk scripts and oral rehearsal)
- Willingness to let the learner co-select supports; self-selected strategies are more consistently used than imposed ones [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Periodic review, since a strength-based support that works in September may be stale or stigmatizing by spring

#### Constraints
- Strengths identified informally or by stereotype (e.g., "visual learner") rather than by evidence produce mismatched supports [~M] — the learning-styles matching hypothesis is not supported, so "strength" must mean a demonstrated competence or interest, not a self-reported modality preference
- Over-reliance on a single strength can narrow the learner's task repertoire and prevent development of weaker channels [-W]
- Supports that visibly single a learner out can reduce engagement through stigma, particularly in adolescence [-M]
- If the underlying task remains overloaded, strength-based cues cannot compensate; the strategy supplements but does not replace [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [-~]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Environmental:** preferential seating, reduced-distraction zones, visual schedules
- **Task-based:** [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) directions, graphic organizers, interest-embedded examples
- **Learner-managed:** checklists, timers, self-monitoring cards the learner operates independently
- **Social:** peer or teacher check-ins that use the learner's relational strengths ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md))

### Target Learners
- Learners with ADHD or other attention-related challenges, for whom externally structured, interest-connected supports have the strongest evidence base [~M]
- Any learner whose engagement drops when tasks sit outside their competence or interest zones [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Less effective for learners who already sustain attention well and find added organizers and check-ins redundant or intrusive [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Executive function: initiating, sustaining, and shifting attention during multi-step tasks
- Self-regulation: building the learner's own repertoire of attention-management strategies [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Prerequisite condition for content goals: attention support enables, but does not itself teach, domain objectives

### Instructions
1. **Profile the learner.** Observe across settings and interview the learner to identify demonstrated strengths and interests — what they attend to readily and what tools they already use unprompted.
2. **Map strength to attention demand.** For each attention breakdown (starting, sustaining, re-orienting), select a support routed through the strength — e.g., a visual learner gets a graphic organizer ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)); a verbal learner gets a self-talk script.
3. **Reduce load first.** Apply [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) to the task itself before layering individual supports.
4. **Co-select and teach the support.** Introduce the tool with the learner, model its use ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)), and fade prompts as the learner takes ownership [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
5. **Monitor and revise.** Use brief [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and work-sample review to judge whether the support is still working; retire or replace stale supports.

## Related Strategies

- [Preferential Seating](../strategies/preferential_seating.md) — the environmental counterpart: positioning the learner to reduce competing attentional demands

## Examples
- A middle-school student with ADHD who is a strong artist receives a visual task card with icons for each step of a lab procedure, plus a self-monitoring checklist they designed; on-task time increases and the teacher fades verbal redirections over six weeks.
- **[CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org)** — the "recruiting interest" and "sustaining effort" guidelines operationalize strengths-based attention support at the curriculum-design level.
- A high-school English class lets students choose their attention support from a menu (noise-reducing headphones, standing desk, check-in timer); choice itself raises task value and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M].
