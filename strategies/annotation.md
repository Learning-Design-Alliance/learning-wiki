# Annotation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Annotation asks learners to actively mark up a source — highlighting, underlining, margin notes, questions, summaries, or links to prior knowledge — rather than reading or viewing it passively. It can be learner-generated (students create the annotations) or instructor-provided (expert annotations scaffold the reading), and applies to text, images, code, and video.

## Design Implications

Annotation works because it is a generative activity: transforming and elaborating on material forces learners to select what matters and connect it to what they know, producing better recall and comprehension than passive reading [Generative processing improves learning from text.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]. However, the *quality* of the annotation matters more than its presence: highlighting alone is one of the weakest study strategies when used without additional processing [Highlighting and rereading are among the least effective study techniques.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M]. Instructor-provided annotations can scaffold novice readers, but learners who construct their own annotations generally learn more than those who only read ones supplied to them [Experimenter-provided underlining is no more effective than student-generated underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A source worth annotating — dense, information-rich material where selection and elaboration pay off
- Explicit guidance on *what* to annotate and *how* (e.g., "mark claims vs. evidence," "write a question in the margin every paragraph"), since unguided annotation often degenerates into indiscriminate highlighting
- A follow-up use for the annotations — discussion, writing, or exam preparation — so the work has a purpose ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Undirected highlighting is near-useless or harmful: learners highlight too much, mistake fluency of marking for understanding, and overestimate later memory for highlighted text [Highlighting and rereading are among the least effective study techniques.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [-M]
- Annotation adds cognitive load; for novices with high-extraneous-load material, the marking task can compete with comprehension rather than support it [Cognitive load theory: guidance for managing instructional load.](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [~M]
- Less effective when the text is simple or short enough that elaboration is redundant

#### Implementation Variability
- **Learner-generated**: students highlight, summarize, question, or diagram in margins; strongest for building comprehension monitoring
- **Instructor-provided**: expert annotations model expert reading of a difficult text; useful as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of disciplinary reading practices
- **Collaborative/social**: shared annotation layers (e.g., Perusall, Hypothesis) make reading visible to peers and support [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Multimedia annotation**: commenting on video timelines or code lines extends the strategy beyond prose

### Target Learners
- Intermediate learners with enough prior knowledge to judge what is important; complete novices often cannot discriminate key from peripheral content and benefit more from instructor-provided annotations [Learner-constructed graphic organizers outperform provided ones only when learners have adequate prior knowledge.](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [~M]
- Students developing disciplinary reading practices (e.g., first-year undergraduates reading primary literature)
- Less beneficial for learners who already have fluent, self-regulated reading strategies, for whom required annotation is redundant

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and retention of complex texts [Generative processing improves learning from text.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]
- Metacognitive monitoring: margin questions and confusion notes surface comprehension gaps
- Close reading and argument analysis: distinguishing claims, evidence, and assumptions

### Instructions
1. Select a rich source and define an annotation scheme (what counts as a highlight, note, question, or connection).
2. Model the scheme by annotating a short excerpt publicly — a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of expert reading ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
3. Have students annotate independently or in shared annotation layers, keeping the scheme visible.
4. Build a follow-on task that uses the annotations: seminar discussion, synthesis writing, or peer review of each other's annotations ([Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md)).
5. Gradually loosen the scheme as students internalize the reading practices ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — social annotation platform where students collectively annotate course readings; the platform scores annotation quality and threads discussion in the margins.
- **[Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is)** — open web annotation used in many LMS courses for collaborative marginalia on public texts.
- **Annotated primary literature in introductory biology (e.g., [Science in the Classroom](https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org))** — instructor-provided annotations scaffold novices through research articles, modeling expert reading before students annotate unannotated papers.
