# Self Monitoring In Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self monitoring in writing is the metacognitive practice of having writers track their own composing process — checking whether their text matches their goals, whether their strategies are working, and where their understanding of the task breaks down. It is typically operationalized through checklists, rubrics, goal-setting prompts, think-aloud protocols, and structured revision passes. It is a core component of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) applied to the writing domain, and is most systematically implemented in Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD).

## Design Implications

Self monitoring converts writing from a single-pass production task into a cyclical process of goal setting, execution, and evaluation, which is characteristic of skilled writers and largely absent in struggling ones (Graham & Harris, 2000) [+S]. Monitoring is only effective when learners have explicit, concrete criteria to monitor *against* — vague instructions to "check your work" produce little change, while criterion-based checklists and rubrics measurably improve revision quality [+M]. Because monitoring consumes working memory, it should be taught as a strategy in its own right and scaffolded with external supports (checklists, structured peer feedback) before being faded toward internalized self-questioning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit success criteria: a rubric, checklist, or model text that defines what "good" looks like for this genre
- Separation of drafting and evaluating — learners need permission to produce imperfect text first, then monitor during a distinct revision pass
- Instruction in *how* to monitor: modeled self-questioning ("Does my opening state my claim? Is this evidence relevant?"), ideally via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration
- Opportunities to act on monitoring results — monitoring without a subsequent revision cycle is inert

#### Constraints
- Monitoring against criteria learners do not understand produces compliance, not improvement; rubric language must be taught and practiced [Checklist-based self-evaluation without training is ineffective in online and novice contexts.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Over-monitoring during drafting disrupts composing fluency; interrupting text generation to evaluate every sentence increases cognitive load and can degrade both fluency and quality [~M]
- Novices often cannot detect their own errors — they monitor with the same faulty knowledge that produced the error — so self-monitoring alone cannot substitute for feedback or instruction on the target skill [-M]
- For learners with strong writing skills, imposed checklists can feel constraining and add little value beyond their existing internal standards [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Process checklists**: fixed sequences (plan → draft → check organization → check evidence → edit) applied across a genre
- **Self-questioning prompts**: embedded questions at pause points ("What am I trying to say here? Does my reader know that?")
- **Structured revision passes**: separate passes for content, organization, and mechanics, each with its own monitoring focus
- **Social monitoring**: [Peer Review](../elements/peer-review.md) and structured peer feedback against the same criteria, which doubles as modeling for internal monitoring
- **SRSD programs**: full instructional packages that combine monitoring with strategy instruction, self-instruction, and goal setting

### Target Learners
- Struggling and novice writers, who typically lack spontaneous monitoring and treat revision as cosmetic proofreading [+S]
- Upper elementary through secondary students — the strongest evidence base is grades 2–8, with effects persisting into postsecondary and adult basic education [+M]
- Students with learning disabilities and ADHD benefit substantially when monitoring is embedded in SRSD with explicit strategy instruction [+S]
- Less beneficial as a standalone intervention for already-proficient writers, who need content-level challenge rather than process scaffolds [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision and evaluation: improving text quality through criterion-based self-assessment
- Procedural fluency in the writing process: internalizing plan–draft–revise cycles
- Metacognitive development: building transferable habits of self-evaluation applicable across genres and domains
- Writing self-efficacy: monitoring visible progress against goals builds writers' sense of control [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Establish criteria first.** Co-construct or explicitly teach a rubric or checklist for the target genre; anchor it with a [Model Text](model-texts.md) and, where useful, a contrasting weak example.
2. **Model the monitoring.** Demonstrate a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) in which you draft briefly, then stop and self-question against the criteria, showing genuine detection and repair of a problem.
3. **Separate drafting from evaluating.** Have students draft without editing, then run a dedicated revision pass using the checklist — one criterion focus per pass to manage [Cognitive Load](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).
4. **Scaffold with prompts, then fade.** Begin with embedded self-questioning prompts or [Checklists](checklists.md); progressively remove them as students internalize the questions.
5. **Close the loop.** Require a brief [Self-Assessment](../elements/self-assessment.md) or [3-2-1 Reflection](../strategies/3-2-1_reflection.md) noting what the monitoring revealed and what was changed; pair with teacher or peer feedback so external and self-evaluation calibrate against each other.

## Examples
- **SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development)** — Graham and Harris's framework (see [SRSD for Writers](https://srsdresearch.org)) teaches students to monitor planning and composing using graphing of story parts and self-statements; dozens of experimental studies support its effects on text quality, especially for students with disabilities.
- **Writing Next recommendations** — Graham and Perin (2007) identify strategy instruction including self-monitoring and "prewriting/organizing" among the highest-effect practices for adolescent writing improvement.
- **Portfolio conferences with rubric-based self-scoring** — students score their own drafts against the class rubric before teacher conferences, then compare judgments; discrepancies become the conference agenda.
