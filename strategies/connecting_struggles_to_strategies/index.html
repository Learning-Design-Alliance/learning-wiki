Connecting Struggles to Strategies¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Connecting struggles to strategies involves explicitly teaching students various problem-solving strategies and helping them understand when and why to use each strategy effectively. Educators create opportunities for students to get productively "stuck" on challenging problems, then guide them to name, reflect on, and record the strategies that helped them get unstuck. Over time this builds a co-authored strategy resource and the habit of treating difficulty as a signal to try a different approach rather than a signal to stop.

Design Implications¶

Strategy instruction paired with metacognitive reflection reliably improves problem-solving and self-regulated learning outcomes, particularly when strategies are taught explicitly and practiced in context rather than mentioned in passing Hattie, Biggs, & Purdie meta-analysis of strategy interventions. [+M]. The struggle itself is the point: students must experience impasse before strategy instruction, or the strategies remain inert labels with no felt purpose. Feedback during the struggle should target the process — "what did you try, what would you try next?" — rather than the answer Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Challenging problems deliberately chosen to produce productive impasse (Challenge Identification)
  • Structured Check-Ins during problem-solving to surface what students are trying
  • Prompts that require students to name and explain their moves (Articulation)
  • A growing, co-authored strategy chart or resource that students refer back to and add to
  • Coaching that models strategy selection aloud (Think-Aloud) before students internalize it

Constraints¶

  • Requires significant time investment and ongoing effort to develop and maintain the strategy resource
  • Some students struggle to identify and articulate their problem-solving processes; without scaffolding, reflection prompts go unanswered
  • Premature strategy instruction — before students have experienced the struggle — produces memorized labels, not transferable habits Strategy instruction without contextual practice yields weak transfer. [~M]
  • Over-scaffolding the "unsticking" removes the productive struggle; support must fade as competence grows Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner. [+M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Whole-class debriefs ("What did you try when you got stuck?") vs. individual conferences vs. peer interviews
  • Domain-specific strategy lists (e.g., "draw a diagram, work backwards" in math) vs. general self-regulation prompts (e.g., "re-read the goal, break it into parts")
  • Strategy charts co-authored on paper, on walls, or in digital notebooks

Target Learners¶

  • Students who struggle with problem-solving and self-regulation benefit most; explicit strategy instruction shows the largest gains for low-achieving and younger learners Strategy instruction effects are larger for lower-achieving students. [~M]
  • Students who abandon tasks at the first sign of difficulty
  • Less beneficial for students who already possess and spontaneously deploy a strategy repertoire, for whom the instruction is redundant Expertise reverses the benefit of added instructional support. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive skill: monitoring one's own problem-solving process and recognizing impasse
  • Conditional knowledge: knowing when and why to apply a given strategy, not just how
  • Self-regulation: building independence and persistence in the face of difficulty (Self-Regulated Learning)

Instructions¶

  1. Pose a problem deliberately designed to produce impasse (Challenge Identification); resist rescuing students immediately.
  2. Circulate and Check In with prompts: "What have you tried? What's getting in your way?"
  3. Model strategy selection aloud with a Think-Aloud: "I'm stuck, so I'm going to try drawing it."
  4. Have students name and explain the strategies they used (Articulation), in pairs or in a whole-class debrief.
  5. Record each named strategy on a co-authored chart; refer to it whenever students are stuck and invite students to add to it.
  6. Gradually reduce prompting and Coaching as students begin self-initiating strategies.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — a strategy students can be taught to use when stuck, and a prerequisite for choosing among strategies
  • Action Planning — the planning half of the self-regulation cycle that strategy use completes
  • Use staged problem-solving reflection assignments to build metacognition around LLM use and surface student help-seeking perspectives

Examples¶

  • A math lesson built around a problem designed to make students feel "stuck," followed by a debrief where students name moves ("draw a picture," "work backwards," "try a smaller case") that are added to a class strategy chart.
  • Writing conferences in which the teacher asks "What did you do when you couldn't think of a word?" and records the student's answer on a personal strategies bookmark.
  • Youcubed mathematical mindset tasks, which are designed for productive struggle and paired with classroom norms about strategies for getting unstuck.
