# Structured Peer Discussion Roles

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structured peer discussion roles assign each member of a small group a specific, named function during discussion — such as facilitator, recorder, summarizer, skeptic, or questioner — often with rotation across sessions. The roles create positive interdependence: the discussion cannot succeed unless each member performs their function, which distributes airtime, prevents social loafing, and requires each learner to engage the content from a distinct cognitive angle.

## Design Implications

Role assignment converts unstructured "discussion" — which often devolves into a few confident students talking — into a cooperative structure where participation is distributed and each learner has a defined stake [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Roles that require generative cognitive work (questioning, summarizing, challenging) push learners beyond listening into elaboration and [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]. The structure matters more than the labels: roles must be interdependent, meaningful, and rotated, or they become performative.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Groups small enough (3–5) that every role is exercised every session
- Role descriptions with concrete behavioral scripts, not just titles ("ask at least two 'why' questions," not "be the questioner")
- A task with genuine discussion potential — ambiguous, multi-perspective, or problem-based — rather than a single lookup answer
- Rotation schedule so learners experience multiple perspectives over time
- Accountability mechanisms: individual [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) components or visible group products ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))

#### Constraints
- Roles imposed on tasks with a single correct answer produce shallow turn-taking, not discussion [-M]
- Poorly specified roles invite procedural compliance ("I said my summary, I'm done") without cognitive engagement [-M]
- Fixed, unrotated roles can entrench status hierarchies — high-status students claim facilitator, low-status students get recorder [-W]
- Overhead of learning role routines consumes class time; benefits emerge only after several cycles [~M]
- For novices lacking domain knowledge, the skeptic or questioner role can exceed their competence and produce empty challenges [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal teaching** (Palincsar & Brown): four fixed roles — summarizer, questioner, clarifier, predictor — applied to reading comprehension, with teacher modeling before student takeover
- **Jigsaw**: each student becomes expert on one piece and must teach it, making the role the content itself
- **Talk moves / Accountable Talk**: roles replaced by sentence stems and norms ("I agree because…", "Can you say more about…")
- **Literature circles**: role sheets (word wizard, illustrator, connector) common in K–6 literacy
- **Fishbowl variants**: outer-circle students take observer/critic roles toward inner-circle discussants

### Target Learners
- Students who otherwise remain silent in whole-class discussion — roles provide legitimate, expected entry points [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and adults; younger children need simpler, heavily modeled roles
- Mixed-ability groups benefit most, since roles let each member contribute at a different depth [~M]
- Less necessary for small seminars where norms already distribute participation

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through elaboration and peer explanation
- Argumentation and critical-evaluation skills [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Collaborative and communication competencies (turn-taking, active listening, group management)
- Reading comprehension strategies (reciprocal teaching variant)

### Instructions
1. Define 3–5 roles matched to the task's cognitive demands; write behavioral scripts for each ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md))
2. Assign roles deliberately — rotate over time, but initially match roles to student strengths to build early success
3. Model each role publicly before first use, ideally via the instructor demonstrating in a fishbowl ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
4. Launch the discussion with a genuinely open task or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)
5. Monitor and intervene on process, not content — coach the roles rather than answering for the group ([Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md))
6. Debrief both content and process: what did each role surface? Rotate roles for the next cycle

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown, 1984) — students rotate through summarizer, questioner, clarifier, and predictor roles when reading expository text; one of the best-validated comprehension interventions.
- **Literature Circles** — widely used K–12 protocol with role sheets (discussion director, literary luminary, connector); see [http://www.litcircles.org](http://www.litcircles.org).
- **POGIL (Process Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning)** — chemistry and other STEM courses assign Manager, Recorder, and Presenter roles within teams working through guided-inquiry worksheets; see [https://pogil.org](https://pogil.org).
