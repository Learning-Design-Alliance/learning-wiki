# Create a Calm-Down Corner

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A calm-down corner (also called a peace corner) is a dedicated classroom space where students can go to regulate strong emotions before rejoining instruction. It is stocked with self-regulation supports — emotion identification charts, breathing guides, fidget tools, and social-emotional books — and is framed as a positive, non-punitive resource rather than a consequence. The corner works as a low-stakes environment for practicing self-soothing and emotional awareness skills that students eventually generalize to unstructured settings.

## Design Implications

A calm-down corner operationalizes social-emotional learning by giving students a concrete context in which to rehearse regulation strategies rather than merely being told about them. Its effectiveness depends on explicit teaching of the tools it contains — students must be coached on *how* to use the space, not just granted access to it [Self-regulation skills can be taught and improve downstream outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Framing the corner as a student-initiated choice, rather than a teacher-directed time-out, supports autonomy and makes students more likely to use it before escalation [Autonomy-supportive environments increase intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A physically defined, comfortable space with reduced stimulation (soft seating, dim or quiet placement away from high-traffic areas)
- Concrete regulation tools: emotion charts or mood meters, breathing visual guides, fidget objects, and books about feelings
- Explicit, repeated instruction in how and when to use the corner, ideally during [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) and morning meetings
- Clear norms distinguishing the corner from punishment, including a simple re-entry routine (e.g., a brief signal or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) before returning to the group)

#### Constraints
- Without taught routines, the corner becomes either unused or a venue for avoidance of academic tasks [~M] — students may use it to escape work rather than to regulate
- Punitive use (sending students to the corner as a consequence) undermines its purpose and reduces voluntary uptake [-M]
- Students in acute dysregulation may not yet have the executive function to self-select the corner; adult prompting is often needed initially [~M]
- Requires ongoing maintenance: tools wear out, and novelty effects fade without periodic refresh and re-teaching
- Not a substitute for individualized support for students with significant trauma or emotional/behavioral disabilities; those students need [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) and often adult co-regulation

#### Implementation Variability
- **Age adaptation:** primary classrooms use picture-based emotion charts and physical tools; upper grades may use journaling, headphones, or a "reset station" with the same function
- **Access structure:** open access at any time vs. a sign-in or timer system (e.g., 5-minute sand timer) that limits use while preserving choice
- **Portable versions:** a "calm-down kit" or designated desk space for classrooms without room for a corner
- **Home extension:** sending a parallel kit home strengthens generalization across settings

### Target Learners
- Elementary students, who are still building foundational emotion-regulation capacity and respond well to concrete, physical regulation tools [+M]
- Students with ADHD, anxiety, or sensory sensitivities, for whom a low-stimulation reset space functions as an embedded [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) [~M]
- Students who experience the corner as a place where they are still accepted — it can support belonging when framed non-punitively [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less appropriate as a standalone support for students whose dysregulation stems from unaddressed trauma or skill deficits requiring intensive intervention

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional regulation: recognizing arousal states and applying self-soothing strategies
- Self-awareness: naming emotions using charts or mood meters
- Self-management: independently selecting and executing a coping strategy, a building block of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Re-entry and repair: returning to the learning community after dysregulation

### Instructions
1. **Design the space** with students where possible — involving them in naming and stocking the corner builds ownership and models that regulation is a shared classroom value [Autonomy-supportive environments increase intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
2. **Teach the tools explicitly** — model each regulation strategy (belly breathing, counting, using the mood meter) through [Practice](../elements/practice.md) during calm moments, not during crises
3. **Establish norms and routines** — when the corner may be used, how long, and the re-entry signal; rehearse the full routine
4. **Integrate with daily emotional check-ins** — regular [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) normalize emotion talk and make students more likely to self-identify dysregulation early
5. **Coach during use** — early on, an adult may accompany the student to [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) the strategy, fading support as independence grows
6. **Debrief and reinforce** — after re-entry, briefly reflect ("What did you try? What helped?") to consolidate the strategy into the student's repertoire

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom** classrooms commonly include a "take a break" spot with a timer, re-entry routine, and teacher-taught procedures ([https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org))
- **RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the Charter and Mood Meter tools are frequently housed in a designated regulation space ([https://ei.yale.edu](https://ei.yale.edu))
- A typical elementary implementation: beanbag seating, a feelings poster, a glitter jar, breathing cards, and a five-minute sand timer, introduced in week one and rehearsed weekly
