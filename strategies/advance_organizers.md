# Advance Organizers

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An advance organizer is introductory material presented *before* the main learning content, written at a higher level of abstraction and inclusivity than the content itself. Introduced by Ausubel, the organizer provides an "ideational scaffold" — anchoring concepts to which new, more detailed material can be meaningfully related during [Assimilation Theory](../theories/information-processing-theory.md) of meaningful learning. Organizers take two forms: *expository* organizers (for unfamiliar material) supply new anchoring concepts, while *comparative* organizers (for familiar material) explicitly link new content to what learners already know.

## Design Implications

Advance organizers work by activating relevant prior knowledge and giving learners a structure into which incoming details can be slotted, reducing the working-memory burden of holding unorganized information [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. A meta-analysis of over 100 studies found small but consistent positive effects on learning and retention, with the strongest effects when organizers were concrete rather than abstract [Luiten et al. meta-analysis.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543050002211) [+M]. The organizer must genuinely overlap with the content that follows — a generic "in this lesson you will learn…" list of objectives is not an organizer; it must supply a conceptual framework or analogy that does cognitive work.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Analysis of what prior knowledge learners actually hold, so the organizer anchors to something real ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
- Content organized hierarchically, with the organizer presenting the superordinate structure before details
- Concrete, familiar language or imagery — analogies, diagrams, or worked mini-cases outperform abstract prose [Mayer's research on concrete organizers.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049003390) [+M]
- Explicit signaling of how the organizer maps onto the material that follows

#### Constraints
- Effects are weak or absent when learners already have well-organized prior knowledge — the organizer adds redundancy rather than structure [~M]
- Abstract, generic organizers ("today we study photosynthesis") show near-zero effects; only organizers that model the *structure* of the content help [~S]
- If the organizer is disconnected from the lesson content, it can misdirect attention and impose extraneous load [-M]
- Organizers cannot compensate for poorly sequenced or incoherent content; they presuppose a logical hierarchy to preview

#### Implementation Variability
- **Expository organizers** — introduce new anchoring concepts for unfamiliar material (e.g., a brief model of the atom before a chemistry unit)
- **Comparative organizers** — relate new material to familiar knowledge (e.g., "the circulatory system works like a city's delivery network")
- **Graphic organizers** — spatial/diagrammatic forms such as concept maps or structured overviews, often more effective than prose for novices
- **Skimming organizers** — a brief preview passage of the actual text, effective for older learners with reading fluency

### Target Learners
- Novices with limited or poorly organized prior knowledge in the domain, who lack anchoring structures of their own [+M]
- Younger learners benefit more from concrete, graphic organizers; abstract verbal organizers suit older, more literate learners [~M]
- Learners with high existing expertise gain little and may find organizers redundant — an expertise-reversal pattern similar to [worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Meaningful verbal learning: retention and transfer of conceptual material
- Schema formation: building hierarchical knowledge structures
- Reading comprehension and expository text learning in content areas

### Instructions
1. Analyze the target content to identify its superordinate concepts and the prior knowledge learners bring ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))
2. Draft an organizer at a higher level of abstraction than the content — a model, analogy, or concept map — using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) or [Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md) where the domain is unfamiliar
3. Present the organizer before the material, explicitly signaling how it will map onto what follows ([Clear Structure Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
4. During instruction, refer back to the organizer so learners can subsume details under it ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
5. Close with a brief synthesis that revisits the organizer, consolidating the hierarchical structure

## Examples
- **Ausubel's original studies** — presenting a 500-word comparative passage on the properties of metals before a detailed metallurgy text, improving subsequent learning [doi:10.1037/h0046669](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046669)
- **Mayer's model-based organizers** — before a lesson on how a bicycle tire pump works, learners study a simple model with labeled parts, producing better creative problem-solving transfer [doi:10.3102/00346543049003390](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049003390)
- **Graphic organizer routines in content-area literacy** — structured overviews and concept maps used before expository reading in programs such as reciprocal teaching's framing activities
