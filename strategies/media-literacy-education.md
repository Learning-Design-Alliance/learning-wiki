# Media Literacy Education

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Media literacy education develops learners' capacity to critically analyze media messages, evaluate source credibility, and produce media themselves. Contemporary implementations emphasize *civic online reasoning* — determining who is behind a piece of information, what evidence supports it, and what other sources say — using techniques such as lateral reading and fact-checking strategies rather than relying on surface features of websites.

## Design Implications

Media literacy instruction works best when it teaches specific, transferable evaluation strategies rather than generic skepticism or checklists. Lateral reading — leaving a page to investigate the source itself — is the strategy professional fact-checkers use and can be taught to students with measurable gains [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. Checklist-based approaches (e.g., judging sites by design features or domain endings) are consistently outperformed by strategy-based instruction [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective for online source evaluation.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-S]. Comparing credible and non-credible sources side by side sharpens discrimination [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M], and structured evaluation tasks strengthen argumentation and reasoning skills [Argumentation improves reasoning.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic online materials, including real examples of misinformation, sponsored content, and manipulated media
- Explicit modeling of expert evaluation moves ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of lateral reading and source investigation)
- Repeated [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback on real-world evaluation tasks, not artificial worksheets
- Attention to emotional manipulation (outrage, fear) as a feature of persuasive media, since affect drives sharing behavior

#### Constraints
- Checklist heuristics (CRAAP-style criteria) fail with sophisticated misinformation and can create false confidence [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective for online source evaluation.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-S]
- One-off lessons or assemblies produce little durable change; benefits require distributed practice across the curriculum [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [~M]
- Generic "be skeptical" messaging can backfire, breeding cynicism that discounts credible sources along with false ones
- Effectiveness drops when examples are politically charged in ways that trigger identity-protective reasoning; depoliticized or cross-cutting examples work better [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Lateral reading routines** (Stanford History Education Group approach): leave the page, open new tabs, search for the source's reputation
- **Media production**: having students create media messages builds understanding of persuasive intent through construction
- **News/media analysis curricula**: analyzing framing, sourcing, and omission in journalism
- **Cross-curricular embedding**: evaluation strategies practiced in science (source of claims), history (primary vs. secondary sources), and civics

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and young adults, who consume most news via social media but evaluate it poorly by default [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Students with low prior knowledge of how the information ecosystem works (algorithms, sponsored content, astroturfing) benefit most from explicit strategy instruction
- Less effective when delivered to learners already fluent in lateral reading without new challenge [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical evaluation: judging credibility, evidence quality, and source motive
- Epistemic cognition: understanding how knowledge claims are produced and contested
- Civic reasoning: making informed decisions as citizens and voters
- Media production: communicating effectively and ethically in media forms

### Instructions
1. Activate prior beliefs about a source or headline to surface assumptions ([Activation](../elements/activation.md))
2. Demonstrate expert evaluation with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): open new tabs, search the organization, check the original source of a claim
3. Have learners [Practice](../elements/practice.md) the same routine on fresh, authentic examples with feedback
4. Use [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) — contrast a credible and a slickly designed non-credible source on the same topic
5. Move to [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md): learners justify credibility judgments with evidence and critique peers' evaluations
6. Culminate in media creation for an [Authentic Audience](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) so learners experience persuasive intent from the producer's side

## Examples
- **[Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu)** — Free, research-tested lessons teaching lateral reading and source evaluation; the primary evidence base for strategy-based media literacy instruction [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- **[News Literacy Project](https://newslit.org)** — "Checkology" virtual classroom with lessons on misinformation, bias, and conspiracy theories used in secondary schools
- **[Center for Media Literacy](https://www.medialit.org)** — Long-running K–12 framework organized around five key questions ("Who created this message?") for analyzing any media text
- **Finnish media literacy curriculum** — Nationally embedded media literacy across subjects, consistently ranked among the most effective national approaches to misinformation resilience
