# Activate Background Knowledge

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Activating background knowledge means deliberately prompting learners to retrieve relevant prior knowledge before new content is introduced — through opening questions, brief discussions, brainstorming, or structured activities such as [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or KWL charts. The goal is to bring relevant schemas into working memory so new information can be connected to, and integrated with, what learners already hold. Retrieval of prior knowledge is itself a form of [Practice](../elements/practice.md) that strengthens the existing schema while preparing it to receive new links.

## Design Implications

New learning is stored in relation to existing knowledge structures; when relevant prior knowledge is active, new material is encoded more meaningfully and with less working-memory strain [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Prior knowledge is one of the strongest predictors of learning outcomes, often outweighing general ability [Prior knowledge is not strongly related to performance.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [~M]. Activation works best when it surfaces *relevant* and *accurate* knowledge — prompting retrieval of what learners know about the topic, not merely asking whether they like it or have heard of it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identification of the prerequisite knowledge the new content depends on
- Prompts that require actual retrieval (recall, prediction, comparison), not just exposure to the topic
- A brief follow-up that connects learner responses to the upcoming content ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md))
- Willingness to diagnose and address misconceptions that surface during activation

#### Constraints
- Activating inaccurate or naive prior knowledge can reinforce misconceptions and interfere with new learning [~M] — activation must be paired with [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) or correction when errors surface
- Open-ended "what do you know about X?" prompts can consume time and activate irrelevant associations, adding extraneous load for novices [~M]
- Learners with genuinely little relevant knowledge gain little from activation; they need knowledge-building first, not retrieval attempts
- Activation prompts that are too difficult can frustrate and lower self-efficacy before instruction begins

#### Implementation Variability
- **Question-based:** 2–3 targeted recall or prediction questions at the start of a lesson
- **Discussion-based:** brief [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or think-pair-share surfacing prior ideas
- **Graphic:** KWL charts, concept maps, or [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that structure what learners recall
- **Analogical:** connecting new content to a familiar domain via [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and prior-knowledge activation routines
- **Diagnostic:** pre-tests or quick writes that simultaneously activate knowledge and inform the instructor's starting point

### Target Learners
- Learners who possess relevant knowledge but do not spontaneously retrieve it — the most common case in classroom settings [+M]
- Intermediate learners building on fragmented prior knowledge; activation helps consolidate and connect it
- Less useful for complete novices, who lack knowledge to retrieve and need direct knowledge-building instead [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: integrating new ideas with existing schemas
- Transfer: establishing the retrieval pathways that later support application in new contexts
- Misconception repair: surfacing naive conceptions so they can be explicitly addressed

### Instructions
1. Identify the prerequisite knowledge and common misconceptions for the new content.
2. Open with a short retrieval prompt — a question, prediction, or quick write — that requires learners to actively recall relevant knowledge rather than merely hear the topic named.
3. Surface responses through [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or pair sharing, making prior ideas visible.
4. Address inaccuracies explicitly using [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) that restructure naive ideas.
5. Bridge to new content with an [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) statement or visual that explicitly links what was recalled to what comes next.
6. Revisit the activated knowledge during [Application](../elements/application.md) tasks so the connection is used, not just stated.

## Examples
- **KWL charts (Ogle, 1986)** — Learners record what they *Know*, what they *Want* to know, and later what they *Learned*; widely used in reading instruction to structure activation before and reflection after a text.
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown, 1984)** — Before reading, students predict and connect to prior knowledge as one of four comprehension strategies, making activation a recurring habit rather than a one-off event.
- **Opening prediction problems in physics courses** — Instructors pose a qualitative question ("Which falls faster, a heavy or light object?") before instruction, surfacing intuitive theories that the lesson then confronts directly.
