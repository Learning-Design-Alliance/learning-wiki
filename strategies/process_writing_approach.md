# Process Writing Approach

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 review), `q3`–`q4` · 3 of 6 report an effect size

## Description
The process writing approach treats composing as a recursive cycle — planning, drafting, revising, editing, publishing — rather than a single-shot product. It includes many related activities: a greatly increased quantity of writing (only some of which is taken to publication), writing integrated throughout the course, mini-lessons on embedded skills (e.g., sentence combining), instructor modeling of writing and revision, and structured peer and teacher feedback. It is also a professional development model: student writing gains correlate with the depth of teachers' training in the approach, most visibly through the [National Writing Project](https://www.nwp.org).

## Design Implications

The approach works because it distributes cognitive load across the composing cycle — writers plan and revise with feedback rather than attempting to plan, compose, and edit simultaneously — and because frequent low-stakes writing builds fluency and automaticity in transcription, freeing working memory for higher-order composition [Collaborative writing with feedback improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence ([Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445)) identifies process writing, strategy instruction, and sentence combining among the most effective practices for adolescent writing improvement [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sustained time blocks for writing across multiple sessions, with a recursive (not linear) cycle
- Instructor modeling: drafting and revising in front of learners, and responding to feedback visibly ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of author decisions)
- Mini-lessons targeting embedded skills (sentence combining, transitions, conventions) delivered just-in-time
- Structured feedback from teacher and peers, with revision expected — feedback without opportunity to revise does not improve writing [Collaborative writing with feedback improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- An authentic audience or publication purpose for at least some pieces ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))
- Sustained, protected writing time with regular opportunities to draft and redraft
- Explicit instruction in specific processes — planning, [revising strategies](../strategies/action-oriented-feedback.md), sentence combining — not just workshop structures [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2006.09.006) [+S]
- Feedback directed at ideas and text structure rather than surface errors [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- A classroom community where peer response and [collaborative learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) are taught and normed, not merely assigned

#### Constraints
- Requires significant instructional time; a full cycle per piece is difficult in courses with heavy content coverage [-M]
- Effectiveness depends on instructor training; untrained implementation often collapses into "draft twice and fix commas," losing the revision focus [-M]
- Heavy emphasis on process can shortchange explicit mechanics instruction for learners with weak transcription skills, whose limited sentence-level fluency consumes the working memory revision requires [~M]
- Peer feedback is unreliable without training and protocols; unstructured peer review can reinforce errors [~M]
- Weak implementations — workshop routines without explicit strategy instruction or substantive teacher feedback — show markedly smaller effects [Graham & Sandmel, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.2010.488703) [~M]
- Unstructured peer response often devolves into praise ("I liked it") unless response protocols and [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) are explicitly taught [-M]
- Overemphasis on process can shortchange transcription fluency; struggling writers whose handwriting and spelling are effortful have little working memory left for revision, so mechanics instruction must not be omitted [-M]
- Full process cycles for every piece are time-expensive; forcing the full cycle on low-stakes writing dilutes both coverage and quality [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Writers' Workshop** (Calkins; Units of Study): mini-lesson → independent writing with conferring → sharing
- **Writing Process portfolios**: multiple drafts and reflective cover letters as assessment artifacts
- **Content-area integration**: short process cycles on disciplinary writing tasks rather than a standalone English class
- **Scaled-down cycles**: single-session plan–draft–revise loops for frequent low-stakes writing
- **Writers' workshop** (Calkins/Units of Study model): mini-lesson → independent writing → conferring → sharing
- **Strategy-infused process** (e.g., Self-Regulated Strategy Development): process stages combined with explicit strategies, self-talk, and goal setting — the highest-effect variant [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2006.09.006) [+S]
- **Digital process writing**: versioned drafting in platforms like Google Docs makes revision history visible and enables asynchronous peer and teacher comment
- **Scaffolded fading**: teacher support moves from heavy modeling and shared writing toward independent management of the full cycle [Support fades as responsibility transfers to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~M]

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults developing compositional competence; effects are strongest in grades 4–12 [Process writing instruction improves writing quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Struggling writers benefit especially from explicit mini-lessons and modeled revision, provided sentence-level skills are also addressed [~M]
- Beginning writers (K–2) need more structured scaffolds within the cycle; a fully open process can overwhelm them [-W]
- Adolescents and older students with enough transcription fluency to benefit from revising [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2006.09.006) [+S]
- Struggling writers, when the process structure is paired with explicit strategies and scaffolds rather than open-ended immersion [Graham & Sandmel, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.2010.488703) [~M]
- Young or novice writers need more direct modeling and shared writing before managing the cycle independently

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: organization, elaboration, coherence, audience awareness
- Writing fluency and volume: building comfort and automaticity with composing
- Self-regulated writing: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own text ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))
- Revision as a disposition: internalizing that writing is re-writing
- Writing quality: coherent, well-developed, audience-appropriate compositions
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own composing [Flower & Hayes, 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/356600) [+M]
- Metacognitive knowledge of writing as a craft, including revision as re-seeing rather than proofreading

### Instructions
1. Launch with frequent low-stakes writing to build volume and fluency ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
2. Model the target genre and revision moves by composing and revising in front of learners, verbalizing decisions
3. Guide planning: audience, purpose, and organization before drafting
4. Support drafting with [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) — teacher conferences and structured peer review focused on content before conventions
5. Deliver mini-lessons on needs revealed by the drafts (e.g., sentence combining); have learners apply them immediately in revision
6. Require revision, not just editing; publish or share selected pieces with an authentic audience
7. Assess growth through portfolios and [Enhance Retention and Transfer](../elements/enhance-retention-and-transfer.md) by applying the cycle to new genres

# Writing Process Approach

## Description
The writing process approach treats composing as a recursive cycle of planning, drafting, revising, and editing rather than a single-pass transcription task, grounded in the cognitive model of writing as goal-directed problem solving [Flower & Hayes, 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/356600) [+S]. Instruction is typically organized as a writers' workshop: extended time for writing, [mini-lessons](../elements/advance-organizers.md), teacher–student conferences, and peer response, with writing framed around authentic audiences and purposes. The teacher acts as coach and fellow writer rather than sole evaluator.

## Design Implications

The approach works because it externalizes and distributes the cognitive demands of composing across time and social support, reducing the working-memory burden of attending to ideas, syntax, and mechanics simultaneously [Flower & Hayes, 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/356600) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows the process approach produces reliable gains in writing quality, roughly 0.34 SD in adolescents [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2006.09.006) [+S], with effects strongest when it includes explicit strategy instruction rather than immersion alone [Graham & Sandmel, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.2010.488703) [~M].

### Instructions
1. **Set an authentic purpose.** Establish a real audience and genre for the piece ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md)).
2. **Model the process.** Demonstrate planning and drafting [think-aloud](../elements/coaching.md) style, making writer decisions visible.
3. **Teach a focused mini-lesson.** Target one strategy (e.g., planning with a graphic organizer, sentence combining) drawn from evidence-based practices [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2006.09.006) [+S].
4. **Support drafting with conferences.** Hold brief individual conferences; give [process-level feedback](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) on ideas and structure [+S].
5. **Structure peer response.** Use taught protocols (e.g., "praise–question–suggest") so feedback is specific and usable.
6. **Require revision and editing as separate passes.** Revision addresses meaning; editing addresses conventions — keep them distinct.
7. **Publish.** Bring the piece to its authentic audience to close the cycle and motivate future effort.
