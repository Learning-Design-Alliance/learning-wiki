# Repeat Key Points

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Repeating key points means deliberately restating the most important ideas of a lesson multiple times and through multiple representations — verbal restatement, visual display, worked example, student paraphrase — so that learners receive several spaced, varied exposures to each core concept. The variation matters as much as the repetition: each restatement should re-encode the idea in a different form or context rather than merely duplicating it.

## Design Implications

Repetition increases the probability that a key idea is encoded, consolidated, and retrievable, but simple verbatim repetition yields diminishing returns; spaced and varied restatements produce stronger retention than massed, identical ones [Spaced repetition improves long-term retention compared with massed repetition.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Combining verbal and visual representations engages dual channels and supports richer encoding than either alone [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]. Emphasis and repetition also direct learner attention to what matters, but only when the emphasized content is genuinely central [Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of emphasized content.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Careful lesson planning to identify the small set of key concepts worth repeating; repetition of everything dilutes the signal
- Varied language and representations for each restatement — plain language, analogy, visual, example ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
- A structure that spaces the repetitions across the lesson or unit (opening preview, mid-lesson application, closing summary) rather than clustering them

#### Constraints
- Verbatim, massed repetition is boring and can reduce attention and perceived relevance [~M] — learners disengage from content they have already heard once
- Repetition without variation can create fluency illusions: learners mistake ease of recognition for understanding and stop studying prematurely [~M]
- Effectiveness depends on learners noticing the repetition as a signal of importance; buried or unmarked restatements are often missed [~W]
- Students with weak self-monitoring may not recognize when they have not processed a repeated point and fail to ask for another pass [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher-led restatement at planned points in a lesson ([Direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) style)
- Student-generated repetition: learners restate key points to a peer, which adds [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) benefits to the exposure
- Distributed repetition across sessions (opening review of prior key points) rather than within a single lesson
- Signaled repetition: explicit framing ("this is the third way to see this idea") to mark importance

### Target Learners
- Learners with speed-of-processing or working-memory challenges who need additional exposures to consolidate an idea [Chunking reduces working-memory load and supports processing of complex content.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Novices, who lack prior knowledge structures to anchor a single hearing of new content
- Less necessary for advanced learners, for whom repeated restatement of familiar content is redundant and can feel patronizing [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention of core facts, definitions, and procedures [Spaced repetition improves long-term retention compared with massed repetition.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Comprehension of key concepts through multiple representations
- Support for learners who need processing time

### Instructions
1. Identify the 2–4 key points of the lesson; plan a distinct representation for each repetition ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
2. Preview the key points at the start of the lesson in plain language ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
3. Revisit each key point mid-lesson in a different modality — visual, example, or demonstration — rather than restating verbatim
4. Have students restate the key points to each other or in writing, converting repetition into retrieval practice ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md), [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
5. Close by summarizing the key points and flagging where each will recur in future lessons

## Related Strategies
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — grouping content so that what gets repeated is a coherent unit, not fragments
- [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) — a predictable lesson structure gives repetitions natural, recognizable slots
- [Spaced practice scheduling](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) — the research basis for distributing repetitions over time

## Examples
- During a science lesson, a teacher states the key idea, shows a diagram of it, works an example using it, and has students explain it to a partner — four varied exposures in one lesson. Resource: Speed of Processing micro-credential, Friday Institute at NC State.
- **Khan Academy** (https://www.khanacademy.org) — video explanations restate key steps verbally and on-screen, followed by practice items that repeat the same concept in varied problem forms.
- Opening "warm-up" problems in published curricula such as **Illustrative Mathematics** (https://illustrativemathematics.org) deliberately re-expose prior key concepts in new contexts at the start of each lesson.
