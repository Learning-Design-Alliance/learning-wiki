Encourage Feedback and Sharing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 1 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Encouraging feedback and sharing means structuring regular opportunities for learners to publish or present work-in-progress to peers — in class, in workshops, or on public platforms — and to give and receive structured critique. The strategy treats writing (or any product-oriented work) as communication for a real audience rather than an exercise for the instructor alone, and uses feedback exchanges as the engine of revision and metacognitive reflection.

Design Implications¶

Sharing work with peers converts writing from a private performance into a social act, which raises task value and engagement Task value increases motivation and engagement. [+M]. Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and the process rather than the person or a grade Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S], so the instructor's main job is not to collect feedback but to teach it: modeling critique, providing rubrics, and requiring revision in response. Giving feedback also benefits the giver — evaluating a peer's work against criteria deepens the reviewer's own understanding of quality Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Structured occasions for sharing (read-arounds, workshops, online forums) with clear norms for critique
  • Feedback guidance: rubrics, sentence stems, or protocols (e.g., "praise–question–suggest") so comments are specific and actionable
  • A revision cycle — feedback that never leads to revision signals that sharing is ceremonial
  • A classroom culture of psychological safety, established before public or online sharing begins

Constraints¶

  • Unstructured or untrained peer feedback is often vague ("good job") or purely evaluative and produces little improvement Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [-S]
  • Harsh or personal criticism can depress confidence and willingness to share, particularly for learners with low writing self-efficacy Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [~M]
  • Public or online sharing raises privacy, safety, and moderation concerns; publishing to the open web without consent or platform vetting is inappropriate for minors and sensitive work
  • Peer feedback on drafts can entrench errors when reviewers lack the expertise to judge correctness — best for audience awareness, clarity, and structure, weakest for factual accuracy

Implementation Variability¶

  • In-class workshop: small groups read aloud and critique drafts with a protocol; lowest risk, fastest turnaround
  • Reciprocal online peer review: a managed platform assigns anonymous reviews (e.g., Calibrated Peer Review, Peergrade/Peerfolio); scales to large classes
  • Public audience: class blogs, wikis, or publishing platforms give authentic readership; strongest motivational effect, highest moderation burden
  • Feedback role rotation: learners alternate author and reviewer roles so both perspectives are practiced

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers who need a sense of audience; writing for real readers improves attention to clarity and purpose [+W]
  • Learners with fragile confidence benefit from norms that require specific, criterion-referenced praise before critique, protecting self-efficacy while work is still in progress Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [~M]
  • Advanced learners benefit most from the reviewer role, where evaluating others' work sharpens their own criteria

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing quality: revision, clarity, audience awareness
  • Critical evaluation: applying quality criteria to texts, including one's own
  • Collaboration and communication: giving and receiving critique professionally
  • Metacognition: reflecting on how readers' responses differ from the writer's intent

Instructions¶

  1. Establish norms and model critique — the instructor shares a draft and thinks aloud while responding to feedback, demonstrating that revision is normal (Coaching, Articulation).
  2. Share drafts in small groups with a structured protocol and rubric so comments target the task and process, not the person (Collaboration, Class Discussion).
  3. Require written, criterion-referenced peer feedback exchanged reciprocally, ideally anonymized on a managed platform.
  4. Build revision into the cycle — authors respond to feedback and document what they changed and why (Assessment as formative, not summative).
  5. Extend to a broader audience once norms are secure: class publication, blog, or showcase, with consent and moderation.

Related Strategies¶

  • Peer review and workshop models — the formalized, assessable version of this strategy
  • Portfolio assessment — sharing and feedback cycles generate the revision evidence portfolios require
  • Writing-across-the-curriculum — sharing situates writing in authentic disciplinary audiences

Examples¶

  • Calibrated Peer Review (https://calibratedpeerreview.org) — learners first calibrate their judgment on sample texts, then review peers' anonymous writing; the calibration step directly addresses the weak-feedback constraint.
  • KQED "Youth Voices" (https://youthvoices.net) — a moderated online community where students publish writing and multimedia and comment on peers' work across schools.
  • Writing workshop (Atwell/Calkins model) — regular author's-chair sharing and peer conferencing embedded in the writing process, with the teacher conferencing alongside peers.
