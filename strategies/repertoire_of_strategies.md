# Repertoire of Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Procedural fluency requires having a repertoire of strategies. Before learners can flexibly choose an appropriate strategy, they must have strategies from which to choose. Strategies are flexible ways to solve a problem; algorithms are step-by-step procedures. Students should be able to flexibly use and adapt strategies and switch to a different strategy when their first choice is not working well. Every student must have the opportunity to learn more than one method — limiting students to only one method puts them at a disadvantage, denying them access to more intuitive methods and the opportunity to flexibly choose a method that fits the problem at hand.

## Design Implications

Building a strategy repertoire shifts instruction from single-procedure mastery toward comparison and adaptive choice. Comparing multiple solution methods for the same problem improves procedural flexibility and conceptual knowledge relative to studying methods in isolation [Comparing solution methods supports flexible procedural knowledge.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The goal is not maximal breadth but a curated set of methods whose trade-offs (speed, reliability, transparency) learners can articulate and justify.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Educators who provide varied methods, promote strategy comparisons, and cultivate a learning environment that values exploration and adaptation
- Deliberate sequencing: introduce a second strategy only after the first is reasonably secure, then compare ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md))
- Tasks that genuinely favor different strategies — if one method always wins, learners will not develop adaptive choice
- Opportunities to explain and justify strategy choice ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Requires significant preparation and instructional time; teaching multiple strategies effectively is slower than teaching one
- Presenting too many methods at once overwhelms novices and increases extraneous load [Example–problem sequences reduce cognitive load for novices.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [-M] — breadth must be built incrementally
- Premature comparison before any method is understood produces shallow, confusable knowledge rather than flexibility
- Weaker students can fixate on an inefficient preferred strategy if comparison is left unstructured [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Comparison-first:** present two worked solutions side by side and discuss trade-offs before practice
- **Invention-first:** let learners attempt problems, then share and compare student-generated methods (as in [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md))
- **Faded repertoire:** begin with one anchor strategy, add alternatives as expertise grows, consistent with the expertise-reversal principle [~S]

### Target Learners
- Learners who have secured at least one workable method and are ready to compare alternatives [Comparing solution methods supports flexible procedural knowledge.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Intermediate learners moving from accuracy toward efficiency and adaptivity; complete novices benefit first from a single clear method
- Students prone to rigid procedure-following, who need explicit permission and practice in switching strategies

### Target Learning Goals
- Flexible problem-solving: selecting and adapting methods to problem context
- Strategic thinking: articulating why a method fits a problem
- Adaptive expertise: switching strategies when the first choice stalls
- Conceptual grounding: understanding why different methods work [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish one reliable anchor strategy through [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md)
2. Introduce an alternative method via [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) — two worked solutions to the same problem, side by side
3. Prompt learners to explain when each method is advantageous ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md))
4. Assign mixed tasks where the "best" method varies, so learners must choose, not just execute
5. [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) on strategy selection and justification, not only on answers

## Examples
- **Number talks (K–6 mathematics):** a teacher poses one computation, students solve mentally with different methods, and the class compares strategies (e.g., decomposition vs. standard algorithm), making the repertoire public and evaluated.
- **Comparison in algebra (Rittle-Johnson & Star's studies):** students studying two worked examples of the same problem side by side, explaining similarities and differences, showed greater procedural flexibility than students studying the examples sequentially.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)** — teachers elicit and map children's informal strategies (direct modeling, counting, derived facts) rather than imposing a single algorithm.
