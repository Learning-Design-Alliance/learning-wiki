Small-group spatial language training sessions for preschoolers using a treasure hunt cover story¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), q3 · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

A concrete training recipe: six sessions of about 20 min in groups of four to eight children, mostly twice a week, training production and comprehension of the terms in front of, behind, to the left, and to the right within the egocentric frame of reference. A treasure hunt cover story motivates children, who name or place a wooden cube relative to a wooden half-sphere, receive corrective feedback, and earn stamps on a treasure map. The intervention significantly improved spatial language and numerical magnitude understanding.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Small groups of four to a maximum of eight children; feedback in each run giving the correct answer or repositioning the cube correctly

Constraints¶

  • Training was restricted to egocentric reference frames and four spatial terms; no significant transfer to verbal number skills was observed

Target Learners¶

  • 4-6-year-old daycare children

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Production and comprehension of spatial terms
  • Relative numerical magnitude understanding

Affordances¶

  • Mental Number Line Spatial Language Account

Claims¶

  • Spatial language training significantly improves 4-6-year-olds' spatial language comprehension compared to controls [+M]
  • Spatial language training improves children's numerical magnitude understanding (number words comparison) [+M]
  • Spatial language training does not significantly improve verbal number skills compared to controls [-M]
  • Training effects on spatial language comprehension and verbal number skills tended to appear in younger rather than older children [~W]
