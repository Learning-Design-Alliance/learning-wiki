# Ask the Right Questions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Before hitting "share," learners interrogate a source with a compact question set: Who made this? How was it made? Why was it made? When was it made? What is it missing? Where do I go from here? The strategy draws on WNYC's *On the Media* "Breaking News Consumer's Handbook" and NewseumED's *Believe It Or Not?* lesson, which walk teachers and learners through basic news literacy using real online sources.

## Design Implications

Teaching learners to ask structured provenance questions converts an amorphous disposition ("be skeptical") into a repeatable procedure, which matters because even college students struggle to evaluate digital information reliably [Breakstone et al., 2021 [+S]]. The questions work by directing attention to source identity, purpose, and omission — the features that most reliably discriminate credible from manipulated content — rather than surface cues like design polish, which novices over-weight [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019 [+S]].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short, memorable question list (the six "consumer questions" or the handbook's guidelines)
- A supply of authentic online sources — including deliberately questionable ones — for practice
- Time for learners to apply the questions to live examples, not just hear them ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Question checklists applied mechanically can produce false confidence: learners who answer the six questions superficially may still miss manipulated content, because expert fact-checkers rely on lateral reading (leaving the page to check the source) rather than on-page inspection alone [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019 [~S]]
- Requires prior critical-reading skill and attention to detail; learners with weak background knowledge of media institutions may not know *what* a satisfying answer to "Who made this?" looks like
- Effectiveness drops when all practice examples are obviously fake or obviously credible — learners need mixed, ambiguous cases to calibrate judgment

#### Implementation Variability
- Use the six consumer questions as a whole-class protocol, or distribute them as roles in small-group evaluation tasks
- Adapt the question set to a domain: scientific claims ("Who funded this study?"), images ("Where did this photo first appear?"), or social posts ("Is this account what it claims to be?")
- Pair with lateral-reading practice: after asking the on-page questions, learners open new tabs to verify the source before concluding

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students encountering social media news feeds as primary information sources [Breakstone et al., 2021 [+S]]
- General adult audiences; the question set is deliberately jargon-free and works without prior media training
- Weakest fit for learners who already have strong fact-checking habits, for whom the checklist adds little beyond what they do spontaneously [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019 [~M]]

### Target Learning Goals
- Source evaluation: judging credibility, provenance, and purpose of online information
- Critical thinking dispositions: habitual verification before sharing
- Media literacy: recognizing how format, funding, and framing shape a message

### Instructions
1. Introduce the six consumer questions with a brief worked demonstration on a live article, thinking aloud at each question.
2. Model lateral reading as a seventh move: leave the page to answer "Who made this?" rather than trusting the site's own About page [Wineburg & McGrew, 2019 [+S]].
3. Have learners apply the full question set to a mixed set of sources in pairs, recording evidence for each answer.
4. Convene a [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) to compare verdicts on ambiguous sources and surface disagreements about what counts as a sufficient answer.
5. Ask learners to write a one-paragraph verdict (share / don't share / needs verification) with justification, transferring the protocol to independent judgment.

## Examples
- **WNYC *On the Media*'s "Breaking News Consumer's Handbook"** ([onthemedia.org](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/otm)) — a one-page set of guidelines for vetting breaking news, usable as the classroom handout.
- **NewseumED "Believe It Or Not?" lesson** — a structured lesson walking learners through evaluating news stories with the six consumer questions.
- **Stanford History Education Group civic online reasoning curriculum** ([sheg.stanford.edu](https://sheg.stanford.edu)) — classroom tasks in which students evaluate real tweets, images, and websites; the evaluation questions embed the same provenance logic.
