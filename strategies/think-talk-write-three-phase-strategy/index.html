Implement think-talk-write as a three-phase cooperative sequence: think, talk, write¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 causal), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The think-talk-write strategy is a cooperative learning model in which students first study material individually, then discuss the results of learning in small groups, and finally write up the ideas obtained from the talk phase. The article describes it as a strategy that "constructs thought, reflection, and organizes ideas". In the study it was implemented in the experimental class in subgroups of 3-5 students during geometry and spatial relations instruction.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Students work in subgroups (3-5 students in the study) so that the talk phase can occur
  • The write phase follows the talk phase so students write the ideas obtained from discussion

Constraints¶

  • Evaluated only in eighth-grade geometry and spatial relations at one junior high school in Semarang, Indonesia; the authors call for a larger demographic to generalize

Target Learners¶

  • eighth-grade junior high school mathematics students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • mathematical representation ability
  • self-efficacy in mathematics

Claims¶

  • Students' mathematical representation ability differs by self-efficacy level, with high self-efficacy students scoring highest [~W]
  • Learning strategy and self-efficacy interact in their effect on mathematical representation ability [+W]
  • The think-talk-write advantage over expository teaching holds at every self-efficacy level and is largest among low self-efficacy students [+W]
  • Eighth-grade geometry students taught with the think-talk-write strategy show higher mathematical representation ability than students taught with the expository strategy [+W]
  • During classroom observation, think-talk-write students appeared more enthusiastic and confident in mathematics than expository-taught students [+W]
  • Higher proportions of think-talk-write students correctly answered the representation problem on all three indicators: visual, mathematical expressions and written texts [+W]
