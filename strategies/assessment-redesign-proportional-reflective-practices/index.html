Address generative AI in higher education through assessment redesign and proportional reflective practices rather than surveillance or punitive controls¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

The article recommends that institutions govern generative AI use by redesigning assessment so that meaningful reflection is credibly valued, rather than relying on detection or punishment. Reflective assessment requires students to articulate, justify, and critically evaluate how AI tools contributed to their work, and the reward for reflection must be proportionate to the effort it demands. The article states this provides "a formal, quantitative foundation for recent calls within higher education to address generative AI through assessment redesign and proportional reflective practices, rather than through increased surveillance or punitive controls".

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Reflective tasks must be credibly aligned with learning goals and their assessment value must be proportionate to the cognitive and time demands they impose

Constraints¶

  • The article notes reflective incentives fail when reflection effort is high relative to its reward, and that the model's predictions are not yet empirically validated

Target Learners¶

  • higher education students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • responsible, transparent, and pedagogically aligned engagement with generative AI

Affordances¶

  • Ai Use Coordination Game Framework

Related Strategies¶

  • Redesign assessments and curricula so AI availability supports rather than impedes expertise growth
  • Integrate GenAI into HSS education through scaffolded, practice-oriented training sequences and discipline-responsive, process-focused assessment
  • Embed AI as a learning partner and redesign assessments for AI-rich contexts
  • Fund AI literacy initiatives integrating AI ethics into curricula and adapting assessments
  • Engage students in active learning assignments using generative AI with structured reflection and critique of AI outputs
  • Redesign assessment reflectively for possible AI use, using supervised tasks for lower-order skills and intentional AI use with reflection for higher-order work
  • Use GenAI-driven disruption as an occasion to redesign assessment practices rather than preserve them
  • Institutional governance should establish practical conditions supporting AI literacy, learner agency, and critical evaluation rather than relying on prohibition and detection
  • Structure GenAI integration in higher education around feedback-literacy curricula and reflective course design
