# Modeled Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Modeled writing is a strategy in which the teacher or expert writer composes text while learners watch, narrating each decision — word choice, sentence construction, planning, and revision — as it is made. Unlike shared or guided writing, the learners do not contribute to the text; their role is to observe the entire generative process, including hesitation, self-correction, and rereading. It is the writing analogue of a live [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) narration.

## Design Implications

Modeled writing makes the normally invisible process of composition visible, converting tacit expert knowledge into observable moves [van Gog & Rummel, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9134-7) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the narration explaining *why* choices are made, not just *what* is written — silent modeling of finished text is far less effective than reasoning-aloud modeling [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [+S]. Because writing is a high-[cognitive load](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) task combining planning, transcription, and revision simultaneously [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S], observing a model lets novices study one layer of the process at a time before coordinating all of them.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine composition task performed live (or on screen), not a pre-written text merely displayed — the value lies in watching decisions unfold
- Verbalized reasoning: the modeler articulates goals, alternatives considered, and self-monitoring ("This sentence sounds flat — let me reread the last one…")
- Text at or slightly above learners' independent level, so the modeling stretches rather than loses them
- A prompt follow-on: [Guided Writing](guided-writing.md), [Practice](../elements/practice.md), or independent drafting of a similar text

#### Constraints
- Observation without subsequent writing produces illusory competence; learners rate modeled text easier to produce than it actually is [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/worked-examples-with-practice-improve-transfer.md) [-S]
- Overly polished, error-free modeling denies learners the chance to see diagnosis and repair — deliberately composing through problems (and even modeling brief error-and-correction) improves discrimination [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- Less effective for learners already fluent in the genre, for whom modeling is redundant [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]
- Long modeling sessions exceed attention limits; keep segments short (5–10 minutes) and focused on one aspect of craft [Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interactive modeling**: pause to poll learners on word choice before committing — a bridge toward [Shared Writing](shared-writing.md)
- **Revision-focused modeling**: start from a rough draft and revise publicly, foregrounding the editing process rather than first-draft generation
- **Video or screencast modeling**: recorded think-aloud writing (e.g., in [Writing Next](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/southeast/pdf/REL_20074024.pdf)-style process instruction) allows replay and self-pacing
- **Faded modeling**: move from full modeling → shared writing → guided writing → independent writing as the [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) sequence

### Target Learners
- Novice writers and emergent literacy learners who have not internalized how writers plan and revise [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]
- English learners, who benefit from hearing the reasoning behind syntactic and vocabulary choices in accessible language
- Struggling writers who tend to treat writing as pure transcription; modeling surfaces planning and self-regulation strategies [+M]
- Less valuable for proficient writers in a familiar genre [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural knowledge of the writing process: planning, drafting, revising, editing
- Genre conventions: how a particular text type is constructed sentence by sentence
- Self-regulated writing: internalizing monitoring and revision moves observed in the model
- Craft skills: sentence combining, word choice, paragraph organization

### Instructions
1. Choose a short, authentic writing task matched to the unit's genre and display the writing surface for all learners.
2. Plan aloud first — model generating and rejecting ideas before any text appears ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Compose slowly, verbalizing each decision: audience, purpose, sentence structure, word choice, and punctuation.
4. Deliberately encounter and resolve a problem (an awkward sentence, a missing transition) so learners see revision as normal, not failure.
5. Reread the completed text aloud, modeling evaluation against the intended purpose.
6. Transition immediately to [Guided Writing](guided-writing.md) or independent drafting of a parallel prompt, with the model text available as a reference.

## Examples
- **Regie Routman's Writing Essentials (2005)** — a widely used K–8 professional framework built around the "demonstration → shared demonstration → guided practice" writing sequence.
- **[The Writing Revolution](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org)** — Hochman & Wexler's method models sentence-level construction (e.g., because/but/so sentences) live before students attempt their own.
- **Early years "Morning Message"** — the teacher composes a daily class message on chart paper, verbalizing letter formation, spacing, and word choice while children observe.
