# Pre-Teaching Vocabulary

## Description
Pre-teaching vocabulary involves explicitly introducing selected new words before learners encounter them in a text, lecture, or lesson. The instructor provides student-friendly definitions, examples, and contexts of use, so that word meanings are available in memory when the text demands them. This front-loads word knowledge so that working memory during reading is spent on comprehension rather than on inferring unfamiliar meanings from context.

## Design Implications

Pre-teaching reduces the lexical processing burden during reading, freeing resources for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows vocabulary instruction reliably improves passage-level comprehension, though effects are strongest when instruction goes beyond definitions to include multiple encounters and rich processing of each word [~M]. Selecting a small number of high-utility words and teaching them deeply outperforms teaching many words superficially.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — that appear in the text and recur across contexts
- Student-friendly definitions, example sentences, and nonlinguistic representations ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md), images, gestures)
- Multiple meaningful encounters with each word before and during reading, ideally with [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) such as [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of usage
- A follow-on reading or lesson where learners encounter the words in context

#### Constraints
- Teaching words in isolation from context produces knowledge that often fails to transfer to comprehension; definitions alone are insufficient [-M]
- Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instructional time and overloads working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M]
- Effects on general vocabulary growth are small when words are taught only once and never revisited; without [Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md), gains fade [-M]
- Learners with strong vocabularies gain little from pre-teaching words they can infer from context — the approach shows an expertise-reversal pattern for known words [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full pre-teaching**: all critical words taught before reading with rich activities — appropriate for dense, unfamiliar texts
- **Selective pre-teaching**: only words essential to the passage's main ideas are taught; other words are handled with marginal glosses or during-reading support
- **Preview-in-context**: brief exposure to words within a related [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or summary rather than isolated word study
- **Learner-generated**: students predict meanings from morphology and context first, then compare with taught definitions — combining pre-teaching with [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers and English language learners, who lack the vocabulary breadth to infer unfamiliar word meanings from context [+M]
- Upper elementary through secondary students reading content-area texts with high densities of technical vocabulary
- Less beneficial for proficient readers with strong background knowledge, for whom brief glosses suffice [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension of texts containing unfamiliar vocabulary [+M]
- Word knowledge depth: multiple meaning facets, collocations, and morphological relatives
- Activation of background knowledge and schema building before reading

### Instructions
1. **Select words**: choose 5–10 words that are central to the text's meaning and high-utility across contexts; skip words learners can infer from context or morphology.
2. **Introduce meanings**: provide student-friendly definitions, example contexts, and nonlinguistic supports using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md).
3. **Process actively**: have learners discuss, sort, compare, or generate their own examples — deep processing of fewer words beats shallow exposure to many.
4. **Read and revisit**: assign the text ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)) and prompt learners to notice target words in context; revisit words in later sessions using [Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md) [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
5. **Assess in context**: check comprehension of the passage, not just word definitions, since the goal is comprehension transfer.

## Related Strategies
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md) — pre-teaching vocabulary is a lexical form of schema activation; both prepare knowledge before the main task
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — limiting the word set to a teachable chunk respects working memory limits
- [Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md) — revisiting pre-taught words across sessions converts initial exposure into durable word knowledge

## Examples
- **Beck & McKeown's Text Talk** — a published instructional routine in which teachers pre-select Tier Two words from read-alouds and engage children in rich, interactive word discussion before and after reading.
- **Word Generation** (https://wordgeneration.org) — a free middle-school curriculum that pre-teaches high-utility academic words weekly across subject areas with discussion and writing activities.
- **Content-area pre-reading in science** — before a unit on photosynthesis, the teacher introduces *chlorophyll*, *glucose*, and *stomata* with diagrams and examples, so the subsequent reading supports concept integration rather than decoding unfamiliar terms.
