Use a battery of standardized group class tests as a preliminary mental survey to rank children and select the lowest-ranked for individual examination¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 5 claims (4 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (2 associational), q2 · 2 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends giving a set of standardized group class tests to an entire institutional population, ranking children by median mental age from the tests, and beginning individual Yerkes or Binet examination with the lowest-ranked. The author argues "a group of well standardized class tests would form an excellent means for the first rough grading of children according to their mentality" and for larger-scale mental surveys. In the county home this located every feeble-minded child while roughly halving individual examinations.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Class tests must be adequately standardized on large numbers of children and chosen to correlate highly with general intelligence; the author was still working toward a well standardized group of class tests.

Constraints¶

  • Efficiency depends on examiner time available; with ample time the author would prefer examining all children individually, and the comparison against superintendent nomination rests on one above-average superintendent.

Target Learners¶

  • dependent children in children's homes and similar institutions

Target Learning Goals¶

  • identification of backward and feeble-minded children for appropriate placement and individual examination

Claims¶

  • Selecting the lowest 23 children by class tests would have detected every feeble-minded child, while the superintendent's nomination list detected only 60% of them [+W]
  • Rankings from a battery of eight group class tests corresponded closely with Yerkes Scale rankings, with an overall correlation of r = .80 across the 88 class-tested cases [+W]
  • If the lowest half of the home children as ranked by class tests had been individually tested, all feeble-minded cases and almost all definitely backward cases would have been detected [+W]
  • Mental index ratings on the group test showed close correspondence over a two-year interval (r = .72, rank correlation .77) among 46 retested children [+W]
  • Against Stanford Binet criteria for 300 grade 3–6 children, the mental index correlated .47, the educational index .52, crude score with mental age .80, mental with educational index .62, and Difference with I.Q. .21 [~W]
