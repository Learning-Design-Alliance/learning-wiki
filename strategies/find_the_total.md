# Find the Total

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners are shown two dot cards and asked to find the total number of dots. In a number-talk format, learners then explain *how* they found the total, making addition strategies (counting all, counting on, making ten, doubles) visible and comparable across the class. The activity pairs a concrete, subitizable visual with verbal strategy articulation, building fluency and part-whole understanding simultaneously.

## Design Implications

Dot arrangements that support perceptual grouping reduce working-memory demand during counting [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S], so cards should use structured patterns (ten-frames, dice patterns) rather than random scatter. Asking learners to verbalize their strategy before the teacher models one promotes strategy discovery and flexible thinking [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The strategy-sharing discussion is where most of the learning happens: hearing multiple solution paths for the same problem builds number sense and supports the part-whole schema that later underpins flexible arithmetic [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Dot cards with clear, countable arrangements (ten-frames, dice patterns, linear rows)
- A brief silent-thinking period before sharing, so all learners attempt a strategy
- A facilitation routine for eliciting and recording multiple strategies ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) norms, revoicing, strategy labeling)
- A way to record strategies visually (equations, arrows, annotated card images)

#### Constraints
- Random or unstructured dot arrangements force one-by-one counting and undermine subitizing and strategy use [-M]
- If the teacher demonstrates a single "correct" strategy first, learners converge on it and the strategy-comparison benefit is lost [-M]
- Dot ranges far above learners' subitizing capacity (typically 3–5 items for young children) overload perception and reduce the activity to rote counting [~M]
- Effectiveness depends on sustained, distributed use; a single session produces little durable fluency [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Ten-frame totals:** cards within ten-frames to surface "make ten" strategies
- **Doubles and near-doubles:** paired identical or near-identical cards to prompt derived-fact strategies
- **Missing-addend variant:** show total and one card, ask learners to find the hidden card
- **Student-created cards:** learners design their own dot cards and challenge peers, shifting to production
- **Rekenrek or dot-slide variant:** physical manipulatives replace cards for learners needing more concrete support

### Target Learners
- Pre-K through Grade 1 learners developing addition concepts and counting-on strategies
- Learners who rely on counting-all and need structured visuals to move toward counting-on and derived facts [~M]
- English language learners benefit from the visual anchor paired with structured math-language routines [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual: part-whole relationships, cardinality, composition of numbers
- Procedural fluency: mental addition within 10–20
- Metacognitive/communication: articulating and comparing solution strategies

### Instructions
1. Display two dot cards briefly (or leave visible for beginners) and pose the question: "How many dots in all?" ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
2. Give silent think time; learners show a quiet signal when they have a total and are ready to explain how they found it.
3. Elicit 2–4 different strategies; have learners explain their thinking aloud ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)), and record each strategy as an equation or annotated drawing.
4. Name and label strategies (counting on, making ten, doubles) so learners acquire the vocabulary for their own thinking.
5. Ask the comparison question — "Which strategies are alike? Which was fastest?" — to prompt reflection on efficiency.
6. Provide immediate, strategy-focused feedback rather than only confirming the total ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
