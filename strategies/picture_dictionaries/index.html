Picture Dictionaries¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 6 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Picture dictionaries are reference tools that use illustrations to define words and concepts, making them accessible to language learners of all ages. Each entry pairs a word with a corresponding image, leveraging the dual-channel structure of memory to bind meaning to form. They range from comprehensive published volumes (e.g., the Oxford Picture Dictionary) to learner-built personal collections, in print or digital form.

Design Implications¶

Pairing words with relevant images supports recall better than words alone, consistent with dual coding theory — verbal and pictorial codes provide two retrieval routes Learning improves when information is presented with complementary visuals rather than words alone. [+S]. However, the image must carry the meaning, not merely decorate the page: illustrations that are attractive but uninformative add no learning value and can distract Decorative illustrations that do not support the learning goal do not improve learning. [-S]. Picture dictionaries work best as one component of vocabulary instruction that includes contextualized encounters and productive use, not as a stand-alone memorization aid.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • High-quality, unambiguous images that directly depict the referent — clarity matters more than artistic polish
  • Thematic organization (e.g., food, travel, workplace) so related vocabulary clusters together and supports Chunking
  • Follow-on activities requiring productive use of the vocabulary (Application), since recognition from a picture does not guarantee production
  • For digital versions: search, audio pronunciation, and translation features extend accessibility

Constraints¶

  • Abstract, relational, and affective vocabulary (e.g., justice, although, hesitate) resists depiction; forcing images onto such words produces misleading or meaningless pictures [~M]
  • Picture-only lookup can anchor learners to a single prototype image, narrowing word meaning; captions and example sentences mitigate this
  • Heavy image dependence can become a crutch that delays reading fluency if learners never transition to text-based reference skills [~W]
  • Poorly chosen or culturally unfamiliar images create confusion rather than comprehension, particularly for learners from different cultural contexts [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Thematic picture dictionaries (Oxford Picture Dictionary) organize vocabulary by real-world context, supporting situated vocabulary learning
  • Learner-generated dictionaries — students draw or collect their own images, which adds generative processing and personal relevance
  • Digital/interactive versions (Merriam-Webster Visual Dictionary Online, Quizlet sets with images) add audio, search, and spaced review
  • Content-area picture dictionaries — customized for science, trades, or medical vocabulary where technical terms benefit from labeled diagrams

Target Learners¶

  • Beginning language learners and ESL students with limited print vocabulary Dual coding supports recall of vocabulary learned with images. [+M]
  • Young children and emergent readers still building the orthography–meaning connection
  • Learners with limited literacy in any language, including adults in workplace or settlement programs
  • Less necessary for advanced learners, who benefit more from monolingual definitions and contextual inference; pictures may add little beyond what text already provides [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Vocabulary acquisition: binding word form to meaning at the beginner stage
  • Comprehension support: enabling independent reading and task completion before full print fluency
  • Concept labeling in content areas: connecting technical terms to visualizable referents

Instructions¶

  1. Select or build a picture dictionary organized around themes relevant to the learners' goals and daily contexts.
  2. Introduce new vocabulary through the image–word pairing, adding pronunciation and an example sentence so the word is encountered in context, not isolation.
  3. Have learners use the vocabulary productively — labeling, describing images aloud, or writing sentences — rather than only recognizing it (Application).
  4. Encourage learners to add personal entries, drawings, or annotations to their own copies (Annotating), deepening processing through generation.
  5. Recycle vocabulary through spaced review and new contexts so knowledge moves beyond picture-dependent recognition.

Related Strategies¶

  • Vocabulary notebooks — picture dictionaries are the visual variant; both organize target vocabulary for personal review
  • Total Physical Response — shares the principle of anchoring meaning in non-verbal channels
  • Pre-teaching vocabulary — picture dictionaries supply the visual support during that pre-teaching
  • Small-group student-constructed reference products such as a Philosophy Dictionary

Examples¶

  • Oxford Picture Dictionary — thematically organized second-language picture dictionary with workbooks and audio; widely used in adult ESL programs.
  • Merriam-Webster Visual Dictionary Online — labeled thematic illustrations for technical and everyday vocabulary.
  • Quizlet — teachers and learners build custom flashcard sets pairing terms with images, adding spaced repetition on top of the picture–word pairing.
