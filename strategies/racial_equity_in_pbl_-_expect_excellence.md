# Racial Equity in PBL - Expect Excellence

## Description
A belief in excellence, demonstrated by setting high expectations, is the fuel that runs a successful PBL culture. Dispel the myth that PBL does not work for "those kids." The challenge lives in ensuring the proper scaffolding, knowing students' current realities, to move them toward the excellence they are capable of without lowering expectations. The strategy pairs an unwavering belief in every student's capacity for rigorous work with the instructional support needed to reach it — high expectations without scaffolding become unrealistic demands, and scaffolding without high expectations becomes lowered standards.

## Design Implications

Teacher expectations are among the most powerful and self-fulfilling forces in the classroom: teachers' beliefs about student capability shape the opportunities, feedback, and challenge students receive, which in turn shapes achievement [~M]. Because students of color are disproportionately subject to lowered expectations [~M], deliberately communicating and scaffolding high expectations is an equity practice, not just a motivational one. In project-based learning, where students must manage extended, ambiguous work, the absence of high expectations leaves underserved students with low-challenge tasks and the absence of scaffolding leaves them without access to the challenge.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A facilitator who genuinely believes in the potential of all students and can articulate that belief publicly and repeatedly
- Knowledge of students' current realities — prior knowledge, out-of-school responsibilities, and academic gaps — so scaffolds meet students where they are
- A repertoire of scaffolding moves ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), graduated task complexity) that make rigorous work accessible without diluting it
- Norms and rituals that publicly treat all students as capable of intellectual work (critique protocols, exhibition of high-quality exemplars from diverse students)

#### Constraints
- High expectations communicated without relational trust or adequate scaffolding can read as indifference or set students up for public failure [-M]
- "Rigor" framed only as volume of work or compliance produces burnout rather than excellence, particularly for students juggling out-of-school responsibilities [-W]
- A single mindset shift by the teacher is insufficient if school-level tracking, grading, and discipline practices continue to signal lowered expectations [-M]
- Expectation effects are weaker and slower when the teacher is the only adult in the building holding them [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Entry points differ: some facilitators begin with publicly exhibited high-quality work to redefine "what excellence looks like here"; others begin with one-on-one conferences establishing individual goals
- Scaffolding intensity varies by student but the destination does not — the same final product and rubric for all, with differentiated support en route
- In elementary settings, expectations are communicated largely through task design and questioning; in secondary settings, through critique protocols, revision requirements, and college/career framing

### Target Learners
- All grade levels, particularly students who are traditionally underserved and most likely to have been exposed to lowered expectations [~M]
- Students whose sense of belonging in rigorous academic settings is fragile; belonging interventions improve outcomes especially for marginalized groups [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less effective as a standalone strategy for students who need intensive foundational skill support — expectations must be paired with explicit skill instruction

### Target Learning Goals
- Culture-building: establishing shared norms of excellence, revision, and critique
- Self-efficacy and academic identity: students coming to see themselves as capable of rigorous work
- Quality craftsmanship: producing, critiquing, and revising work against high standards
- Transferable habits of mind: persistence through ambiguity inherent in [project-based learning](project-based-learning.md)

### Instructions
1. **Interrogate your own expectations.** Audit which students you call on, which tasks you assign, and whose work you display; identify where you have quietly lowered the bar.
2. **Make excellence public.** Display and critique high-quality exemplars, including work from students with backgrounds similar to your current students, so "excellence" is concrete rather than abstract.
3. **Model the standard.** Use [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) to demonstrate what rigorous work in the discipline looks like, including revision and struggle.
4. **Coach toward the bar, never move the bar.** Use [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and critique protocols to give every student feedback against the same rubric, differentiating the *support*, not the *standard*.
5. **Require revision.** Build cycles of critique and refinement into every project so that "done" means "meets the standard," not "finished."
6. **Know students' realities.** Use [Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and conferences to understand what support each student needs to reach the shared standard.

## Examples
- **EL Education (formerly Expeditionary Learning)** — network of schools built on the explicit principle of "high expectations for all students," with public exhibition of student work as a norm; supports the "Deeper Learning" PBL model. [https://eleducation.org](https://eleducation.org)
- **High Tech High (San Diego, CA)** — project-based schools serving a diverse population where all students complete and publicly exhibit rigorous projects; the school's adult culture explicitly rejects deficit framing of any student group. [https://hightechhigh.org](https://hightechhigh.org)
- **Ron Berger's "Ethic of Excellence"** — documented practice of using authentic audiences and multiple drafts to hold all students, including those in high-poverty rural schools, to professional-quality standards (Berger, 2003).
