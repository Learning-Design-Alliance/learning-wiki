# Value Affirmation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Value affirmation asks learners to select their most important values from a list (e.g., relationships, creativity, community) and write for 10–15 minutes about why those values matter to them. The technique comes from self-affirmation theory: affirming a source of self-worth unrelated to the threatened domain reduces defensive responses to stress and stereotype threat, freeing cognitive resources for learning. It is typically delivered as a short in-class or online writing activity at the start of a course or before high-stakes assessment.

## Design Implications

Value affirmation addresses the motivational and identity-based barriers that depress performance for learners who feel their group is negatively stereotyped in a domain — the writing task itself teaches no content, but removes an affective impediment to demonstrating what learners know [Brief belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Effects are strongest when the threat is real but the activity is framed as a routine classroom exercise, not a remedial intervention; labeling it as "support for struggling students" can itself signal threat and backfire [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A values list and structured writing prompt focused on *why* values matter personally (not why they matter to the domain)
- Delivery early in a course or before evaluative moments, when identity threat is salient
- A neutral, universal framing — all students complete it, not only targeted groups

#### Constraints
- Effects are unreliable for students not experiencing identity threat; affirmed-advantage groups often show null or negligible gains [-M]
- One-shot administrations produce effects that fade; benefits depend on threat recurring and being re-buffered, so single-session designs overstate durable impact [~M]
- If the learning environment itself is threatening (hostile climate, biased feedback), a writing task cannot compensate — affirmation buffers threat, it does not remove its source [-M]
- Asking students to write about values *relevant to the threatened domain* (e.g., "why math matters") can intensify rather than reduce threat [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written essays** (the classic protocol): rank values, then write 10–15 minutes about the top one
- **Repeated mini-affirmations**: short paragraph at each exam or unit start, sustaining the buffer over a term
- **Online variants**: LMS-embedded reflection at course entry, scalable in large enrollments
- **Combined designs**: paired with [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) activities or belonging-focused framing to address both identity threat and belonging uncertainty

### Target Learners
- Students from groups negatively stereotyped in the domain (e.g., women in physics, Black and Latino students in mathematics) — the original Cohen et al. seventh-grade study showed durable GPA effects for African American students [+M]
- First-generation college students and others experiencing belonging uncertainty [Brief belonging interventions improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students under evaluative stress generally, though effect sizes shrink as threat decreases [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Not a content-learning goal: the strategy targets *performance under threat* — closing achievement gaps on assessments
- Persistence and course completion in gatekeeper STEM and mathematics courses
- Reducing the defensive disengagement ("this domain doesn't matter to me") that precedes dropout

### Instructions
1. Open the term or unit with a universal, low-stakes writing task: students rank a values list and write about why their top value matters ([Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) framing; no reference to the course domain)
2. Repeat briefly before major evaluative events (exams, portfolio deadlines) rather than treating it as one-shot
3. Frame the activity as a standard classroom exercise for everyone — never as remediation
4. Follow with normal instruction; the affirmation works by removing threat, not by teaching, so pair it with sound design such as [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) and [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)
5. Avoid collecting or grading the writing; evaluative framing undermines the self-integrity mechanism

## Related Strategies
- [Belonging interventions](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) — the closely related family of social-psychological interventions; both target threat rather than content
- [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) — routine affective monitoring that sustains the same psychological safety over time

## Examples
- **Cohen et al. (2006, 2009)** — Seventh-grade students completed a values-affirmation writing assignment early in the term; African American students' grade gaps were substantially reduced, with effects persisting through middle school.
- **Miyake et al. (2010)** — A 15-minute values-affirmation at the start of an introductory physics course narrowed the gender gap in exam scores and course grades for women.
- **Introductory gateway courses** — Many institutions embed a values-affirmation or belonging module in the LMS during the first week of calculus or chemistry, repeated before each midterm.
