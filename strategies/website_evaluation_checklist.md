# Website Evaluation Checklist

## Description
A website evaluation checklist is a structured heuristic that guides learners through explicit criteria when judging the credibility of online sources — typically including corroboration of information across sources, author and publisher reliability, currency, documentation of claims, advocacy or bias, and conflicts of interest. Learners apply the checklist to real websites, often including deliberately unreliable ones, until the criteria become internalized evaluation habits.

## Design Implications

Checklists work by converting expert tacit knowledge into an external procedure, reducing the working-memory and knowledge demands of evaluation for novices [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on pairing with practice on authentic sites: checklists applied to real, contrasting cases build evaluation behavior, while checklists discussed abstractly do not [Fostering evaluation behavior requires practice with authentic tasks.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.09.021) [+M]. Contrasting reliable with unreliable sites sharpens discrimination in a way that examining only good sources does not [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short, concrete set of criteria with observable indicators (e.g., "Are claims linked to sources?" not "Is it trustworthy?")
- Authentic websites for practice, including unreliable or biased ones ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
- Modeling of expert application — an instructor thinking through a site aloud ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Follow-up tasks that require learners to justify their verdicts with evidence from the checklist

#### Constraints
- Checklist-only instruction without practice on real sites produces little transfer to actual search behavior [Walraven et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.09.021) [-M]
- Surface-feature checklists can be gamed: professional-looking design and .org domains mislead learners into false confidence, and most students fail such evaluations [Breakstone et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654321998074) [-S]
- Vertical (on-page) criteria underperform lateral reading — leaving the site to check what others say about it — for professional fact-checkers and students alike [Wineburg & McGrew (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912100103) [~S]
- Long checklists (10+ items) invite mechanical box-ticking rather than genuine judgment

#### Implementation Variability
- **CRAAP-style rubrics** (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) applied item-by-item
- **Lateral-reading checklists** — criteria about what to do off-page (open new tabs, search the organization, check Wikipedia) rather than on-page features
- **Contrast sets** — learners compare two sites on the same topic (e.g., a climate misinformation site vs. a scientific source) using the same criteria
- **Faded checklists** — full checklist first, then abbreviated prompts, then independent evaluation ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))

### Target Learners
- Secondary and postsecondary students conducting research for the first time, who default to surface features like design polish and domain endings [Breakstone et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654321998074) [+S]
- Novices with little prior knowledge of a topic, who cannot evaluate content directly and need procedural supports
- Less necessary for domain experts, who evaluate sources through content knowledge and lateral verification habits [Wineburg & McGrew (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912100103) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: judging source credibility and reliability
- Critical evaluation: discriminating evidence-based claims from advocacy
- Transferable inquiry habits: applying verification procedures across subjects and contexts

### Instructions
1. Introduce the criteria with a brief rationale for each, connecting them to how misinformation actually spreads.
2. Model application on one site using a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), making each criterion's evidence visible.
3. Have learners apply the checklist to a [Case Study](../elements/case-studies.md) pair — one reliable, one unreliable — and justify each rating.
4. Include unreliable examples deliberately; contrasting flawed sources builds sharper discrimination than good examples alone [Erroneous examples build conceptual knowledge.](../claims/erroneous-examples-build-conceptual-knowledge.md) [+M]
5. Teach lateral moves alongside on-page criteria: search for the author or organization, check corroborating sources ([3-Source Rule](../strategies/3-source_rule.md)).
6. [Fading](../elements/fading.md): reduce checklist support across successive tasks until learners evaluate independently.

## Examples
- **Stanford History Education Group (SHEG) "Civic Online Reasoning" curriculum** ([https://cor.stanford.edu](https://cor.stanford.edu)) — free lessons and assessments in which students evaluate real websites, tweets, and search results; built on the lateral-reading research [Wineburg & McGrew (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912100103).
- **CRAAP Test (Meriam Library, CSU Chico)** — a widely adopted checklist rubric used in library instruction sessions, typically paired with hands-on evaluation of student-selected sources.
- **News Literacy Project — Checkology®** ([https://checkology.org](https://checkology.org)) — interactive lessons where students apply evaluation criteria to authentic social media posts and news sites.
