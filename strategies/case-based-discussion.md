# Case Based Discussion

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Case based discussion places learners in front of a rich, authentic case — a patient history, a business dilemma, a legal dispute, an engineering failure — and asks them to analyze it aloud together, typically in a facilitated small-group or seminar format. The instructor acts as discussion leader rather than lecturer, probing reasoning, surfacing assumptions, and withholding the "answer" until learners have committed to and defended a position. It is the interactive core of [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md), distinct from merely reading or being told about a case.

## Design Implications

Discussion of authentic cases forces learners to apply concepts under the ambiguity of real-world conditions, converting inert knowledge into usable judgment [Case-based learning improves exam performance over lecture in health professions education.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. Because a single case is one instantiation of a concept, discussing multiple contrasting cases supports flexible, transferable schema formation [Multiple contrasting cases support flexible knowledge transfer.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M]. The facilitator's role is critical: learning gains depend on learners articulating and defending reasoning, not on the case document itself.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-written, authentic case with genuine ambiguity and multiple defensible positions
- Learners who have prepared — read the case and done preliminary analysis before discussion
- A facilitator skilled in questioning, redirecting, and managing turn-taking without lecturing
- Explicit discussion norms so learners commit to positions and respond to one another, not just the instructor

#### Constraints
- Fails when learners arrive unprepared; discussion collapses into the instructor analyzing the case alone [-M]
- Ineffective for novices lacking the foundational concepts the case requires — they cannot reason about what they do not yet know [~M]
- Large groups suppress participation; without structure, a few voices dominate and most learners passively observe [-M]
- Weak facilitation that rushes to "the right answer" reduces the activity to guess-the-instructor's-thought [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Harvard Business School method**: cold-calling in a plenary session, with the case as the sole preparation for class
- **Small-group jigsaw**: subgroups analyze different facets or different cases, then teach one another
- **Written pre-analysis**: learners submit a position memo before discussion, raising accountability
- **Flipped variant**: content delivery moves online; class time is devoted entirely to case discussion ([Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md))

### Target Learners
- Advanced novices and intermediate learners who have foundational concepts but lack application experience [Case-based learning improves exam performance over lecture in health professions education.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]
- Professional learners (medicine, law, business, teaching) who must develop judgment under ambiguity
- Not suitable as a first exposure for complete novices, who need direct instruction before case analysis [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied problem solving and decision-making in ill-structured domains
- Argumentation: constructing, defending, and revising positions in response to evidence and peers
- Professional reasoning and identity: practicing the habits of mind of a discipline
- Transfer: recognizing when and how concepts apply across varied situations

### Instructions
1. Select or write an authentic case with genuine ambiguity and stakes; prepare discussion questions that require decisions, not recall.
2. Assign the case in advance with a preparation task (position memo, decision recommendation) to ensure readiness.
3. Open with a brief framing of the decision to be made, then hand analysis to learners — use [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) moves such as probing ("What evidence supports that?") and redirecting ("Does anyone see it differently?").
4. Push learners to [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md): require them to commit to a position, defend it, and respond to counterarguments from peers.
5. Facilitate as [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — diagnose reasoning errors in the moment and probe rather than correct.
6. Close with an instructor wrap-up that names the concepts the case illustrates, contrasts defensible positions, and connects to other cases or principles — after learners have committed, not before.
7. Follow with a second, contrasting case or a written reflection to consolidate the schema ([Anchored Instruction](../elements/anchored-instruction.md)).

## Examples
- **Harvard Business School case method** — MBA students analyze a business case daily in plenary discussion led by cold-calling; the model for the [case-based learning pattern](../patterns/case-based-learning-harvard-method.md). [https://www.hbs.edu/mba/academic-experience/Pages/the-hbs-case-method.aspx](https://www.hbs.edu/mba/academic-experience/Pages/the-hbs-case-method.aspx)
- **Harvard Business School Publishing case collections** — curated cases with teaching notes used across business education. [https://hbsp.harvard.edu](https://hbsp.harvard.edu)
- **Problem-based learning in medical education** — tutorial groups discuss patient cases (e.g., the Maastricht and McMaster PBL curricula), with the case triggering self-directed learning followed by re-discussion.
- **National Center for Case Study Teaching in Science (University at Buffalo)** — peer-reviewed teaching cases with facilitation notes for STEM discussion. [https://nccsts.lib.buffalo.edu](https://nccsts.lib.buffalo.edu)
