# Laboratory Reports

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A laboratory report is a written artifact produced after hands-on experimentation, following the canonical structure of scientific writing: introduction with hypothesis, methods, results, and discussion. Learners must translate a concrete experience into formal scientific prose, which forces them to articulate procedures precisely, represent data accurately, and connect findings to theory. The report functions simultaneously as a learning activity (structuring and consolidating the experience) and as an assessment of both conceptual understanding and scientific communication.

## Design Implications

Writing a lab report requires learners to transform raw experience into organized, theory-linked knowledge — a generative process that strengthens retention and reveals misconceptions that hands-on activity alone leaves hidden [~M]. The discussion section carries most of the learning load: interpreting results against predictions engages [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) and [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md), converting procedural experience into transferable conceptual understanding. Reports also enact [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) when paired with rubrics and feedback, since the draft-report-feedback-revision cycle makes scientific norms visible and improvable [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- An actual experiment or data set the learner generated or can interrogate — reports written about fabricated data undermine authenticity and motivation
- An explicit structure or template (IMRaD or equivalent) so novices know the genre conventions
- A rubric distinguishing procedural accuracy, data representation, and quality of interpretation
- Feedback that addresses reasoning in the discussion, not just formatting and mechanics [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- When learners follow cookbook procedures without understanding, the report becomes a transcription exercise with minimal learning value [-M] — the write-up cannot rescue an experiment the learner did not understand
- Heavy emphasis on formatting conventions can crowd out scientific reasoning; novices often spend their effort on surface structure rather than interpretation [-M]
- Grading reports purely on matching "expected results" punishes honest data and teaches that science is about confirming answers, not interpreting evidence [-S]
- Writing load can impose extraneous [cognitive load](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) for learners still mastering the content, degrading both the writing and the learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Scaffolded templates**: sentence frames and section prompts for novices, faded toward open reports as expertise grows
- **Partial reports**: assigning only the discussion or only the results analysis when time is limited, targeting the highest-value section
- **Peer review**: structured peer feedback on drafts before submission builds evaluation skills and exposes learners to alternative interpretations
- **Post-lab data comparison**: pooling class data and having learners compare their results to others' supports [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) and normalizes variability in empirical data [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students in science courses encountering experimental method
- Novices benefit from explicit templates and rubrics; advanced students should write open-ended reports on self-designed investigations [~M]
- Students learning discipline-specific communication norms (the report is the genre of professional science)

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: connecting experimental evidence to theory in the discussion section
- Procedural and epistemic knowledge: understanding how methods, data, and claims relate
- Scientific communication: writing in the conventions of the discipline
- Data literacy: representing and interpreting quantitative results

### Instructions
1. Before the lab, activate relevant theory and have learners state a testable hypothesis ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Activation](../elements/activation.md))
2. During the experiment, require structured recording of procedures and raw data ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)) so the report has authentic material to draw on
3. After data collection, support analysis with [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of data into tables and graphs before any prose is written
4. Have learners draft the report using a template, with the discussion requiring explicit comparison of results to the original hypothesis and to theory
5. Provide rubric-based feedback prioritizing interpretation, and allow revision ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
6. Optionally, run structured peer review between drafting and submission

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](../strategies/case-based-learning.md) — reports on real datasets or published studies share the same evidence-to-claim reasoning structure

## Examples
- **[5E Learning Cycle](../patterns/5e-learning-cycle.md)** — the lab report serves the "Explain" and "Evaluate" phases, where learners formalize what the "Explore" phase surfaced
- **[Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)** — instructor annotation of a model report makes expert scientific writing visible before students draft their own
- **[Constructive Alignment](../patterns/constructive-alignment.md)** — report rubrics aligned to stated learning outcomes ensure the writing task assesses the intended reasoning, not formatting compliance
- **CUREs (Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences)**, e.g., the [Tiny Earth](https://tinyearth.wisc.edu) network — students write reports on genuine original research, raising authenticity and ownership
