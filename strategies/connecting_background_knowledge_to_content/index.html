Connecting Background Knowledge To Content¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 2 mixed) · 9 studies (3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 2 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 9 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

This strategy deliberately links new content to what learners already know — their prior academic learning, everyday experiences, and cultural practices. It is carried out by surfacing relevant knowledge before or during instruction (through discussion, questioning, or advance organizers), then explicitly bridging that knowledge to the new material with analogies, examples drawn from learners' lives, and comparisons. The goal is to give new content a familiar "hook" so it is comprehensible and meaningful rather than arbitrary.

Design Implications¶

New knowledge is stored in relation to existing knowledge structures; instruction that activates and builds on relevant prior knowledge produces better comprehension and retention than instruction that presents content in isolation Activation of prior knowledge improves comprehension of new material. [+M]. Comprehension depends less on the text or content itself than on whether the learner can relate it to a familiar knowledge schema — famously demonstrated by studies where identical passages become comprehensible only once a relevant schema is named Prior knowledge determines comprehension of ambiguous text. [~M]. The strategy also manages cognitive load: when content connects to existing schemas, learners process it as structured chunks rather than as isolated elements Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Knowledge of learners' actual backgrounds — gathered through Activating Prior Knowledge routines, interest surveys, or community input — not assumed backgrounds
  • Explicit bridging moves: analogies, comparisons, or examples that connect the familiar to the new (Analogies, Advance Organizers)
  • Willingness to revise examples and materials so they reflect learners' experiences, not just the textbook's

Constraints¶

  • Activating the wrong prior knowledge — misconceptions, stereotypes, or irrelevant associations — interferes with learning more than activating nothing Erroneous prior knowledge interferes with new learning. [~M]
  • Superficial "cultural decoration" (renaming characters or swapping foods) without conceptual connection does not improve learning and can feel tokenizing [-W]
  • Assumes shared background that may not exist; heterogeneous classrooms require multiple entry points rather than one assumed common experience [~M]
  • Over-reliance on familiar contexts can limit transfer to unfamiliar or formal contexts if the bridge is never faded [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pre-reading/pre-instruction activation: brief discussions or Advance Organizers that surface relevant schemas before new content
  • Culturally responsive content design: selecting texts, problems, and cases set in contexts learners recognize (e.g., word problems using local economics, literature from learners' communities)
  • Funds-of-knowledge approaches: treating learners' home and community practices as curricular resources, inviting learners to bring expertise into the classroom
  • Analogical bridging: mapping a new abstract concept onto a familiar concrete one, then explicitly noting where the analogy breaks down

Target Learners¶

  • Learners whose home or community knowledge differs from the default academic culture — the strategy reduces the comprehension gap these learners otherwise face [+M]
  • Novices in a domain, who lack the schemas that would otherwise let them organize new content Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Less necessary for learners with strong domain expertise, who already possess the relevant schemas and may find extensive scaffolding redundant [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of new concepts and texts
  • Conceptual understanding and schema formation
  • Engagement and motivation through perceived relevance
  • Transfer, when connections are made explicit and then generalized beyond the initial familiar context

Instructions¶

  1. Surface relevant prior knowledge with a brief activation routine — discussion, quick-write, or question — before presenting new content (Activation)
  2. Bridge the familiar to the new using an analogy, comparison, or example drawn from learners' experience (Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation)
  3. Organize the new content under a structure that names its relationship to what learners already know (Advance Organizers)
  4. Check that the activated knowledge is accurate and relevant; address misconceptions directly rather than building on them (Cognitive Conflict)
  5. Fade the familiar context, gradually presenting content in more formal or unfamiliar settings so learners generalize beyond the bridge

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the activation routine this strategy builds on; connecting goes further by explicitly bridging to new content
  • Accessing Students' Background Knowledge — the diagnostic step of learning what learners actually bring
  • Activate Background Knowledge — the general principle of readiness before instruction
  • Check context familiarity by asking the class to share what they know about a problem's context before working on it

Examples¶

  • Anchored Instruction (e.g., The Adventures of Jasper Woodbury mathematics series) — embeds new math content in a rich video story world that gives all learners a shared experiential anchor.
  • Funds of Knowledge projects (Moll and colleagues, Tucson, AZ) — teachers visit students' households, document household practices (mechanics, farming, finance), and build lessons that use those practices as the context for academic content.
  • Khan Academy — math word problems increasingly use varied real-world contexts; teachers using the platform are advised to preview contexts and reframe problems using local examples when contexts are unfamiliar.
  • Facing History and Ourselves — begins history units with students' own identities and community experiences before bridging to historical case studies.
