# In A Hot Seat

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
In A Hot Seat is a drama-based strategy in which a learner adopts a character — typically constructed from a stimulus such as a photograph, artifact, or historical document — and sits in a designated "hot seat" while classmates ask questions that the learner answers in role. The technique, developed within Dorothy Heathcote's process drama tradition, uses improvised role-play to make learners reason from inside another person's situation rather than about it. It is carried out in three phases: character construction from the stimulus, improvised questioning by the group, and debriefing out of role.

## Design Implications

Hot seating works by forcing situated perspective-taking: constructing and sustaining a character requires learners to infer motives, constraints, and knowledge states of someone unlike themselves, which improves understanding of others' perspectives [Situated perspective-taking reduces stereotyping and improves intergroup attitudes.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.06.008) [+M]. Because answers are improvised, learners must activate and organize relevant knowledge quickly, making gaps in understanding visible to themselves and the teacher in ways a written report would not. The public, performative format also raises engagement and accountability compared with individual written response [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich stimulus (photo, object, text, or scenario) that grounds the character in enough detail to reason from
- Clear role boundaries: the class understands who may ask what, and that questions stay in the fictional frame
- A debrief phase out of role, where learners articulate what they inferred and what evidence supported it
- Teacher facilitation skill — modeling question types and stepping in when a role stalls ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners with strong social anxiety or low verbal fluency can experience the public improvisation as threatening rather than engaging; forcing participation without an opt-in pathway reduces the strategy's benefits [~M]
- If the stimulus is thin or stereotyped, learners may rehearse caricature rather than perspective-taking, reinforcing exactly the biases the activity aims to reduce [~M]
- Improvised answers can entrench factual errors when learners invent details beyond their knowledge; without a debrief that separates invention from evidence, misconceptions persist [-M]
- Empathy gains from single perspective-taking episodes are often short-lived and may not generalize to the group as a whole [Perspective-taking improves attitudes toward individuals but generalization to the whole group is weaker.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.73.1.105) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher in the hot seat** — the instructor plays a contested figure (e.g., a factory owner in an industrialization unit), letting learners practice questioning and evidence-pressing
- **Object hot seat** — a learner speaks *as* an artifact or concept (a volcano, a primary source document), serving conceptual rather than empathic goals
- **Written hot seat** — learners answer prepared questions in character on paper, lowering the performance barrier for anxious students
- **Repeated hot seating** — the same character returns across a unit as knowledge deepens, allowing more sophisticated answers over time

### Target Learners
- Middle and secondary students in humanities, history, literature, and social studies, where character reasoning maps directly onto disciplinary thinking
- Learners who benefit from embodied, social encoding over purely written formats [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Groups working on intergroup understanding, where situated perspective-taking outperforms abstract discussion for reducing stereotyping [Situated perspective-taking reduces stereotyping.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.06.008) [+M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for learners with high communication anxiety or very limited language proficiency in the language of instruction

### Target Learning Goals
- Perspective-taking and empathy: understanding the motives, constraints, and emotions of people in other roles or eras
- Conceptual understanding: articulating how a system, event, or concept would "experience" a situation
- Oral reasoning and evidence use: answering unscripted questions requires on-demand justification
- Historical or literary interpretation: inhabiting a viewpoint to test its coherence against source material

### Instructions
1. **Select and introduce the stimulus.** Present a photo, object, or document and briefly establish the situation. Keep framing minimal so learners construct the character themselves.
2. **Build the character.** Learners (individually or in pairs) generate the character's name, circumstances, concerns, and what they would know and not know. Pair construction reduces the load on anxious students ([Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)).
3. **Seat the character.** The learner sits in the hot seat and answers questions in role, improvising from the constructed background ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)).
4. **Facilitate questioning.** Coach the audience toward open and probing questions rather than yes/no ones; press for evidence from the stimulus ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. **Debrief out of role.** Step out of the fiction and ask: What did we infer? What evidence supported it? What did we invent? This separates grounded inference from fabrication and consolidates learning ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).

## Examples
- **History: trench letter hot seat.** After reading soldiers' letters from WWI, a student sits in the hot seat as a soldier; classmates ask about conditions, morale, and decisions, then the class checks answers against the primary sources.
- **Science: the hot-seated river.** In an ecology unit, a learner speaks as a polluted river ecosystem, answering questions from "stakeholder" classmates — a variant Heathcote-style practitioners use to make system interactions concrete.
- **Mantle of the Expert classrooms** ([Drama for Learning](https://www.heinemann.com)), where hot seating is a recurring move inside an extended fictional enterprise, e.g., questioning the "company archaeologist" about a find.
