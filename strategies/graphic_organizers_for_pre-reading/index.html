Graphic Organizers for Pre-Reading¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 11 studies (4 causal, 4 review, 3 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 3 of 11 report an effect size

Description¶

Students identify the text structure (compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution, sequence) before reading and complete a graphic organizer that mirrors that structure as they read. The organizer functions as an Advance Organizer: it provides a skeletal framework that learners populate during reading, converting prose into a spatial representation of the text's logic. KWL charts and similar pre-reading frames also activate prior knowledge and set a purpose for reading.

Design Implications¶

Graphic organizers reduce extraneous load during reading by externalizing the text's macrostructure, letting working memory attend to meaning-making rather than holding intermediate ideas Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Their effectiveness depends on matching the organizer to the actual text structure — a mismatched organizer forces learners to impose the wrong schema Advance organizers improve learning. [~M]. Combining verbal and spatial representation also exploits dual coding, giving learners two retrieval routes into the content Dual coding improves recall. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Reading materials whose structure can be reliably identified in advance
  • Organizer templates matched to common expository structures (compare/contrast matrix, cause/effect chain, timeline, problem/solution frame)
  • Brief Demonstration of how to identify the structure and populate the organizer, especially for students new to the practice
  • Activation of prior knowledge before reading (e.g., the K and W columns of a KWL chart)

Constraints¶

  • Students must be taught to identify text structures; without this training they cannot select or benefit from a structure-matched organizer [-M]
  • Organizers can oversimplify complex or multi-structural texts, imposing a single schema on layered argumentation [~M]
  • Pre-filled or overly detailed organizers encourage copying rather than comprehension; learners who complete organizers passively show weaker gains than those who must generate the relations themselves [~M]
  • Benefits diminish for skilled readers with strong prior knowledge of the topic, for whom the organizer adds redundant processing [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Structure-identified: teacher names the structure and supplies the matching organizer (strongest for novices)
  • Structure-hunted: students infer the structure from a preview, then select an organizer (builds transferable structure awareness)
  • KWL charts: activate prior knowledge and set purpose; weaker structure support but strong Activation function
  • Partially completed organizers: some cells pre-filled to model the format, remaining cells completed during reading (a form of Fading)

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary through secondary students reading expository text, particularly struggling readers and English learners Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]
  • Students with limited prior knowledge of the topic, who benefit most from the activated schema and structural frame
  • Less beneficial for advanced readers already familiar with the text structure and topic [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension of expository and informational text
  • Text-structure awareness: recognizing compare/contrast, cause/effect, and problem/solution patterns as transferable schemas
  • Prior-knowledge activation and purpose-setting before reading Activation improves learning. [+M]
  • Summarization and note-taking from informational sources

Instructions¶

  1. Preview the text and identify its dominant structure (or have students infer it from headings and a first-draft skim).
  2. Select or have students select the organizer that matches that structure.
  3. Activate prior knowledge: brainstorm what students already know and what they want to learn (KWL "K" and "W" columns).
  4. Demonstrate completing one section of the organizer with a think-aloud, modeling how textual signals ("however," "as a result") map to organizer cells.
  5. Students read and complete the organizer, using Annotating to mark structure signals in the text.
  6. Debrief: students use the completed organizer to summarize or discuss, converting the visual frame back into connected prose.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the KWL "K" phase is a pre-reading activation routine that pairs naturally with organizers
  • Annotating — marking structure signals in the text complements populating the organizer
  • Question-Answer Relationships — another comprehension strategy that sets a purpose before reading

Related Elements¶

  • Advance Organizers — the theoretical ancestor; Ausubel's framework for providing an anchoring structure before learning
  • Demonstration — modeling how to identify structure and fill the organizer
  • Activation — the pre-reading knowledge-elicitation step
  • Chunking — organizers segment text into manageable structural units

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Direct Instruction — the teacher-led modeling and guided completion sequence
  • Reciprocal Teaching — organizers can serve as the concrete support for the predicting and summarizing roles

Examples¶

  • Students use a cause-and-effect chain diagram while reading about the American Revolution, first identifying "cause/effect" from the chapter headings and a teacher think-aloud. See Cult of Pedagogy's graphic organizer collection.
  • Reading Rockets provides structure-matched organizer templates (compare/contrast matrix, story map, KWL) with implementation guidance: https://www.readingrockets.org/classroom/classroom-strategies/graphic-organizers
  • A science teacher uses a problem/solution frame for a text on ocean acidification, pre-filling the "problem" cell to model the format before students complete the rest during reading.
