Small-Group Scaffolding Tool¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 unmarked) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 associational), q3 · 1 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Standard scaffolding theory (contingency, fading, transfer of responsibility) is normally operationalized at the level of an individual student's zone of proximal development, even inside a group setting. The Small-Group Scaffolding Tool (SGS-Tool) instead trains teachers to diagnose and respond to the group's maximum level of understanding as a collective unit. It is a six-step decision procedure: the teacher first determines whether the group as a whole is stuck (not just one member), and only then diagnoses the group's collective understanding, checks that diagnosis with the group, provides content support pitched to that collective level, checks whether the group can now proceed unaided, and — if so — deliberately steps back into process support (encouraging students to explain to and question each other) rather than continuing to supply content. See Scaffolding for the underlying element this strategy specializes.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Short, structured professional development combining individual tool explanation with on-the-job coaching during live lessons and post-lesson debriefs — a written procedure alone was not the delivery method in the source study
  • A collaborative task structure (small-group mathematical discussion, in the source study) where a group can plausibly reach a genuinely shared sticking point, as opposed to independent seatwork

Constraints¶

  • Teachers in the source study found Step 4 (content support) the hardest step to limit — the impulse to over-explain persisted even after training, and over-explaining caused Steps 5–6 to be skipped
  • The tool changed teachers' behavior (more diagnosis, more process support, more student participation) but did not significantly change the contingency of the content support they gave (p = .134) — training changed which steps teachers used, not necessarily how well-calibrated their content explanations were
  • Step 5 (checking whether the group can continue unaided) is precisely the "timely fading" check that Students' application of teacher support predicts accurate answers in small-group work found necessary for students to actually apply support afterward — skipping straight from Step 4 to withdrawal risks the same untimely-fading failure mode documented in that separate study

Implementation Variability¶

  • The six steps can be introduced as a flowchart teachers consult in the moment; the source study paired this with ongoing coaching rather than a one-time training session

Target Learners¶

  • Small groups of students engaged in collaborative discussion (studied here in 7th-grade mathematics) where a teacher circulates between groups
  • Link to sub-claim: Group-level scaffolding training increases teacher process support and student participation

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Increasing the proportion of group members actively participating in teacher–group interactions, rather than one student acting as the group's sole interlocutor with the teacher
  • Link to sub-claim: Group-level scaffolding training increases teacher process support and student participation

Instructions¶

  1. Determine whether the group's maximum level of understanding has actually been reached — is the question coming from the whole group being stuck, or just one student? If not the whole group, skip to Step 6 (process support) to keep the group's own discussion going.
  2. Diagnose the group's collective maximum level of understanding by observing the group's activity and asking about the work already done.
  3. Check the diagnosis by summarizing back what the group has said and confirming accuracy.
  4. Provide contingent content support pitched to the group's diagnosed maximum level — not to any one student's level.
  5. Check whether at least one group member has now understood the support well enough for the group to continue unaided.
  6. Provide process support — explicitly prompt group members to explain to, question, and critique each other — and disengage, returning responsibility to the group.
