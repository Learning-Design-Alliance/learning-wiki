# Direct Instruction: Genre-specific Writing Strategies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy makes the features of a target genre — its purpose, audience, structure, and language conventions — explicit, then teaches the planning, drafting, and revising strategies expert writers use within that genre. Instruction typically proceeds through explanation, [modeling](../elements/modeling.md) of a mentor text, guided practice, and independent application. It is a core component of [explicit writing instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) and is among the most consistently supported approaches in the writing intervention literature [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis found strategy instruction among the largest effects on writing quality.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-writing-quality.md) [+S]

## Design Implications

Genre knowledge is largely tacit; learners who have not absorbed it through wide reading benefit from having it made visible and teachable. Explicit strategy instruction reduces the unguided search that otherwise dominates novice writing effort, functioning as a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) by supplying a structural plan the writer does not have to invent [+M]. Analysis of contrasting mentor texts supports abstraction of genre features better than exposure to a single exemplar [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic mentor texts in the target genre, ideally several for comparison
- An explicit, teachable description of the genre's purpose, structure, and language features ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) work well as framing devices)
- Teacher modeling of writing decisions, not just finished products ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) while composing)
- Guided practice with feedback before independent writing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))

#### Constraints
- Overly rigid genre formulas (e.g., the five-paragraph essay as a universal template) produce formulaic writing that fails to transfer to authentic tasks [~M] — the genre's *rhetorical purpose* must be taught, not just its surface shape
- Less effective when genre features are taught as decontextualized rules divorced from audience and purpose [-M]
- Effects diminish for learners who already control the genre's conventions; continued explicit scaffolding can suppress development of flexible, self-regulated composing [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Text-analysis-first**: students infer genre features from contrasting mentor texts before receiving explicit naming of them
- **Strategy-first**: teacher presents the genre's structural plan (e.g., POW+TREE for persuasive writing) and models its use
- **Writing across the curriculum**: the same genre is taught within subject-area content, strengthening both genre control and content learning
- **SRSD adaptation**: genre strategies are embedded in self-regulated strategy development, adding goal-setting and self-monitoring

### Target Learners
- Struggling and novice writers, who show the largest gains from explicit genre strategy instruction [+S]
- Adolescent writers who have not absorbed genre conventions implicitly through reading [+M]
- Multilingual learners, for whom explicit genre conventions reduce reliance on intuitions from their first language's rhetorical norms [+M]
- Advanced writers in a genre they already control benefit less; instruction should shift toward self-regulation and stylistic refinement [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Genre control: producing texts that meet the structural and conventional expectations of a discourse community
- Rhetorical awareness: matching content, structure, and tone to purpose and audience
- Planning and revising skill: applying genre-specific strategies for generating, organizing, and evaluating text
- Transferable writing knowledge: understanding how genres vary so students can analyze unfamiliar genres independently

### Instructions
1. **Set purpose and audience.** Frame the genre by its communicative purpose — who writes it, for whom, and why — using an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md).
2. **Analyze mentor texts.** Guide students through comparing several exemplars to identify structural and linguistic features; contrasting cases sharpen feature detection [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
3. **Model composition.** Compose a text in the genre aloud, verbalizing planning and revision decisions ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)); model both a strong and a weak draft to build evaluative criteria.
4. **Guide practice.** Students plan or draft collaboratively or with a graphic organizer embodying the genre's structure, with teacher feedback at the process level [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
5. **Fade support.** Move from collaborative to independent composition, removing organizers as students internalize the genre's structure [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
6. **Consolidate through self-explanation.** Have students explain how their choices serve the genre's purpose, strengthening metacognitive control [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

## Examples
- **POW+TREE** (Graham & Harris) — a mnemonic strategy for persuasive writing: Pick ideas, Organize notes, Write and say more; Topic sentence, Reasons, Explanations, Ending — taught through explicit modeling and guided practice, with strong meta-analytic support [+S]
- **The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler)** — explicit, genre-sequenced sentence-to-composition instruction embedded in content areas ([https://www.thewritingrevolution.org](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org))
- **Reading–Writing Project genre units** — units of study in narrative, opinion, and informational writing that cycle through mentor-text study, modeling, and independent drafting
