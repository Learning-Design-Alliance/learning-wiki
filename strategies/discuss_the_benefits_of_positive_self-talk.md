# Discuss the Benefits of Positive Self-Talk

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy involves explicitly teaching learners about self-talk — the internal dialogue that accompanies task performance — and the evidence that its content and tone affect motivation, anxiety, and persistence. Rather than simply modeling positive self-talk, the instructor names it, explains the mechanism (self-efficacy beliefs and appraisal of difficulty), and discusses with learners where and why it helps. The discussion itself is the intervention: making the technique visible and credible so learners adopt it deliberately rather than incidentally.

## Design Implications

Self-talk interventions show small-to-moderate positive effects on sport and academic task performance, with instructional (e.g., "elbow up") and motivational self-talk serving different functions [~M]. Discussing the benefits up front increases learner buy-in and the likelihood that learners will use self-talk spontaneously during difficulty, supporting the self-efficacy beliefs that predict academic persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. The strategy works best when paired with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and opportunities to practice reframing negative statements during actual tasks, not just in discussion.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A brief, accurate explanation of self-talk and its mechanism (appraisal → internal dialogue → effort/persistence)
- Concrete, task-specific examples of positive self-talk ("I've solved problems like this before") rather than generic affirmations
- A low-stakes opportunity for learners to try it and reflect on the effect ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) or journaling)

#### Constraints
- Generic positive affirmations ("I'm great") can backfire for learners with low self-esteem, who reject statements that conflict with their self-view [-M]
- Overly positive framing can suppress accurate self-assessment; self-talk should support realistic [Self-Monitoring](../elements/self-monitoring.md), not replace it [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [~M]
- Discussion alone, without practice during challenging tasks, rarely changes habitual internal dialogue [-W]
- For complex new tasks, motivational self-talk is less useful than instructional self-talk that cues specific steps [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Instructional self-talk ("break the word into parts") for procedural tasks; motivational self-talk ("I can keep going") for effortful or anxiety-provoking tasks
- Written self-talk prompts on worked examples or feedback sheets as a lighter-weight variant
- Group discussion of reframe examples before individual use, leveraging social modeling ([Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md))

### Target Learners
- Learners facing high-stakes or anxiety-provoking performance situations (tests, public performance) [+M]
- Novices who attribute early failure to fixed ability; self-talk reframing supports the growth-oriented beliefs that sustain persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Learners developing self-regulation, where self-talk functions as an early form of private speech guiding behavior [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation and persistence during difficult tasks
- Emotional regulation and anxiety management
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own internal dialogue

### Instructions
1. Name the phenomenon: ask learners to notice what they say to themselves when stuck, connecting it to [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).
2. Present evidence briefly: self-talk affects effort, anxiety, and performance, with task-specific statements outperforming generic ones [~M].
3. Model reframing aloud using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): convert "I can't do this" into "I haven't found the next step yet — what do I know?"
4. Have learners generate their own task-specific self-talk statements and try them during [Practice](../elements/practice.md).
5. Debrief with a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md): what did learners say to themselves, and what effect did they notice?

## Examples
- A middle-school math teacher opens a unit on multi-step problems by discussing how athletes use self-talk, then has students write one instructional cue ("find the hidden question first") to use during problem sets.
- Sport psychology programs such as those following Hatzigeorgiadis et al.'s meta-analytic protocol train athletes to pair specific self-cue phrases with specific skills (e.g., "smooth" for putting).
- Test-preparation courses teach students a pre-exam routine combining breathing with a rehearsed motivational statement, practiced under timed conditions.
