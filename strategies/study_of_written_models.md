# Study of Written Models

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Study of written models asks learners to closely examine exemplary texts — their formats, structures, tones, vocabulary, and sentence patterns — and then imitate or adapt those features in their own writing. The strategy treats expert writing as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) in text form: the model makes visible what a successful product looks like, and guided analysis makes its reasoning and craft explicit. It is especially valuable for learners unfamiliar with a genre, who otherwise must infer its conventions from vague instructions alone.

## Design Implications

Models work the way worked examples do in problem solving: they eliminate unguided search by showing a complete, correct product before learners attempt their own [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. But the benefit depends on analysis, not just exposure — learners who explain *why* a model works (its moves, its structure) gain more than those who merely read it [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Models should therefore be paired with annotation, comparison, and imitation tasks rather than presented as reading material.

### Context
#### Requirements
- High-quality, authentic models of the target genre, ideally more than one [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- Guided analysis prompts that direct attention to specific craft features (structure, tone, sentence patterns)
- An imitation or application task immediately following analysis ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Feedback on how well learners' imitations capture the model's features ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))

#### Constraints
- Overreliance on a single model can anchor learners to one structure and suppress voice and creativity [-M] — contrasting multiple models mitigates this
- Weak or inauthentic models teach flawed conventions as readily as good ones
- Benefits diminish for experienced writers who already know the genre's conventions [Guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Copying surface features without understanding function produces formulaic, low-transfer writing [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Mentor texts**: published works studied for craft moves, common in writing workshop approaches
- **Imitation exercises**: sentence-level or paragraph-level mimicry of a model's syntax or structure
- **Contrasting pairs**: comparing a strong and weak model to sharpen criteria ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md))
- **Progressive independence**: moving from full models to partial models to no model as competence grows ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))

### Target Learners
- Novice writers and students with low literacy skills, who lack internalized genre knowledge [+S]
- Adult and second-language learners encountering unfamiliar academic or workplace genres
- Less beneficial for advanced writers, for whom heavy modeling can be redundant or constraining [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Genre awareness: recognizing the conventions, formats, and registers of a text type
- Procedural writing skill: acquiring reusable structural and syntactic moves
- Stylistic development: expanding vocabulary and sentence-structure repertoire

### Instructions
1. Select one or more authentic models of the target genre, matched to learners' proficiency level.
2. Present the model as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) — read it aloud or display it while naming its key features.
3. Guide analysis with prompts that require learners to explain the model's choices (structure, tone, transitions), leveraging [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md).
4. Compare multiple models to help learners abstract the genre's underlying patterns rather than one text's idiosyncrasies.
5. Assign an imitation task ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)) in which learners apply the identified moves to new content.
6. Provide feedback specifically on how well the imitation captures the model's craft features ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)), then fade the model as competence grows.

## Examples
- **Writing workshop mentor texts** (e.g., Lucy Calkins' Units of Study, [Heinemann](https://www.heinemann.com)) — published excerpts are studied for craft moves, then imitated in students' own pieces.
- **"They Say / I Say" templates** (Graff & Birkenstein, [W. W. Norton](https://wwnorton.com/books/9780393631678)) — academic writing moves presented as imitable sentence templates; widely used in first-year composition.
- **National Writing Project** ([nwp.org](https://www.nwp.org)) — teacher consultants model the study of mentor texts as a core professional development practice.
