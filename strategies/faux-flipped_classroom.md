# Faux-Flipped Classroom

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The faux-flipped classroom adapts the [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) model for young learners. Instead of sending instructional videos home as homework, students watch short teacher-created videos *during* class — often in a station-rotation or small-group structure — and the teacher then circulates to provide one-to-one guidance, feedback, and coaching on practice. The "faux" label reflects that the out-of-class/in-class inversion is softened: the goal is not homework replacement per se but freeing the teacher's attention for individualized support while students still have access to direct instruction.

## Design Implications

The model preserves the core benefit of flipped instruction — shifting first-exposure content out of whole-class delivery so class time can be spent on active learning [active-learning-improves-exam-performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S] — while accommodating young learners' limited independent study skills and home technology access. Because young students cannot be relied upon to engage with pre-class material, the video becomes an in-class station, and the teacher's role shifts from presenter to [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and feedback provider [feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Short (ideally under 5–6 minutes), focused instructional videos matched to a single objective, following multimedia design principles such as segmenting and signaling [cognitive-load-management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)
- Devices and headphones sufficient for a station or small group to view videos simultaneously
- A structured rotation or schedule so students not watching videos are productively engaged
- Teacher time and a plan for circulating during practice — the model's payoff depends on the quality of one-to-one interaction, not the videos themselves

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness collapses if students disengage from the video segment; unlike adolescent flipped models, young learners cannot be assigned accountability for at-home viewing [-M]
- Producing a library of quality videos imposes a substantial up-front creation burden on teachers [-W]
- If videos simply reproduce the whole-class lecture verbatim, the model adds technology without changing the instructional core and yields little benefit [~M]
- Students without home devices cannot extend the model to genuine homework flipping, capping its flexibility

#### Implementation Variability
- **Station rotation:** one station watches the video, others work on practice, [collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) tasks, or hands-on activities
- **Whole-class-then-practice:** the video replaces the live mini-lesson, then all students practice while the teacher pulls individuals or small groups
- **Re-teach library:** videos are archived so students can rewatch explanations on demand, supporting [spaced-repetition-improves-retention](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- **Parent-facing variant:** videos are shared with families so caregivers can support practice at home without re-teaching content incorrectly

### Target Learners
- Elementary and early-middle-grade students too young for reliable independent homework [~M]
- Struggling learners who benefit from rewatchable instruction plus immediate teacher support [self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Classrooms with heterogeneous readiness, where one-to-one time during practice allows differentiated support
- Less suitable for advanced learners who master content from the video alone and need extension rather than re-explanation [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Foundational procedural skills (e.g., arithmetic strategies, phonics patterns, writing conventions) that benefit from modeled, rewatchable instruction
- Consolidation and fluency practice, since freed class time is devoted to supervised application
- Early self-regulation: students learn to use video resources and seek help rather than waiting for whole-class redirection

### Instructions
1. **Create or curate a short instructional video** for the day's single objective, applying [cognitive-load-management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) (segmenting, signaling, conversational narration).
2. **Set up the rotation or viewing block** — students watch the video in class, individually or in a small group, with headphones.
3. **Launch guided practice immediately** so viewing connects to [application](../elements/application.md) of the skill while it is fresh.
4. **Circulate for one-to-one coaching**, using observation of student work to target the learners who need re-teaching, and provide specific [assessment](../elements/assessment.md)-informed feedback.
5. **Close with individual reflection or a quick check** so students and teacher can see what was learned and what needs revisiting.

## Examples
- Elementary classrooms using the model popularized by teacher-bloggers after Bergmann and Sams's flipped work: a second-grade math class watches a 4-minute teacher video on regrouping at a station, then rotates into practice while the teacher works individually with students who stalled.
- [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) used as the video station in elementary classrooms, with the teacher using its progress dashboard to decide whom to coach during practice.
- [Edpuzzle](https://edpuzzle.com)-embedded videos with embedded questions, so the teacher can verify engagement during the in-class viewing block.
