# Teach-OK

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Teach-OK is a peer teaching strategy that begins with the teacher spending a few minutes introducing a concept to the class. The teacher then calls "Teach!", the class responds "Okay!", and pairs of learners take turns re-teaching the concept to each other in short, energetic bursts. It resembles [Think-Pair-Share](../elements/class-discussion.md) but is faster-paced, focuses on re-teaching rather than open sharing, and encourages learners to use gestures to animate their explanations. The routine originated in Whole Brain Teaching (Biffle) and has been adapted from K–12 through college classrooms.

## Design Implications

Teach-OK converts passive listening into [retrieval and explanation](../elements/peer-teaching.md), which produces deeper processing than re-exposure to material [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The re-teaching turn forces learners to reconstruct the teacher's [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) in their own words, and the listening turn gives them a second, peer-phrased encoding of the same content. The gesture requirement adds a kinesthetic and dual-coding channel that can support recall, though its contribution is smaller than the explanation itself [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-defined concept or chunk small enough to re-teach in 30–60 seconds ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the content before the routine)
- Pre-assigned or quickly formed pairs and a clear turn-taking structure
- Clear instructions and a signal protocol ("Teach!" / "Okay!") so transitions cost seconds, not minutes
- Teacher monitoring during pair turns to catch and correct misconceptions

#### Constraints
- May be challenging for learners who are shy or reluctant to perform for a peer; forcing the "Teach!" call-and-response with adolescents or adults can feel contrived [~W]
- Requires careful monitoring to ensure accurate re-teaching — errors propagate quickly when a confused learner teaches a confused partner [-M]
- Illusions of fluency can arise when learners parrot the teacher's phrasing without understanding; varying the wording or requiring an example reduces this [~M]
- Poor fit for content that is genuinely new and complex — learners cannot re-teach what they have not yet understood; the initial teacher presentation must be sufficient [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full/switch**: one partner teaches the whole chunk, then roles switch; teacher may call "switch!" mid-explanation to keep both partners accountable
- **Mirror words**: learners repeat the teacher's words and gestures in unison before pair re-teaching, adding a rehearsal step for novices
- **Scoreboard variants**: points for energetic, accurate teaching add gamified motivation, especially in younger classrooms
- **Higher-education adaptation**: drop the call-and-response framing; keep the timed pair re-teaching structure, which works as a lecture-embedded active-learning break

### Target Learners
- Novices reviewing or consolidating recently introduced concepts, where re-teaching forces retrieval and organization [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Learners who benefit from structured, low-stakes speaking practice (language learners, students building academic discourse)
- Less suitable as a first exposure to complex material — the initial presentation must carry the instructional load, and expertise-reversal concerns apply if the routine adds redundant structure for advanced learners [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation and active recall of recently taught concepts
- Verbal fluency and communication skills in the subject domain
- Monitoring one's own understanding — gaps surface immediately when learners attempt to explain [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Present** the concept in a short, chunked mini-lesson with gestures or visual anchors ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
2. **Signal** the transition: teacher calls "Teach!", class responds "Okay!", pairs turn to each other
3. **Partner A re-teaches** the concept in their own words with gestures, in 30–60 seconds; Partner B listens, then briefly praises and corrects ([Peer Teaching](../elements/peer-teaching.md))
4. **Switch roles** so Partner B re-teaches, ideally paraphrasing rather than repeating A
5. **Monitor and close**: circulate, listen for misconceptions, correct whole-class, then move to [Guided Practice](../elements/guided-practice.md) or independent application

## Examples
- **Whole Brain Teaching** ([wholebrainteaching.com](https://wholebrainteaching.com)) — the source of the routine; K–12 classrooms use "Teach!/Okay!" with gesture mirroring and scoreboard variants across subjects
- **College lecture break** — after 10 minutes of direct instruction, the instructor asks pairs to re-teach the last concept in 45 seconds each; used as a low-prep alternative to clicker questions
- **Language classrooms** — learners re-teach a grammar rule or vocabulary set with gestures, adding obligatory spoken production to the review cycle
