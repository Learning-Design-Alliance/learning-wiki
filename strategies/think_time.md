# Think Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Think time (often called "wait time" in classroom research) is the deliberate extension of silence after a question, prompt, or task before any response is expected or solicited. It is carried out by the instructor pausing — typically 3–5 seconds or longer — after asking a question, after a learner responds, and before giving feedback, and by allowing re-reading time for text-based tasks rather than rewarding speed of response.

## Design Implications

Extending post-question pauses reliably increases the length and quality of learner responses and raises the frequency of higher-cognitive-level contributions [~S]. The effect depends on the instructor actually sustaining the silence: most teachers wait under one second, and untrained wait time produces short, hedged answers regardless of learner ability [~S]. Think time also functions as a [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) move — processing and re-reading reduce the working-memory burden of on-the-spot generation [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom or course culture where silence is treated as thinking, not failure; the instructor must explicitly reinforce and protect the pause
- Questions worth pausing for — think time after low-level recall questions yields little benefit
- Consistent norms so faster responders do not fill the silence (e.g., write-then-share, turn-to-partner structures)

#### Constraints
- Learners may not be self-aware enough to request more processing time; the burden is on the instructor to build it in rather than offer it on demand [~M]
- Extended think time during timed assessment raises fairness and comparability questions; extended-time accommodations benefit learners with processing-speed challenges but can inflate scores for others when applied indiscriminately [~M]
- In fast-paced interactive formats (fluency practice, timed retrieval), extended pauses can reduce desirable retrieval difficulty and weaken practice effects [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Post-question pause** (3–5+ seconds before calling on anyone) — the classic wait-time intervention
- **Post-response pause** (silence after a learner answers, before the teacher reacts) — invites elaboration and peer response
- **Structured think-pair-share** — private think time, then rehearsal with a peer, then public sharing
- **Re-reading time** — scheduled second passes over complex text before discussion or assessment
- **Extended-time assessment** — time-and-a-half or untimed formats as an [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) measure

### Target Learners
- Learners with speed-of-processing challenges, including many with ADHD or language-processing differences [~M]
- Second-language learners, who need additional time for translation and formulation [~M]
- All learners benefit from post-question pauses, but the largest response-quality gains appear for lower-achieving students, who are otherwise called on least and cut off most [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Higher-order reasoning: generating, justifying, and revising answers rather than recalling them
- Comprehension of complex text through re-reading
- Discussion quality and academic language production

### Instructions
1. Ask a question and state that you will wait; hold silence for 3–5 seconds minimum before calling on anyone.
2. After a learner responds, pause again before evaluating or redirecting — this invites self-correction and peer elaboration.
3. For complex tasks, pair think time with [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) prompts or brief written notes so the pause is productive rather than idle.
4. Use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) or written responses to make thinking visible during the pause, especially for learners reluctant to speak.
5. Build re-reading time into reading assignments and assessments; avoid speed-based scoring unless fluency itself is the goal.
6. Apply extended-time [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) selectively for learners with documented processing-speed needs, and monitor whether the format change is doing its job.

## Examples
- **Wait-time training in science classrooms** — Rowe's classic studies trained teachers to extend post-question pauses to 3+ seconds, producing longer student responses, more student-to-student exchanges, and more evidence-based reasoning.
- **Think-pair-share in large lectures** — instructors pose a conceptual question, enforce 60–90 seconds of silent individual thinking, then pair discussion before whole-class sharing.
- **Extended-time testing** — disability services offices commonly grant 1.5× testing time for learners with documented processing-speed or ADHD diagnoses; the Friday Institute at NC State's Speed of Processing micro-credential offers practitioner guidance on identifying learners who need this support.
