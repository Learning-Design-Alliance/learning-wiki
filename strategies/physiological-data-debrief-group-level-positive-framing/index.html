Use physiological synchrony and arousal data in simulation debriefs with group-level presentation, positive framing, and educator preparation¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 9 claims (5 for, 4 mixed) · 3 studies (3 associational), q2–q3 · 0 of 3 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The educators proposed concrete ways to integrate physiological indicators into post-hoc reflective practice: presenting physiological data during debriefs to foster reflection, framing arousal positively as engagement rather than difficulty, and showing group-level rather than individual results to avoid singling out students. One educator saw the data as helping students reflect on their feelings, noting arousal 'doesn't always mean that it's challenging, or it's hard'.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Educators need further understanding and education sessions before discussing physiological data with students
  • Debrief time is limited, so integration requires deciding what content to remove

Constraints¶

  • Educators recommend caution due to limited debrief time and the need for education before use
  • Presenting group-level data is preferred to avoid discomfort from individual results

Target Learners¶

  • undergraduate nursing students in high-fidelity collaborative simulations

Target Learning Goals¶

  • reflection on stress and engagement during collaborative clinical simulations
  • collaboration and task performance satisfaction

Claims¶

  • Educators confirmed the ecological validity of the physiological findings and identified scenario discrepancy and practical utility themes [+W]
  • In Scenario B, higher group-level physiological arousal is associated with greater task performance satisfaction [+W]
  • In Scenario B, higher group-level physiological synchrony is associated with lower collaboration performance satisfaction ratings [~W]
  • In structured Scenario B simulations, higher group-level physiological synchrony is associated with lower post-simulation stress levels [+W]
  • Individual-level physiological synchrony predicts lower post-stress and arousal predicts higher task satisfaction in Scenario B, with no effects on collaboration satisfaction [+W]
  • Physiological synchrony and arousal show no significant effects on stress or satisfaction outcomes in Scenario A [~W]
  • Increased physiological arousal and higher physiological synchrony relate to more monitoring with negative valence [~W]
  • During-simulation physiological synchrony is positively correlated with team leaders' CRM performance scores (preliminary, 9 teams) [+W]
  • Higher physiological synchrony relates to less monitoring with positive valence [~W]
