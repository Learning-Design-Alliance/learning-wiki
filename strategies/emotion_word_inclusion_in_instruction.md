# Emotion Word Inclusion in Instruction

## Description
Emotion word inclusion means the educator routinely and deliberately uses emotion vocabulary in instructional talk: naming their own emotional states ("I'm frustrated this didn't work — let me try again"), labeling the learner's apparent feelings ("You seem disappointed with that score"), and naming emotions in stories, cases, or historical figures ("How did she feel when…?"). The practice serves two functions: it gives learners the words to identify and communicate emotional states, and it signals that emotions are legitimate, discussable parts of the learning environment.

## Design Implications

Naming emotions supports emotion understanding and regulation, which in turn underpin engagement and persistence in learning contexts [Durlak et al.'s meta-analysis found social-emotional learning programs produce measurable gains in attitudes and academic performance](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564) [+S]. Emotion labeling works partly through externalization: putting a diffuse feeling into words makes it an object of reflection rather than an unexamined state, consistent with affect-labeling research showing that naming an emotion reduces its intensity and improves regulation [+M]. The educator's own emotion words function as a model — a form of [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) for emotional communication — while labeling characters' emotions in content builds the vocabulary through low-stakes, third-person contexts.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, differentiated emotion vocabulary on the educator's part (beyond "happy/sad/mad" — e.g., frustrated, proud, anxious, relieved)
- Genuine attunement: labels must match the learner's actual state, or the practice erodes trust
- A classroom climate where emotional expression is safe and norms for discussion are established
- Integration into existing talk routines (feedback, check-ins, content discussion) rather than a separate "emotions lesson" only

#### Constraints
- Mislabeled or dismissive labeling ("You're not really upset, you're just tired") teaches learners their emotional experience is unreliable [-M]
- Overuse or performative emotion talk can feel intrusive to adolescents and may reduce disclosure [-W]
- Emotion labeling alone, without follow-up strategies for regulation or problem-solving, produces vocabulary gains but limited behavioral change [~M]
- Cultural variation in norms around emotional display means the same words carry different acceptability across communities; educators must calibrate to learners' cultural context [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Educator self-labeling** — modeling ("I'm nervous about this demo too") normalizes emotion talk and shows regulation in action
- **Learner labeling** — inviting learners to name their own states, e.g., through a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine or a mood-meter tool such as the RULER approach
- **Third-person labeling** — discussing emotions of characters in texts, cases, or history, which builds vocabulary without requiring personal disclosure
- **Feedback framing** — pairing academic feedback with emotion acknowledgment ("This result is disappointing; here's what to do next")

### Target Learners
- Young children, for whom emotion vocabulary is still developing and adult labeling is a primary source of emotion concepts [+M]
- Learners with language delays, autism spectrum profiles, or limited emotional literacy, for whom explicit labeling provides needed structure [+M]
- Adolescents benefit most when labeling is indirect (third-person, written) rather than put-on-the-spot public disclosure [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: identifying, differentiating, and naming emotional states
- Self-regulation: using labels as a first step toward managing emotional responses
- Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' emotions in social and academic content ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Classroom community: establishing psychological safety for discussion-based work

### Instructions
1. **Audit your own talk.** Notice how often you currently name emotions; identify routine moments (feedback, transitions, conflict) where a label could be added naturally.
2. **Model self-labeling with regulation.** Name your own state and the strategy you use ("I'm feeling overwhelmed, so I'm going to make a list first") — this pairs labeling with visible coping, a form of [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md).
3. **Label learner emotions tentatively.** Use hedges that invite correction ("You seem frustrated — is that right?") rather than definitive pronouncements.
4. **Embed emotion talk in content.** When discussing narratives, cases, or historical events, ask learners to identify and justify emotion attributions — third-person labeling builds vocabulary without personal exposure.
5. **Follow labels with action.** Pair naming with a regulation or problem-solving move; a label alone does not teach what to do with the feeling.
6. **Build vocabulary deliberately.** Introduce increasingly precise emotion words (annoyed → exasperated; pleased → proud) and use them consistently in [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines.

## Examples
- **RULER (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the "Mood Meter" prompts learners to locate and label their emotional state with specific words at the start of class; teachers are trained to label emotions in their own instructional talk. [https://ei.yale.edu](https://ei.yale.edu)
- **Responsive Classroom morning meetings** — daily greetings and sharing routines in which teachers model and elicit emotion vocabulary ("How are you feeling this morning — excited? nervous?").
- **Emotion coaching in feedback conversations** — a teacher returning an essay says, "I can see you're discouraged by this grade — I'd be too. Let's look at what to revise," pairing acknowledgment with next steps.
