# Choice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Choice is a strategy in which learners are given structured options over some aspect of their learning — the topic they study, the format of their product, the sequence of tasks, or the level of challenge. It is carried out by designing a bounded set of meaningful alternatives rather than a single prescribed path, then letting learners select among them.

## Design Implications

Choice supports motivation primarily by satisfying the need for autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S], consistent with [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md). Its effects on *learning outcomes* are more conditional than its effects on motivation: choice improves engagement and persistence, but benefits achievement mainly when options are instructionally relevant and the learner has the knowledge to choose well [~M]. Unbounded or trivial choice adds decision-making load without payoff, and can interact poorly with [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A bounded set of 2–5 genuinely distinct options, each of which leads to the same learning goal
- Options that differ in surface features (topic, format, sequence) rather than in instructional quality
- Enough prior knowledge or guidance for learners to make an informed selection ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or brief option descriptions help)
- Alignment of all options to the same assessment criteria so choice does not change what is assessed

#### Constraints
- Too many options or options with unclear consequences increase decision load and can reduce performance, especially for novices [~M] — the effect parallels the [expertise-reversal pattern](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md), where guidance that helps novices becomes overhead for experts
- Choice over *what to learn* (content) is riskier than choice over *how to learn* (process or product) when learners lack the knowledge to judge relevance [-M]
- Trivial or cosmetic choice (e.g., font, avatar color) can consume attention without building autonomy [~W]
- When options differ in difficulty and learners systematically avoid challenge, choice can widen achievement gaps [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Topic choice** — learners select the subject of an essay, project, or case study within a constraint set
- **Product choice** — learners demonstrate the same competency via different formats (video, essay, presentation); a [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md) is the common vehicle
- **Sequence choice** — learners order tasks or select which [Practice](../elements/practice.md) set to attempt next
- **Challenge choice** — learners self-select difficulty levels, ideally with calibration feedback from [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)
- **No-choice control framing** — when choice is impractical, explaining the rationale for assignments recovers some of the autonomy benefit [~M]

### Target Learners
- Learners with sufficient prior knowledge to evaluate options meaningfully; novices benefit from narrower, better-described option sets [~M]
- Disengaged or low-autonomy learners, for whom even small choices can increase ownership and persistence [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Younger learners generally need fewer, simpler options; older learners can handle broader menus

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement goals: increasing task persistence, ownership, and intrinsic motivation
- Self-regulation goals: practicing decision-making and self-assessment as part of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Not well suited to goals requiring strict sequencing of prerequisite knowledge, where prescribed paths outperform

### Instructions
1. Define the non-negotiable learning goal and assessment criteria first; every option must serve them.
2. Design 2–5 options that vary in topic, format, or sequence — not in instructional quality ([Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md)).
3. Write a one-line description of each option's demands so learners can choose with information, not guesswork.
4. Set a time limit for the choice itself to prevent deliberation from consuming learning time.
5. Follow choice with structured work and feedback ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) so autonomy does not become abandonment.
6. Debrief the choice ("What did you pick, and was it a good fit?") to build evaluative judgment for future selections.

## Examples
- **Menu-based projects in AP courses** — students choose among essay, podcast, or infographic formats to demonstrate the same rubric-based competency.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — learners choose which skill to practice next within a mastery map; the platform constrains options to prerequisites already met.
- **[Duolingo](https://www.duolingo.com)** — learners select lesson topics and daily goals, with the underlying sequence fixed — process choice over content choice.
- **Writer's Workshop (elementary)** — students choose their own writing topics while the teacher prescribes the genre and minilesson focus.
