# Recognizing the Value of Learning from Mistakes

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy changes perceptions about mistakes, treating them as diagnostic information and learning opportunities rather than failures to be penalized. Core moves include explaining *why* an answer is wrong rather than only marking it incorrect, giving learners structured opportunities to correct errors, weighting improvement in evaluation, and responding constructively to errors raised in discussion. The aim is a classroom norm in which risk-taking is safe and errors are actively mined for what they reveal about understanding.

## Design Implications

Errors are cognitively valuable: being corrected after committing an error produces better retention than studying the correct answer alone, because error correction recruits deeper processing and attention [~S]. But this benefit is conditional — errors help when followed by corrective feedback, and harm learning when errors are encoded without correction. The strategy therefore pairs a normative shift (mistakes are welcome) with a procedural one (mistakes are always followed by explanation and revision). Allowing learners to attempt problems before instruction, even unsuccessfully, can improve subsequent learning from the corrective teaching that follows [Productive failure: generating and failing on problems before instruction improves conceptual learning.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom climate that supports risk-taking; learners must believe errors will not cost them socially or academically [Interventions that affirm belonging reduce threat and improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Feedback that specifies what is wrong and how to improve, not just a grade [Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, where to next?" improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Structured revision opportunities: time, and credit, for correcting errors
- Teacher modeling of constructive responses to errors in public settings (discussion, board work)

#### Constraints
- Errors without timely, specific correction can entrench misconceptions; simply "celebrating" mistakes while leaving them uncorrected degrades learning [-S]
- High-stakes grading that punishes early errors suppresses the risk-taking the strategy depends on [-M]
- Learners with low prior knowledge may generate errors they cannot self-correct; unguided error generation alone is insufficient without follow-up instruction [~M]
- Shifting evaluation practices can face resistance from students and parents accustomed to traditional marking

#### Implementation Variability
- **Error analysis routines**: regular "favorite mistake" discussions or error-based warm-ups where the class diagnoses a common wrong answer
- **Revision policies**: allow resubmission of assessed work with credit for demonstrated improvement
- **Pre-testing with feedback**: low- or no-stakes pretests that surface errors before instruction
- **Productive failure sequencing**: let learners attempt challenging problems before teaching, then compare their approaches to canonical solutions

### Target Learners
- Students in environments where mistakes are heavily stigmatized, especially adolescents anxious about public error [Interventions that affirm belonging reduce threat and improve outcomes for at-risk students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Learners developing conceptual understanding, where diagnosing *why* an answer is wrong builds more durable knowledge than re-reading correct answers [~S]
- Teachers themselves, whose beliefs about error determine whether classroom norms support or suppress disclosure of confusion

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: diagnosing the source of an error exposes faulty mental models
- Self-regulated learning: learners who analyze their own errors develop monitoring and revision habits
- Risk-taking and independent thinking: reducing fear of failure increases willingness to attempt unfamiliar problems

### Instructions
1. **Set the norm explicitly.** Open the term by discussing how error drives learning (e.g., famous productive mistakes); establish that errors in discussion and drafts carry no grade penalty.
2. **Diagnose, don't just mark.** When responding to work, explain why an answer is wrong and what misconception it reflects — use [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) that names the gap and the next step.
3. **Require correction.** Build in structured [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on revised versions of missed problems or passages; require learners to rework errors, not just read corrections.
4. **Discuss errors publicly and safely.** Use [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or [Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) to analyze a common error as a class, framing it as informative rather than shameful.
5. **Weight improvement in evaluation.** In [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md), include growth from first attempt to revision as a graded component.

## Related Strategies
- **Productive failure** — a formal instructional sequence built on this strategy: attempt first, fail, then learn from canonical instruction
- **Assessment for learning** — supplies the feedback loop that makes error correction effective
- **Growth mindset framing** — provides the motivational belief system ("ability is malleable") that underwrites risk-taking

## Examples
- **Revision-based grading**: a writing course where essays receive feedback-only first drafts, and the final grade weights the revision; students must annotate what they changed and why.
- **"My favorite no"** routine (widely used in math classrooms): the teacher collects anonymous wrong answers, selects an instructive one, and the class identifies what is correct in the reasoning and where it goes wrong.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — mastery-based practice where incorrect answers trigger hints and explanations rather than penalties, and learners retry until proficient.
- **Productive failure in math** (Kapur's published studies): students attempt novel problems in small groups before instruction, then compare their (failed) methods with the standard solution, outperforming direct-instruction-only peers on conceptual measures.
