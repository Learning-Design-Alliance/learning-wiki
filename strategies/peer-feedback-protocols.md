# Peer Feedback Protocols

## Description
Peer feedback protocols are structured routines — rubrics, sentence stems, staged review cycles, and role scripts — that govern how learners critique one another's work. The protocol converts the vague instruction "give feedback" into a repeatable procedure: examine the work against explicit criteria, identify specific strengths and gaps, suggest actionable revisions, and do so in language the receiver can act on.

## Design Implications

Peer feedback works when it functions as formative assessment in the hands of learners, not as grading [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The protocol is the load-bearing component: unstructured peer comments tend toward vague praise ("good job") or personal taste, while criterion-referenced protocols produce feedback that correlates with instructor feedback in quality and improves the quality of subsequent work [Peer assessment can be as effective as teacher assessment when criteria and training are provided.](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969594X.2019.1690144) [+S]. Giving feedback is often as valuable as receiving it — the reviewer must evaluate work against criteria, which deepens their own understanding of quality [Peer learning benefits both giver and receiver.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543068003289) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit success criteria or a [rubric](../elements/assessment.md) the reviewer applies, ideally co-constructed or practiced with exemplars first
- Training and rehearsal: learners need to see and critique sample feedback before reviewing real work
- Sentence frames and role scripts that specify *what* to comment on (e.g., two strengths, one question, one suggestion)
- Time for the receiver to act on feedback — a revision cycle, not just a read-through
- Psychological safety norms, since peers evaluate work publicly

#### Constraints
- Without training and criteria, peer feedback degrades into social politeness and unactionable praise [-M]
- Receivers often discount peer feedback on high-stakes work, treating it as less credible than instructor feedback [~M] — pairing peer review with instructor spot-checks mitigates this
- Reviewers cannot give useful feedback on content beyond their own expertise; protocols work best for structure, clarity, and criteria-visible features rather than deep domain accuracy [-M]
- Anonymity reduces social bias but can strip context needed to interpret the work [~W]
- Grading peers introduces status dynamics and reciprocity concerns ("you scratch my back") that distort comments [-M]; keep peer feedback ungraded or grade only the quality of feedback given

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-stars-and-a-wish / Praise-Question-Polish**: lightweight stems for younger or novice reviewers
- **Calibrated Peer Review**: reviewers first score an instructor-anchored exemplar; only calibrated reviews count, used in systems like [Calibrated Peer Review](https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu/) and Peergrade
- **Gallery walk / silent critique**: written sticky-note feedback on displayed work, reducing social pressure
- **Reciprocal pairs**: stable partners alternate reviewer roles across drafts, building trust over time
- **Author-driven**: the receiver poses specific questions ("Does my argument in paragraph 2 follow?"), focusing reviewer attention

### Target Learners
- Effective across K–12 through higher education, with strongest effects in secondary and tertiary writing contexts [Peer assessment improves academic performance compared with no assessment or teacher-only assessment.](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969594X.2019.1690144) [+S]
- Developing writers benefit from seeing peers' approaches and receiving multiple readings of a draft [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Novice reviewers gain the most from the *giving* side; experts may find protocol steps redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality criteria internalization: learning what "good work" looks like by applying criteria to real cases
- Revision and self-regulation: using external feedback to plan improvements [Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, what next?" improves learning.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00405840709536524) [+S]
- Evaluative judgment: building the capacity to assess one's own work independently

### Instructions
1. Establish criteria: share or co-construct a rubric and study annotated exemplars at different quality levels ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
2. Model the protocol: the instructor reviews a sample work aloud, demonstrating how to apply criteria and phrase actionable comments ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
3. Pair or group reviewers and exchange drafts; reviewers apply the protocol stems (e.g., two strengths, one question, one concrete suggestion) within a time limit
4. Receivers paraphrase the feedback back and select which comments to act on, making revision decisions explicit
5. Revise: receivers produce a new draft, closing the loop so feedback demonstrably matters
6. Debrief the feedback itself: which comments were most useful and why, building reviewers' skill for the next cycle

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (CPR)** (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu/) — web system where students first calibrate on instructor-scored exemplars, then review peers' writing in large STEM courses.
- **Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms** — anonymous distribution of student work with rubric-driven comment fields and feedback-quality ratings by receivers.
- **Two-stars-and-a-wish** in primary writing workshops — reviewers name two strengths and one wish, keeping feedback criterion-anchored and age-appropriate.
- **Writing workshop protocols** (e.g., National Writing Project response protocols) — author reads aloud, peers respond with "I noticed… I wondered…", author speaks last.
