# Token Economies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A token economy is a behavior management system in which learners earn tokens (points, stickers, chips) contingent on specified target behaviors, then exchange accumulated tokens for backup reinforcers chosen to be motivating. It operationalizes operant conditioning: the token functions as a generalized conditioned reinforcer because it is paired with multiple primary and secondary reinforcers. Effective systems define target behaviors precisely, deliver tokens immediately after the behavior, and include a menu of reinforcers at varied "prices."

## Design Implications

Token economies reliably increase targeted on-task and prosocial behaviors in classroom settings, with the strongest evidence in special education and clinical populations [~S]. Their effect on *learning outcomes* is indirect: they shape the behaviors that make instruction possible (attention, task completion) rather than cognition itself. Because tokens are extrinsic reinforcers, poorly designed systems can undermine intrinsic motivation for activities learners already find interesting [autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M] — the risk is greatest when rewards are contingent on task engagement rather than on meeting performance standards, and when the reward feels controlling rather than informational.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Operationally defined target behaviors that learners can clearly discriminate
- Immediate, consistent token delivery following the target behavior
- A reinforcer menu with varied items at varied costs, refreshed to prevent satiation
- A transparent exchange system and explicit rules for earning and losing tokens
- A plan for fading tokens as target behaviors become habitual

#### Constraints
- Rewards framed as controlling ("you must do X to get Y") reduce intrinsic motivation for the target activity once rewards stop [autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-S]
- Effects often fail to generalize beyond the setting or behaviors explicitly reinforced; untargeted behaviors may even deteriorate [~M]
- Token systems applied to tasks learners already enjoy can convert play into work, suppressing engagement when the economy is removed [~S]
- Response-cost (fines) variants can produce resentment and avoidance without careful, positive-first framing [-M]
- Satiation: a single backup reinforcer loses potency quickly; menus must rotate

#### Implementation Variability
- **Level systems** (points accumulate toward status tiers) common in residential and alternative schools
- **Class Dojo and similar digital point systems** — automate tracking but risk public comparison; private delivery reduces social costs
- **Response cost** variants subtract tokens for misbehavior; more effective when paired with abundant earning opportunities
- **Self-monitoring hybrids** — learners record their own token earnings, building self-regulation and improving maintenance [~M]
- **Fading schedules** — shift from continuous to intermittent reinforcement, then to natural reinforcers (task completion, peer approval)

### Target Learners
- Students with behavioral, emotional, or developmental challenges for whom natural consequences are insufficiently salient [~S]
- Young children, who respond strongly to immediate concrete reinforcement [~S]
- Less appropriate for adolescents and adults in typical academic settings, where controlling rewards can signal distrust and depress motivation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Classroom conduct and procedural compliance (on-task behavior, transitions, task completion)
- Habit formation for routines that instruction depends on
- Not well suited to conceptual understanding or higher-order skills, where the target is cognitive rather than behavioral

### Instructions
1. Define 2–4 target behaviors in observable, countable terms; start with behaviors occurring at moderate frequency so learners experience early success.
2. Select backup reinforcers via learner preference survey; include low-cost social and activity reinforcers alongside tangible items.
3. Set exchange rates so initial reinforcement is frequent, then thin the schedule as behavior stabilizes.
4. Deliver tokens immediately with brief behavioral feedback naming the behavior ("You started work right away — that's a token"), pairing the reinforcer with informational feedback.
5. Fade systematically: move to intermittent delivery, then replace tokens with natural consequences and self-monitoring.

## Examples
- **Class Dojo** (https://www.classdojo.com) — digital point awarding for named behaviors; most defensible when points are private and tied to specific, stated behaviors rather than public leaderboards.
- **Check-In/Check-Out (CICO)** in School-Wide Positive Behavior Interventions and Supports (PBIS, https://www.pbis.org) — students receive daily point sheets scored by teachers, exchanged for brief adult mentoring sessions; a token economy embedded in a tier-2 support framework.
- **Level systems in specialized day-treatment classrooms** — students progress through privilege tiers (e.g., more unstructured time, peer-choice seating) as points accumulate, with privileges functioning as the backup reinforcer menu.
