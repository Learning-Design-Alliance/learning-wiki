Connect with Parents Early in the School Year¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Connecting with parents as soon as possible in the school year through personalized phone calls, emails, in-person conversations, or letters home. The goal is to establish open, comfortable communication channels and mutual respect before problems arise. Contacting parents early — especially with positive, student-specific feedback — builds a foundation of trust that supports collaboration, home reinforcement of learning, and faster resolution of issues throughout the year.

Design Implications¶

Early, positive teacher–family contact increases parental engagement and, through it, student engagement and behavior [~M]. Experimental work shows that personalized teacher outreach (calls, texts, or notes about specific students) improves homework completion, class participation, and student motivation relative to no-contact controls [~M]. The mechanism is relational: early positive contact reframes the teacher–parent relationship as partnership rather than complaint-driven, which makes later corrective communication more likely to be received cooperatively. Effects are strongest when contact is two-way and sustained, not a one-time broadcast [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Accurate contact information and awareness of families' preferred languages and channels (phone, text, email, in person)
  • Time allocated in the first weeks of school for individualized outreach, not just mass newsletters
  • Something genuinely positive and specific to say about each student — the first contact should not be a complaint
  • A simple system for logging contacts so communication is continuous rather than episodic

Constraints¶

  • Effectiveness drops sharply when contact is generic or mass-produced; parents detect form letters and respond accordingly [-W]
  • One-way, deficit-framed contact (calling only about problems) erodes trust and reduces future parental responsiveness [-M]
  • Time-intensive for teachers of large caseloads; without scheduling protection, outreach is typically the first thing displaced [-W]
  • Families with negative prior school experiences, language barriers, or inflexible work schedules may be unreachable through standard channels; repeated failed attempts can widen rather than close engagement gaps [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Positive phone calls or texts home in the first two weeks (the most-studied variant)
  • "Sunshine calls" or welcome letters before school begins
  • Home visits or community-based meetings for hard-to-reach families
  • Structured programs such as the Teacher Home Visit program (Flamboyan Foundation) or academic-parent-teacher teams that institutionalize early contact

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 students generally; effects on engagement and behavior are documented from upper elementary through high school [~M]
  • Students at risk of disengagement benefit disproportionately when early contact leads to sustained home–school alignment [~M]
  • Students whose families face language or logistical barriers need adapted channels, or the strategy may not reach them [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Behavioral and affective outcomes: engagement, attendance, homework completion, classroom behavior [~M]
  • Academic outcomes indirectly, mediated by parental involvement and student motivation [~M]
  • Social-emotional goals: student sense of belonging and teacher–student relationship quality [~W]

Instructions¶

  1. Collect contact information and family preferences before the year begins; note preferred language and best times to reach each family.
  2. In the first two weeks, make one personalized, positive contact per family — a specific observation about the student, not a generic greeting.
  3. Ask parents about their goals and concerns for the child, and record them; this functions as an early check-in with the family rather than the student.
  4. Establish a lightweight communication rhythm (brief updates every few weeks) so the initial contact becomes a channel, not an event.
  5. When problems arise later, open with the established positive frame — reference earlier successes before discussing concerns.
  6. Log contacts and outcomes to identify families not yet reached, and adapt channel or timing for them.

Related Strategies¶

  • Check-ins — the same relational logic applied to students rather than families
  • Building empathy — early family contact gives teachers the contextual knowledge empathy requires
  • Two-visit structure: relationship-building visit first, academic-focused visit later
  • SIG-funded parent liaisons paired with structured parent contact practices to increase family engagement
  • Schedule teacher home visits before the start of the school year to build family relationships
  • Parents should reinforce on-time attendance, communicate with teachers, and make academics the freshman's top priority
  • Combine traditional and nontraditional approaches to family engagement for ELL families

Examples¶

  • Kraft & Dougherty's personalized communication study — high school teachers sent brief individualized calls/texts about specific students; treated students showed improved homework completion, class participation, and engagement relative to controls.
  • Flamboyan Foundation (Washington, DC) — trains teachers in relationship-building home visits and positive first contacts; partner schools showed gains in trust and attendance.
  • "Positive phone calls home" routines — many schools institutionalize a first-weeks requirement that every family receives one good-news contact before any negative contact occurs.
