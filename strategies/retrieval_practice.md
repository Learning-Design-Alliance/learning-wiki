# Retrieval Practice

## Description
Retrieval practice involves actively recalling information from memory without having it in front of you. This strategy strengthens long-term retention and promotes deeper learning by encouraging learners to actively use and recall information rather than passively re-reading or re-studying. Effective retrieval practice includes spacing (practicing at intervals), feedback (providing the correct answer if the learner struggles), and aligning practice with assessment (using similar question types).

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires structured activities such as quizzes, discussions, or flashcard exercises. Immediate feedback mechanisms and spaced repetition schedules enhance effectiveness. Low-stakes environment encourages participation.
#### Constraints
- May lead to rote learning if not combined with deeper understanding activities. Requires careful design to align with assessment goals. Learners need clear instructions to avoid ineffective strategies (e.g., superficial re-reading).

### Affordances
- Strengthens memory through active recall, improves self-assessment skills, identifies knowledge gaps, and facilitates better transfer of learning to new contexts. Teachers can use it formatively to adjust instruction.

### Personalization
- Can be adapted with different formats such as quizzes, brain dumps, flashcards, or discussions. Difficulty can be adjusted by providing cues or scaffolding. Learners can choose topics or questions.

## Assessment Evidence
- Measure improvement in test scores, quality of participation in discussions, and self-assessment accuracy. Track the type of questions answered correctly or incorrectly to identify areas of strength and weakness.

## Impact
- Studies show that retrieval practice leads to better retention compared to restudying. It also fosters deeper learning by improving transfer to new forms of questions and enhances metacognition.
