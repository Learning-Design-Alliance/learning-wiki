Daily Review¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal), q2–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Daily review opens each lesson with a brief (5–10 minute) activity that reactivates previously studied material — key concepts, vocabulary, formulas, homework errors, or problem areas identified by learners. Common formats include flashcards, low-stakes quizzes, games, oral questioning, and worked corrections of prior mistakes. The mechanism is retrieval practice: successfully recalling material strengthens memory more than rereading it [Roediger & Karpicke, 2006] [+S], and distributing that retrieval across days exploits the spacing effect [Cepeda et al., 2006] [+S].

Design Implications¶

Daily review converts the lesson opening from administrative routine into distributed retrieval practice, directly attacking the forgetting curve. Its effectiveness depends on review requiring retrieval rather than restatement — learners must produce answers, not merely hear the material repeated [Dunlosky et al., 2013] [+S]. Rosenshine's (2012) synthesis of classroom process-product research found daily review a hallmark of the most effective teachers, who opened nearly every lesson with 5–8 minutes of review of prior material and homework [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • 5–10 minutes of protected lesson time at a consistent point in the routine
  • Review items selected deliberately: high-leverage concepts, prerequisite knowledge for today's lesson, and known error patterns — not a re-teaching of everything
  • A response mechanism for every learner (whiteboards, response cards, quick quizzes) so review is retrieval for all, not performance by a few
  • Brief feedback on review responses Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Review that consists of re-explaining rather than asking learners to recall produces weak benefits and can create fluency illusions [Dunlosky et al., 2013] [-M]
  • Overlong review (beyond ~10 minutes) crowds out new instruction and loses its consolidating function [~M]
  • Reviewing material learners have already mastered to automaticity wastes time and can disengage them [~M]
  • Poorly targeted review of peripheral detail does not build the prerequisite knowledge that makes new learning possible [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Oral questioning chains: rapid whole-class questioning on prior content (Rosenshine's model)
  • Cumulative quizzes: short low-stakes quizzes sampling from all prior units, not just yesterday
  • Homework correction: reviewing errors from previous assignments as the review content
  • Game formats: flashcard competitions or team games; engagement gains are real but the retrieval demand must remain intact [~W]
  • Spaced schedules: reviewing material at expanding intervals (1 day, 3 days, 1 week) rather than only the immediately preceding lesson [Cepeda et al., 2006] [+S]

Target Learners¶

  • All levels, K–12 through adult education; effects are robust across ages [Dunlosky et al., 2013] [+S]
  • Novices and learners with weak foundational knowledge, for whom cumulative review prevents early material from decaying before it is automatized [+M]
  • Learners building vocabulary, mathematics fluency, and other cumulative knowledge domains where later content depends on earlier content [+S]
  • Advanced learners still benefit, but review of fully mastered material yields diminishing returns [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Long-term retention of declarative and procedural knowledge [Dunlosky et al., 2013] [+S]
  • Automaticity of foundational skills (facts, formulas, vocabulary) that frees working memory for new learning Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Cumulative schema building: connecting new lessons to a stable base of prior knowledge

Instructions¶

  1. Select 3–6 review items: yesterday's key content, known error patterns, and prerequisites for today's lesson.
  2. Pose review questions that require retrieval — learners write or say answers before any re-explanation (Practice).
  3. Give immediate, brief corrective feedback on responses Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  4. Re-teach only items a substantial share of the class missed; note persistent gaps for future review cycles.
  5. Bridge explicitly from review to the new lesson, activating the prior knowledge the new content builds on (Activation).
  6. Rotate formats (quiz, flashcards, oral chains, games) to sustain engagement without lowering retrieval demand.

Related Strategies¶

  • Spaced practice — daily review is the classroom implementation of spacing; expanding intervals outperform massed review
  • Retrieval practice — the underlying mechanism; review must test recall, not re-present content
  • Direct Instruction — daily review is the opening event in the standard DI lesson cycle
  • Formative Assessment — review responses double as daily formative data on retention
  • Deploy spaced-repetition vocabulary practice as a low-burden daily supplement of a few minutes without changing existing pedagogy

Examples¶

  • Direct Instruction programs (e.g., Reading Mastery, McGraw-Hill) open every lesson with scripted review of previously taught sounds, words, and facts — a core design feature, not an add-on.
  • A middle-school math teacher begins class with five problems: two from yesterday, two from last week, one from a prior unit, then moves directly into new material — a cumulative schedule consistent with spacing research [Cepeda et al., 2006] [+S].
  • Anki-style flashcard warm-ups in language courses, where each lesson opens with scheduled card reviews at expanding intervals before new vocabulary is introduced.
