# Modified Jigsaw Activities

## Description
The jigsaw strategy divides a task into subtopics, assigning one to each group member. Students first work in "expert groups" with peers who share the same subtopic, then return to their home groups to teach what they learned, making each member uniquely responsible for a piece of the whole. In the modified peer-tutoring variant, tutor–tutee pairs are given the same subtopic and work side-by-side to master it before presenting to their team together, so the tutee is supported rather than left to teach unprepared material.

## Design Implications

Jigsaw enforces positive interdependence — each member holds information the others need — which is the structural feature that distinguishes effective cooperative learning from mere group work [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. The modification adds a second layer of [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md): paired rehearsal before teaching reduces the risk that a weakly prepared student transmits errors or flounders publicly. Because every student must both receive and explain content, jigsaw combines [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) with [Peer Teaching](../elements/peer-teaching.md), and the act of explaining produces the self-explanation and elaboration effects that deepen retention.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content that decomposes cleanly into interdependent subtopics of roughly equal difficulty
- Expert-group materials at readable levels, with time to process them before teaching ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) source material helps)
- Structured prompts or graphic organizers for the teaching phase, so explanations are complete rather than improvised
- Training in how to explain and how to question; students need to be taught to give constructive feedback, not just assigned to do it
- Individual accountability — a quiz or product covering *all* subtopics — so students listen as seriously as they present

#### Constraints
- Without individual accountability, students may "free-ride" on their group's experts, and learning gains collapse [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Poorly matched subtopic difficulty produces overload: students asked to teach material they have not yet understood can encode errors that persist [-M]
- Less effective for integrative or evaluative goals where the subtopics cannot be separated without losing meaning; forcing a jigsaw structure onto non-decomposable content fragments understanding [~M]
- Requires more planning and coordination than whole-class instruction; timing expert and home phases poorly leaves some groups finished and idle while others struggle

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jigsaw II (Slavin):** all students read a common base text first, then specialize — reduces the risk of uneven background knowledge
- **Paired jigsaw:** tutor–tutee pairs master one subtopic together before teaching, as in the modified version described here
- **Reverse jigsaw:** experts teach the whole class rather than their home group, useful when home groups are large
- **Jigsaw with [Concept Mapping](../elements/concept-mapping.md):** groups assemble a shared map from each member's subtopic, forcing integration at the end

### Target Learners
- Students from upper elementary through higher education; effects on achievement and intergroup relations are documented across age bands [+M]
- English learners and students who benefit from low-stakes rehearsal before speaking to a larger group [~M]
- Less beneficial for students who already know most subtopics, for whom the expert phase is redundant and the teaching phase adds little [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of multi-part topics (e.g., causes of an event, stages of a process, categories of a classification)
- Communication and explanation skills — learning by teaching reinforces the explainer's own understanding [~M]
- Intergroup cooperation and interdependence; the original Jigsaw Classroom was designed to reduce racial tension in desegregated classrooms [+M]

### Instructions
1. Select a topic that decomposes into 4–5 interdependent subtopics and prepare expert materials for each.
2. Form home groups of 4–5; assign each member one subtopic (or one tutor–tutee pair per subtopic in the modified format).
3. Send students to [Expert Groups](../elements/expert-groups.md) to study their subtopic with peers who share it, using a graphic organizer to structure what they will teach.
4. In the modified variant, pair each tutor with a tutee on the same subtopic; they discuss and complete the activity together, rehearsing the explanation before presenting.
5. Return to home groups; each expert (or pair) teaches their subtopic while listeners take structured notes covering all subtopics.
6. Assess individually over *all* subtopics and debrief on both content and collaboration quality ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) or exit ticket).

## Examples
- **The Jigsaw Classroom** ([jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)) — Elliot Aronson's original implementation for desegregated elementary classrooms, now maintained with implementation guides.
- **Jigsaw II in science:** all students read a common lab-background text, then split into expert groups on variables, procedure, and analysis before teaching home groups — used widely in POGIL-style materials ([pogil.org](https://pogil.org)).
- **History inquiry:** a class studying multiple perspectives on a historical event assigns each member one perspective's primary sources; home groups must synthesize all perspectives into a shared account.
