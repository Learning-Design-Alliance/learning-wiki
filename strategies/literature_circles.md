# Literature Circles

## Description
Literature circles organize students into small, temporary groups that each select a book to read, meet on a recurring schedule to discuss it, and produce reflective or project-based responses demonstrating understanding. Discussion is student-led: the teacher selects and curates book choices, observes, and offers feedback, while students prepare using notes and marked passages. The approach emphasizes simplicity, adaptability, and student ownership of both text selection and conversation.

## Design Implications

Literature circles combine [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) with student autonomy, and their effectiveness rests on structured preparation: students who arrive with annotated passages and discussion notes sustain higher-quality peer talk than those who arrive cold. Choice of text increases investment and effort [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M], and discussing interpretations with peers promotes the elaborative processing that improves conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Multiple copies of a range of books at varied difficulty levels so groups can exercise genuine choice
- A recurring discussion schedule with protected class time
- Preparation structures — annotation, role sheets, or discussion notes — so students arrive with something to say ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Teacher facilitation: modeling discussion moves early, then receding into observation and feedback

#### Constraints
- Unstructured discussion without preparation requirements degrades into plot summary or social talk [-M] — the benefit depends on students arriving with prepared contributions
- Assessment is difficult to standardize when groups read different books of varying difficulty [~W]
- Groups with large differences in reading fluency can produce unequal participation, with stronger readers dominating [-M]
- Students with very weak decoding skills may be excluded from meaningful participation unless texts are carefully matched or supported with [Audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Role-based circles**: rotating roles (discussion director, literary luminary, connector, word wizard) scaffold participation for novices; roles should be faded once discussion norms are established
- **Non-role circles**: mature groups discuss freely with only prepared passages as the entry point
- **Cross-age or club formats**: some implementations extend across a grading period or run as extracurricular book clubs
- **Multimedia responses**: projects may range from written reflection to maps, timelines, puppet shows, or digital storytelling

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through high school students reading at roughly comparable levels within a group
- Developing readers who benefit from peer models interpreting text aloud [~M]
- Disengaged readers, for whom text choice and social accountability increase task value [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension and interpretive discussion of extended texts
- Oral language, argumentation, and turn-taking skills
- Independent reading habits and reader identity through autonomy and choice [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Curate and present a set of book options; form groups around student choice, matching text difficulty to group reading levels ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))
2. Model discussion moves and preparation strategies with a whole-class text before releasing groups ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
3. Students read assigned sections, annotating passages and preparing notes or role sheets ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
4. Groups meet to discuss; teacher observes, takes notes, and offers targeted feedback afterward ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md))
5. Students reflect individually on their reading and their group's discussion ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md))
6. Groups or individuals complete a project demonstrating understanding ([Application](../elements/application.md))

## Related Strategies

- [Jigsaw](../strategies/jigsaw.md) — an alternative cooperative structure with interdependent roles and individual accountability

## Examples
- A sixth-grade teacher offers six novel options; students rank preferences, form groups, and choose a shared theme and final project — producing maps, acrostic poems, and timelines tied to their books.
- First-grade teachers run picture-book circles with puppet shows, dioramas, and a story quilt as response options, using roles to structure early discussions.
- **[Book Club for Multilingual Learners](https://www.stenhouse.com)** — Daniels' published framework has been implemented widely in secondary classrooms, with role sheets and discussion protocols available in the accompanying materials.
