# Introduce Evaluation Concepts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy gives learners a shared vocabulary for judging information quality — terms such as *relevance*, *accuracy*, *bias*, *reliability*, and *credibility* — and then uses those terms routinely in discussion, reading, and assessment. The goal is to move source evaluation from an occasional activity to a habitual stance: learners come to ask, by default, who produced a source, why, and how well it supports the claim at hand.

## Design Implications

Naming and repeatedly applying evaluation criteria converts tacit "gut feelings" about sources into explicit, teachable judgments. Critical-thinking instruction is most effective when it is embedded in subject content and involves explicit criteria plus guided practice, rather than generic reasoning drills [Explicit instruction embedded in domain content improves critical-thinking outcomes.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. The vocabulary itself is only the entry point: concepts become durable when learners apply them to real sources repeatedly, with feedback, across varied contexts [Conceptual knowledge applied across multiple varied cases supports transfer.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md) of each concept with a clear definition, a worked example, and a [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) contrast (e.g., a reliable vs. a biased account of the same event)
- Authentic sources to analyze — primary documents, news articles, social media posts — not invented textbook examples
- Regular integration into discourse: the terms must appear in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), reading prompts, and [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) criteria, not just in one introductory lesson
- [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md) that fades: from teacher-led analysis, to guided group analysis, to independent evaluation

#### Constraints
- A one-off vocabulary lesson does not produce transfer; without repeated application across contexts, learners revert to accepting sources at face value [Civic online reasoning instruction improves evaluation only when taught with repeated practice on live sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [-W]
- Learners may apply the terms superficially — labeling everything "biased" without justification — if criteria are not tied to evidence-gathering routines
- Novices can experience [cognitive overload](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M] when asked to evaluate a source on many dimensions at once; introduce criteria incrementally
- Concept knowledge alone does not guarantee skilled evaluation; strategy instruction (e.g., checking who is behind a site) outperforms definitional knowledge for online sources [Civic online reasoning instruction improves evaluation.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Discipline-specific framing: historians emphasize sourcing and corroboration; scientists emphasize methodology and replication; media studies emphasize framing and audience
- Age adaptation: younger learners use simplified criteria ("Who made this? Why? Is it true?"); older learners use full credibility frameworks
- Depth variation: from quick heuristic checklists to full [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) analyzing contested events with multiple conflicting sources

### Target Learners
- Middle and high school students encountering primary and secondary sources for the first time [Civic online reasoning instruction improves evaluation.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Learners with limited prior exposure to media literacy, who tend to judge credibility by surface features (design, tone) rather than evidence [Civic online reasoning instruction improves evaluation.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+M]
- Less effective without adaptation for very young learners, who lack the background knowledge to judge accuracy or detect bias meaningfully

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical evaluation: judging the relevance, accuracy, and reliability of information sources
- Epistemic cognition: understanding that authors have motivations and perspectives that shape texts
- Disciplinary literacy: applying domain-specific sourcing norms (e.g., corroboration in history) [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Name and define each concept with an example and a [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) contrast, using [Direct Instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md).
2. Model an evaluation aloud on a live source, verbalizing the criteria in use ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
3. Guide whole-class application during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), requiring learners to justify judgments with the shared vocabulary.
4. Move to small-group analysis of conflicting sources ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)), then to independent evaluation with [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) rubrics that score use of the criteria.
5. Fade support over time ([Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md)), so evaluation terms become self-initiated habits rather than teacher prompts.

## Examples
- A history teacher introduces *relevance, accuracy, bias,* and *reliability* when comparing a government communiqué and a soldier's letter about the same battle, then requires students to use the terms in every subsequent document analysis.
- The [Stanford History Education Group's Civic Online Reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu) teaches evaluation concepts through short lessons in which students judge real websites, tweets, and news articles, with practice items scored against explicit criteria.
- A science teacher applies the same vocabulary to evaluating claims about a controversial study, asking whether the source is *reliable* (peer-reviewed?) and whether reporting is *accurate* relative to the original paper.
