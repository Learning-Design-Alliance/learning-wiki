# Use LLMs to improve and proofread your own writing while keeping your voice, define prompts carefully, and treat all LLM output as a draft to edit critically

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 against) · 1 study (1 theoretical), `q1` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article recommends using large language models to enhance language skills, proofread, find inconsistencies, and seek inspiration when stuck on a title, sentence or paragraph, "whilst maintaining your style and voice, as opposed to using LLMs to write from scratch". Effective use requires descriptive, detailed prompts; providing samples of your own writing helps the LLM match your style. Outcomes must be edited, corrected, proofread and checked by others.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Very specific instructions are needed to generate useful output; clearly define parameters and guidelines of the proposal
#### Constraints
- Sharing proposals with AI tools, especially when reviewing, is often against funding-organisation rules and may make them public; LLMs can limit your learning, lead to plagiarism, produce inconsistent styles, tend to be verbose, and may invent facts and references

### Target Learners
- researchers writing in English as a second language, including students taught to frame LLM prompts

### Target Learning Goals
- improving English writing quality and efficiency in proposal preparation
