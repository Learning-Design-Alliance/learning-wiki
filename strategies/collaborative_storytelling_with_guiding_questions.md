# Collaborative Storytelling with Guiding Questions

## Description
Collaborative storytelling involves students working in pairs to co-create a narrative by responding to a series of guiding questions. Each student first spends a minute thinking silently about their individual response to a question. Then, the pair shares their answers and decides together which one to write down. After answering all questions, students write a simple version of their story with illustrations, practice telling it, and present it to another small group. This method enhances collaboration by ensuring individual thought precedes group discussion, leading to a final product superior to what a student might create alone. The questions prompt students to consider setting, characters (protagonist and antagonist), the main problem, character histories, internal thoughts, dialogue, a positive event, a negative event, and the story's ending.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Guiding questions, large easel paper for each pair, writing and drawing materials, time for individual thinking, pair sharing, story writing, practice, and presentation.
#### Constraints
- The number of questions should be limited to avoid overwhelming students. Requires careful facilitation to ensure all students participate actively. Effectiveness depends on the quality of guiding questions and the structure of the activity.

### Target Learners
<!-- Link to sub-claims: [[claims/claim-slug]] -->
- Elementary to high school students, particularly beneficial for English language learners.

### Target Learning Goals
<!-- Link to sub-claims: [[claims/claim-slug]] -->
- Develop narrative writing skills, enhance collaboration, improve task fulfillment, grammatical accuracy, and complexity in writing, foster critical thinking and communication skills.

### Affordances
- Reduces anxiety and increases self-confidence and motivation among students, especially ELLs. Promotes active participation, enhances collaborative opportunities, and improves the quality of written texts. Structured questioning promotes deeper thinking and better story elements.

### Personalization
- The teacher can modify the number and type of guiding questions used based on the learners' needs and abilities. They can also adjust the amount of thinking and sharing time provided.

## Assessment Evidence
- Evaluate the quality and complexity of the written stories, observe student participation and collaboration during pair work, assess the clarity and creativity of story presentations.

## Impact
- Research indicates that ELLs working in pairs produce shorter but better texts in terms of task fulfillment, grammatical accuracy, and complexity. Collaborative writing lowers anxiety and increases self-confidence and motivation.
