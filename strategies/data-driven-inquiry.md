# Data Driven Inquiry

## Description
Data driven inquiry is a structured, collaborative cycle in which educators examine evidence of student learning — assessment results, work samples, attendance, engagement data — to identify learning gaps, generate hypotheses about their causes, and design and test instructional responses. It is typically carried out in teams (data teams, professional learning communities) using a repeated protocol: collect data, analyze for patterns, infer causes, act, and re-examine. The strategy treats instructional decisions as hypotheses to be tested rather than assumptions to be defended.

## Design Implications

Data driven inquiry operationalizes [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) at the instructional-program level: evidence is used to adapt teaching rather than merely to certify outcomes [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the inquiry, not the quantity of data — teams that move quickly from data to a pre-selected solution, or that read data without surfacing underlying assumptions about learners, show little instructional change [~M]. Structured protocols that slow the leap from observation to action are what distinguish genuine inquiry from data theater.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Valid, timely data that actually maps to the learning goals being taught (not only summative accountability data)
- A facilitator and a disciplined protocol (e.g., data-driven dialogue, "notice/wonder" rounds) that separates observation from interpretation
- Protected collaborative time and psychological safety, since honest analysis of data implicates everyone's practice
- Authority and time to change instruction based on findings — inquiry without decision rights produces cynicism

#### Constraints
- Summative, delayed, or coarse-grained data (e.g., annual test scores) arrives too late and at too low a resolution to inform instruction [~M]
- Data conversations dominated by a single interpretation or by accountability pressure lead to defensive, surface-level analysis rather than changed practice [-M]
- Over-reliance on easily quantified measures narrows instruction toward what is measured [-M]
- Teams without facilitation tend to stop at describing the data ("the scores are low") without causal reasoning or action

#### Implementation Variability
- **Data teams / PLC cycles** (e.g., Solution Tree's PLC model): recurring grade-level or department teams analyzing common assessment results
- **Instructional rounds**: school-wide observation and evidence collection focused on a chosen problem of practice
- **Classroom-level formative cycles**: individual teachers using exit tickets, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), and item analysis to adjust the next lesson
- **Design-based research partnerships**: researchers and practitioners co-analyzing data over extended cycles

### Target Learners
- Indirect: all students, but the strongest documented gains occur when inquiry focuses on specific, item-level learning gaps rather than aggregate scores [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Struggling learners benefit most when inquiry leads to targeted reteaching and [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) rather than generic whole-class repetition
- Teachers new to a curriculum or to interpreting assessment evidence gain the most from facilitated team inquiry

### Target Learning Goals
- Instructional improvement goals: diagnosing and closing specific skill or concept gaps
- Teacher professional learning: building assessment literacy and causal reasoning about student learning
- Equity goals: surfacing patterns in which student groups are underserved by current instruction

### Instructions
1. **Frame a question of practice** grounded in a learning goal, not a data source ("Why are students failing items on proportional reasoning?").
2. **Collect and display relevant evidence** — common [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) results, anonymized student work, and classroom evidence at a grain size actionable within weeks.
3. **Observe before interpreting** — use a protocol that forces "noticings" to be separated from explanations, guarding against confirmation bias.
4. **Generate multiple causal hypotheses**, including ones about instruction rather than only about students; prioritize using [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) norms that invite dissent.
5. **Act on the leading hypothesis** — design a targeted instructional response (reteaching, [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), revised tasks) and commit to a timeline.
6. **Re-assess and iterate** — collect new evidence on the same goal and repeat the cycle; treat the first response as a test, not a fix.

## Examples
- **[Data Wise](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/data-wise)** (Harvard Graduate School of Education) — an eight-step protocol used by schools and districts to organize faculty data inquiry, emphasizing "creating a climate for data use" before analysis begins.
- **PLC common assessment cycles** (Solution Tree / AllThingsPLC, https://www.allthingsplc.info) — grade-level teams administer common formative assessments, analyze item-level results by student group, and plan tiered reteaching.
- **[Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cgi-for-math.md)** — teachers analyze student strategy data on math problems to infer children's informal reasoning and adapt instruction accordingly.
