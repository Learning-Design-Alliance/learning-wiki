# Graphic Organizers / Guided Notes (for Processing Speed)

## Description
During lecture, learners are provided with graphic organizers or guided notes to help pull out the important points. These tools structure the information being presented, making it easier to process and retain. Guided notes prompt learners to fill in key information — blanks, prompts, or diagram nodes — promoting active engagement during the lecture rather than verbatim transcription.

## Design Implications

By externalizing the lecture's structure, these tools reduce the working-memory burden of simultaneously listening, deciding what matters, and writing — a triple load that disproportionately penalizes learners with slow processing speed [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Instructor-provided organizers are especially effective for novices, who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content on their own [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]. The fill-in format converts passive listening into generative processing, which improves retention over instructor-complete notes [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prepared organizer or guided-notes handout aligned to the lecture's actual structure (sequence, hierarchy, compare/contrast)
- Blanks or prompts at the key points, not scattered trivially — the gaps should mark what matters
- Instructor cues ("this goes in your chart") signaling when to write, so learners with slow processing are not caught mid-sentence
- Writing utensils or digital input tools; allow drawing or typing as alternatives

#### Constraints
- Requires substantial preparation time for the instructor; poorly aligned organizers add load rather than reducing it
- Some learners find the structure too rigid if not tailored; over-specified notes can suppress note-taking skill development [~M]
- If blanks are too frequent, learners fall behind the lecture and lose the thread — the tool then *creates* processing-speed problems
- Benefits shrink for learners with strong prior knowledge, who may find the scaffolding redundant [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Partial outlines (headings + key terms) vs. heavily scaffolded diagrams (flowcharts, matrices, concept maps)
- Pre-lecture distribution for preview vs. in-lecture completion; preview supports learners who need extra processing time
- Digital versions with typed or drag-and-drop input for learners with writing difficulties
- Fading the scaffold over a term — progressively more open notes as note-taking skill develops

### Target Learners
- Learners who struggle with processing speed, who cannot both transcribe and comprehend in real time [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Students with learning disabilities or ADHD; graphic organizers show consistent positive effects for students with LD [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Novices who cannot yet judge what is important in a lecture [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for experts or students with fluent note-taking skills [Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional guidance.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of key concepts during expository presentation
- Note-taking skill development (the organizer models what good notes look like)
- Retention of lecture content through generative completion [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — the organizer offloads structure-holding and importance-judging to the artifact, freeing capacity for comprehension
- [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) — organizers present information in pre-chunked units rather than a continuous stream
- [Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md) — the organizer makes the lecture's skeleton visible before and during delivery
- [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — distributed before the lecture, the same artifact functions as an advance organizer priming relevant schema

### Personalization
- Customize organizers to content type (timeline for history, matrix for comparisons, cycle diagram for processes) and to learner needs
- Offer multiple response modes: writing, drawing, typing, or selecting from word banks

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content and choose an organizer structure that matches its logic (hierarchy, sequence, comparison).
2. Create the guided-notes version: full headings and key terms, with blanks at the most important points.
3. [Provide guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md) — distribute the organizer before class, briefly model how to use it, and cue fill-in moments during the lecture.
4. Follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) — have learners use the completed organizer to summarize, answer questions, or study, so the notes become a retrieval tool rather than a product.
5. Review completed organizers to check comprehension and adjust blank density for the next session.

## Examples
- **Guided notes in inclusive classrooms** — Teachers provide lecture outlines with key-term blanks and cue words ("remember this!"), a format validated across studies of students with disabilities ([Konrad et al. meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0741932509336240)).
- **Cornell-notes hybrids** — A guided-notes left column with instructor-provided questions, right column with learner-completed blanks, and a summary section completed after lecture.
- **Science lecture concept maps** — Instructor projects a partially completed concept map; learners fill in linking words and missing nodes during the explanation.
