    Reflection with Others¶

    Strategy · All strategies

    Description¶

    Reflection with Others is a professional learning strategy in which teachers examine their own teaching through structured collaboration with colleagues — inviting peers to observe lessons and give non-evaluative feedback, discussing what is and isn't working, and jointly analyzing specific strategies and techniques. Unlike solitary reflection, it externalizes the reflective process: a colleague notices what the teacher cannot see from inside the lesson, and dialogue converts private impressions into shared, actionable analysis. It is most commonly organized around a subject area (e.g., colleagues who teach similar math content) so that feedback is specific rather than generic.

    Design Implications¶

    Collaborative reflection works because it combines external perspective with social accountability: teachers who receive focused, non-evaluative feedback and discuss instruction with peers show measurable gains in instructional quality, and coaching-style arrangements built on this principle show consistent positive effects on both instruction and achievement Kraft et al. meta-analysis of teacher coaching [+S]. The reflective dialogue itself matters — reflection that is structured around specific evidence (student work, observation notes) produces more change than unstructured conversation [~M]. The non-evaluative framing is essential: when observation is tied to evaluation, teachers perform rather than reveal, and the feedback loop collapses.

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Protected, scheduled time for peer meetings and lesson observation — this is the most common failure point
    • A norm of non-evaluative, descriptive feedback (what happened, not whether the teacher is good)
    • A shared focus: a specific strategy, technique, or content area both parties teach or understand
    • Trust and psychological safety; pairs or small groups should be voluntary where possible
    • Some structure for the conversation (e.g., observation protocol, focus question agreed in advance)

    Constraints¶

    • Without dedicated time, peer reflection degrades into occasional hallway conversation with little instructional impact [-M]
    • If observation is linked to formal evaluation or administrative judgment, teachers hide difficulties and feedback loses its diagnostic value [-M]
    • Unstructured "reflection" without evidence or focus produces camaraderie but little change in practice [~M]
    • Feedback from colleagues who do not know the content area tends toward generic advice (engagement, pacing) rather than discipline-specific improvement [~W]
    • Voluntary participation without any accountability structure often leads to attrition; some external facilitation or scheduling support sustains it [~W]

    Implementation Variability¶

    • Peer observation pairs: reciprocal observation with a pre-observation focus question and post-observation debrief
    • Lesson study (jugyō kenkyū): a team jointly plans a research lesson, one member teaches while others observe students, then the group revises — the most structured form
    • Critical friends groups / professional learning communities: ongoing small groups that examine student work and teaching practice against shared protocols
    • Instructional coaching: a trained coach replaces or supplements a peer, trading reciprocity for expertise
    • Video clubs: teachers analyze recordings of their own or others' lessons, which removes the live-observation burden and allows re-examination

    Target Learners¶

    • Practicing teachers at any career stage, particularly those seeking to refine specific techniques rather than learn content from scratch
    • Early-career teachers benefit from the modeling and normalization of struggle that peer dialogue provides [+W]
    • Less useful for teachers facing fundamentally different problems than their peers (e.g., the only teacher of a subject in a building) unless matched across schools

    Target Learning Goals¶

    • Improvement of specific instructional techniques (questioning, discourse facilitation, task selection)
    • Development of reflective practice as a professional habit — learning to analyze one's own teaching against evidence
    • Transfer of practices between classrooms: seeing a strategy work with real students makes adoption more likely than reading about it

    Instructions¶

    1. Choose a focus with a colleague who teaches similar content — one specific strategy or question, not "general improvement" (see Check-In for establishing the partnership norm)
    2. Agree on a non-evaluative observation protocol: what the observer will look for and record (time stamps, student talk, teacher moves), and what will not be discussed
    3. The observer visits the lesson, collecting descriptive evidence rather than judgments
    4. Debrief within 48 hours: the teacher reflects first, then the observer presents evidence; the conversation ends with one concrete adjustment to try
    5. Reciprocate — the observer teaches while the original teacher observes, closing the loop
    6. Revisit the focus after several weeks to examine the effect of the change, ideally using student work as evidence

    Related Strategies¶

    • Lesson Study — the most formalized version of collaborative reflection, built around a jointly planned research lesson
    • Professional Learning Communities — the ongoing team structure within which peer reflection typically lives
    • Instructional Coaching — replaces the reciprocal peer with an expert partner

    Examples¶

    • Lesson study in Japan and the U.S. — Teams of teachers jointly plan a "research lesson," observe it live while collecting data on student thinking, then revise and reteach it in another class. Documented in the U.S. via the Mills College Lesson Study Group (lessonresearch.net).
    • Video clubs at the University of Pittsburgh — Researchers and teachers met regularly to analyze classroom video clips, with the facilitator steering discussion toward student mathematical thinking rather than teacher performance (van Es & Sherin's work on video clubs).
    • A math department peer-observation cycle — Two algebra teachers agree that the observer will record every question the teacher asks and every student response, then categorize them as open or closed; the debrief focuses solely on the question distribution and one change for the next unit.
