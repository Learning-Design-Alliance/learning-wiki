# Follow-Up After Disciplining a Student

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
After disciplining a learner, the instructor deliberately re-engages rather than letting the incident define the relationship. The follow-up has three components: a private check-in to repair the relationship, explicit acknowledgment of the learner's post-disciplinary successes, and sustained effort over time — because learners accustomed to negative attention do not recalibrate after a single positive interaction.

## Design Implications

Discipline is most effective when embedded in a relationship the learner perceives as respectful and trusting; punishment delivered by a teacher the learner believes is biased or has "given up" increases defiance and recidivism [Students who perceive disciplinary interactions as disrespectful show more defiance and teachers escalate punishment in a vicious cycle.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~M]. A brief empathic follow-up reframes the discipline as care rather than rejection, cutting suspension rates in field experiments [A brief empathic-discipline intervention reduced suspension rates among middle school students.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S]. Persistence matters: learners with histories of negative attention test whether the teacher's warmth is genuine, so early negative responses should not end the effort.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Willingness to invest individual time in a follow-up conversation after the emotional intensity of the incident has passed
- A private, low-stakes setting — public follow-ups can re-shame the learner
- Genuine attention to the learner's post-disciplinary behavior so successes can be named specifically
- A relationship repair orientation: the goal is reconnection, not re-litigating the incident

#### Constraints
- Some learners respond negatively to positive attention at first — testing whether it is sincere — and instructors who give up at this point confirm the learner's expectation of abandonment [-W]
- Follow-up without changed instructional practice reads as insincere; discipline paired with continued friction undermines the repair [~M]
- Instructors under time pressure or high disciplinary load may follow up inconsistently, which is worse than a predictable, lighter-touch routine [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- A brief same-day check-in versus a next-day conversation, depending on the learner's need for cooling-off time
- Nonverbal follow-up (a positive note, a greeting, an assigned responsibility) for learners who resist direct conversation
- Systematic versions such as daily [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines (e.g., Check In/Check Out behavior programs) that build the relationship before incidents occur

### Target Learners
- Learners with repeated disciplinary histories who expect rejection and disengage preemptively [Students who perceive disciplinary interactions as disrespectful show more defiance and teachers escalate punishment in a vicious cycle.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [~M]
- Adolescents, who are especially sensitive to perceived disrespect and social status in teacher interactions
- All learners benefit, but the effect is strongest where the teacher–learner relationship has been damaged by the disciplinary event

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral and self-regulatory growth: supporting the learner's return to productive participation after discipline
- Relationship repair: maintaining the trust that makes future [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) effective
- Belonging: signaling that the learner remains a full member of the classroom community

### Instructions
1. Discipline privately and with respect, framing the behavior — not the learner — as the problem.
2. Within a day or two, [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) privately: ask how the learner is doing, listen, and avoid re-arguing the incident.
3. Watch for and specifically acknowledge post-disciplinary successes ("I noticed you got started right away today") rather than generic praise.
4. Persist through initial coolness or testing behavior; expect the cycle of negative attention to take time to break.
5. Pair follow-up with continued instructional support — [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) on academic work, not just behavior — so the relationship is not solely about discipline.

## Examples
- **Check In/Check Out (CICO)** — a widely implemented Tier 2 behavior program in which a mentor greets the student each morning, reviews goals, and checks out at day's end with positive feedback; the daily structure operationalizes persistent follow-up (see [Intervention Central](https://www.interventioncentral.org)).
- **Empathic discipline intervention** — Okonofua, Paunesku, and Walton's "wise feedback"-style teacher activity reframed discipline as encouragement, reducing suspensions by roughly half among middle schoolers in a randomized study.
- **Restorative practices programs** (e.g., [International Institute for Restorative Practices](https://www.iirp.edu)) — post-incident restorative conferences formalize the follow-up conversation as a structured step after discipline.
