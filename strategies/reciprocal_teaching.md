# Reciprocal Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reciprocal Teaching is a structured, collaborative reading-comprehension strategy in which small groups of learners rotate the role of discussion leader while applying four concrete strategies: **predicting** what comes next, **questioning** the group about the text, **clarifying** confusing words or ideas, and **summarizing** the main points. The teacher initially models each strategy through [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration, then progressively transfers responsibility to students as they take turns leading [Fading](../elements/fading.md).

## Design Implications

Reciprocal Teaching works because it combines explicit strategy instruction with guided social practice: students observe expert strategy use, then rehearse it with immediate peer and teacher feedback until the strategies become self-sustaining habits [Palincsar & Brown's original studies showed large comprehension gains for poor readers.](https://doi.org/10.2307/1170147) [+S]. The dialogue format forces learners to externalize and negotiate meaning, which functions as a social form of [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and improves conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The gradual handover of the leader role enacts scaffolding and promotes transfer of responsibility from teacher to learner [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text at an appropriate instructional level — challenging but decodable with effort
- Explicit teacher modeling of all four strategies before students lead ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with think-aloud narration)
- Small groups (typically 4–6) so every student can hold the leader role
- Prompt cards or bookmarks listing the four strategies to structure early dialogue
- Teacher [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) during early student-led sessions, with prompts fading over time [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Fails when the teacher hands over leadership before students have seen enough modeled examples — groups drift into turn-taking without genuine strategy use [-S]
- Less effective with very short texts or texts students can already comprehend easily; the strategies become ritualistic rather than functional [~M]
- Requires sustained implementation (typically 15–20+ sessions) — effects shrink in brief interventions [~M]
- Groups without a trained teacher circulating lose structure; purely peer-run versions with no expert modeling show markedly weaker effects [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Classic Palincsar–Brown format**: whole-class modeling, then small-group dialogue with rotating leaders
- **Reciprocal Teaching in content areas**: the four strategies applied to science or social studies texts rather than narrative reading
- **Video- or chat-based variants**: asynchronous online discussions where students post predictions, questions, clarifications, and summaries in turn
- **Reduced-strategy versions**: some implementations begin with only questioning and clarifying for younger or struggling readers

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers in upper elementary through secondary school, who show the largest comprehension gains [+S]
- Learners with adequate decoding but weak comprehension monitoring — the clarifying and questioning strategies target exactly this gap
- Less beneficial for fluent comprehenders, for whom the strategies are often already automatized [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: constructing and monitoring meaning from extended text
- Metacognitive strategy acquisition: internalizing predict–question–clarify–summarize as self-regulated habits [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Collaborative discourse skills: asking questions, giving and receiving feedback at the task and process level [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Select a text segmented into sections and pre-teach any vocabulary critical to comprehension ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
2. Model the four strategies aloud on the first section, thinking aloud at each decision point ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
3. Assign the leader role to one student for the next section; the leader prompts the group to predict, question, clarify, and summarize while the teacher coaches from the side ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. Rotate the leader each section or session, fading teacher prompts as groups sustain the dialogue independently ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).
5. Debrief with the class on which strategies helped and where comprehension broke down, connecting group dialogue to independent reading ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).

## Examples
- **Palincsar & Brown (1984)** — seventh-grade poor comprehenders improved from roughly 30% to 70–80% on comprehension assessments after ~20 sessions of reciprocal teaching dialogue.
- **Reciprocal Teaching modules in [Read Naturally](https://www.readnaturally.com)** and commercial guided-reading programs embed the four strategies in small-group lesson structures.
- **Content-area adaptation** — science teachers using the four strategies with textbook chapters, where "clarifying" targets technical terminology and "predicting" uses section headings and diagrams.
