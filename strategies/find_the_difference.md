# Find the Difference

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners are shown two dot cards, identify which card has more dots, and determine the difference — how many more dots are on the greater card. The comparison structure ("how many more?") gives subtraction a concrete meaning: rather than executing an abstract operation, learners physically or mentally match dots one-to-one and count the leftover set. This grounds the difference interpretation of subtraction in a visual, countable representation before learners encounter symbolic number sentences.

## Design Implications

Comparison-based subtraction tasks connect the operation to a meaningful question rather than a procedure, which supports conceptual understanding alongside fluency [Visual and concrete representations in early mathematics support number sense and operational understanding.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [~M]. Well-structured dot arrangements (subitizable groups, consistent layouts) reduce counting errors so working memory is spent on the comparison, not on re-counting [Chunking dot patterns into recognizable groups reduces working-memory load during comparison.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Two dot cards per round with clearly countable arrangements (e.g., dice patterns, ten-frames)
- A number range matched to learners' current counting and comparison skill
- A way for learners to show or explain the difference: matching dots one-to-one, counting on from the smaller card, or covering the extra dots

#### Constraints
- Random or cluttered dot arrangements force effortful counting and can overload working memory, shifting attention away from the comparison [Cognitive overload from poorly structured visual displays degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Learners who have not yet established cardinality or reliable counting will compare inaccurately; the activity does not itself teach counting
- If the routine always uses the same small range, learners may answer by pattern recognition rather than subtraction reasoning

#### Implementation Variability
- **Matching mode:** learners physically overlay counters or match dots one-to-one, then count unmatched dots — the most concrete version
- **Counting-on mode:** learners say the smaller quantity, then count up to the larger, tracking the count as the difference — bridges toward mental subtraction
- **Symbolic bridge:** after finding the difference, learners write the matching number sentence (e.g., 8 − 5 = 3)
- **Reveal variation:** briefly flash both cards, then hide one, forcing mental imagery of the comparison

### Target Learners
- Pre-K through Grade 1 learners developing subtraction concepts and comparison language ("more than," "fewer than")
- Learners who benefit from concrete, visual entry points before symbolic practice
- Less appropriate for learners still acquiring one-to-one correspondence; they need counting-level activities first

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of subtraction as difference/comparison (not just "take away")
- Comparison and relational number sense
- Early mental math and counting-on strategies

### Instructions
1. Show two dot cards side by side and ask, "Which card has more? How do you know?" ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
2. Have learners find the difference by matching dots one-to-one or counting on from the smaller card; ask them to explain their strategy aloud ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
3. Record the matching number sentence together and connect it to the dots ("8 minus 5 is 3 — see the 3 unmatched dots")
4. Repeat with new pairs, gradually increasing the range and reducing the time cards stay visible ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) — observe which strategy each learner uses)
