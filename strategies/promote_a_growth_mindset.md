# Promote a Growth Mindset

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Promoting a growth mindset means structuring feedback, tasks, and classroom language so learners come to see ability as malleable — built through effort, effective strategies, and help-seeking — rather than as a fixed trait. In practice this goes beyond praising effort: teachers attribute success and failure to controllable factors (strategy use, persistence, practice), normalize errors as information, and provide concrete routes for improvement. In mathematics especially, students often believe intelligence is fixed; reframing difficulty as a signal of learning rather than of low ability changes how they respond to challenge.

## Design Implications

Growth mindset interventions have produced measurable gains in achievement for struggling and at-risk students, though effects are small on average and depend heavily on context [~M]. The mechanism is motivational: students who believe ability is developable persist after setbacks and recover from failure rather than disengaging. Critically, praise for effort alone can backfire if it is not tied to strategy and progress — students can perceive "you worked hard" as a consolation for failure [~M]. Effective implementation pairs mindset framing with [actionable feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) and genuine opportunities to improve, so the message that ability grows is credible.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Feedback that is specific and strategy-focused — naming what the learner did and what to try next — not generic effort praise ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
- Tasks that are genuinely revisable: learners must be able to act on the feedback and resubmit or retry, or the "you can improve" message rings hollow
- Teacher language and assessment practices aligned with the message; a growth-oriented speech followed by norm-referenced grading undermines it
- Normalization of error and struggle, e.g., analyzing common mistakes as a class rather than hiding them

#### Constraints
- Effects are strongest for academically at-risk or low-achieving students and are near zero for high achievers already confident in their ability [~M]
- Brief stand-alone interventions (a single lesson or video) show weak, quickly-decaying effects unless the surrounding environment supports the message [~M]
- Effort praise without strategy guidance can reduce performance; students may conclude that effort alone suffices and persist with ineffective approaches [-M]
- In classrooms where grades, tracking, or public rankings emphasize fixed ability, mindset messaging is contradicted by daily experience and loses credibility [-M]
- Students may experience repeated "try harder" messaging as blaming when the task or instruction is genuinely beyond their current skills [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Embedded approach: teacher language, feedback routines, and assessment design carry the mindset message continuously across the term
- Structured intervention: short sequenced modules (e.g., Yeager et al.'s two-session online intervention) that teach the malleability of ability and how the brain changes with practice
- Content-specific framing: "everyone can learn math to high levels" (Youcubed) versus generic "everyone can learn" messaging — subject-specific versions counter domain stereotypes more directly
- Error-analysis routines: celebrating and dissecting mistakes so struggle is reframed as part of learning

### Target Learners
- Low-achieving or at-risk students, who show the largest achievement gains from mindset interventions [~M]
- Students experiencing stereotype threat or belonging uncertainty in a domain; mindset framing overlaps with [belonging interventions](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Students transitioning into harder material (e.g., middle school, first-year STEM), where early setbacks would otherwise trigger disengagement
- Less beneficial for high-achieving students, who typically already hold adaptive views of their ability [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivational: persistence through challenge, resilience after failure, willingness to attempt difficult tasks
- Metacognitive: attributing outcomes to strategy and effort rather than fixed ability, and revising strategies accordingly
- Attitudinal: self-efficacy and a positive orientation toward learning and [assessment as information](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Audit your own language and grading practices for fixed-ability messages (ranking, "smart" praise, ability grouping) before adding growth framing.
2. Deliver feedback that names effective strategies and next steps, not just effort ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
3. Normalize error: have students analyze mistakes and revise work, so struggle becomes part of the routine rather than a verdict.
4. Teach the science of malleability explicitly — how practice changes the brain — so the belief has a factual basis, not just encouragement.
5. Build in opportunities for students to reflect on their strategy use and progress ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)), connecting effort to outcomes they can see.
6. Offer choice and voice in how students work and demonstrate learning, reinforcing that they control their learning trajectory and supporting autonomy [+M].

## Related Strategies
- Provide specific, strategy-focused feedback — the operational core of mindset promotion; mindset framing without it is empty praise
- Normalize mistakes and revision — makes the "ability grows" message credible through daily experience
- Scaffold help-seeking — teaches students *how* to improve, not just that they can

## Examples
- **[Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org)** (Jo Boaler, Stanford) — mathematical mindset lessons and teacher resources built around the claim that everyone can learn math to high levels; includes error-normalization routines and "low floor, high ceiling" tasks.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — embeds growth mindset messaging ("you can learn anything") alongside mastery-based practice where students retry exercises until proficient, making improvement visible.
- **Yeager et al. (2019) National Study of Learning Mindsets** — a short online intervention delivered in regular school time raised GPA among lower-achieving high school students, but only in contexts where peer and teacher norms supported the message.
- **Praise reframing in elementary math** — replacing "you're so smart" with "you tried a new strategy when the first one didn't work" (Mueller & Dweck's classic studies), shifting attribution from trait to process.
