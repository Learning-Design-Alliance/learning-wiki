Use a repeated-trials research model, repeating intervention studies within the same schools to build fidelity and cumulative validity¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 1 claim (1 mixed) · 1 study (1 associational), q3 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

The authors propose that single studies of instructional technology cannot validate results with certainty, and that repeating research within the same schools builds confidence. They write that "By repeating research within the same schools, fidelity is given to curriculum, instructional resources and products, delivery systems, assessments, the social-cultural context of the students and the school community and to a cadre of experienced teachers." The accumulation of repeated results is said to lead to greater certainty and validity for the effects of instructional technology tools on student achievement.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Same school, similar grade levels, same instructional software and system, students with similar socio-cultural backgrounds, a consistent cadre of classroom teachers, and essentially the same kinds of measures, as the article lists as common elements

Constraints¶

  • The three investigations described were not replications, since replication was not the purpose at the outset, and differed in sample sizes, intervention lengths and data analyses

Target Learners¶

  • K-12 students in schools adopting instructional technology

Target Learning Goals¶

  • reading achievement
  • validity of instructional technology effectiveness research

Claims¶

  • Failure learning and transfer parameters are not sustained in later trials, suggesting repeating the same feedback does not provide sustained assistance [~W]

Related Strategies¶

  • Build research programs combining studies optimized for internal validity with studies optimized for ecological validity
  • Replicate an intervention with the same teachers to isolate teacher experience, and embed fidelity measurement to identify critical program components
