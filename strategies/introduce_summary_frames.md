# Introduce Summary Frames

## Description
Learners read a section of text to themselves before verbally summarizing the passage to a partner or completing sentence frames. Frames are generic text-structure templates — e.g., "_____ wanted _____, but _____, so _____" for problem/solution text, or "_____ happens because _____" for cause/effect — that prompt learners to identify main ideas and the logical connectives that hold a passage together. The strategy combines independent reading, structured oral rehearsal, and generative restatement, all of which force the learner to construct rather than merely recognize meaning.

## Design Implications

Summarizing is a generative task: producing a condensed restatement requires selecting main ideas, deleting redundancy, and building a coherent macro-structure, which is exactly the processing that improves comprehension and retention [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. Frames scaffold this by supplying the rhetorical skeleton of a text type, so working memory is spent on content rather than on inventing a structure [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Oral summarizing to a partner adds a comprehension check: breakdowns in the partner's understanding surface gaps the summarizer must repair.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text chunked into summarizable sections, matched to learners' reading levels ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Sentence frames matched to the actual text structure of the passage (problem/solution, cause/effect, compare/contrast, sequence)
- A partner or small-group structure for oral summaries, with norms for listening and questioning
- Instructor modeling of a completed summary before learners attempt their own

#### Constraints
- Over-reliance on frames produces formulaic summaries that list frame slots without genuine condensation; learners may fill in blanks mechanically rather than select main ideas [-M]
- Frames matched to the *wrong* text structure distort comprehension — forcing a cause/effect frame onto a descriptive passage teaches a false macro-structure [-M]
- For learners with strong summarization skill, frames add little and can constrain more capable restatements [Guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Frames alone, without feedback on summary accuracy, can entrench inaccurate main-idea selection; feedback should target the summary's content and structure [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Oral-first:** partners alternate summarizing sections aloud; frames serve as speaking prompts
- **Written frames:** learners complete sentence frames individually, then compare with a partner
- **Fading:** begin with full frames, move to word-bank prompts, then to unframed summaries as skill develops
- **Reciprocal format:** pairs alternate roles of summarizer and questioner, as in reciprocal teaching
- **Disciplinary variants:** frames reflect discipline-specific structures (lab report claim–evidence–reasoning; historical cause–consequence chains)

### Target Learners
- Developing readers and multilingual learners who benefit from explicit language scaffolds for academic discourse [+M]
- Novices in a discipline who do not yet know its conventional text structures
- Less valuable for advanced readers who already summarize flexibly [Guidance that helps novices can become redundant or counterproductive as expertise grows.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and text structure
- Summarization skill: condensing, selecting, and organizing information
- Disciplinary literacy: internalizing the rhetorical patterns of academic language
- Oral academic language production through structured rehearsal

### Instructions
1. Select a text and divide it into coherent sections; identify each section's dominant text structure.
2. Model a summary using the frame, thinking aloud about how you selected main ideas ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. Learners read the next section independently ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Learners summarize orally to a partner using the frame, or complete it in writing; partners ask clarifying questions ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Provide feedback on accuracy and completeness of main ideas, then fade the frame over successive sections.

## Related Strategies
- **Reciprocal Teaching** — pairs summary frames with prediction, questioning, and clarifying roles in a structured dialogue
- **Think-Pair-Share** — the oral-summary-to-partner step is a summarizing variant of this routine
- **Annotating** — margin notes made during reading supply raw material for the frame slots ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))

## Related Elements
- [Practice](../elements/practice.md) — each read-then-summarize cycle is a spaced practice opportunity
- [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) — instructor modeling and corrective feedback during frame completion
- [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) — whole-class comparison of summaries sharpens main-idea judgments

## Patterns That Use This Strategy
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — the modeling step makes expert summarizing visible before learners attempt it
- [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) — frames function as the guided-practice scaffold between modeling and independent work

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching of reading (Palincsar & Brown, 1984)** — seventh graders with poor comprehension learned to summarize paragraphs within a dialogue routine; comprehension gains of roughly two standard deviations on assessments were sustained at follow-up.
- **Sentence frames in sheltered/SIOP instruction** — content-area teachers provide frames such as "The data show that _____, which suggests _____" so multilingual learners rehearse disciplinary language while summarizing lab results.
- **Reading Apprenticeship (WestEd)** — disciplinary teachers use structured summary routines and metacognitive conversation starters during content-area reading ([https://readingapprenticeship.org](https://readingapprenticeship.org)).
