# Identify Feelings

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q3` · 2 of 4 report an effect size

## Description
Identify Feelings is a strategy in which students recognize, name, or match emotional states — typically from facial expressions, body language, tone, or situational cues — and label them with accurate vocabulary. It is carried out through activities such as emotion-card matching, feelings check-ins, analyzing characters' emotions in stories, or photographing and categorizing expressions. The core move is converting an internal or observed affective state into a precise verbal label.

## Design Implications

Labeling emotions is the foundational skill of emotional literacy: students cannot regulate or empathize with feelings they cannot name. Affect labeling research shows that putting feelings into words reduces amygdala reactivity and increases prefrontal regulation [Lieberman et al. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M], and broader social-emotional learning programs that include emotion identification improve both behavior and academic outcomes [Durlak et al. (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A graded vocabulary of emotion words, from basic (happy, sad, angry) to granular (frustrated, anxious, proud), introduced progressively ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Varied exemplars — photos, video clips, story characters, real classroom situations — so students generalize beyond a single face or context
- A low-stakes routine for practice, such as a daily [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)
- Teacher modeling of accurate, non-judgmental labeling of both student and adult emotions
- Rich feeling-word resources: emotion wheels, facial expression cards, graded vocabulary lists (e.g., [RULER](https://ei.yale.edu/ruler/ruler-approach) Mood Meter)
- Authentic contexts for application — literary texts, video vignettes, real classroom situations ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))

#### Constraints
- Static facial-expression matching overstates how readable emotions are; emotion recognition depends heavily on context, culture, and individual differences, so decontextualized face-matching tasks transfer weakly [~M]
- Students with alexithymia or limited interoceptive awareness may accurately match pictures without connecting labels to their own felt experience — pairing with body-cue attention ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)) is needed
- Treating some emotions as "bad" teaches suppression rather than literacy; all labels should be framed as acceptable, with behavior — not feeling — as the target of guidance
- Facial expression recognition is not universal: display rules and interpretation of expressions vary across cultures, so decontextualized face-matching can mislead [Emotion perception is culturally situated rather than universal.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616689956) [~M]
- Forcing public emotional disclosure can harm learners with trauma histories or alexithymia; labeling should always be optional and private where possible [-M]
- Isolated face-card drills produce recognition of posed expressions but transfer weakly to real social perception; context-rich practice is needed [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Matching format:** emotion cards, sorting games, or digital tools for younger learners
- **Narrative embedding:** identifying characters' feelings during read-alouds, which adds perspective-taking to labeling
- **Self-report routines:** mood meters (e.g., the RULER approach's Mood Meter) where students locate their own state on valence–arousal axes
- **Multimodal:** voice tone, posture, and situational cues, not faces alone
- **Literature-based**: analyzing characters' feelings in fiction, which supports both comprehension and emotion vocabulary [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]
- **Somatic**: learners scan bodily sensations and match them to labels ("tight chest = worried?")
- **Intensity mapping**: placing feelings on a pleasantness/energy grid (Mood Meter) rather than binary categories
- **Game formats**: matching, charades, or [Act-It-Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) for younger learners

### Target Learners
- Early childhood and primary students, for whom emotion vocabulary is still developing [+S]
- Students with autism or language delays, for whom explicit instruction in emotion cues is more effective than incidental exposure [~M]
- Older students benefit from *granularity* — finer emotion distinctions predict better regulation — rather than basic matching [+W]
- Early childhood and elementary learners, for whom emotion vocabulary is still developing [+M]
- Learners with autism or alexithymia who benefit from explicit instruction in emotion recognition [-M] (they need it most, but require more scaffolding and repetition)
- Adults in social-emotional learning, counseling training, or workplace emotional-intelligence programs

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: building a vocabulary for internal states
- Self-awareness: recognizing one's own affect as input to [self-regulation](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Empathy and perspective-taking: reading others' states as a prerequisite for [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)
- Emotional literacy: building differentiated vocabulary for internal states
- Self-awareness and self-regulation: recognizing one's own feelings as they arise
- Social perception: reading others' emotional cues in context
- Reading comprehension: inferring character motivation and affect

### Instructions
1. **Model:** Teacher names emotions aloud in authentic moments ("I'm feeling frustrated because the projector failed"), demonstrating that labeling is normal and safe.
2. **Introduce vocabulary:** Present 3–5 target emotion words with faces, body cues, and situation examples ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the set to limit load).
3. **Match and sort:** Students sort photos, cards, or story events by emotion, justifying their choices with observable evidence ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. **Apply to self:** Students use a routine [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) (mood meter, feelings chart) to label their own current state.
5. **Apply to others:** During stories, role-plays, or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md), students infer and label characters' or peers' feelings and discuss how they knew.
6. **Extend granularity:** Gradually replace broad labels with precise ones (mad → annoyed, frustrated, furious) as vocabulary grows.

## Examples
- **RULER (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the Mood Meter asks students to plot their state on valence and energy axes, then select a precise word; used school-wide at [https://ei.yale.edu](https://ei.yale.edu)
- **Second Step (Committee for Children)** — early-lesson "emotion cards" and photo-sorting activities teach recognition of facial and body cues; [https://www.secondstep.org](https://www.secondstep.org)
- **Zones of Regulation** — students sort feelings into four color-coded zones and match them to regulation strategies; [https://zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com)
- **[RULER Approach](https://ei.yale.edu/ruler/ruler-approach)** (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — the Mood Meter has learners locate feelings on a pleasantness–energy grid; a whole-school SEL curriculum built around emotion labeling.
- **Zones of Regulation** (Kuypers) — color-coded zones (blue, green, yellow, red) that young learners use to categorize and communicate their arousal states.
- **Literature discussion** — during read-alouds, pausing to ask "What is this character feeling, and how do you know?" links emotion identification to text evidence.
