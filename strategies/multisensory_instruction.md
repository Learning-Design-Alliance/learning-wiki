# Multisensory Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 2 mixed) · 14 studies (7 review, 4 causal, 2 theoretical, 1 quant-synthesis), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 14 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Multisensory instruction teaches content through two or more sensory modalities at once — learners see a letter, say its sound, and trace or write it in the same lesson. The approach originated in the Orton-Gillingham-Stillman tradition for dyslexia remediation and underpins most structured literacy programs. Its core mechanism is not "matching learning styles" but pairing explicit, systematic phonics with simultaneous multimodal encoding and motor output.

## Design Implications

Multisensory instruction is best understood as explicit, systematic phonics instruction enriched with simultaneous multimodal input and motor engagement. The evidence base is strongest for the underlying explicit phonics component; the added value of the multisensory (tactile-kinesthetic) layer itself is less well isolated [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. Dual coding theory predicts benefits when verbal and visual channels carry complementary, non-redundant information [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M] — but adding a second channel that merely duplicates the first adds extraneous load rather than benefit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructors trained in a structured, sequential, cumulative scope and sequence (e.g., Orton-Gillingham certification)
- Simultaneous — not merely sequential — use of modalities: see it, say it, hear it, write it within one activity
- Systematic phoneme–grapheme mapping as the instructional core, not sensory activities as an end in themselves
- [Explicit teaching](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) structure: [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), guided [practice](../elements/practice.md), and immediate corrective [feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)
- A systematic scope and sequence of phoneme–grapheme correspondences, not ad hoc sensory activities
- Simultaneity: the modalities must be linked to the *same* item in the same moment, not delivered as separate activities
- Learner production (saying, writing, tracing), not just exposure to multiple formats
- Small enough group or 1:1 setting to monitor and correct each learner's responses

#### Constraints
- Resource-intensive: requires specialized instructor training and small-group or 1:1 delivery, limiting scalability
- Meta-analytic evidence for the Orton-Gillingham approach specifically is promising but limited by few rigorous studies; effects should not be attributed to the multisensory component alone [~W]
- The "match instruction to a learner's preferred modality" rationale is discredited — tailoring to self-reported learning styles does not improve outcomes [X]
- For learners with substantial expertise, added multimodal scaffolding can become redundant and inefficient [Instructional guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Sensory activities without a systematic phonics core (e.g., sand-tray work detached from phoneme–grapheme mapping) consume instructional time without measurable literacy gains [-W]
- Sensory activity can become decorative — tracing sand letters without phoneme-level focus adds cognitive load without adding learning ([Cognitive Load Theory](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)) [-W]
- Evidence for kinesthetic/tactile components specifically is thin; most gains are attributable to explicitness, intensity, and feedback [~W]
- Resource-intensive: typically requires trained specialists and 1:1 or very small-group delivery, limiting scalability [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Structured literacy programs (Wilson, Barton, SPIRE) implement it as a fully scripted, cumulative curriculum
- Classroom adaptations embed multisensory routines (air writing, letter tiles, color-coding) within whole-class phonics lessons
- Extended to content areas: manipulatives in mathematics, gesture-plus-speech in vocabulary instruction — with correspondingly thinner evidence
- Orton-Gillingham: full VAKT cycle (drill, dictation, reading) in 1:1 tutorial format
- Classroom adaptation: finger-spelling, air-writing, and letter-tile manipulation during whole-group phonics
- Secondary/adult literacy: same principle applied to morphemes and syllable division rather than single letters
- Non-reading domains: the analogous move is pairing verbal explanation with immediate physical enactment ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)) or annotation ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers and learners with dyslexia or word-level reading disabilities — the population for which the approach was developed and where evidence is strongest [+M]
- Early literacy learners building phoneme–grapheme mappings, where motor output (writing) reinforces letter knowledge
- Less beneficial for fluent readers or advanced learners, for whom the added sensory layer is redundant [~M]
- Beginning readers and students with word-reading difficulties, including dyslexia, for whom structured literacy programs are designed [+S]
- Struggling decoders who need redundant retrieval routes because single-channel phonological representations are fragile [~M]
- Not uniquely beneficial for typical readers beyond what explicit phonics already provides; no evidence of advantage for proficient learners [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Decoding, encoding (spelling), and word recognition — automatic word-level skill [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Fluency and reading accuracy as prerequisites for comprehension
- Retention of paired verbal–visual associations generally [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M]
- Phoneme–grapheme correspondence and decoding (alphabetic principle)
- Encoding (spelling) through simultaneous dictation routines

### Instructions
1. Begin with [direct instruction](../elements/direct-instruction.md) of the target phoneme–grapheme correspondence, following a cumulative scope and sequence
2. [Demonstrate](../elements/demonstration.md) the sound–symbol link while learners watch the mouth shape and letter formation
3. Guide simultaneous multisensory [practice](../elements/practice.md): learners say the sound while tracing, writing, or building the letter with tiles
4. [Provide feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) immediately and correct errors by re-modeling, not by re-explaining alone
5. Review previously taught patterns in each session (cumulative review) before introducing new material

## Description
Simultaneous multisensory instruction (VAKT: visual–auditory–kinesthetic–tactile) teaches a linguistic unit — a phoneme, grapheme, or word — through multiple sensory channels at once: the learner sees the letter, hears its sound, says it aloud, and traces or writes it in the same lesson segment. It is the core delivery method of structured literacy programs such as [Orton-Gillingham](https://www.ortonacademy.org) and its derivatives (Wilson Reading System, SPIRE). The intent is to build redundant, interconnected memory traces linking phonological, orthographic, and motor representations.

## Design Implications

The strategy pairs naturally with [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md): verbal and nonverbal codes for the same item create two retrieval routes, and motor enactment adds a third [Clark & Paivio's dual coding account of verbal and imagistic processing.](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [~W]. However, the strongest evidence base is for the *explicit, systematic phonics* component that multisensory programs embed, not for the sensory pairing itself — meta-analytic support for systematic phonics is robust, while the added value of kinesthetic/tactile tracing over well-designed explicit instruction remains weakly supported [Ehri et al.'s meta-analysis of systematic phonics.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. Practitioners should treat multisensory delivery as a vehicle for explicit instruction, not as a substitute for it.

### Instructions
1. Select one target phoneme–grapheme correspondence from a systematic sequence.
2. Present the letter visually while the instructor models its sound (visual + auditory).
3. Have the learner say the sound while tracing or writing the letter (auditory + kinesthetic/tactile) — simultaneity is the defining feature.
4. Dictate sounds, then words, then sentences; the learner writes while verbalizing ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Review previously taught correspondences in cumulative mixed practice before introducing new ones.
