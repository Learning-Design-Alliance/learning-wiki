# Movement Breaks

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), `q3` · 1 of 2 report an effect size

## Description
Movement breaks are brief, structured physical activities — stretching, dancing, simple exercises, or movement-based games — inserted into classroom or training sessions to energize learners and restore focus. They typically last 1–5 minutes and are deployed after periods of sedentary work, at natural transition points, or before demanding tasks. Beyond physical activation, they give learners a structured opportunity to release energy and manage stress, supporting self-regulation.

## Design Implications

Movement breaks leverage the link between physical arousal and cognitive readiness: acute bouts of physical activity produce small but reliable improvements in attention and on-task behavior [Classroom-based physical activity improves attention and on-task behavior.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. Their effect is on *readiness to learn* rather than on learning content directly — breaks work best as preparation for focused instruction, not as a substitute for it. Integrating academic content into the movement (e.g., spelling while stretching) can combine the attentional benefit with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) [+W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- 1–5 minutes of class time, roughly 2–3 times per day at natural transition points
- Minimal equipment (music, spot markers, beanbags) or none at all
- Teacher facilitation and participation to model engagement and ensure inclusivity
- Adaptations for learners with mobility limitations — seated variations, adjusted rules, varied intensity
- A predictable routine: consistent signal, defined duration, and a clear re-entry cue so the break does not become an unstructured transition
- Activities that are genuinely physical but low in cognitive demand — the goal is restoration, not adding a second learning task
- Timing aligned to attention cycles: after sustained seatwork or before a demanding new segment

#### Constraints
- Poorly timed or overlong breaks disrupt instructional flow and can increase off-task behavior rather than reduce it [-M] — breaks placed mid-task or used as rewards for completion lose their attentional benefit
- Learners who feel self-conscious or resistant to public physical activity may disengage; forced participation undermines the stress-management purpose [~M]
- Effects on academic achievement are small and indirect; breaks do not compensate for weak instruction [-W]
- High-energy activities immediately before tasks requiring calm focus can leave learners over-aroused [~M]
- Poorly bounded breaks increase off-task behavior and transition time; unstructured "free movement" often costs more re-focusing time than it buys [~M]
- High-arousal activities (loud games, competition) can leave learners dysregulated rather than refreshed, especially for students with attention or sensory regulation difficulties [~W]
- Effects on achievement are not established; breaks should not be justified as direct instructional time [~W]
- Overuse fragments lessons — frequent interruptions prevent learners from engaging in sustained effort on complex tasks [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content-integrated breaks**: movement combined with academic rehearsal (spelling, counting, vocabulary)
- **Energizing vs. calming breaks**: vigorous activity to raise arousal after sedentary periods; stretching or breathing to lower arousal before tests
- **Learner-led breaks**: students suggest and lead activities, building ownership and inclusivity
- **Seated or low-mobility variants**: chair stretches, hand movements, or rhythm activities for accessibility
- **Energizing breaks** (jumping, quick games) before low-arousal periods such as morning work
- **Calming breaks** (stretching, breathing with movement) after high-arousal activities or before assessments
- **Content-integrated movement** — acting out concepts, moving to stations — which doubles as [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) and blurs the line between break and instruction
- **Learner-led breaks**, where students select or lead the activity, adding an autonomy component consistent with [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md)

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, especially younger children whose attention spans make long sedentary stretches counterproductive [+M]
- Learners with ADHD or high energy needs, for whom structured movement supports self-regulation [+W]
- Adult learners in sedentary professional development settings, though effects are less studied [+W]
- Elementary-age learners, whose sustained attention spans are shorter and who benefit most from scheduled movement [~M]
- Learners with ADHD or self-regulation difficulties; brief exercise shows moderate acute benefits for attention in this population [~M]
- Adolescents in long block schedules, where attention lapses mid-lesson are common [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Attention and readiness: restoring focus for subsequent instruction
- Self-regulation: recognizing and managing arousal and stress states
- Procedural rehearsal: when content is integrated, practicing recall under movement conditions
- Attention and self-regulation: restoring readiness to learn rather than teaching content directly
- Behavioral goals: increasing time-on-task and reducing disruption during subsequent instruction
- Indirect support for any content goal by improving the conditions under which [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) occurs

### Instructions
1. Identify natural transition points (after lunch, between topics, before assessments) where attention typically dips.
2. Select a 1–5 minute activity matched to the desired arousal state — energizing after sedentary work, calming before high-stakes tasks.
3. Provide adaptations so all learners can participate at their own level of intensity and mobility.
4. Participate yourself and keep the routine predictable so transitions stay quick.
5. Return to instruction with a clear cue; follow with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or [Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md) while the attentional benefit is active.

## Examples
- **GoNoodle** ([gonoodle.com](https://www.gonoodle.com)) — short, energetic "brain break" videos widely used in elementary classrooms; teachers select videos by duration and energy level.
- **Spelling-in-motion** — an elementary teacher has students stretch and move while spelling words aloud, combining the attentional reset with content rehearsal.
- **Pre-test breathing and stretching** — a secondary teacher leads a 2-minute seated stretch routine before exams to reduce arousal and stress.
- **GoNoodle** (https://www.gonoodle.com) — a widely adopted platform of short guided movement and mindfulness videos designed for classroom breaks, organized by energy level and duration.
- **Energizers published by East Carolina University's Activity Promotion program** — free classroom-based movement routines developed for North Carolina schools, integrating movement with academic content.
- **Mindful stretching before tests** — many elementary classrooms use a 2-minute standing stretch and breathing routine before assessments to reduce arousal and reset attention.

# Movement Breaks

## Description
Movement breaks are short (1–5 minute), structured physical activities — stretching, rhythmic movement, brief games, or walking — inserted between instructional segments. They are carried out as deliberate transitions: the teacher signals a pause in academic work, leads or cues a defined physical activity, and then re-enters instruction with a clear transition routine.

## Design Implications

Sustained seated attention degrades over the course of a lesson, particularly for younger learners; brief physical activity appears to restore on-task behavior and attention following the break [~M]. The evidence base is strongest for effects on time-on-task and classroom behavior, weaker and more mixed for direct effects on academic achievement [~W]. Breaks work as attention management rather than as a learning mechanism themselves — their value is in preparing learners to engage with subsequent instruction, making them a complement to [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) rather than a substitute for it.
