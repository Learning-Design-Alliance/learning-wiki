# Process Writing

## Description
Process writing treats composing as a recursive cycle — planning, drafting, revising, and editing — rather than a single-pass product to be graded. Instruction makes each stage explicit, provides [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) and [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) during composition, and gives learners time and structure to revise before publication or assessment.

## Design Implications

Process writing works because it externalizes and distributes the cognitive demands of composing: novice writers juggle planning, transcription, and revision within working memory, and staging the work reduces that load [Working memory overload degrades learning and performance.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows process writing instruction produces substantial gains in writing quality, especially when it includes explicit strategy instruction and structured peer collaboration [Process writing instruction improves writing quality.](../claims/process-writing-instruction-improves-writing-quality.md) [+S]. The approach shifts assessment from a single summative judgment to [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md), with feedback timed to be actionable during drafting rather than after grading.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit teaching of each stage (planning, drafting, revising, editing) — not just time to write
- Multiple drafting cycles with feedback between drafts, from teacher, peers, or both
- Mini-lessons targeting specific skills (e.g., topic sentences, evidence integration) delivered when learners need them
- Authentic audiences or purposes so revision has a reason beyond the grade ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))

#### Constraints
- Unstructured "just write and revise" versions without explicit strategy instruction show much weaker effects [Process writing instruction improves writing quality.](../claims/process-writing-instruction-improves-writing-quality.md) [~S] — the cycle alone is not the active ingredient
- Weak peer feedback can mislead; peer review requires training and protocols to be productive
- Over-emphasis on process can stall fluent writers in endless revision; editing must be separated from composing or transcription quality suffers
- Time-intensive: multiple full cycles per piece reduce the number of compositions per term

#### Implementation Variability
- **Strategy-focused** (e.g., Self-Regulated Strategy Development): teaches genre-specific planning and revising strategies with self-regulation supports — strongest evidence base
- **Workshop model** (e.g., Calkins' Units of Study): mini-lesson, sustained writing time, conferring, share
- **Collaborative composing**: joint drafting and peer response; collaborative writing improves text quality when roles and accountability are structured [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- **Digital tools**: version history and commenting (Google Docs, Perusall) make revision visible and feedback asynchronous

### Target Learners
- Struggling and novice writers, who benefit most from explicit stage-by-stage structure and modeled planning [Process writing instruction improves writing quality.](../claims/process-writing-instruction-improves-writing-quality.md) [+S]
- English learners, when paired with [accessible vocabulary and syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and sentence-level scaffolds
- Proficient writers need less stage scaffolding; excessive structure can suppress their own composing strategies [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Writing quality: organization, elaboration, audience awareness
- Self-regulated composing: planning, monitoring, and revising one's own text
- Genre knowledge: understanding how purpose and audience shape text structure

### Instructions
1. **Model the stage**: think aloud while planning or revising a sample text, making expert decisions visible (cf. [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) modeling phase)
2. **Guide planning**: use graphic organizers or strategy prompts (audience, purpose, ideas) before drafting
3. **Draft without editing**: separate idea generation from transcription to protect working memory
4. **Structured feedback**: peer or teacher response against a criteria rubric between drafts
5. **Revise for meaning, then edit for mechanics**: two distinct passes with different focus
6. **Publish and reflect**: share with a real audience and have writers articulate what they changed and why

## Related Strategies
- Peer review and structured feedback protocols — the engine of the revision stage
- Strategy instruction in specific genres — supplies the content of mini-lessons
- Conferencing and writing conferences — individualized coaching within the cycle

## Examples
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD)** — a well-validated process approach embedding goal setting and self-monitoring in the writing cycle; see resources at [IRIS Center](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu/)
- **Calkins' Units of Study** ([Heinemann](https://www.heinemann.com/unitsofstudy/)) — workshop-model writing curriculum with mini-lessons, conferring, and publication
- **National Writing Project** ([nwp.org](https://www.nwp.org)) — teacher network built around process approaches to writing instruction
