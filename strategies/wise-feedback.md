# Wise Feedback

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Wise feedback is critical feedback deliberately framed to convey two messages at once: "I'm giving you this feedback because I have high standards" and "I'm giving it because I know you can meet them." It pairs substantive, actionable critique with explicit statements of the giver's confidence in the learner's capacity to improve, so that criticism is interpreted as investment rather than as evidence of low ability or bias.

## Design Implications

Wise feedback works because critical feedback is ambiguous: learners — especially those from stigmatized or historically underserved groups — may read harsh comments as confirmation that they do not belong or cannot succeed. Explicitly attributing the critique to high standards and confidence resolves that ambiguity, protecting the learner's sense of [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) while preserving the informational value of the critique [~S]. Field experiments show that a brief wise-framing note attached to instructor feedback improved revision rates and essay quality, with the largest gains among Black and Latino students [~S]. The framing is not a substitute for feedback quality: the critique itself must be specific, actionable, and genuinely aimed at improvement.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Substantive, specific, actionable critique — wise framing amplifies real feedback, it cannot replace it
- An explicit attribution statement linking the critique to high standards and expressed confidence in the learner
- A credible relationship or at least a plausible signal of investment; framing from a disengaged source rings hollow
- A revision or application opportunity so the learner can act on the feedback

#### Constraints
- Ineffective or backfiring when the accompanying feedback is vague, purely evaluative, or not actually actionable — the confidence statement then reads as insincere [~M]
- Requires consistency: a single wise-framed note amid otherwise impersonal or dismissive communication can increase perceived hypocrisy [~W]
- Adds instructor time per learner; scaling typically requires templates or structured peer-feedback protocols rather than fully individualized prose
- Less necessary when the learner already trusts the giver and has an established history of success; the framing is redundant for confident high-achievers [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Written preamble on graded work ("I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know you can reach them")
- Verbal framing in conferences or office hours
- Structured peer feedback protocols that teach students to open critique with standards-and-confidence statements
- Automated feedback systems that include adaptive, effort-and-strategy-attributed framing language

### Target Learners
- Students from groups subject to negative ability stereotypes, for whom ambiguous criticism is most likely to be interpreted as bias [~S]
- Students early in a course or program, before trust with the instructor is established
- Struggling learners who need critique but are most vulnerable to disengaging from it; related belonging work shows similar populations benefit from social-psychological framing [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Improving product quality through revision (writing, design, problem solutions)
- Sustaining motivation and engagement through cycles of critique and improvement
- Building learners' tolerance for and use of critical feedback — a metacognitive and dispositional outcome

### Instructions
1. Diagnose the work and write specific, actionable critique tied to criteria, as in any [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) approach.
2. Add a brief framing statement attributing the critique to high standards and confidence in the learner's capacity ("I wouldn't take the time to write this if I didn't think you could do substantially better").
3. Ensure the critique names concrete next steps the learner can take.
4. Provide a revision opportunity so the feedback can be acted on immediately.
5. Repeat the framing consistently across the term so it becomes a credible pattern, not a one-off.

## Examples
- **Yeager & Cohen (2012) field experiments** — Seventh-grade teachers attached a single wise-framing sentence to essay feedback; students who received it were roughly twice as likely to revise and improved essay quality, with the largest effects among Black and Latino students.
- **Writing-intensive college courses** — Instructors open graded comments with a standards-and-confidence preamble before detailed critique, then require a revision pass.
- **Peer review protocols (e.g., [Peergrade](https://peergrade.io))** — Structured peer feedback rubrics prompt reviewers to state criteria and constructive intent before listing weaknesses.
