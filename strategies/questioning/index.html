Questioning¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 6 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 1 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Questioning is the deliberate use of teacher- and learner-posed questions to elicit thinking, assess understanding, and drive discussion. Effective questioning yields immediate feedback on student understanding, supports informal and formative assessment, and captures feedback on the effectiveness of teaching strategies. It opens opportunities for students to discuss, argue, and express alternative points of view, and can create links between content and students' lives.

Design Implications¶

Questioning functions as both an assessment mechanism and a learning mechanism: when learners answer and explain, they engage in generative processing that strengthens conceptual understanding Answering and explaining questions improves conceptual understanding more than passive reception. [+S]. The quality of learning depends less on the number of questions than on their cognitive level and the time given to answer — extending wait time beyond three seconds increases response length and quality [~S]. Questions should be planned, sequenced from recall toward reasoning, and distributed across the room rather than concentrated on volunteers.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Prepared, purposeful questions aligned to learning objectives, with a mix of Conceptual Questioning and lower-order checks
  • Adequate wait time and a classroom climate where wrong answers are treated as information, not failure
  • Facilitation skill: probing follow-ups, redirecting to other students, and connecting responses (Eliciting Student Thinking)

Constraints¶

  • Rapid-fire, low-level questioning can devolve into guess-the-teacher's-answer and suppress deep thinking [-M]
  • Public questioning can intimidate anxious or low-confidence students; cold-calling without norms of psychological safety reduces participation [-M]
  • Closed recall questions dominate in many classrooms and rarely elicit reasoning [~S]
  • Whole-class questioning gives the teacher feedback on only the respondent; unresponsive students' misconceptions remain hidden [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Think-pair-share and no-hands strategies (random name selection) distribute participation
  • Hinge questions: single multiple-choice items with diagnostic distractors used at a lesson's critical point
  • Student-generated questions shift learners from answering to interrogating content
  • Digital response systems (e.g., Peer Instruction with clickers) aggregate anonymous answers, lowering social risk

Target Learners¶

  • All learner levels; question difficulty can be tiered to individual readiness
  • Reluctant or quiet learners benefit from structures that lower the social cost of responding [~M]
  • Novices need more scaffolded, lower-floor questions; experts-reversal logic applies — highly guided questioning becomes redundant as expertise grows Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Formative assessment: surfacing misconceptions and adjusting instruction in real time
  • Conceptual understanding: questions that require explanation and justification Answering and explaining questions improves conceptual understanding more than passive reception. [+S]
  • Critical thinking and argumentation: comparing alternatives and defending positions
  • Classroom discourse norms: learning to ask and respond to questions of peers

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the key ideas of the lesson and draft questions in advance, sequenced from recall to reasoning (Conceptual Questioning)
  2. Open with a question that activates relevant prior knowledge (Activation)
  3. Pose the question to the whole class, then pause at least 3–5 seconds before calling on anyone
  4. Use Eliciting Student Thinking follow-ups: "Why?", "How do you know?", "What would happen if…?"
  5. Distribute responses across the room; use think-pair-share or random selection to avoid volunteer-only participation
  6. Treat incorrect answers as diagnostic data — probe the reasoning behind them and adjust instruction (Assessment for Learning)
  7. Close by having students summarize or pose their own questions, consolidating the discussion

Related Strategies¶

  • Class Discussion — questioning is the primary engine of productive discussion
  • Cold Calling — a distribution technique that pairs with questioning norms
  • Think-Pair-Share — lowers the stakes of public questioning and increases response quality
  • Formative Assessment — questioning is the most frequent formative-assessment tool in classrooms

Related Elements¶

  • Eliciting Student Thinking — the diagnostic purpose of questioning
  • Conceptual Questioning — question types that target underlying structure rather than recall
  • Class Discussion — the discourse format questioning drives
  • Coaching — one-on-one questioning as guided practice

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Formative Assessment — questioning as the eliciting-evidence loop
  • Direct Instruction — checks for understanding between segments
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — questioning during the articulation and reflection phases

Examples¶

  • Dylan Wiliam's formative-assessment work in UK classrooms — teacher-developed hinge questions and no-hands-up policies as core questioning routines (https://www.dylanwiliam.org)
  • Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — conceptual multiple-choice questions answered individually, discussed in pairs, and re-answered; questioning drives the entire learning cycle
  • Socratic seminars in ELA classrooms — student-led questioning of a shared text, with the teacher modeling question quality early and fading
